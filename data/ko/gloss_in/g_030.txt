=== KEY: Concrete_c02221
HEADWORD: Concrete
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bitumen | Cement | Colosseum | Composite material | Steel
ENTRY:
[[Composite material]] of aggregate bound together with a fluid cement that cures to a solid. It is the most widely used building material and the most-manufactured material in the world; only water is used more. Cement-bound concrete differs from the less rigid, less durable asphaltic concrete, which has a bituminous binder.

When aggregate is mixed with dry Portland [[Cement|cement]] and water, the mixture forms a fluid slurry that can be poured and molded. The cement reacts with the water in an exothermic process called hydration, which hardens it over several hours into a durable, stone-like matrix. Most structural concrete is poured with embedded [[Steel|steel]] rebar to provide tensile strength, yielding reinforced concrete. Non-cementitious types include asphalt concrete, with a [[Bitumen|bitumen]] binder frequently used for road surfaces, and polymer concretes.

The word comes from the Latin *concretus*, 'compact' or 'condensed'. Concrete floors were found in the royal palace of Tiryns, Greece, dated roughly to 1400–1200 BC, and the Assyrian Jerwan Aqueduct (688 BC) used waterproof concrete. The Romans used concrete extensively from 300 BC to AD 476, making it from quicklime, pozzolana, and an aggregate of pumice. It freed Roman builders from the limits of stone and brick, and the [[Colosseum]] was built largely of it; the Pantheon has the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome. Modern tests show Roman concrete had a compressive strength similar to modern Portland-cement concrete, c. 20 MPa, but a far lower tensile strength. Its durability is attributed to volcanic rock and ash in the mix, and it resists seawater erosion better than modern concrete.

=== KEY: Concubinage_d5148b

Sexual relationship between two people who do not, or cannot, marry. Concubinage and marriage are often regarded as similar but mutually exclusive. === KEY: Concussion_f3c678
HEADWORD: Concussion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: CT scan | Chronic traumatic encephalopathy | Depression (mood) | Parkinson's disease
ENTRY:
Mild traumatic brain injury that temporarily affects brain function. A concussion results from a direct blow to the head or from forces transmitted to the head from elsewhere on the body. Symptoms may include headache, dizziness, difficulty concentrating, sleep disturbances, brief memory loss, blurred vision and mood changes, usually appearing within 72 hours. They can last two weeks in adults and four weeks in children. Common causes include motor vehicle collisions, falls, sports injuries and bicycle accidents. A [[CT scan|CT scan]] may be used to rule out severe head injuries, since routine imaging is normal after a concussion. Treatment involves relative rest for no more than one or two days, followed by gradual return to activity, and aerobic exercise may improve recovery. Worldwide, concussions affect more than 3.5 per 1,000 people a year. Repeated concussions may increase the risk of [[Chronic traumatic encephalopathy|chronic traumatic encephalopathy]], [[Parkinson's disease|Parkinson's disease]] and [[Depression (mood)|depression]].

=== KEY: Condensation_d6b59d
HEADWORD: Condensation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cloud chamber | Distillation | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Change of state of matter from the gas phase into the liquid phase. Condensation is the reverse of vaporization and most often refers to the [[Water cycle|water cycle]]. It is initiated by the formation of molecular clusters within a gas or at contact between a vapor and a liquid or solid surface, such as cloud condensation nuclei. It most commonly occurs when a vapor is cooled or compressed to its saturation limit. Direct transition from gas to solid is called deposition. Condensation is a crucial component of [[Distillation|distillation]], and structures such as air wells and fog fences collect water from it. Ions produced by a particle act as nucleation centers in a [[Cloud chamber|cloud chamber]], forming visible trails. Unwanted condensation in buildings can cause dampness, mold, wood rot and corrosion, and it is usually addressed by lowering indoor humidity or improving ventilation.

=== KEY: Condensed_matter_physics_2627fb
HEADWORD: Condensed matter physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Magnetism | Metallurgy | Quantum mechanics | Solid-state physics | Statistical mechanics | Superconductivity
ENTRY:
Branch of physics concerned with solids, liquids and other condensed phases of matter. The field studies systems of many strongly interacting constituents, applying [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], electromagnetism and [[Statistical mechanics|statistical mechanics]] to predict the properties of very large groups of atoms. Subjects include [[Superconductivity|superconductivity]], [[Magnetism|magnetism]] and Bose–Einstein condensates. Crystallography, [[Metallurgy|metallurgy]] and elasticity were treated as separate areas until the 1940s, when they were grouped as [[Solid-state physics|solid-state physics]]; the study of liquids was added around the 1960s. As of 2009, one-third of American physicists identified as condensed matter physicists. According to physicist Manuel Cardona, [[Albert Einstein|Albert Einstein]] created the modern field with his 1905 article on the photoelectric effect and his 1907 article on the specific heat of solids.

=== KEY: Confederation_9bfe77

ENTRY:
Formal alliance of [[Sovereign state|sovereign states]], usually created by a [[Treaty|treaty]], for common action. Confederations are typically formed to deal with issues such as defense, foreign relations, internal trade or currency, with the central government supporting all members. Member states retain their sovereignty and so generally have an implicit right of secession, although legal arrangements or centralization may constrain it. === KEY: Confidence_interval_a2fa2d
HEADWORD: Confidence interval
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bayesian inference | Sampling (statistics)
ENTRY:
Range of values likely to contain an unknown statistical parameter, such as a population mean. In frequentist inference, a confidence interval has lower and upper bounds derived from a [[Sampling (statistics)|random sample]], at a pre-defined confidence level typically 95%. The level describes long-run performance: if the same sampling procedure were repeated 100 times, approximately 95 of the intervals would contain the true population mean. === KEY: Conformity_4300da
HEADWORD: Conformity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Culture | Psychology | Social norm
ENTRY:
Matching of attitudes, beliefs and behaviors to [[Social norm|group norms]]. Conformity may result from subtle unconscious influences or direct social pressure, and it can occur when a person is alone, as when following social norms while eating or watching television. In Solomon Asch's line-judgment experiments, about one-third (32%) of participants sided with a clearly incorrect majority on critical trials, and about 75% conformed at least once across 12 critical trials. Conformity can also be beneficial, as in driving on the conventionally approved side of the road.

=== KEY: Confucianism_6a3934
HEADWORD: Confucianism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Analects | Buddhism | Confucius | Han dynasty | Qin dynasty | Song dynasty | Taoism
ENTRY:
System of thought and behavior originating in ancient China, variously described as a tradition, philosophy, religion, theory of government, or way of life. Also called Ruism, it was founded by [[Confucius]] in the Hundred Schools of Thought era (c. 500 BCE) and integrates philosophy, ethics, and social governance, with a core focus on virtue, social harmony, and familial responsibility.

Confucianism emphasizes virtue through self-cultivation and communal effort. Key virtues include *rén* (benevolence), *yì* (righteousness), *lǐ* (propriety), *zhì* (wisdom), and *xìn* (sincerity). These are tied to *tiān* (Heaven), which, though not an omnipotent deity, is upheld as a transcendent moral order. Tradition holds that he authored or edited the Five Classics, though scholars debate how far they took shape in his hands. His sayings are preserved in the [[Analects]].

Suppressed under the Legalist [[Qin dynasty]] (c. 200 BCE), Confucianism flourished under the [[Han dynasty]] (c. 130 BCE), becoming the dominant ideological framework. Under the Tang dynasty (c. 600 CE), in response to the rising influence of [[Buddhism]] and [[Taoism]], Neo-Confucianism developed; it became central to the imperial examination system and the scholar-official class of the [[Song dynasty]]. The abolition of the examination system in 1905 marked the decline of state-endorsed Confucianism. In the early 20th century Chinese reformers associated it with China's Century of Humiliation and embraced other ideologies, including Sun Yat-sen's Three Principles of the People and later Maoism. Critics argue that it can entrench hierarchy and authority; others credit it with fostering social cohesion and East Asian economic development. It remains influential in China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, and Chinese diaspora communities.

=== KEY: Confucius_286bd9
HEADWORD: Confucius
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Analects | Confucianism | Emperor Wu of Han | Golden Rule | Shandong | Spring and Autumn period | Warring States period
ENTRY:
Chinese philosopher of the [[Spring and Autumn period]], traditionally considered the paragon of Chinese sages. Born Kong Qiu, he is known in Mandarin as Kongzi, 'Master Kong'; the Latinized name was coined in the late 16th century by Jesuit missionaries. His teachings, called [[Confucianism]], emphasized personal and governmental morality, harmonious social relationships, righteousness, kindness, sincerity, and a ruler's responsibility to lead by virtue.

According to tradition he was born in Zou, in modern Qufu, [[Shandong]], in the state of Lu. His father, an elderly commandant of the local garrison, died when Confucius was three, and his mother raised him in poverty. He married at 19. In 501 BCE he was appointed governor of a town and eventually rose to Minister of Crime.

Confucius considered himself a transmitter of the values of earlier periods, which he claimed had been abandoned in his time. He advocated filial piety, endorsing strong family loyalty, ancestor veneration, and respect for elders. He championed the Silver Rule, a negative form of the [[Golden Rule]]: "Do not do unto others what you do not want done to yourself."

His ideas gained prominence during the [[Warring States period]] but suffered a setback after the Qin conquest. Under [[Emperor Wu of Han]] they received official sanction, and affiliated works became mandatory reading for careers in officialdom. Tradition credits him with authoring or editing the Five Classics, but modern scholars are cautious about attributing specific assertions to him, since some of the texts and philosophy were of older origin. Aphorisms of his teaching were compiled in the [[Analects]] many years after his death.

=== KEY: Conglomerate_geology_5da74e
HEADWORD: Conglomerate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Breccia | Calcium carbonate | Clay | Glacier | Sand | Sedimentary rock
ENTRY:
[[Sedimentary rock|Sedimentary rock]] made of rounded gravel-sized pieces, called clasts, set in a finer matrix. The matrix, which may be [[Sand|sand]], silt or [[Clay|clay]], is typically cemented by [[Calcium carbonate|calcium carbonate]], iron oxide, silica or hardened clay. Conglomerates form when rounded gravels deposited by water or [[Glacier|glaciers]] solidify and cement over time. They are closely related to sandstones in origin and probably make up less than 1% of all sedimentary rocks by weight. [[Breccia|Breccias]] are similar but have angular clasts. Conglomerates contain at least 30% rounded to subangular clasts larger than 2 mm in diameter. Orthoconglomerates, whose clasts touch each other, are typically cross-bedded and associated with flowing water, while paraconglomerates float in matrix and are often glacial tills or debris flow deposits.

Second-longest river in Africa, after the [[Nile|Nile]], flowing through Central Africa. ENTRY:
Belt of lowland tropical moist broadleaf forest across the [[Congo River|Congo River]] basin in Central Africa. === KEY: Congress_of_Vienna_a572d8
HEADWORD: Congress of Vienna
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: German Confederation | Hundred Days | Kingdom of the Netherlands | Klemens von Metternich | Napoleon | Vienna
ENTRY:
Series of diplomatic meetings held in [[Vienna|Vienna]] from September 1814 to June 1815 to settle Europe after the downfall of [[Napoleon|Napoleon]]. Chaired by Austrian statesman [[Klemens von Metternich|Klemens von Metternich]], the Congress aimed to balance the major powers so they could remain at peace, while restraining republican, liberal and revolutionary movements. France gave up Napoleon's conquests, while Prussia, Austria and Russia made major territorial gains. Historians disagree about its legacy. Critics argue that it suppressed national, democratic and liberal movements, while others credit it with protecting Europe from large wars for almost a century.

=== KEY: Conic_section_4d2ed5
HEADWORD: Conic section
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Analytic geometry | Apollonius of Perga | Circle | Ellipse | Hyperbola | Orbit | Parabola
ENTRY:
Curve formed when a plane intersects the surface of a cone. The three types are the [[Ellipse|ellipse]], [[Parabola|parabola]] and [[Hyperbola|hyperbola]]; a [[Circle|circle]] is a special case of the ellipse. Ancient Greek mathematicians studied conic sections, culminating around 200 BC in the systematic work of [[Apollonius of Perga|Apollonius of Perga]]. Conic sections also describe planetary [[Orbit|orbits]] and satellite trajectories.

=== KEY: Conifer_7997c9

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Group of woody, mostly evergreen [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperm]] plants that bear cones. There are over 600 living species. Each tree produces separate male and female cones, and the seeds are usually dispersed by wind. Conifers first appeared more than 300 million years ago in the [[Carboniferous|Carboniferous]] and dominated land plants during the Mesozoic, until [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] took over many ecosystems in the [[Cretaceous|Cretaceous]]. === KEY: Conjecture_6702eb

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Mathematical proposition put forward on a tentative basis, without proof. A conjecture is considered proven only when it is shown to be logically impossible to be false, and it becomes a [[Theorem|theorem]] once proved. Finding no counterexample is not proof: the [[Collatz conjecture|Collatz conjecture]] has been tested for all integers up to 1.2 trillion, yet a very large counterexample could still exist. === KEY: Conjunction_grammar_27b2b2

ENTRY:
Part of speech that connects words, [[Phrase|phrases]] or [[Clause|clauses]], called its conjuncts. A conjunction is an invariant, non-inflecting particle that stands between its conjuncts. Whether a word is a conjunction depends on each language's [[Grammar|grammar]]; in English, *after* can be a preposition or a conjunction, depending on syntax. === KEY: Consciousness_021c54
HEADWORD: Consciousness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coma | John Locke | Mind | Neuroscience | René Descartes | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Awareness of something internal to oneself or of states or objects in one's external environment. It has been the topic of debate among philosophers, scientists, and theologians for millennia. There is no consensus on what exactly needs to be studied, or whether consciousness can be considered a scientific concept. In some accounts it is synonymous with [[Mind|mind]]; in others it is an aspect of it. Medicine also uses a definition that distinguishes [[Coma|coma]] from other states.

The English words date to the 17th century and derive from the Latin *conscius*, 'knowing with', especially in sharing a secret. [[Thomas Hobbes]] wrote in 1651 that when two or more people know the same fact, they are said to be conscious of it to one another. [[René Descartes]] is generally taken as the first philosopher to use the Latin *conscientia* in a sense closer to modern 'conscience', and the modern concept of consciousness is often attributed to [[John Locke]], who in 1690 defined it as 'the perception of what passes in a man's own mind'.

Philosophers frame the central question as the hard problem: why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience. Distinctions are drawn between phenomenal experience, the 'what it is like' quality of feeling, and access consciousness, the availability of information for reporting and decision-making. In [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], research seeks the neural correlates of consciousness. Prominent theories include higher-order theories, global neuronal workspace theory, predictive processing theories, and integrated information theory, which quantifies consciousness as irreducible causal integration. Whether it can arise in artificial systems remains debated.

=== KEY: Consequentialism_e4ea1d
HEADWORD: Consequentialism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deontology | Jeremy Bentham | John Stuart Mill | Pleasure | Utilitarianism
ENTRY:
Class of normative ethical theories that judge conduct by its consequences. An act is right if it will probably produce, or is intended to produce, a greater balance of good over evil than any available alternative. Theories differ in what they count as good, with candidates including [[Pleasure|pleasure]], the absence of pain, the satisfaction of preferences and the general good. [[Utilitarianism]], a prominent variant, is associated with [[Jeremy Bentham]] and [[John Stuart Mill]]. Consequentialism is contrasted with [[Deontology|deontology]], which derives rightness from the character of the act itself, and with virtue ethics, which concerns the character of the agent. G. E. M. Anscombe coined the term in 1958. Critics argue that it can endorse sacrificing an innocent individual if the net outcome is positive, and that it subordinates individual rights to collective gains. Derek Parfit argued that, properly understood, rule consequentialism, Kantian deontology and contractualism would prescribe the same behavior.

=== KEY: Conservation_of_energy_1c1e6c
HEADWORD: Conservation of energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: General relativity | Kinetic energy | Mass–energy equivalence | Noether's theorem | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Law of physics and chemistry on the conservation of total energy in isolated systems. Energy can be neither created nor destroyed, only transformed or transferred from one form to another. In a closed system, total energy changes only when energy enters or leaves it. When dynamite explodes, chemical energy becomes the [[Kinetic energy|kinetic]] and potential energy of the fragments, heat and sound, and their sum equals the decrease in chemical energy. Classically, energy and mass were treated as separate, but [[Special relativity|special relativity]] links them through [[Mass–energy equivalence|mass–energy equivalence]] (E = mc²), so mass-energy as a whole is conserved. Mass can be converted to energy, as observed in the nuclear binding energy of atomic nuclei. [[Noether's theorem|Noether's theorem]] proves conservation from the continuous time-translation symmetry of physical laws. It follows that a perpetual motion machine of the first kind cannot exist. Depending on how energy is defined, [[General relativity|general relativity]] arguably violates conservation on the cosmological scale.

=== KEY: Conservation_of_mass_84fc92
HEADWORD: Conservation of mass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alchemy | Antoine Lavoisier | Chemistry | Fluid dynamics | General relativity
ENTRY:
Scientific law stating that the mass of a closed system remains constant. Mass can be neither created nor destroyed, though it may be rearranged in space or changed in form. In chemical reactions the mass of the reactants equals the mass of the products. Jean Rey demonstrated this in 1630, and [[Antoine Lavoisier]] confirmed it in the late 18th century, a formulation central to the progress from [[Alchemy|alchemy]] to modern [[Chemistry|chemistry]]. The law is widely used in chemistry, mechanics and [[Fluid dynamics|fluid dynamics]], where the continuity equation expresses it. It holds only in the classical limit. In [[General relativity|general relativity]], mass–energy conservation is subject to different definitions, and mass is not generally conserved in open systems.

=== KEY: Conservatism_6ed17f

Cultural, social, and political philosophy and ideology that seeks to promote and preserve traditional institutions, customs, and values. In Western culture, depending on nation and period, conservatives have sought to preserve institutions such as the nuclear family, organized religion, the military, the nation-state, constitutions, property rights, the rule of law, aristocracy, and [[Monarchy|monarchy]].

Huntington described conservatism as a situational ideology that defends the established institutions of its time. === KEY: Constitution_ae93e5
HEADWORD: Constitution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constitution of India | Constitution of the United States | Pope | San Marino | Treaty | United Kingdom | William Blackstone
ENTRY:
Body of fundamental principles or established precedents that form the legal basis of a state, organization, or other entity and commonly determine how it is governed. A constitution written down in one or more documents is a written constitution; one contained in a single comprehensive document is codified. The [[United Kingdom]] is a notable example of an uncodified constitution, set out instead in numerous acts of a legislature, court cases, and [[Treaty|treaties]]. A treaty establishing an international organization is also its constitution.

Within states, a constitution defines the principles on which the state is based and the procedure by which laws are made, and by whom. Some constitutions, especially codified ones, also limit state power by establishing lines rulers cannot cross, such as fundamental rights. Changes frequently require consensus or a supermajority. In most modern states the constitution has supremacy over ordinary statutory law, and an official act beyond the powers it grants is null and void from inception.

The word comes through French from Latin *constitutio*, used for imperial enactments, and was later used in canon law for a decree issued by the [[Pope]]. [[William Blackstone]] used it for violations of public trust serious enough to justify a revolutionary response.

The [[Constitution of India]] is the longest written constitution of any country, with 146,385 words in English; Monaco's is the shortest, with 3,814. The constitution of [[San Marino]] might be the oldest active written one, with core documents in operation since 1600, while the [[Constitution of the United States]] is the oldest active codified one. The historical life expectancy of a written constitution since 1789 is approximately 19 years.

=== KEY: Continent_0bb9e1
HEADWORD: Continent
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Americas | Eurasia | Madagascar | Pangaea | Zealandia
ENTRY:
Large terrestrial geographic region, identified by convention rather than strict criteria. A continent may be a single large landmass, a landmass with nearby islands, or part of a larger landmass, as Asia and Europe are within [[Eurasia]]. The number recognized varies from seven to as few as four. Most English-speaking countries recognize seven; in order of area, they are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia (or Oceania or Australasia). Variants merge regions, for example North and South America into the [[Americas]].

The criterion "large" is arbitrary: Greenland, at 2,166,086 sq km, is classed as the largest island, while Australia, at 7,617,930 sq km, is deemed the smallest continent. Most Pacific island countries and territories are grouped with Australia as Oceania.

In geology, a continent is "one of Earth's major landmasses, including both dry land and continental shelves." Geological continents correspond to seven large areas of continental crust on the tectonic plates and exclude small fragments such as [[Madagascar]], called microcontinents. Four criteria apply: high elevation relative to the ocean floor; a wide range of silica-rich rocks; crust thicker than the surrounding oceanic crust; and well-defined limits around a large enough area. Continental crust is known only on Earth, and emerged continental land covers approximately 29% of its surface. Debate continues over the submerged [[Zealandia]].

The English word dates from the 16th century, from Latin *terra continens*, "continuous land," and was once applied to small areas. Continental drift, which gained recognition in the 20th century, holds that the present continents formed from the breakup of the supercontinent [[Pangaea]] hundreds of millions of years ago.

=== KEY: Continental_shelf_6b1a20

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Submerged, gently sloping margin of a [[Continent|continent]], extending seaward from the shore to the shelf break. Much of the shelf was exposed by drops in [[Sea level|sea level]] during glacial periods. It is covered largely by terrigenous [[Sediment|sediment]] eroded from the land, and its width varies considerably. === KEY: Continuous_function_0ef133
HEADWORD: Continuous function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculus | Limit (mathematics) | Mathematical analysis | Metric space | Polynomial | Topological space | Topology
ENTRY:
Mathematical function with no abrupt changes in value. A function is continuous if small changes in its argument produce at most small changes in its value, so that its graph can be drawn without lifting the pencil. Continuity is one of the core concepts of [[Calculus|calculus]] and [[Mathematical analysis|mathematical analysis]]. Formally, a function is continuous at a point when the [[Limit (mathematics)|limit]] of the function there equals its value. Until the 19th century mathematicians relied largely on intuitive notions of continuity, and the epsilon–delta definition of a limit was introduced to formalize it. The concept has been generalized to functions between [[Metric space|metric spaces]] and [[Topological space|topological spaces]], the latter forming the basis of [[Topology|topology]]. The height of a growing flower over time is continuous, whereas the money in a bank account jumps at each deposit or withdrawal and is discontinuous. All [[Polynomial|polynomial]] functions are continuous everywhere.

=== KEY: Continuum_mechanics_e30137

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Differential equation | Elasticity (physics) | Fluid mechanics | Plasticity (physics) | Tensor
ENTRY:
Branch of mechanics treating materials as continuous media rather than collections of discrete particles. It deals with deformable bodies and with the transmission of forces through them. The model assumes that the substance completely fills the space it occupies, which gives an accurate description at length scales much greater than interatomic distances. Behavior is described by [[Differential equation|differential equations]] expressing conservation of mass, momentum and energy, together with material-specific constitutive relationships. === KEY: Contradiction_05f28a

Logical incompatibility of a [[Proposition|proposition]] with itself, with established [[Fact|fact]], or with another proposition. It is often used as a tool to detect disingenuous [[Belief|beliefs]] and [[Bias|bias]]. === KEY: Control_theory_c84c20
HEADWORD: Control theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dynamical system | Economics | James Clerk Maxwell | Operations research | Robotics
ENTRY:
Branch of engineering and applied mathematics dealing with the control of [[Dynamical system|dynamical systems]]. It seeks a model or algorithm that applies system inputs to drive a system to a desired state, minimizing delay, overshoot and steady-state error while keeping the system stable. A controller compares the controlled process variable with a set point, and the resulting error is fed back to generate a corrective action. Control theory underlies the automation that revolutionized manufacturing, aircraft and communications and created [[Robotics|robotics]]. Formal analysis began in 1868, when [[James Clerk Maxwell|James Clerk Maxwell]] analyzed the centrifugal governor. Stability criteria were contributed by Edward Routh and Adolf Hurwitz, and PID control was developed from 1922 by Nicolas Minorsky. As the general theory of feedback systems, control theory is useful in [[Economics|economics]], [[Operations research|operations research]] and the life sciences.

=== KEY: Cooking_3b9625
HEADWORD: Cooking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Columbian exchange | Fire | Homo habilis | Industrial Revolution | Maillard reaction | Potato | Pottery
ENTRY:
Art, science, and craft of using heat to make food more palatable, digestible, nutritious, or safe. Also called cookery, it ranges from grilling over an open [[Fire|fire]] to ovens and boiling, reflecting local conditions and traditions. It is an aspect of all human societies and a cultural universal. Preparing food with heat is an activity unique to humans.

Archaeological evidence of cooking fires dates to at least 300,000 years ago, though some estimate that humans began cooking as early as 2 million years ago. Controlled fire at Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa dates to 1 million years ago, and the oldest evidence of cooking, heated fish teeth from a deep cave, dates to about 780,000 years ago. Richard Wrangham suggested that early [[Homo habilis]] cooked regularly, but unequivocal evidence for controlled fire begins at 400,000 BCE. Widespread cooking fires are thought to have begun about 250,000 years ago, when hearths first appeared.

Techniques include dry-heat methods such as grilling and frying, moist-heat methods such as steaming and simmering, and combination methods such as braising. Cooking manipulates the chemistry of the proteins, carbohydrates, and fats in ingredients; heating sugars with proteins causes the [[Maillard reaction]], a basic flavor-enhancing process.

The invention of [[Pottery|pottery]] for boiling water expanded techniques. The [[Columbian exchange]] transformed cuisines: [[Potato|potatoes]], tomatoes, maize, chocolate, and vanilla reached the Old World, while cattle, wheat, rice, and apples reached the New. In the 19th century, cuisine became a symbol of national identity. The [[Industrial Revolution]] brought mass production, canning, and standardization of food, and fast food restaurants emerged in the 1920s.

=== KEY: Coordinate_system_a6e912

=== KEY: Coordinated_Universal_Time_8ef376

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Primary time standard used worldwide to regulate clocks and set civil time. UTC is based on International Atomic Time (TAI), a weighted average of hundreds of [[Atomic clock|atomic clocks]] worldwide. It stays within about one second of mean solar time at 0° longitude, the [[Prime meridian|prime meridian]], and it is not adjusted for [[Daylight saving time|daylight saving time]]. Top-flight men's national [[Association football|football]] tournament of [[South America]], held every four years. Eight of the ten CONMEBOL national teams have won the title, with Ecuador and Venezuela yet to win. [[Argentina]] has won the most titles, 16, and has hosted the tournament nine times. Since 1993 the field has generally included 12 teams: all 10 CONMEBOL teams and two from other confederations. The 2016 and 2024 editions had 16 teams. The United States hosted in 2016 and 2024.

Capital and most populous city of [[Denmark]], mainly on the island of Zealand. Settled as a Viking fishing village in the 10th century, it became the Danish capital in the early 15th century, with an urban population of 1.4 million. === KEY: Copper_a4090f
HEADWORD: Copper
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze | Cyprus | Latin | Malachite | Michigan | Smelting | Statue of Liberty
ENTRY:
Soft, malleable, ductile metallic element (symbol Cu, from [[Latin]] *cuprum*; atomic number 29). Pure copper is orange-red, one of few metals with a natural color other than gray or silver. Its electrical conductivity is very high (59.6×10⁶ S/m), and its thermal conductivity is second only to silver among pure metals. It is used as a conductor, as a building material, and in alloys such as sterling silver, cupronickel (marine hardware and coins), and constantan (thermocouples). Electrical applications account for over 60% of consumption.

Copper is one of the few native metals, occurring naturally in usable form, which led to human use from c. 8000 BC. It was the first metal to be [[Smelting|smelted]] from sulfide ores (c. 5000 BC), the first cast in a mold (c. 4000 BC), and the first purposely alloyed with tin to make [[Bronze|bronze]] (c. 3500 BC). The name derives from Latin *aes cyprium*, "metal of [[Cyprus]]."

Copper does not react with water but slowly forms a brown-black oxide layer that, unlike rust on iron, protects the metal from further corrosion. Green verdigris forms on old copper roofs and on the [[Statue of Liberty]]. Copper(II) salts give blue or green colors to minerals such as azurite, [[Malachite|malachite]], and turquoise and have been used as pigments. Copper compounds also serve as fungicides and wood preservatives.

There are 29 isotopes; ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu are stable. Copper makes up about 50 ppm of Earth's crust, the 26th most abundant element. The largest mass of elemental copper found weighed 420 tonnes, discovered in 1857 on the Keweenaw Peninsula in [[Michigan]]. Copper is essential to all aerobic organisms; adult humans contain 1.4 to 2.1 mg per kilogram of body weight.

[[Oriental Orthodox Churches|Oriental Orthodox]] Christian church based in [[Egypt]], also known as the Coptic Orthodox Patriarchate of Alexandria. Its head is the pope of Alexandria, who presides from Saint Mark's Coptic Orthodox Cathedral in [[Cairo]]. Tradition holds that Saint Mark the Evangelist founded the church in the middle of the 1st century AD. [[Christianity|Christian]] ethnoreligious group native to [[Egypt]], descended from the [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egyptians]]. Most belong to the [[Coptic Orthodox Church]], with a minority in the Coptic Catholic Church. Copts are the largest Christian population in Egypt and the [[Middle East]], and make up roughly 5 to 15 percent of Egypt's population. Their traditional language, Coptic, is a direct descendant of Demotic Egyptian. Copts have faced cycles of persecution and relative tolerance. Most live in Upper Egypt, where they often work in blue-collar jobs; those in Cairo and Alexandria have relatively high educational attainment.

=== KEY: Copyright_6016a2
HEADWORD: Copyright
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Intellectual property | Printing press | Public domain | United Kingdom
ENTRY:
Form of [[Intellectual property|intellectual property]] giving exclusive rights to copy, distribute, adapt and perform a creative work for a limited time. Copyright protects the original expression of an idea, not the idea itself. Its limits include fair use in the United States and fair dealing in the [[United Kingdom]]. Rights normally include reproduction, control over derivative works, distribution, public performance and moral rights such as attribution. Copyright usually expires 50 to 100 years after the creator dies, depending on jurisdiction, and the work then enters the [[Public domain|public domain]]. The concept developed after the [[Printing press|printing press]] came into use in Europe. The Statute of Anne of 1710 was the first legislation to protect copyrights in England and Scotland. The Berne International Copyright Convention of 1886 gave protection among signatory countries, but the United States did not join until 1989.

=== KEY: Coral_61db47
HEADWORD: Coral
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calcium carbonate | Cnidaria | Dinoflagellate | Photosynthesis | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Colonial marine invertebrate of the subphylum Anthozoa, in the phylum [[Cnidaria]]. A coral colony consists of many genetically identical polyps, each a sac-like animal with tentacles around a central mouth. Each polyp secretes a [[Calcium carbonate|calcium carbonate]] exoskeleton, and over many generations a colony can form a skeleton several meters across. Colonies grow by asexual reproduction, and corals also spawn sexually, often around a full moon. Most corals obtain most of their energy from [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] by symbiotic [[Dinoflagellate|dinoflagellates]] called zooxanthellae, which give them their color. Cold-water corals such as *Lophelia* survive at depths of up to 3,300 meters. Until the 18th century corals were thought to be plants, but [[William Herschel]] used a microscope to show that they have the thin cell membranes of animals.

=== KEY: Coral_Sea_f1d4c1
HEADWORD: Coral Sea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australia | Empire of Japan | Great Barrier Reef | Port Moresby | Tropical cyclone | World Heritage Site | World War II
ENTRY:
Marginal sea of the South Pacific off the northeast coast of [[Australia]]. It contains numerous islands and reefs, and includes the [[Great Barrier Reef]], which UNESCO declared a [[World Heritage Site]] in 1981. The Battle of the Coral Sea, a major confrontation of [[World War II]], was fought in May 1942 between the navies of the [[Empire of Japan]] and the United States and Australia. The battle thwarted Japanese plans to invade [[Port Moresby]] in New Guinea. Much of the sea is protected, including by the French Natural Park of the Coral Sea and the Australian Coral Sea Marine Park. Its warm climate brings frequent rains and [[Tropical cyclone|tropical cyclones]], and its reefs and islands are a popular tourist destination.
