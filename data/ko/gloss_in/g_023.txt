Force on the macroscopic boundaries of a confined space, arising from quantum fluctuations of a [[Field (physics)|field]]. It was named after Dutch physicist Hendrik Casimir, who predicted it for electromagnetic systems in 1948. === KEY: Caspian_Sea_af033a
HEADWORD: Caspian Sea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Azerbaijan | Endorheic basin | Iran | Kazakhstan | Russia | Sturgeon | Turkmenistan | Volga
ENTRY:
World's largest inland body of water, also described as the largest lake, in an [[Endorheic basin|endorheic basin]] between Europe and Asia. Its surface area is 371,000 km² (excluding the highly saline Garabogazköl lagoon) and its volume 78,200 km³. It stretches 1,200 km north to south with an average width of 320 km, and its surface lies about 27 m below sea level. Salinity is approximately 1.2% (12 g/L), about a third that of average seawater.

The sea is bounded by [[Kazakhstan]] to the northeast, [[Russia]] to the northwest, [[Azerbaijan]] to the southwest, [[Iran]] to the south, and [[Turkmenistan]] to the southeast. Its main freshwater inflow is the [[Volga]], Europe's longest river, which enters at the shallow north end. The name comes from the ancient Caspi people. Several states have called its status neither fully sea nor lake; a 2018 convention on its legal status set out cooperative management of resources.

The South Caspian Basin is a remnant of the ancient Paratethys Sea and has an oceanic basalt floor. The sea is estimated to be about 30 million years old and became landlocked in the Late Miocene, about 5.5 million years ago, through tectonic uplift and a fall in sea level.

The sea is home to a wide range of animal species, including [[Sturgeon|sturgeon]], and is famous for its caviar and oil industries. Pollution from the oil industry and dams on rivers draining into it have harmed its ecology. Its depth is predicted to decrease by 9–18 m during the 21st century because of global warming and desertification, a change described as leading to ecocide.

=== KEY: Cat_fa3ebd
HEADWORD: Cat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Carl Linnaeus | Carnivora | Cyprus | Domestication | Felidae | Fertile Crescent | House mouse
ENTRY:
Small domesticated carnivorous mammal (*Felis catus*), also called the domestic cat or house cat, and the only domesticated species of the family [[Felidae]]. It belongs to the order [[Carnivora]]. An obligate carnivore, it has retractable claws adapted to killing small prey such as mice and rats, a strong flexible body, quick reflexes, sharp teeth, and well-developed night vision and sense of smell. It is a social species but a solitary, crepuscular hunter. It communicates by meowing, purring, trilling, hissing, growling, body language, and scent marking, and it secretes and perceives pheromones.

The name *Felis catus* was proposed by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758. In 2017 the IUCN Cat Classification Taskforce regarded the domestic cat as a distinct species. The genus *Felis* diverged from other felids around 6 to 7 million years ago. The domestic cat and its closest wild ancestor both have 19 chromosome pairs and roughly 20,000 genes.

It was long thought that [[Domestication|domestication]] began in [[Ancient Egypt]], where cats were venerated from around 3100 BC. The earliest known indication of taming, however, is an African wildcat (*Felis lybica*) excavated near a Neolithic grave at Shillourokambos in southern [[Cyprus]], dating to about 7500–7200 BC. Scientists assume wildcats were drawn to early settlements in the [[Fertile Crescent]] by rodents, in particular the [[House mouse|house mouse]].

Today cats occur across the globe. Of an estimated 600 million domestic cats worldwide, 400 million live in Asia, including 58 million in China; about 73.8 million live in the United States. Feral cats avoid human contact, and their growing numbers, driven partly by pet abandonment, have contributed to the decline of bird, mammal, and reptile species.

=== KEY: Catalysis_be6374
HEADWORD: Catalysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catalytic converter | Chemical equilibrium | Chemical reaction | Enzyme | Hydrogen peroxide | Nitric oxide | Second law of thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Acceleration of a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] by an added substance, the catalyst, that is not consumed and remains unchanged afterward. It works by opening an alternative mechanism with a lower activation energy, which may be much faster than the uncatalyzed route. Because the uncatalyzed route remains possible, a catalyst can only increase the total rate, never decrease it. A catalyst has no effect on the [[Chemical equilibrium|chemical equilibrium]] of a reaction: one that shifted an equilibrium would amount to a perpetual motion machine, contradicting the [[Second law of thermodynamics]].

Catalysis is classed as homogeneous, when the catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (usually gaseous or liquid), or heterogeneous, when it is not, as with a solid surface acting on gases or liquids. [[Enzyme|Enzymes]] and other biocatalysts are often considered a third category. Manganese dioxide speeds the decomposition of [[Hydrogen peroxide|hydrogen peroxide]] into water and oxygen (2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂); in living organisms the enzyme catalase does the same work. [[Nitric oxide]] catalyzes the oxidation of sulfur dioxide (2 SO₂ + O₂ → 2 SO₃). Platinum in [[Catalytic converter|catalytic converters]] helps purify exhaust gas.

The SI derived unit of catalytic activity is the katal, measured in moles per second. Estimates hold that 90% of all commercially produced chemical products involve catalysts at some stage of manufacture. The term derives from the Greek *kataluein*, 'loosen' or 'untie'; Jöns Jacob Berzelius is credited with coining it in 1835.

Ballistic device that launches projectiles using stored potential energy, without gunpowder or other propellants. Most catapults release tension or torsion that was built up slowly by hand, using springs, bows or twisted rope. === KEY: Causality_physics_330a20
HEADWORD: Causality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bell's theorem | Fundamental interaction | General relativity | Quantum entanglement | Quantum mechanics | Special relativity | Speed of light
ENTRY:
Physics of the cause–effect relation. Causality requires that the cause of an event lie in the past light cone of its result and be ultimately reducible to [[Fundamental interaction|fundamental interactions]]; a cause cannot have an effect outside its future light cone. The weak causality principle operates at the microscopic level and need not lead to information transfer, whereas the strong principle forbids information transfer faster than the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. In [[Special relativity]], the cause must precede its effect for all inertial observers, since a faster-than-light signal would travel backward in time for some of them. [[General relativity]] extends the rule to curved spacetime. In quantum field theory, microcausality requires that measurements at spacelike-separated points cannot affect each other. [[Bell's theorem]] shows that local causality, tested in experiments on [[Quantum entanglement|quantum entanglement]], produces non-classical correlations predicted by [[Quantum mechanics]].

=== KEY: Cell_biology_71764b
HEADWORD: Cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Bacteria | Cell membrane | Cytoplasm | Microscope | Red blood cell | Robert Hooke
ENTRY:
Basic structural and functional unit of all forms of life. The term comes from the Latin *cellula*, 'small room'. A biological cell consists of a semipermeable [[Cell membrane|cell membrane]] enclosing [[Cytoplasm|cytoplasm]] that contains genetic material. Most cells are visible only under a [[Microscope|microscope]]. Except for highly differentiated types such as [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]] and gametes, most cells can replicate and synthesize protein, and some are motile. Cells emerged on Earth about four billion years ago.

Cells were discovered by [[Robert Hooke]] in 1665, who named them for their resemblance to the cells of a monastery. Cell theory, developed through the work of Matthias Jakob Schleiden in 1838 and Theodor Schwann in 1839, established the cell as the basic structural unit of plants and animals. It states that all organisms are composed of one or more cells, that cells are the fundamental unit of structure and function, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells.

All organisms are grouped into prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Prokaryotes, which include [[Archaea|archaea]] and [[Bacteria|bacteria]], are single-celled and lack a membrane-bound nucleus, having a nucleoid region instead. Most are the smallest of all organisms, 0.1 to 5.0 μm in diameter; the largest known bacterium, *Thiomargarita magnifica*, averages 1 cm in length and can reach 2 cm. A bacterial cell is enclosed in an envelope, typically a plasma membrane covered by a cell wall of peptidoglycan. Its DNA usually forms a single circular chromosome, and extra genes, such as those for antibiotic resistance, may sit on circular plasmids. Archaea have unique ether-linked membrane lipids, a form of metabolism called methanogenesis, and many extremophile species that thrive in extreme heat, cold, acidity, alkalinity, or salt. No archaean pathogens are known.

Eukaryotes can be single-celled, as in diatoms, yeasts, and some algae, or multicellular, as in animals, plants, most fungi, and seaweed. Their cells can be 2 to 100 times larger in diameter than a typical prokaryotic cell and have a membrane-bound nucleus, the largest organelle. Other organelles present in all eukaryotic cells include the endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, the Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes, endosomes, and vesicles. Plant cells also contain chloroplasts, which make sugars by photosynthesis. The diploid cells that form the body of an animal or plant are called somatic cells, which excludes the haploid gametes.

Study of the structure, function and behavior of the basic units of life. The field is interconnected with [[Genetics|genetics]], [[Molecular biology|molecular biology]], [[Microbiology|microbiology]] and [[Immunology|immunology]]. === KEY: Cell_cycle_f8441a
HEADWORD: Cell cycle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | DNA replication | Mitosis | Mutation | Neuron | Prokaryote | Protist | Skin
ENTRY:
Events leading to the division of a cell into two daughter cells. In eukaryotes, including animal, plant and [[Protist|protist]] cells, the cycle consists of interphase and the M phase, which includes [[Mitosis|mitosis]] and cytokinesis. During interphase the cell grows and replicates its [[DNA replication|DNA]], and checkpoints control whether it proceeds. Membrane-bound organelle of [[Eukaryote|eukaryotic]] cells, containing nearly all of the cell's genome. === KEY: Cell_signaling_c43e4f

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Cell membrane | Diabetes | Hormone | Ion | Lipid | Peptide
ENTRY:
Biological process by which cells interact with themselves, with other cells and with their environment. Signals are mostly chemical molecules that bind and activate a specific receptor, such as [[Ion|ions]], [[Lipid|lipids]] and [[Peptide|peptides]], though physical cues such as pressure, voltage, temperature and light also serve. === KEY: Cellular_respiration_a54b70

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Amino acid | Citric acid cycle | Fatty acid | Fermentation | Glycolysis | Oxygen | Sugar
ENTRY:
Process by which cells oxidize fuels such as [[Sugar|sugars]], [[Amino acid|amino acids]] and [[Fatty acid|fatty acids]] to produce [[Adenosine triphosphate|adenosine triphosphate]] (ATP), which stores chemical energy in a usable form. === KEY: Cenozoic_b054cc
HEADWORD: Cenozoic

ENTRY:
Current geological era of Earth's history, covering the last 66 million years and the most recent of three eras of the [[Phanerozoic|Phanerozoic]] Eon. Its name means "new life," and the era is characterized by the dominance of [[Mammal|mammals]], insects, birds and flowering plants. It began with the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event|Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]], which killed many species, including the non-avian [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]], and which most experts attribute to the Chicxulub impactor. Because mammals dominated the land in both hemispheres, the era is often called the Age of Mammals. Mammals and birds diversified greatly after the extinctions, and the continents moved into their present positions. Early Cenozoic climates were warmer than today, particularly during the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum, but the Eocene to Oligocene transition and the Quaternary glaciation cooled and dried the Earth. The era is divided into the [[Paleogene|Paleogene]], [[Neogene|Neogene]] and [[Quaternary|Quaternary]] periods.

Censorship may be carried out by governments or private institutions, and creators may censor their own works, which is called self-censorship. It applies to books, music, films, the press, broadcasting and the [[Internet|Internet]]. Stated justifications include [[National security|national security]], control of obscenity, [[Pornography|pornography]] and hate speech, protection of children, promotion or restriction of political or religious views, and prevention of slander and libel. Rules differ between legal jurisdictions and private organizations. Examples include the 1933 book burnings in Nazi Germany and state control of publishing in the [[Soviet Union|Soviet Union]].

=== KEY: Census_9e150a
HEADWORD: Census
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin | Roman Republic | United Nations
ENTRY:
Procedure of systematically acquiring, recording and calculating information about the members of a population, usually presented as statistics. The word comes from the [[Latin|Latin]] *censere*, "to assess." Most censuses are national population and housing counts, though others cover agriculture, businesses and traffic. The [[United Nations|United Nations]] recommends population censuses at least every ten years and defines their essential features as individual enumeration, universality within a defined territory, simultaneity and defined periodicity. In the [[Roman Republic|Roman Republic]], the census listed all adult males fit for military service. Modern censuses are essential for international comparisons of statistics. The United States has held a decennial census since 1790, as required by its Constitution to apportion representation and direct taxes.

=== KEY: Centaur_461384
HEADWORD: Centaur
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Greek mythology | Horse | Roman mythology
ENTRY:
Creature from [[Greek mythology|Greek mythology]] with the upper body of a human and the lower body and legs of a [[Horse|horse]]. Centaurs were said to live in the mountains of Thessaly and were often portrayed as wild as untamed horses. In the Centauromachy, a legendary battle with the Lapiths, a human tribe of Thessaly, centaurs who attacked the guests at a wedding feast were driven out. Chiron, an exception, was a wise teacher of heroes including Achilles and Heracles. Centaurs also appear in [[Roman mythology|Roman mythology]] and the medieval bestiary, and remain a staple of modern fantastic literature.

=== KEY: Centaurus_87ac09
HEADWORD: Centaurus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alpha Centauri | Centaur | Constellation | Dwarf galaxy | Milky Way | Omega Centauri | Ptolemy | Solar System
ENTRY:
Bright [[Constellation|constellation]] in the southern sky, one of the largest, and among the 48 constellations listed by the 2nd-century astronomer [[Ptolemy|Ptolemy]]. It remains one of the 88 modern constellations. In Greek mythology it represents a [[Centaur|centaur]], a creature that is half human and half horse. Notable stars include [[Alpha Centauri|Alpha Centauri]], the nearest star system to the [[Solar System|Solar System]], its neighbor in the sky Beta Centauri, and HR 5171, one of the largest stars yet discovered. The constellation also contains [[Omega Centauri|Omega Centauri]], the brightest globular cluster visible from Earth and the largest identified in the [[Milky Way|Milky Way]], possibly a remnant of a [[Dwarf galaxy|dwarf galaxy]].

=== KEY: Centaurus_A_c79960
HEADWORD: Centaurus A
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Centaurus | Star formation | Supermassive black hole
ENTRY:
Radio galaxy in the southern constellation [[Centaurus|Centaurus]], also known as NGC 5128. Scottish astronomer James Dunlop discovered it in 1826 from Parramatta, New South Wales. It is the closest radio galaxy to Earth and the fifth-brightest galaxy in the sky, though its distance (11–13 million light-years) and Hubble type, lenticular or giant elliptical, are debated. Its central [[Supermassive black hole|supermassive black hole]], with a mass of 55 million solar masses, ejects a relativistic jet that produces X-ray and radio emissions. Radio observations show the inner jet moving at about half the speed of light, and the radio jets extend more than a million light-years. The galaxy is also one of the nearest large starburst galaxies, and a galactic collision is suspected of triggering an intense burst of [[Star formation|star formation]].

=== KEY: Centipede_d51f75
HEADWORD: Centipede
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arachnid | Arthropod | Cuticle | Desert | Insect | Millipede | Tropical rainforest | Venom
ENTRY:
Predatory [[Arthropod|arthropod]] of the class Chilopoda, a group in the subphylum Myriapoda that includes [[Millipede|millipedes]]. Centipedes have elongated, segmented bodies with one pair of legs per segment. Despite the name, no species has exactly 100 legs; they have an odd number of leg pairs, ranging from 15 to 191. All are [[Venom|venomous]], injecting venom through pincer-like forcipules, which are modified front legs rather than fangs. Predominantly generalist carnivores, they live in habitats from [[Tropical rainforest|tropical rainforests]] to [[Desert|deserts]]. Lacking the waxy [[Cuticle|cuticle]] of [[Insect|insects]] and [[Arachnid|arachnids]], they need moist microhabitats and avoid direct sunlight, staying under cover or active at night.

=== KEY: Central_Africa_f9ba65
HEADWORD: Central Africa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angola | Cameroon | Central African Republic | Chad | Democratic Republic of the Congo | East Africa | Equatorial Guinea | Gabon
ENTRY:
Subregion of the African continent, defined differently by different bodies. The United Nations uses the term Middle Africa for nine countries: [[Angola|Angola]], [[Cameroon|Cameroon]], the [[Central African Republic|Central African Republic]], [[Chad|Chad]], the [[Democratic Republic of the Congo|Democratic Republic of the Congo]], [[Equatorial Guinea|Equatorial Guinea]], [[Gabon|Gabon]], the Republic of the Congo, and São Tomé and Príncipe. Its UN office also covers Burundi and Rwanda, which the geoscheme places in [[East Africa|East Africa]]. Six of these countries also belong to the Economic and Monetary Community of Central Africa and share the Central African CFA franc. The African Development Bank defines the region as seven countries. The Congo Basin, the world's second-largest river basin and a major tropical rainforest system, dominates the region's environment.
