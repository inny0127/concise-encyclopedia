=== KEY: Diabetes_52d261
HEADWORD: Diabetes
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amputation | Cardiovascular disease | Glucose | Hypoglycemia | Insulin | Metformin | Obesity | Pancreas
ENTRY:
Group of common endocrine diseases characterized by sustained high blood sugar levels (diabetes mellitus). It results from reduced production of the hormone [[Insulin|insulin]] by the [[Pancreas|pancreas]] or from unresponsiveness of bodily cells to its effects, and tends to progress in severity. Classic symptoms are excessive thirst, urination, and hunger, with weight loss and blurred vision. Untreated, it can damage the cardiovascular system, eyes, kidneys, and nerves.

The major types are type 1 and type 2. Type 1 is an autoimmune condition in which the immune system attacks the beta cells of the pancreas, preventing insulin production; it typically begins early in life and is treated with insulin replacement. Type 2, the most common type, occurs when the body becomes resistant to insulin, so [[Glucose|glucose]] remains in the bloodstream; it is managed with medications such as [[Metformin|metformin]], semaglutide, or tirzepatide and with lifestyle changes, and is strongly linked to [[Obesity|obesity]], physical inactivity, and dietary excess. Gestational diabetes sometimes arises during pregnancy and normally resolves shortly after delivery.

[[Hypoglycemia]] is a recognized complication of insulin treatment, with sweating, trembling, confusion, seizures, and rarely death.

Long-term complications arise from damage to blood vessels. Diabetes doubles the risk of [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]], and about 75% of deaths in people with diabetes are due to coronary artery disease. Diabetic retinopathy is the most common cause of blindness in people of working age. Diabetic nephropathy accounts for over 50% of patients on dialysis in the United States, and diabetic neuropathy can cause sensory loss, pain, and foot ulceration, the most common cause of non-traumatic lower-limb [[Amputation|amputation]].

=== KEY: Dialect_888845
HEADWORD: Dialect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: French language | Language | Morphology (linguistics) | Phonology | Social class | Syntax | Vocabulary
ENTRY:
Variety of a [[Language|language]] spoken by a particular group of people. Dialects differ mainly in [[Phonology|phonology]], [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphology]], [[Syntax|syntax]] and [[Vocabulary|vocabulary]]. The term most often denotes regional varieties, while varieties defined by [[Social class|social class]] are called sociolects and those of ethnic groups are ethnolects. An idiolect is the speech of one individual. A standard dialect is supported by institutions such as government recognition, schooling, dictionaries and grammars; the [[French language|French]] language, for example, is supported by the Académie Française. Nonstandard dialects have complete grammars but little institutional support. The line between dialect and language is often arbitrary and based on social, political or historical considerations.

=== KEY: Dialectic_21bfac
HEADWORD: Dialectic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Aristotle | Contradiction | Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel | Immanuel Kant | Karl Marx | Plato | Theodor W. Adorno
ENTRY:
Method of philosophical argument that advances thought by resolving contradictions. The term comes from [[Ancient Greek]] *dialegesthai*, meaning "to converse," and was popularized by [[Plato]]'s Socratic dialogues, in which people with different views sought truth through reasoned argument. [[Aristotle]] redefined it as a method for examining reputable opinions. === KEY: Diamond_8f7671
HEADWORD: Diamond
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abrasive | Antoine Lavoisier | Carbon | Carbon dioxide | Graphite | Igneous rock
ENTRY:
Mineral form of [[Carbon|carbon]], with its atoms arranged in a cubic crystal structure. It is the hardest and most thermally conductive natural material, which makes it useful for [[Abrasive|cutting]] and polishing tools. Its rigid lattice admits few impurities, but about one per million lattice atoms can color it blue (boron), yellow (nitrogen), brown or green. Most natural diamonds are between 1 billion and 3.5 billion years old and formed 150 to 250 km deep in the Earth's mantle. Volcanic eruptions carried them to the surface in [[Igneous rock|igneous]] kimberlites and lamproites. In 1772 [[Antoine Lavoisier]] showed that burning diamond in oxygen produced only [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], proving it is carbon. [[Graphite|Graphite]] is the stable form of carbon at room temperature and pressure.

=== KEY: Diary_edcf7e

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ben Jonson | Giza | Marcus Aurelius
ENTRY:
Written or audiovisual record of events, with discrete entries arranged by date. Diaries were traditionally handwritten but are now often digital. A personal diary may record a writer's experiences, thoughts and feelings, and someone who keeps one is a diarist. Diaries kept for institutional purposes include government records such as Hansard, business ledgers and military records. Personal diaries are generally intended to remain private. === KEY: Diatom_964d49
HEADWORD: Diatom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jurassic | Plankton | Silicon
ENTRY:
Usually single-celled alga with a silica cell wall. Diatoms generate about 20 to 50 percent of the oxygen produced on Earth each year and take in over 6.7 billion tonnes of [[Silicon|silicon]] annually. Their shells are a significant component of marine sediment. The silica wall, called a frustule, produces structural coloration and has earned diatoms the names "jewels of the sea" and "living opals." About 12,000 species have been documented. Under adequate nutrients and sunlight, a population doubles approximately every 24 hours. Diatoms are phytoplankton, the most common type of [[Plankton|plankton]]. Fossil evidence suggests they originated during or before the early [[Jurassic]] period. Diatomaceous earth, a deposit of their shells, is used for water filtration and as a mild abrasive.

=== KEY: Dictatorship_6a010c
HEADWORD: Dictatorship
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | Dictator | Fascism | Latin America | Propaganda | Roman Republic
ENTRY:
Form of government in which a leader or small group holds absolute or near-absolute political power. A [[Dictator|dictator]] rules through an inner circle of elites, including advisers, generals, and other high officials, whom the dictator appeases while repressing opposition such as rival parties, armed resistance, or disloyal insiders. Dictatorships may arise from a military coup or from a self-coup in which elected leaders make their rule permanent. They are authoritarian or totalitarian and are classified as military, one-party, or personalist.

The word *dictator* originated in the early [[Roman Republic]] for a constitutional office granting temporary absolute power to handle an emergency. Modern dictatorships first developed in the 19th century, including Bonapartism in Europe and caudillos in [[Latin America]]. In the 20th century fascist and communist dictatorships rose in Europe; [[Fascism|fascism]] was largely eradicated after World War II in 1945, while communism spread to other continents and remained prominent until the end of the [[Cold War]] in 1991. Personalist dictatorships in Africa and military dictatorships in Latin America became prominent in the 1960s and 1970s.

Dictatorships suppress freedom of thought and speech and use [[Propaganda|propaganda]] against alternative systems. They often present a democratic facade, holding elections that are not competitive. Stability rests on coercion and political repression: restricting information, tracking the opposition, and committing acts of violence. Those that fail to repress opposition are vulnerable to coups or revolutions, whereas those that came to power through revolutions tend to be exceptionally durable.

Some dictatorships have sustained economic development through decisive policy, but evidence on economic outcomes compared with democracies is mixed.

=== KEY: Dielectric_9d5ce2
HEADWORD: Dielectric
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capacitor | Michael Faraday
ENTRY:
Electrical insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field. Unlike a conductor, a dielectric has no loosely bound, free electrons to carry current. In an electric field its charges shift slightly from their equilibrium positions, producing an internal field that reduces the overall field. Dielectric properties govern the storage and dissipation of electric and magnetic energy, and they are important in electronics, optics and solid-state physics. A dielectric is the insulating material between the plates of a [[Capacitor|capacitor]], where polarization increases the capacitor's surface charge. The term was coined by William Whewell at the request of [[Michael Faraday]].

=== KEY: Diesel_engine_20dcc2
HEADWORD: Diesel engine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bus | Locomotive | Rudolf Diesel | Steam engine | Submarine | Truck
ENTRY:
Internal combustion engine that ignites its fuel through the heat of compression. Diesel engines compress only air, or air mixed with recirculated exhaust gas, and injected fuel ignites in the hot air. Power is controlled by the amount of fuel injected rather than by throttling the air, so the air-fuel ratio is usually high. Diesel engines have the highest thermal efficiency of any practical internal or external combustion engine, and low-speed marine engines can reach effective efficiencies of up to 55 percent. Named after German engineer [[Rudolf Diesel]], the engine was first used as a more efficient replacement for [[Steam engine|steam engines]]. Since the 1910s it has powered [[Submarine|submarines]] and ships, and it is used in [[Locomotive|locomotives]], [[Bus|buses]] and [[Truck|trucks]]. Controlling its air pollution is harder than for gasoline engines, so in the United States its use is now largely limited to larger vehicles.

=== KEY: Differential_equation_774ea8

ENTRY:
Equation relating one or more unknown functions and their [[Derivative|derivatives]]. In applications the functions generally represent physical quantities, the derivatives represent their rates of change, and the equation defines a relationship between the two. Such equations are central to mathematical models and scientific laws in [[Physics|physics]], engineering, economics and biology. Branch of [[Mathematics|mathematics]] concerned with smooth shapes and spaces, known as [[Manifold|smooth manifolds]]. === KEY: Diffraction_d14764
HEADWORD: Diffraction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustin-Jean Fresnel | Gravitational wave | Light | Siméon Denis Poisson | Thomas Young (scientist) | Wave | X-ray
ENTRY:
Deviation of [[Wave|waves]] from straight-line propagation, caused by an obstacle or aperture without any change in their energy. It is the same physical effect as interference, but the term diffraction is used when many waves are superposed. The resulting pattern maps the directions in which the waves travel after diffraction. Diffraction occurs for all waves, including [[Light|light]], sound, water waves, [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]], [[X-ray|X-rays]], radio waves, electrons and neutrons. The Italian scientist Francesco Maria Grimaldi coined the word and first recorded accurate observations of the effect in 1660. [[Thomas Young (scientist)|Thomas Young]] developed the first wave treatment in 1800, and [[Augustin-Jean Fresnel]] later devised a wave theory based on Huygens' principle. In 1818 [[Siméon Denis Poisson]] challenged Fresnel's theory by showing that it predicted light in the shadow of a circular obstruction, and Dominique-François-Jean Arago then demonstrated experimentally that such light exists.

=== KEY: Diffraction_grating_555c4d
HEADWORD: Diffraction grating
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DVD | Isaac Newton | Prism (optics) | Rainbow | Spectrometer
ENTRY:
Optical component of periodic grooves or slits, splitting light into several beams. Each beam, called a diffracted order, travels in a different direction. The angles depend on the angle of incidence, the spacing of the grooves or slits, and the wavelength of the light. Because a grating disperses light by wavelength, it is used in [[Spectrometer|spectrometers]] and monochromators. Reflective gratings have ridges on their surface, while transmissive gratings have slits. James Gregory observed diffraction patterns from a bird feather about a year after [[Isaac Newton]]'s [[Prism (optics)|prism]] experiments, effectively the first diffraction grating. The first human-made grating was made around 1785 by David Rittenhouse, who strung hairs between two finely threaded screws. Joseph von Fraunhofer, who made a wire grating in 1821, was the first to obtain line spectra with a grating. Closely spaced tracks on CDs and [[DVD|DVDs]] produce [[Rainbow|rainbow]] colors through the same effect.

=== KEY: Diffusion_c9fc47
HEADWORD: Diffusion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood | Convection | Gibbs free energy | Latin | Lung | Statistics | Stochastic process
ENTRY:
Net movement of anything, such as atoms, ions, molecules or energy, from a region of higher concentration to one of lower concentration. It is driven by a gradient in [[Gibbs free energy|Gibbs free energy]] or chemical potential, and it can occur uphill, as in spinodal decomposition. Because particles move by random walk, diffusion mixes matter without directed bulk motion, which is the characteristic of advection; [[Convection|convection]] combines the two. Processes described by Fick's laws are called normal, or Fickian, diffusion. The word derives from [[Latin]] *diffundere*, "to spread out." As a [[Stochastic process|stochastic process]], diffusion is used in models in [[Statistics|statistics]], probability theory, information theory, neural networks, finance and marketing. In breathing, air moves into the [[Lung|lungs]] by bulk flow, and oxygen then diffuses from the alveoli into the [[Blood|blood]].

=== KEY: Digestion_c7783f
HEADWORD: Digestion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Enzyme | Esophagus | Gastrointestinal tract | Liver | Pancreas | Small intestine | Starch | Stomach
ENTRY:
Breakdown of large insoluble food compounds into small water-soluble components that can be absorbed into the blood plasma. It is a form of catabolism, usually divided into mechanical digestion, the physical breakdown of food into smaller pieces, and chemical digestion, in which [[Enzyme|enzymes]] break food down into compounds the body can use.

External digestion, in which enzymes are secreted into the surroundings and some products diffuse back, developed earlier in evolutionary history; most fungi still rely on it. Animals have a tube, the [[Gastrointestinal tract|gastrointestinal tract]], in which internal digestion occurs; it is more efficient because more of the broken-down products can be captured.

In humans, chewing and the wetting action of saliva begin digestion in the mouth. Saliva contains salivary amylase, which starts the digestion of [[Starch|starch]]; about 30% of starch is hydrolyzed into disaccharide there. The food, now a slurry mass called a bolus, travels down the [[Esophagus|esophagus]] to the [[Stomach|stomach]] by peristalsis. Gastric juice, mainly hydrochloric acid and pepsin, starts protein digestion; mucus and bicarbonates protect the stomach wall. Pepsin breaks proteins into peptides, which enzymes in the [[Small intestine|small intestine]] break down further into dipeptides and amino acids.

Partially digested food, called chyme, enters the duodenum, where it mixes with enzymes from the [[Pancreas|pancreas]] and bile juice from the [[Liver|liver]]. About 95% of nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine. Water and minerals are reabsorbed in the colon, where bacteria produce some vitamins that are also absorbed. Waste material is eliminated from the rectum during defecation.

=== KEY: Dinosaur_756fe9
HEADWORD: Dinosaur
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cretaceous | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Jurassic | Mesozoic | Triassic | Triassic–Jurassic extinction | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Member of the clade Dinosauria, a diverse group of reptiles that dominated land ecosystems through most of the [[Mesozoic]] era. Dinosaurs first appeared early in the [[Triassic]] period and became the dominant terrestrial [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] after the [[Triassic–Jurassic extinction|Triassic–Jurassic extinction event]] 201.3 million years ago, a dominance that continued through the [[Jurassic]] and [[Cretaceous]] periods.

Birds evolved from earlier theropods and are the only dinosaur lineage known to have survived the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]], approximately 66 million years ago. Dinosaurs are therefore divided into avian dinosaurs (birds) and the extinct non-avian dinosaurs. Birds number over 11,000 living species, while paleontologists have identified over 900 distinct genera and more than 1,000 species of non-avian dinosaurs, found on every continent.

Dinosaurs are usually defined as the descendants of the most recent common ancestor of *Triceratops* and modern birds. Major groups include ankylosaurians, stegosaurians, ceratopsians, ornithopods, theropods, and sauropodomorphs, divided between the Ornithischia and Saurischia. A 2017 analysis proposed a radical revision, placing Ornithischia closer to Theropoda than to Sauropodomorpha.

Dinosaurs had erect legs rather than a sprawling stance, laid eggs, and were ancestrally bipedal, though many groups became quadrupedal. The largest sauropods are estimated to have reached lengths of 39.7 m and heights of 18 m, making them the largest land animals of all time. Through most of the 20th century, scientists presumed dinosaurs to be sluggish and cold-blooded, but most research since the 1970s indicates active animals with elevated metabolisms.

The name was coined by Sir Richard Owen in 1842, from Ancient Greek words meaning "terrible lizard." Mounted skeletons are major museum attractions.

=== KEY: Diophantine_equation_775bea

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Polynomial equation with [[Integer|integer]] coefficients for which only integer solutions are sought. A linear Diophantine equation, *ax + by = c*, has a solution if and only if the greatest common divisor of *a* and *b* divides *c*, and then it has infinitely many solutions. === KEY: Diplomacy_d38f4a
HEADWORD: Diplomacy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Canaan | Diplomatic mission | French Revolution | Ottoman Empire | Ptolemaic Kingdom | Seleucid Empire | Sun Tzu
ENTRY:
Communication by representatives of states, intergovernmental institutions, or non-governmental institutions intended to influence events in the international system. It is the main instrument of foreign policy, and international treaties, agreements, and alliances are usually the result of diplomatic negotiations.

The term derives from the 18th-century French *diplomate*, based on the ancient Greek *diplōma*, roughly "an object folded in two," a folded document conferring an official privilege. Some scholars have criticized this account, pointing to the term's origin in the political context of the [[French Revolution]].

Some of the earliest known diplomatic records are the Amarna letters, exchanged in the 14th century BC between the pharaohs of Egypt and rulers of [[Canaan]]. After the Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC, the pharaoh and the ruler of the Hittite Empire concluded one of the first known international peace treaties. Ancient Greek city-states sent envoys for specific issues but kept no regular resident representatives. The Hellenistic states that succeeded [[Alexander the Great]], such as the [[Ptolemaic Kingdom]] and the [[Seleucid Empire]], often negotiated peace treaties through marriage alliances. In East Asia, rival states of the era of the strategist [[Sun Tzu]] relied on diplomacy to establish allies, barter land, and sign peace treaties.

Relations with the [[Ottoman Empire]] were particularly important to Italian states such as Genoa and Venice.

Modern diplomatic practice originated largely from 17th-century European customs. Beginning in the early 20th century, diplomacy became professionalized. The 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, ratified by most sovereign states, provides a framework for diplomatic procedures and conduct. Most diplomacy is now conducted by accredited officials, such as envoys and ambassadors, through [[Diplomatic mission|diplomatic missions]], most commonly consulates and embassies.

=== KEY: Disability_a15e51
HEADWORD: Disability
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Experience of any condition that makes it more difficult for a person to do certain activities or have equitable access within a society. Disabilities may be cognitive, developmental, intellectual, mental, physical, sensory, or a combination of these. They can be present from birth or acquired, and may be visible or invisible.

The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities describes disability as including long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments which, in interaction with various barriers, may hinder full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others. One estimate puts the number experiencing significant disability at about 1.3 billion people, or 16% of the global population.

Two main models seek to explain disability. The medical model treats disability as an undesirable medical condition requiring specialized treatment, and focuses on causes and cures, including assistive technology. The social model locates the limitation in society and focuses on accessibility and social and cultural attitudes. Critics of the social model argue that it underemphasizes biological constraints; its supporters emphasize the role of societal barriers.

The concept is debated among academics, the medical and legal worlds, and the disability community; one point of contention is whether to use person-first language (a person who is disabled) or identity-first language (a disabled person). Discrimination against disabled people is called [[Ableism|ableism]].

Historians dispute how disabled people were treated in antiquity. Some argue that the ancient Greeks practiced infanticide, citing the writings of [[Plutarch]], [[Plato]], and [[Aristotle]]. Others cite Hippocratic accounts and archaeological evidence of efforts to cure and assist disabled people, and argue that those writings reflect philosophical ideals rather than practice. Disability [[Activism|activism]] has pursued equal rights under the law and fostered a disability [[Culture|culture]].

=== KEY: Discrete_mathematics_27ce97
HEADWORD: Discrete mathematics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coding theory | Computer science | Euclidean geometry | Georg Cantor | Graph theory | Information theory | Mathematical logic
ENTRY:
Study of mathematical structures that are "discrete," in the sense of being countable or having a one-to-one correspondence with the natural numbers, rather than "continuous." Objects studied include integers, graphs, and statements in logic. The field excludes topics of continuous mathematics such as real numbers, calculus, and [[Euclidean geometry]]. It has been characterized as the branch of mathematics dealing with countable sets, whether finite or infinite, although there is no exact definition of the term.

Research increased in the latter half of the 20th century partly because of digital computers, which operate in discrete steps and store data in discrete bits. Its concepts are used in [[Computer science|computer science]], including algorithms, programming languages, cryptography, automated theorem proving, and software development.

Major topics include theoretical computer science, which draws heavily on [[Graph theory|graph theory]] and [[Mathematical logic|mathematical logic]] and covers computability, complexity, automata theory, and formal language theory; [[Information theory|information theory]] and [[Coding theory|coding theory]], which design efficient and reliable data transmission and storage methods; logic, the study of valid reasoning and inference; set theory, whose beginning is usually marked by [[Georg Cantor|Georg Cantor]]'s work distinguishing kinds of infinite set; and combinatorics, which studies the ways in which discrete structures can be combined or arranged. Number theory, probability, and proof techniques such as induction also feature prominently.

In university curricula, the subject appeared in the 1980s, initially as a computer science support course, and has since become a first-year course intended to develop mathematical maturity. The Fulkerson Prize is awarded for outstanding papers in the field.

ENTRY:
[[Probability distribution]] over a finite set of outcomes that are all equally likely, each with probability 1/n for n outcomes. It is a symmetric distribution studied in [[Probability theory|probability theory]] and [[Statistics|statistics]]. A fair six-sided [[Dice|die]] is a common example, with each face having probability 1/6, although the distribution of the sum of two dice rolls is not uniform. Such distributions can be defined over any finite set, including random [[Permutation|permutations]]. When the outcomes are the [[Integer|integers]] in an interval [a,b], a and b are the distribution's parameters and n = b − a + 1. Estimating the maximum of such a distribution from a sample is known as the German tank problem, after Allied forces used it in [[World War II]] to estimate German tank production.

Process of making unjustified distinctions between people based on the groups, classes, or other categories to which they belong or are perceived to belong, usually in a way that deprives them of legal or human rights. The group may be identified by features such as race, gender, height, age, class, religion, disability, or [[Sexual orientation|sexual orientation]]. It involves depriving members of one group of opportunities or privileges available to members of another.

The English word appeared in the early 17th century, from Latin *discriminare*, "to distinguish between," and was long a synonym for discernment, as in "taste and discrimination." Since the [[American Civil War]], the term evolved in [[American English]] to mean prejudicial treatment based on race, later generalized to membership in any socially disfavored group.

Moral philosophers often define discrimination as acts, practices, or policies that wrongfully impose a relative disadvantage on persons because of their membership in a salient social group; an individual need not be actually harmed, only treated worse than others for an arbitrary reason. A non-moralized definition does not treat discrimination as wrong by definition.

Recognized types include age, [[Caste|caste]], citizenship, and disability discrimination (see [[Ableism|ableism]]). According to [[UNICEF]] and Human Rights Watch, caste discrimination affects an estimated 250 million people worldwide.

Discriminatory laws and practices exist in many countries. Audit studies have found persistent disparities in hiring and lending, but researchers dispute their causes, including how far they reflect prejudice or inferences from observable signals of productivity. Countermeasures such as quotas and affirmative action, sometimes called reverse discrimination or positive discrimination, have been controversial: supporters say they redress past and present injustice, while critics cite stigma for beneficiaries and resentment among non-preferred groups.
