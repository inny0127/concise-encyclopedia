Indian mathematician-astronomer. Former Akan state in present-day [[Ghana|Ghana]], 1701–1902. === KEY: Asbestos_a63b61
HEADWORD: Asbestos
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carcinogen | Finland | Lung cancer | Mesothelioma
ENTRY:
Group of six naturally occurring fibrous silicate minerals that is toxic and [[Carcinogen|carcinogenic]]. Asbestos has been used for at least 4,500 years, when people in the Lake Juojärvi region of [[Finland|East Finland]] strengthened pottery with it. Large-scale mining began at the end of the 19th century, and for much of the 20th century asbestos was widely used as a fire-retardant building material until its health effects were more widely recognized in the 1970s. Asbestos is completely banned in 66 countries and strictly regulated in many others, yet around 255,000 people are thought to die each year from asbestos-related diseases. Russia, the top producer, produced an estimated 790,000 tonnes in 2020.

=== KEY: Ascomycota_4810de

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Phylum of fungi, known as sac fungi or ascomycetes, and the largest phylum of fungi. Together with [[Basidiomycota]] it forms the subkingdom Dikarya. Its defining feature is the ascus, a microscopic sexual structure in which nonmotile ascospores form, although some species are asexual. Familiar members include morels, [[Truffle|truffles]], brewers' and bakers' [[Yeast|yeast]], and the fungal partners of most [[Lichen|lichens]]. Ascomycetes ferment bread, alcoholic beverages and cheese, and produce [[Antibiotic|antibiotics]]. Many are [[Pathogen|pathogens]] of animals, including humans, and of plants, causing diseases such as apple scab, rice blast and powdery mildews. Species of [[Cordyceps]] parasitize and kill insects, and [[Model organism|model organisms]] such as Neurospora crassa and Aspergillus species are used in genetics research.

=== KEY: Asexual_reproduction_acb4b0
HEADWORD: Asexual reproduction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Bacteria | Fission (biology) | Komodo dragon | Parthenogenesis | Strawberry | Tulip
ENTRY:
Reproduction without the fusion of gametes or change in chromosome number. Offspring inherit the full set of genes of a single parent and are genetically identical to it, or clones. Asexual reproduction is the primary form of reproduction for single-celled organisms such as [[Archaea|archaea]] and [[Bacteria|bacteria]], which divide by binary [[Fission (biology)|fission]]. Some cells divide by budding, as baker's yeast does, and hydras reproduce by budding. Plants reproduce vegetatively, for example through [[Strawberry|strawberry]] runners and [[Tulip|tulip]] bulbs. In vertebrates the most common form is [[Parthenogenesis|parthenogenesis]], and some monitor lizards, including [[Komodo dragon|Komodo dragons]], can reproduce asexually. Asexual reproduction is fast and needs no mate, but it produces limited genetic diversity, which leaves populations vulnerable to environmental change, disease or predators.

=== KEY: Ashoka_dc8437
HEADWORD: Ashoka
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Afghanistan | Aramaic | Bangladesh | Buddhism | Flag of India | Magadha | Maurya Empire
ENTRY:
Emperor of [[Magadha]] and third ruler of the [[Maurya Empire]] (r. c. 268–232 BC), also spelled Asoka.

His empire covered much of the Indian subcontinent, from present-day [[Afghanistan]] in the west to present-day [[Bangladesh]] in the east, with its capital at Pataliputra. According to his edicts, in his eighth regnal year (c. 260 BC) he conquered Kalinga after a brutal war; the edicts claim over 100,000 deaths and 150,000 deportations and express his remorse. He then devoted himself to *dhamma*, or righteous conduct, a policy emphasizing non-violence, tolerance of sects, animal welfare, and public works.

The edicts, inscribed in Prakrit in Brahmi script on rocks and pillars, with versions in Greek and [[Aramaic]] in western provinces, are the earliest self-representations of imperial power in the Indian subcontinent. They suggest that a few years after the Kalinga War he was gradually drawn toward [[Buddhism]]. Buddhist legends credit him with establishing many stupas, patronizing the Third Buddhist council, and supporting missionaries, including his son Mahinda to Sri Lanka.

The sources have limits. The inscriptions focus mainly on dhamma, and the American scholar John S. Strong has suggested they are sometimes best read as propaganda presenting a favorable image of Ashoka. The legends were composed by Buddhist authors long after his reign and fall into North Indian and Sri Lankan traditions that differ on events such as the Third Buddhist council.

Ashoka's existence as a historical emperor had almost been forgotten until the 19th-century decipherment of sources in Brahmi script. The State Emblem of the Republic of India is an adaptation of the Lion Capital of Ashoka, and the Ashoka Chakra appears at the center of the [[Flag of India|national flag]].

ENTRY:
King of the [[Neo-Assyrian Empire]], 669–631 BC. He inherited the throne as the favored heir of his father, Esarhaddon, and his 38-year reign was among the longest of any Assyrian king. === KEY: Asia_154a67
HEADWORD: Asia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caucasus Mountains | Eurasia | Herodotus | Himalayas | Mesopotamia | Silk Road | Suez Canal | Ural Mountains
ENTRY:
Largest continent in the world by both land area and population, forming the eastern part of the landmass of [[Eurasia]].

Asia covers more than 44 million sq km, about 30% of Earth's total land area and 8% of its total surface area. Its 4.7 billion people make up roughly 60% of the world's population. It is bounded on the east by the Pacific Ocean, on the south by the Indian Ocean, and on the north by the Arctic Ocean. The boundary with Europe is a historical and cultural construct, since there is no clear physical separation. A commonly accepted division places Asia east of the Turkish straits, the [[Ural Mountains]] and Ural River, and south of the [[Caucasus Mountains]] and the Caspian and Black seas. The boundary with Africa follows the [[Suez Canal]], the Gulf of Suez, the Red Sea, and the Bab-el-Mandeb, which makes Egypt a transcontinental country with the Sinai Peninsula in Asia. The border with Oceania is usually placed in the Indonesian Archipelago, near the Wallace Line.

The concept of Asia derives from the term for the eastern region from a European perspective, and the boundary has moved since classical antiquity. Anaximander placed it along the Phasis River in Georgia, a convention [[Herodotus]] followed; in the Hellenistic period it was moved to the Tanais, the modern Don. In 1730 Philip Johan von Strahlenberg proposed the Ural Mountains, and the Ural River prevailed in the mid-19th century. The name is believed to originate in the Bronze Age toponym Assuwa, which referred to a portion of northwestern Anatolia and appears in Hittite records of a rebellion against King Tudhaliya I around 1400 BC. The Roman writer Pliny was among the first to apply it to the whole continent.

Asia was the site of many of the first civilizations, including those of [[Mesopotamia]], the Indus Valley, and ancient China, and the birthplace of most mainstream religions, including Islam, Judaism, Christianity, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism. China and India traded places as the largest economies in the world from 1 to 1800 CE. The [[Silk Road]] became the main east–west trading route in the Asian interior, and the Straits of Malacca a major sea route.

The continent varies greatly in ethnic groups, cultures, economies, and governments. Its terrain includes the [[Himalayas]], whose Mount Everest rises 8,848.86 m, the Tibetan Plateau, and deserts such as the Gobi and Arabian, and its climates range from equatorial to subarctic and polar. It contains 48 sovereign countries.

=== KEY: Assassination_f5719d
HEADWORD: Assassination
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Arthashastra | Chanakya | Chandragupta Maurya | Julius Caesar | Maurya Empire | Order of Assassins | Philip II of Macedon
ENTRY:
Willful killing, by sudden or secret attack, of a prominent or important person, typically for political, social or ideological reasons.

Assassinations may be ordered by individuals or organizations and carried out by the orderers themselves or by hired accomplices. Assassination is one of the oldest tools of power politics. The Egyptian pharaoh Teti, of the 23rd century BC, is thought to be the earliest known victim. *The Art of War*, a 5th-century BC Chinese military treatise, mentions assassination tactics.

The word *assassin* is believed to derive from *hashshashin*, the name of a group of Nizari Ismailis known as the [[Order of Assassins]], who were active in the Near East from the 11th to the 13th centuries. === KEY: Asteroid_7bf3c0
HEADWORD: Asteroid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid belt | Ceres (dwarf planet) | Comet | Meteoroid | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Rocky, metallic, or icy minor planet that orbits within the inner Solar System or is co-orbital with a planet, as the Trojan asteroids are. Asteroids are larger than a [[Meteoroid|meteoroid]] (1 meter or larger) and are neither planets nor identified [[Comet|comets]]; a body that shows a coma when warmed by solar radiation is classed as a comet. They have no atmosphere and are broadly classified as C-type (carbonaceous), M-type (metallic), or S-type (silicaceous). Sizes range from rubble piles under a kilometer across to [[Ceres (dwarf planet)|Ceres]], a dwarf planet almost 1000 km in diameter.

Of the roughly one million known asteroids, the greatest number lie between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter, approximately 2 to 4 astronomical units from the Sun, in the main [[Asteroid belt|asteroid belt]]. Their combined mass is only 3% that of Earth's Moon. Most main-belt asteroids follow slightly elliptical, stable orbits and take three to six years to circle the Sun.

Ceres, discovered in 1801, was first considered a new planet. [[William Herschel]] proposed the term asteroid, from Greek words meaning "star-like."

The Galileo spacecraft made the first close-up observation of an asteroid. Later missions include NEAR Shoemaker at Eros, Dawn at Vesta and Ceres, and the Japanese Hayabusa and Hayabusa2, which returned samples of Itokawa and Ryugu. OSIRIS-REx collected a sample from Bennu in 2020 and delivered it to Earth in 2023. In September 2022 the Double Asteroid Redirection Test was deliberately crashed into the asteroid Dimorphos to test planetary defense technology. Near-Earth asteroids could cause catastrophe if they struck Earth, as the Chicxulub impact is widely thought to have done in causing the Cretaceous–Paleogene mass extinction.

ENTRY:

[[Ceres (dwarf planet)|Ceres]], about 950 km in diameter, is the only object in the belt large enough to be classified as a dwarf planet. LINK TARGETS: Antikythera mechanism | Astrology | Astrophysics | Cosmology | Hipparchus | Nicolaus Copernicus | Ptolemy | Stonehenge
ENTRY:
Natural science that studies celestial objects and the phenomena occurring in the cosmos. It uses mathematics, physics, and chemistry to explain the origin and evolution of planets, moons, stars, nebulae, galaxies, meteoroids, asteroids, and comets, and of phenomena such as supernova explosions, gamma ray bursts, quasars, blazars, pulsars, and cosmic microwave background radiation. More generally, it studies everything that originates beyond Earth's atmosphere. [[Cosmology]] is the branch that studies the universe as a whole. Observational astronomy collects data with telescopes, theoretical astronomy builds mathematical models, and astrometry measures the positions and motions of celestial bodies. Modern usage treats astronomy and [[Astrophysics|astrophysics]] as broadly synonymous. The word comes from the Greek *astron*, "star," and *nomos*, "law" or "rule." Unlike [[Astrology|astrology]], the belief that human affairs correlate with the positions of celestial objects, astronomy is supported by physics; the two fields share a common origin. Amateurs still play an active role, especially in discovering and observing transient events such as new comets.

Astronomy is among the oldest natural sciences. Early development was driven by practical needs such as agricultural calendars. Sites such as [[Stonehenge]] and the Nebra sky disc, and the megalithic structures at Nabta Playa in Upper Egypt, show ancient interest in the sky. The Babylonians began mathematical astronomy and found that lunar eclipses recur in the saros cycle of 223 synodic months. Greek astronomers sought rational explanations: Aristarchus of Samos proposed a heliocentric model in the 3rd century BC, and in the 2nd century BC [[Hipparchus]] cataloged 1020 stars and observed the drift now attributed to precession. The [[Antikythera mechanism]] (c. 150–80 BC) was an early analog computer for predicting the positions of the Sun, Moon, and planets. [[Ptolemy]]'s 13-volume work, known in Arabic translation as the *Almagest*, made the geocentric system the primary reference for over a thousand years.

Indian astronomers including Āryabhaṭa, Varāhamihira, and Brahmagupta integrated Greek models, and the Kerala school later refined planetary and eclipse calculations. Observatories were established in the Islamic world by the early 9th century. In 964 Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi described the Andromeda Galaxy, and the SN 1006 supernova was observed in 1006 by Ali ibn Ridwan and Chinese astronomers. In medieval Europe, Richard of Wallingford built an astronomical clock, Nicole Oresme discussed evidence for the Earth's rotation, and Jean Buridan developed the theory of impetus. [[Nicolaus Copernicus]] placed the Sun at the center in 1543, followed by Kepler's laws of planetary motion and Newton's law of universal gravitation. Space telescopes such as the Hubble Space Telescope now observe wavelengths blocked by the atmosphere.

=== KEY: Astrophysics_178c78
HEADWORD: Astrophysics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Cosmic microwave background | Dark energy | Dark matter | Electromagnetic spectrum | Galaxy | Gustav Kirchhoff
ENTRY:
Branch of astronomy applying the methods and principles of physics and chemistry to the study of celestial objects and phenomena. Subjects include the Sun, other stars, [[Galaxy|galaxies]], extrasolar planets, the interstellar medium and the [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]]. Emissions are examined across the [[Electromagnetic spectrum|electromagnetic spectrum]], and the properties studied include luminosity, density, temperature and chemical composition. Research covers [[Dark matter]], [[Dark energy]], [[Black hole|black holes]], and the origin and ultimate fate of the universe. The field developed from spectroscopy: by 1860 [[Gustav Kirchhoff]] and the chemist Robert Bunsen showed that dark lines in the solar spectrum matched bright lines of known gases, proving that the elements in the Sun and stars were also found on Earth.

=== KEY: Atheism_de0f15
HEADWORD: Atheism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Agnosticism | Classical antiquity | Theism
ENTRY:
Absence of belief in the existence of deities, contrasted with [[Theism|theism]], the belief that at least one deity exists. Less broadly, it is a rejection of the belief that any deities exist; in the narrowest sense, the position that there are none. Writers disagree on how to define it, and the norm is to define it as an explicit stance against theism. Philosophers distinguish positive (strong) atheism, the explicit affirmation that gods do not exist, from negative (weak) atheism, which covers all other forms of non-theism. Atheism has been regarded as compatible with [[Agnosticism|agnosticism]] but also contrasted with it, and many agnostics see their view as distinct.

Atheistic viewpoints can be traced to [[Classical antiquity]] and early Indian philosophy. In the Western world atheism declined after Christianity gained prominence, then resurged in the 16th century and the [[Age of Enlightenment]]. It achieved a significant position worldwide in the 20th century, and estimates of those lacking theistic belief range from 500 million to 1.1 billion people. Atheist organizations have defended the autonomy of science, freedom of thought, secularism, and secular ethics.

Arguments for atheism include the lack of evidence, the problem of evil, the argument from inconsistent revelations, and the rejection of concepts that cannot be falsified. Nonbelievers contend that atheism is the more parsimonious position and that the burden of proof lies on the theist. In some countries with apostasy or blasphemy laws, open identification as an atheist can bring imprisonment, violence, or the death penalty. Critics of state-mandated atheism, as in the Soviet Union, point to its accompanying suppression of religion.

=== KEY: Atlantic_Ocean_04f7e6
HEADWORD: Atlantic Ocean
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic slave trade | Atlas (mythology) | Christopher Columbus | Columbian exchange | Gulf Stream | Mid-Atlantic Ridge
ENTRY:
Second largest of the world's five oceans, an elongated, S-shaped basin lying between Europe and Africa to the east and the Americas to the west. Its area is about 85,133,000 sq km, roughly 17% of Earth's surface and 24% of its water surface. The Equator divides it into the North and South Atlantic. It connects to the Arctic Ocean in the north, the Pacific Ocean in the southwest, the Indian Ocean in the southeast, and the Southern Ocean in the south. Including marginal seas, its coastline measures 111,866 km.

The seafloor is split by the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]], a divergent tectonic boundary where new oceanic crust forms and the basin widens by 2 to 5 cm per year. The warm [[Gulf Stream]] carries heat northward along the western boundary and influences the climate of Europe and North America. The South Atlantic stays warm year-round, while the North Atlantic is temperate.

The name derives from [[Atlas (mythology)|Atlas]], the Titan of Greek mythology who supported the heavens; it appears in Stesichorus in the mid-sixth century BC and in the *Histories* of Herodotus around 450 BC. Early Greek sailors thought the Atlantic part of Oceanus, the great river encircling all land.

The Norse were the first known humans to cross the Atlantic, but the 1492 expedition of [[Christopher Columbus]] proved the most consequential, opening an age of European exploration and colonization of the Americas. From the 16th to the 19th centuries the ocean was the center of the [[Atlantic slave trade]] and the [[Columbian exchange]]. After World War II, notable conflicts included the Cuban Missile Crisis and the Falklands War. The ocean remains a core component of world trade.

Fictional island described in [[Plato]]’s dialogues *Timaeus* and *Critias*. Plato presents Atlantis as a naval empire from the west that had conquered most of Europe and Libya, an allegory on the hubris of nations. Set against the land-based [[Achaemenid Empire]] of the east, it loses divine favor after an ill-fated campaign against a fictionalized Athens and submerges into the [[Atlantic Ocean]]. === KEY: Atmosphere_9e607d
HEADWORD: Atmosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Jupiter | Mercury (planet) | Pluto | Titan (moon)
ENTRY:
Layer of gases that envelops an astronomical object, held in place by its gravity. An object acquires most of its atmosphere in its primordial epoch, through accretion or outgassing of volatiles. A body keeps an atmosphere longer when gravity is high and temperature low, and the stellar wind strips away the outer atmosphere. Aside from [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]], all Solar System planets have substantial atmospheres, as do the dwarf planet [[Pluto]] and the moon [[Titan (moon)|Titan]]. Atmospheres have also been detected around exoplanets such as HD 209458 b and Kepler-7b.

=== KEY: Atmosphere_of_Earth_10520d
HEADWORD: Atmosphere of Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric science | Climate change | Cyanobacteria | Greenhouse effect | Kármán line | Ozone depletion | Stratosphere
ENTRY:
Multi-layered gas mixture, commonly called air, that surrounds the Earth's surface and is retained by gravity. It contains variable quantities of suspended aerosols and particulates that create clouds and hazes. It shields the surface from most meteoroids and ultraviolet solar radiation, reduces temperature extremes between day and night, keeps the surface warm through the [[Greenhouse effect|greenhouse effect]], redistributes heat and moisture through air currents, and provides the conditions that allow life to exist and evolve. Average surface temperature is approximately 288 K.

By mole fraction, dry air is 78.08% nitrogen, 20.95% oxygen, 0.93% argon, and 0.04% carbon dioxide, with small amounts of other trace gases, including methane, nitrous oxide, ozone, and the noble gases neon, helium, krypton, and xenon. Water vapor averages around 1% at sea level and 0.4% over the entire atmosphere, accounting for roughly 0.25% by mass. Xenon is far scarcer than the other noble gases, a puzzle known as the missing xenon problem. The average molecular weight of dry air is about 28.946 or 28.964 g/mol. Up to about 100 km, turbulence mixes the gases so that their relative concentrations stay the same; above that, in the heterosphere, lighter gases become relatively more abundant.

The primordial atmosphere was mainly hydrogen and helium accreted from the solar nebula. Atmospheric escape, volcanism and outgassing, impact events, and weathering changed it into a secondary atmosphere, dominated by nitrogen and methane, by the Archean eon. The rise of [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]], which release oxygen as a byproduct of photosynthesis, eventually converted the reducing atmosphere into an oxidative one in the Great Oxygenation Event at the end of the Archean. Further rounds of oxygenation in the Proterozoic and Phanerozoic eons produced today's nitrogen-and-oxygen atmosphere. Human activity has contributed to [[Climate change|climate change]], [[Ozone depletion|ozone depletion]], and acid deposition.

The atmosphere has a mass of about 5.15×10¹⁸ kg, three-quarters of it within about 11 km of the surface. It thins with altitude and has no definite upper boundary; the [[Kármán line]] at 100 km is often used as a conventional edge of space. Five layers are distinguished by temperature behavior: the troposphere (0–12 km), where weather occurs and air is suitable for terrestrial plants and animals; the [[Stratosphere|stratosphere]] (12–50 km), which holds the ozone layer; the mesosphere (50–80 km); the thermosphere (80–700 km); and the exosphere (700–10,000 km). The study of the atmosphere is called [[Atmospheric science|atmospheric science]]; early pioneers include Léon Teisserenc de Bort and Richard Assmann.

=== KEY: Atmospheric_circulation_df71d4
HEADWORD: Atmospheric circulation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hadley cell | Jet stream | Ocean current | Trade winds
ENTRY:
Large-scale movement of air that, together with [[Ocean current|ocean circulation]], redistributes thermal energy across Earth’s surface. Driven by solar heating, the circulation carries energy absorbed near the tropics toward the poles. Its large-scale structure remains fairly constant, while smaller weather systems are chaotic and cannot be predicted in practice beyond about ten days. Each hemisphere has three cells: the [[Hadley cell]], the Ferrel cell and the polar cell. In the Hadley cell, moist air rises at the equator, moves poleward and descends near the 30th parallel. Surface air flowing back toward the equator forms the [[Trade winds]]. Over hundreds of millions of years, tectonic uplift can alter the [[Jet stream]].

Pressure exerted by the weight of Earth’s atmosphere, also called air pressure or barometric pressure. It decreases with altitude because less air lies above. A column of air with a cross-sectional area of 1 square centimeter weighs about 10.1 newtons from mean sea level to the top of the atmosphere, a pressure of 101 kPa. The standard atmosphere, 101.325 kPa, is roughly equal to mean sea-level pressure of 1,013.25 hPa. The highest sea-level pressure occurs in [[Siberia]], where the Siberian High often exceeds 1,050 hPa, with record highs close to 1,085 hPa. The lowest measurable sea-level pressure is found at the centers of [[Tropical cyclone|tropical cyclones]] and [[Tornado|tornadoes]], with a record low of 870 hPa. Pressure differences drive winds, and pressure is measured with a [[Barometer]].

=== KEY: Atmospheric_science_c99abe
HEADWORD: Atmospheric science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climatology | Meteorology | Weather forecasting
ENTRY:
Study of the Earth’s atmosphere and its physical processes, extended to the atmospheres of other planets. [[Meteorology]] includes atmospheric chemistry and physics, with a major focus on [[Weather forecasting|weather forecasting]]. [[Climatology]] studies average conditions and their variability over timescales longer than weather. Aeronomy examines the upper atmosphere, where dissociation and ionization are important. Instruments include satellites, radiosondes, weather balloons, radars and lasers. Atmospheric chemistry studies the composition of the atmosphere and its effects on living organisms, and has addressed problems such as acid rain, photochemical smog and global warming. Early pioneers included Léon Teisserenc de Bort and Richard Assmann.

=== KEY: Atoll_7d4849
HEADWORD: Atoll
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Lagoon | Maldives | Pacific Ocean
ENTRY:
Ring-shaped coral reef that encloses a lagoon. Atolls form in warm tropical or subtropical seas where corals can develop, and most are in the [[Pacific Ocean]]. In [[Charles Darwin|Darwin]]’s subsidence model, a coral fringing reef grows around a volcanic island. As the island sinks and erodes, the reef becomes a barrier reef and eventually a ring around a [[Lagoon|lagoon]], which is not the former volcanic crater. For the atoll to persist, coral growth must keep pace with rising sea level or sinking land. An alternative, the antecedent karst model, holds that rainfall dissolves a flat-topped reef into a saucer-shaped island with a raised rim, on which corals regrow. The word derives from the Dhivehi *atholhu*, a language spoken in the [[Maldives]].

=== KEY: Atom_0d218a
HEADWORD: Atom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical element | Democritus | Ernest Rutherford | Ion | Isotope | J. J. Thomson | John Dalton | Niels Bohr
ENTRY:
Smallest unit of a chemical element that retains its chemical properties, consisting of a nucleus of protons and generally neutrons surrounded by a swarm of electrons. The number of protons distinguishes one [[Chemical element|chemical element]] from another: any atom with 11 protons is sodium, and any with 29 is copper. Atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons are [[Isotope|isotopes]]. Atoms are typically around 100 picometers across, so a human hair is about a million carbon atoms wide. Their behavior cannot be accurately predicted by classical physics because of quantum effects.

More than 99.94% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus. Protons carry positive charge, neutrons none, and electrons negative charge; the opposing charges bind electrons to the nucleus. A charged atom is an [[Ion|ion]]: a negative ion has more electrons than protons, a positive ion fewer. Atoms attach to one another by chemical bonds to form molecules or crystals, and this ability underlies most physical changes in nature.

The word comes from the Greek *atomos*, "uncuttable," used by [[Democritus]] and his teacher Leucippus in the 5th century BC, but their idea rested on philosophical reasoning rather than scientific evidence. In the early 1800s [[John Dalton]] found that elements combine in multiples of a basic unit, which he called atoms. In 1897 [[J. J. Thomson]] identified the electron. In 1911 [[Ernest Rutherford]]'s gold foil experiments revealed the nuclear structure and showed atoms to be mostly empty space. In 1913 [[Niels Bohr]] introduced electrons in fixed energy levels, and James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932. Protons and neutrons are themselves made of quarks.

=== KEY: Atomic_bombings_of_Hiroshima_and_Nagasaki_21d295
HEADWORD: Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Manhattan Project | Nuclear weapon | War crime | World War II
ENTRY:
Nuclear attacks on the Japanese cities of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, August 6 and 9, 1945. The aerial bombings killed 150,000 to 246,000 people, most of them civilians, during the final days of [[World War II]]. They remain the only uses of [[Nuclear weapon|nuclear weapons]] in an armed conflict. The uranium gun-type bomb Little Boy was dropped on Hiroshima, and the plutonium implosion-type bomb Fat Man on Nagasaki, both produced by the [[Manhattan Project]]. The Japanese government ignored the Potsdam Declaration of July 26, 1945. Japan announced its surrender on August 15, six days after the bombing of Nagasaki and the Soviet Union’s declaration of war. Supporters argue that the bombings ended the war with fewer casualties and helped create the Long Peace. Critics argue that the bombings were unnecessary for the war’s end and were a [[War crime|war crime]].

=== KEY: Atomic_clock_ddcc27
HEADWORD: Atomic clock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caesium | Coordinated Universal Time | Earth's rotation | Global Positioning System
ENTRY:
Clock that measures time by monitoring the resonant frequency of atoms. The SI second is defined by the fixed frequency of the hyperfine transition of the [[Caesium|cesium]]-133 atom, 9,192,631,770 hertz. An ensemble of atomic clocks worldwide maintains the time scale on which [[Coordinated Universal Time]] (UTC), the basis of civil time, is built. UTC uses leap seconds to keep clock time within one second of [[Earth's rotation]]. Atomic clocks also support satellite navigation, including the [[Global Positioning System]], since a timing error of 1 nanosecond corresponds to a positional error of roughly 30 cm. The main type in use employs cesium atoms cooled near absolute zero. In 2025, the National Institute of Standards and Technology reported an optical clock based on a trapped aluminum ion with a systematic uncertainty of around 19 decimal places.

=== KEY: Atomic_mass_80fe86
HEADWORD: Atomic mass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dalton (unit) | Mass–energy equivalence
ENTRY:
Mass of a single atom, derived mainly from the protons and neutrons in its nucleus. Atomic mass is often measured in [[Dalton (unit)|daltons]] (Da), also called unified atomic mass units (u). One dalton is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom, so the atomic mass of a nuclide in daltons is close to its mass number. An atom’s mass is slightly less than the sum of its protons, neutrons and electrons, owing to mass defect, which is explained by [[Mass–energy equivalence|mass–energy equivalence]] (E = mc²). Relative isotopic mass is the dimensionless ratio of an isotope’s mass to one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. Because natural substances are usually mixtures of isotopes, the elemental atomic weight is the average of isotope masses weighted by abundance.
