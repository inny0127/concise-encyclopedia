PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Treatise on ethics by [[Aristotle]], in ten books. It asks how humans should live and identifies the good for human life as the goal at which all actions aim. Aristotle treats ethics as practical rather than theoretical, and he connects it to his [[Politics]]. The title may refer to Aristotle's son Nicomachus or to his father. The text appears to be lecture notes rather than a work Aristotle prepared for publication. In the Middle Ages it was one of the core works of philosophy, and [[Thomas Aquinas]] developed a synthesis of Aristotelian ethics with Christian theology. It remained the main authority on ethics at Protestant universities well into the 17th century, and it inspired the modern revival of [[Virtue|virtue]] ethics.

=== KEY: Nicotine_dfa7a5
HEADWORD: Nicotine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alkaloid | Carcinogen | Cigarette | Dopamine | Eggplant | Potato | Tobacco | Tomato
ENTRY:
Chemical stimulant and [[Alkaloid|alkaloid]] found primarily in plants of the nightshade family, notably [[Tobacco|tobacco]], and is also synthesized. Tobacco leaves contain about 0.6–3% nicotine by dry weight, with smaller traces in [[Tomato|tomatoes]], [[Potato|potatoes]] and [[Eggplant|eggplants]]. In humans nicotine acts mainly as a stimulant, activating nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and releasing [[Dopamine|dopamine]], which produces increased alertness, reduced anxiety and mild euphoria. Nicotine is highly addictive. An average [[Cigarette|cigarette]] yields about 2 mg of absorbed nicotine. Nicotine replacement products such as gums, patches and lozenges deliver it in slower, lower doses and are used to help people quit smoking; a 2018 Cochrane Collaboration review found that they raise the chances of quitting by 50–60%. Nicotine is not classified as a [[Carcinogen|carcinogen]] by the International Agency for Research on Cancer or the U.S. Surgeon General. At high doses it can cause poisoning and respiratory paralysis.

Main river of West Africa, about 4,180 kilometers (2,600 miles) long and the third-longest in Africa, after the [[Nile]] and the [[Congo River]]. Its drainage basin covers 2,117,700 km² (817,600 sq mi). The river rises in the Guinea Highlands near the Sierra Leone border and runs in a crescent through southwest [[Mali]], then along the border with [[Benin]] and through [[Nigeria]]. === KEY: Nigeria_5d8391

PERSON: no

Federal republic in West Africa (pop., more than 242 million), capital [[Abuja]]. It lies between the [[Sahel]] to the north and the [[Gulf of Guinea]] to the south, covers 923,769 sq km, and borders [[Niger]], [[Chad]], [[Cameroon]], and [[Benin]]. It is the most populous country in Africa and the sixth most populous in the world. It has 36 states and the Federal Capital Territory; the largest city is [[Lagos]].

More than 250 ethnic groups speaking 500 distinct languages live in Nigeria. The three largest, the Hausa, Yoruba, and Igbo, together make up over 60% of the population. English is the official language. The constitution ensures freedom of religion, and the population is divided roughly in half between Muslims and Christians.

The modern state originated with British colonization, taking its present shape in 1914 when the Southern and Northern Nigeria Protectorates merged. The name comes from the Niger River.

Nigeria became independent on October 1, 1960. A civil war (1967–70) was followed by military rule, with an unstable democracy from 1979 to 1983, until stable democracy was established in 1999. The electoral commission has been criticized for lacking transparency. Nigeria is one of Africa's largest economies and belongs to the African Union, the Commonwealth of Nations, and OPEC.

=== KEY: Night_0edc9b
HEADWORD: Night
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Circadian rhythm | Horizon | Mercury (planet) | Photosynthesis | Sun | Venus
ENTRY:
Period of darkness when the [[Sun]] is below the [[Horizon|horizon]], the opposite of daytime. Earth's rotation causes sunrise and sunset, and moonlight, airglow, starlight and light pollution dimly illuminate the night. Its duration varies with the time of year and the observer's latitude. Night lasts about 58 Earth days on [[Venus]], and nights on [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]] are also much longer than on Earth. Organisms respond to darkness, lower temperatures and higher humidity, and their behavior is governed in part by [[Circadian rhythm|circadian rhythms]]. Plants store energy from [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] as starch to use at night, and many desert plants gather [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] overnight for daytime photosynthesis. Light pollution is especially harmful to night-flying insects. Historically, night was a time of increased danger, and the fear of the dark remains common, especially among children. Artificial lighting has extended daytime activities into the night.

Family (Caprimulgidae) of medium-sized nocturnal or crepuscular [[Bird|birds]] in the order Caprimulgiformes, characterized by long wings, short legs and very short bills. Some [[New World|New World]] species are called nighthawks. Nightjars occur on every continent except Antarctica, and on island groups such as the Seychelles and New Caledonia. They usually nest on the ground and often rest on roads. Their cryptic plumage and unusual perching habits help conceal them during the day, although many have contrasting pale patches or spots. The common poorwill undergoes a form of hibernation, becoming torpid with a much reduced body temperature for weeks or months. Traditionally the family was divided into two subfamilies, but molecular studies show that they are not monophyletic. The closest known relatives of nightjars are the extinct Archaeotrogonidae, known from the [[Eocene|Eocene]] and Oligocene of Europe.

=== KEY: Nile_3d6ff2
HEADWORD: Nile
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blue Nile | Ethiopia | Khartoum | Lake Victoria | Mediterranean Sea | South Sudan | Uganda | White Nile
ENTRY:
Major north-flowing river of northeast Africa, emptying into the [[Mediterranean Sea]]. At 7,088 km it is the longest river in the world, although it carries much less water than the Amazon or the Congo. Its length was measured in 2009 by satellite imagery, from a source in Nyungwe National Park, Rwanda.

The Nile has two major tributaries. The [[White Nile]] is longer and is considered the headwaters; it begins near [[Lake Victoria]] and flows through [[Uganda]] and [[South Sudan]]. The [[Blue Nile]] begins near Lake Tana in [[Ethiopia]] and contributes over twice the water volume of the White Nile. They meet at [[Khartoum]], from which the river flows north through the Nubian Desert to Cairo and empties near Alexandria, forming a large river delta. The Nile has followed its present course for about 15,000 years. Its drainage basin spans eleven countries. Egypt and Sudan, largely desert, withdraw its water for irrigation.

The river was the foundation of Ancient Egyptian civilization. Annual floods deposited nutrient-rich silt that supported crops, and the Nile facilitated trade, communication, transportation, and governance. Upstream of the second cataract lay Nubia, home of the Kerma culture and the Kushite Empire. European explorations around Lake Victoria in the late 19th century located the source of the river.

Today the Nile is critical to the economies of Egypt and Sudan. Over a dozen dams have been built in the basin since the late 20th century for irrigation and electricity. They have altered the flood cycle and restricted downstream silt, causing the delta to shrink. Dams such as the Aswan High Dam and the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam have been the subject of international disputes over water scarcity, safety, food security, and displacement.

Large crocodilian of Africa's freshwater habitats. The Nile crocodile (*Crocodylus niloticus*) is present in 26 countries and is widely distributed in sub-Saharan Africa. Adult males are typically 3.5 to 5 m long, and specimens over 6.1 m have been recorded. It is Africa's largest predator and the second-largest living reptile, after the [[Saltwater crocodile]]. === KEY: Nitrogen_1e9ef3
HEADWORD: Nitrogen
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ammonia | Antoine Lavoisier | Gas | Henry Cavendish | Liquid nitrogen | Milky Way | Nonmetal | Solar System
ENTRY:
Chemical element with symbol N and atomic number 7, a [[Nonmetal|nonmetal]] and the lightest member of group 15 of the periodic table, the pnictogens. At standard temperature and pressure two atoms bond to form N₂, a colorless, odorless diatomic [[Gas|gas]] that makes up about 78% of Earth's atmosphere, the most abundant chemical species in air. It is estimated to be seventh in abundance in the [[Milky Way]] and the [[Solar System]].

The Scottish physician Daniel Rutherford is credited with discovering nitrogen in 1772, calling it noxious air; Carl Wilhelm Scheele and [[Henry Cavendish]] did so independently. [[Antoine Lavoisier]] proposed the name *azote*, from the Greek for "no life," because it is an asphyxiant; the name remains in many languages and in compounds such as hydrazine. The English name derives from *nitrogène*, coined in 1790 by Jean-Antoine Chaptal.

The extremely strong triple bond in N₂, the second strongest in any diatomic molecule after carbon monoxide, dominates nitrogen chemistry. It makes converting N₂ into useful compounds difficult, yet decomposing nitrogen compounds releases large amounts of energy. Elemental nitrogen is usually produced from air by pressure swing adsorption. About two-thirds is used as an inert gas, for example in food packaging, and much of the rest as [[Liquid nitrogen|liquid nitrogen]] in cryogenics. Important compounds include [[Ammonia|ammonia]], nitric acid, organic nitrates, and cyanides. Synthetic ammonia and nitrates are key fertilizers, and fertilizer nitrates are key pollutants in the eutrophication of water systems.

Nitrogen occurs in all organisms, mainly in amino acids, nucleic acids, and Adenosine triphosphate. It is about 3% of the human body by mass, the fourth most abundant element after oxygen, carbon, and hydrogen.

=== KEY: Noether_s_theorem_88392f

ENTRY:
Statement that every continuous symmetry of the action of a physical system with conservative forces has a corresponding conservation law. === KEY: Non_fiction_35c0a5
HEADWORD: Non-fiction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlas | Biography | Fiction | User guide | Virginia Woolf
ENTRY:
Any document or media content that attempts, in good faith, to convey information only about the real world rather than being grounded in imagination. Non-fiction typically aims to present topics objectively on historical, scientific, and empirical information, although some ranges into more subjective territory, including sincerely held opinions on real-world topics. The term often refers specifically to prose writing, where it is one of two fundamental approaches to storytelling, in contrast to narrative [[Fiction|fiction]], which is largely populated by imaginary characters and events.

While specific claims in a non-fiction work may prove inaccurate, the sincere author aims to be truthful at the time of composition. Writers may use features such as graphs, diagrams, timelines, headings, footnotes, maps, and indices to help readers find information. Simplicity, clarity, and directness are among the most important considerations, and the narrative techniques of fiction are generally thought inappropriate.

The boundary with fiction is continually argued over, especially in [[Biography|biography]]; [[Virginia Woolf]] described the biographer's problem of welding the granite-like solidity of truth with the rainbow-like intangibility of personality. Knowingly including untrue information is usually regarded as dishonest. Some works, such as journals, letters, and magazine articles, can legitimately be either fiction or non-fiction. Publishers and booksellers sometimes use the term "creative nonfiction" for works with a more literary or intellectual bent.

By the author's purpose, the main genres are instructional, explanatory, discussion-based, report-based, opinion-based, and chronological recounting. Common forms include academic texts, history books, life writing such as autobiographies and diaries, criticism, news stories, documentary films, essays, reference works such as encyclopedias and [[Atlas|atlases]], textbooks, and [[User guide|user guides]].

Nonlinear programming (NLP), also called nonlinear optimization, is the sub-field of [[Mathematical optimization|mathematical optimization]] dealing with problems in which some constraints are not linear equalities or the objective function is not linear. The Euclidean norm, or 2-norm, is defined as the square root of the inner product of a vector with itself, and gives the magnitude of a vector in [[Euclidean space|Euclidean space]]. The norm of a vector z is usually written as double vertical lines, a notation proposed by [[Stefan Banach|Stefan Banach]] in his doctoral thesis from 1920.

Continuous [[Probability distribution|probability distribution]] for a real-valued [[Random variable|random variable]]. Also called the Gaussian distribution, it is informally known as the bell curve, although many other distributions are bell-shaped. Its parameter μ is the mean, which is also the median and [[Mode (statistics)|mode]], and its parameter σ² is the [[Variance|variance]]; the positive value σ is the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]]. Normal distributions are important in statistics and are often used to represent variables whose distributions are not known. Their importance is partly due to the [[Central limit theorem|central limit theorem]], which states that the average of many independent samples of a random variable with finite mean and variance converges to a normal distribution. Physical quantities that are the sum of many independent processes, such as measurement errors, are therefore often nearly normal. Any linear combination of independent normal deviates is itself normal. The standard normal distribution has mean 0 and variance 1.

PERSON: yes

American agronomist and plant pathologist who received the Nobel Peace Prize. Borlaug led initiatives that increased agricultural production in what became known as the [[Green Revolution|Green Revolution]]. He received a B.S. in forestry in 1937 and a PhD in plant pathology and genetics from the University of Minnesota in 1942. In [[Mexico|Mexico]] he developed semi-dwarf, high-yield, disease-resistant [[Wheat|wheat]] varieties, and he led their introduction to Mexico, [[Pakistan|Pakistan]] and [[India|India]]. Mexico became a net exporter of wheat by 1963, and between 1965 and 1970 wheat yields nearly doubled in Pakistan and India. He is credited with saving over a billion people from starvation, a figure traced to Gregg Easterbrook's 1997 article. He was awarded the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize for his contributions to world peace through increasing food supply, and is one of only seven people to have received the Nobel Peace Prize, the Presidential Medal of Freedom and the Congressional Gold Medal.
