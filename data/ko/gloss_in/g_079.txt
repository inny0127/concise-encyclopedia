King of the Belgians, 1865–1909, and sole owner of the [[Congo Free State]]. German historian and a founder of modern source-based history. Ranke set standards for much of later historical writing. He introduced reliance on primary sources ([[Empiricism|empiricism]]) and emphasized narrative history, especially international politics. His 1824 work *Histories of the Latin and Teutonic Peoples* drew on memoirs, diaries, government documents and eyewitness accounts. From 1825 he taught at the University of [[Berlin]] for nearly fifty years, where he established the first historical seminar and taught students to check the value of sources. He criticized the [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegelian]] view of history as a one-size-fits-all approach. Ranke was ennobled in 1865, adding "von" to his name. Critics note that his Lutheran belief that [[God]]'s actions were manifest in history shaped his view that the [[German Empire]] was a manifestation of God's intent.

=== KEY: Lepton_b2a909

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | J. J. Thomson | Strong interaction | Weak interaction
ENTRY:
Class of elementary particles with half-integer spin that do not undergo the [[Strong interaction|strong interaction]]. There are two main classes: charged leptons, including the [[Electron|electron]], muon, and tau, and neutrinos, which are electrically neutral and rarely interact with other matter. Charged leptons combine with other particles to form atoms. Six leptons, known as flavors, are grouped in three generations: the electron and its neutrino, the muon and its neutrino, and the tau and its neutrino. Electrons have the least mass of the charged leptons. Muons and taus decay rapidly into electrons and neutrinos, so electrons are the most common charged lepton in the universe. All leptons are subject to gravitation, the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]], and electromagnetism. The electron was discovered in 1897 by [[J. J. Thomson]], and the muon was discovered in 1936 by Carl D. Anderson. The term was introduced by Léon Rosenfeld in 1948.

=== KEY: Lever_e04561
HEADWORD: Lever
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Archimedes | Simple machine
ENTRY:
Simple machine consisting of a beam or rigid rod pivoted at a fixed hinge, or fulcrum. It is one of the six [[Simple machine|simple machines]] identified by Renaissance scientists. A lever amplifies an input force to provide a greater output force, a gain called mechanical advantage, equal to the ratio of output force to input force.

For an ideal lever, with no friction in the hinge or bending in the beam, power into the lever equals power out. The ratio of output to input force then equals the ratio of the distances from the fulcrum to the points where the forces are applied, a relation known as the law of the lever.

Levers are classified by the relative positions of fulcrum, effort, and resistance (load). In a Class I lever the fulcrum lies between effort and resistance, as in a seesaw, crowbar, or claw hammer; its mechanical advantage may be greater than, less than, or equal to 1. In a Class II lever the resistance lies between effort and fulcrum, as in a wheelbarrow or nutcracker; its mechanical advantage is always greater than 1. In a Class III lever the effort lies between resistance and fulcrum, as in a hoe, tweezers, or a fishing rod; its mechanical advantage is always less than 1.

The earliest known evidence of a lever mechanism dates to [[Ancient Egypt]] c. 5000 BC, in a simple balance scale. In Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC the shadouf, a crane-like device, was invented, and Egyptian workmen used levers to move obelisks weighing more than 100 tons. The earliest surviving writings on levers date from the third century BC and are commonly attributed to [[Archimedes]], who is quoted as saying, "Give me a lever (long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it), and I shall move the world."

ENTRY:
Work of [[Political philosophy|political philosophy]] by English thinker [[Thomas Hobbes]], published in 1651. Its full title is *Leviathan, or The Matter, Forme and Power of a Commonwealth Ecclesiasticall and Civil*. Written during the [[English Civil War]], it is regarded as one of the earliest and most influential works of social contract theory. Hobbes argues that civil war and the "nasty, brutish and short" state of nature could be avoided only through a strong, undivided government under an absolute [[Sovereignty|sovereign]]. Royalists condemned the book for elevating the state over monarchy, and clergy for bordering on atheism.

=== KEY: Liberalism_a81c66
HEADWORD: Liberalism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | American Revolution | Fascism | French Revolution | Glorious Revolution | John Locke | Social liberalism
ENTRY:
Political and moral philosophy based on the rights of the individual, liberty, consent of the governed, political equality, private property, and equality before the law. Liberals hold varying and sometimes conflicting views, but generally support liberal democracy, market economies, individual rights, secularism, the rule of law, and freedoms of speech, the press, assembly, and religion.

Liberalism became a distinct movement in the [[Age of Enlightenment]]. It sought to replace hereditary privilege, state religion, absolute monarchy, and the divine right of kings with representative democracy and equality under the law, and to replace mercantilism and royal monopolies with free trade. [[John Locke]] is often credited with founding the tradition on the social contract, arguing that each man has a natural right to life, liberty, and property. Leaders of the [[Glorious Revolution]] of 1688, the [[American Revolution]] of 1776, and the [[French Revolution]] of 1789 used liberal philosophy to justify overthrowing royal sovereignty.

Before 1920 its main opponents were communism, conservatism, and socialism; it then faced challenges from [[Fascism]] and Marxism–Leninism. Liberal democracies were among the victors in both world wars and the Cold War. Later liberals campaigned for civil rights, racial and gender equality, and universal suffrage. In Europe and North America, [[Social liberalism]] became a key component in expanding the welfare state.

The word derives from Latin *liber*, "free"; "liberalism" first appeared in English in 1815. In Europe and Latin America the term means a moderate form of classical liberalism, while in North America it almost exclusively means social liberalism. Classical liberals favor minimal state intervention and credit free markets with accelerating economic growth; variants emphasizing redistribution have prompted debate over balancing equality with limited government.

=== KEY: Liberty_84e1da
HEADWORD: Liberty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Isaiah Berlin | John Locke | John Stuart Mill | Latin | Maurya Empire | Thomas Hobbes | United States Declaration of Independence
ENTRY:
State of being free within society from restrictions imposed by authority on one's way of life, behavior, or political views. In U.S. constitutional law, ordered liberty means a balanced society in which individuals can act without unnecessary interference (negative liberty) and have access to opportunities and resources to pursue their goals (positive liberty). Some writers distinguish liberty from freedom, using "freedom" for the ability to do as one wills and "liberty" for the absence of arbitrary restraints, subject to the rights of others. The word comes from [[Latin]] *liber*.

The concept has long occupied philosophers. [[Thomas Hobbes]] held that a free man is one not hindered from doing what he has the will to do. [[John Locke]] rejected that definition: in political society, liberty consists of being under no lawmaking power except that established by consent, and freedom is constrained by laws in both the state of nature and political society. [[John Stuart Mill]]'s *On Liberty* (1859) was the first to recognize the difference between liberty as freedom to act and liberty as absence of coercion. [[Isaiah Berlin]] framed this as the distinction between negative liberty, protection from tyranny and arbitrary authority, and positive liberty, self-mastery over inner compulsions such as weakness and fear. Critics argue that positive liberty can justify coercion in the name of collective goals.

The political concept originates in Greek ideas of freedom and slavery: to be free was not to have a master. [[Aristotle]] linked this to democracy, though it applied only to free men. In the [[Maurya Empire]], citizens of all religions and ethnic groups had some rights to freedom and tolerance. The phrase "Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness" appears in the [[United States Declaration of Independence]], and France's motto is "Liberté, égalité, fraternité."

=== KEY: Life_dea525
HEADWORD: Life
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Biosphere | Carl Linnaeus | Cell (biology) | Cyanobacteria | Extremophile
ENTRY:
Capacity in matter, formed of one or more units called cells, for processes such as cell signaling, homeostasis, metabolism, cell growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. All life eventually reaches a state of death. Life exists all over Earth in air, water, and soil, with many ecosystems forming the [[Biosphere|biosphere]]; some harsh environments are occupied only by [[Extremophile|extremophiles]]. The life of a particular ecosystem is called its biota. Life is known to exist only on Earth, though extraterrestrial life is thought to be probable.

Defining life has long challenged scientists and philosophers, partly because life is a process, not a substance. At least 123 definitions have been compiled. With no consensus, most definitions in biology are descriptive, listing traits: homeostasis, organization in one or more cells, metabolism, growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. From a physics perspective, an organism is a thermodynamic system with an organized molecular structure that can reproduce itself and evolve. A NASA committee adopted the definition "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution," based on a suggestion by Carl Sagan; critics note that by it a single sexually reproducing individual is not alive. Systemic definitions instead describe living things as self-organizing and autopoietic (self-producing). Whether viruses are alive is controversial: they possess genes, evolve by natural selection, and replicate by self-assembly, but do not metabolize and require a host cell.

Living things are composed of biochemical molecules formed mainly from a few core chemical elements. All contain two types of macromolecules, proteins and nucleic acids (usually both DNA and RNA); the nucleic acids carry the information needed to make each type of protein, and the proteins carry out the chemical processes of life. The [[Cell (biology)|cell]] is the structural and functional unit of life. Prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) consist of small single cells, while eukaryotes may be single-celled or multicellular. Life is organized hierarchically, from molecular machines to cells, organs, organisms, populations, ecosystems, and the whole biosphere.

Study of life dates to ancient times. Empedocles (430 BC) held that everything is made of four eternal elements, earth, water, air, and fire, while [[Aristotle]]'s hylomorphism held that living things have souls and embody both form and matter. Classification also began with Aristotle; modern classification began with [[Carl Linnaeus]]'s binomial nomenclature in the 1740s.

Life originated at least 3.5 billion years ago, producing a universal common ancestor from which all existing species evolved, along with many extinct species, some of which left fossils. Photosynthetic [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] oxygenated the atmosphere around 2.4 billion years ago. An estimated 8.7 million eukaryotic species exist, of which more than 2.2 million have been formally described. === KEY: Light_9914a0
HEADWORD: Light
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert A. Michelson | Electromagnetic radiation | Infrared | Léon Foucault | Photon | Sun | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Electromagnetic radiation perceivable by the human eye. It is usually defined as having wavelengths of 400–700 nanometers (nm), corresponding to frequencies of 750–420 terahertz; sources define the range as narrowly as 420–680 nm and as broadly as 380–800 nm. Visible light lies between [[Infrared|infrared]], with longer wavelengths, and [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]], with shorter ones. In physics, "light" may also refer to [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] of any wavelength, including gamma rays, X-rays, microwaves, and radio waves.

The primary properties of light are intensity, propagation direction, frequency or wavelength spectrum, and polarization. All electromagnetic radiation shows properties of both particles and waves. Single quanta of light, called [[Photon|photons]], can be detected with specialized equipment, while phenomena such as interference are described by waves. Most everyday interactions can be understood with geometrical optics.

Infrared is invisible to humans because its photons lack the energy to cause a lasting change in the visual molecule retinal. Ultraviolet is invisible mostly because the cornea absorbs wavelengths shorter than 360 nm and the lens those shorter than 400 nm. The main natural source of light on Earth is the [[Sun]]; historically another important source was fire, now largely replaced by electric lighting.

The speed of light in vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 m/s, a fundamental constant that defines the meter in SI units; all electromagnetic radiation travels at this speed in vacuum. Ole Rømer made an early measurement in 1676 from discrepancies in the orbit of Jupiter's moon Io. Hippolyte Fizeau measured it in 1849 using a rotating cog wheel, and [[Léon Foucault]] used rotating mirrors in 1862 to obtain 298,000,000 m/s. [[Albert A. Michelson]] conducted experiments on the speed of light from 1877.

ENTRY:

The color of the light depends on the energy needed for electrons to cross the band gap of the semiconductor. === KEY: Lightning_457ba6
HEADWORD: Lightning
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ball lightning | Electric charge | Electromagnetic radiation | Thunder | Thunderstorm | Wildfire
ENTRY:
Natural phenomenon consisting of electrostatic discharges through the atmosphere between two electrically charged regions, which are partly or wholly neutralized afterward. A flash releases energy averaging between 200 megajoules and 7 gigajoules and heats the surrounding air to about 30,000 °C (54,000 °F). It emits [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] across a wide range of wavelengths, some visible as a bright flash, and causes [[Thunder|thunder]], the sound of the shock wave from the suddenly heated gases.

It occurs most commonly in a [[Thunderstorm|thunderstorm]] but also in other energetic systems such as volcanic eruptions. Charge separation arises from collisions between ice particles and graupel in updrafts. Lightning is a natural ignition source of [[Wildfire|wildfires]] and is considered an Essential Climate Variable by the World Meteorological Organization; its scientific study is called fulminology.

Three primary forms are distinguished: intra-cloud, cloud-to-cloud, and cloud-to-ground, called a lightning strike. Intra-cloud and cloud-to-cloud are more common, but cloud-to-ground is best understood, easier to study because it ends on a physical object, and poses the greatest threat to life and property. Other variants include volcanic lightning, dry lightning, which can cause forest fires, and [[Ball lightning|ball lightning]], which is rarely observed scientifically.

Most cloud-to-ground lightning is negative. An average negative bolt carries a current of 30,000 amperes (30 kA), transferring 15 C of [[Electric charge|electric charge]] and 1 gigajoule of energy. Positive lightning makes up less than 5% of strikes, but large bolts can reach 120 kA and transfer 350 C. Because positive flashes can travel long distances through clear air, they are known as "bolts from the blue."

=== KEY: Limit_mathematics_628186
HEADWORD: Limit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustin-Louis Cauchy | Calculus | Continuous function | Derivative | Euclid's Elements | G. H. Hardy | Isaac Newton | Karl Weierstrass
ENTRY:
In mathematics, the value that a function or sequence approaches as the argument or index approaches some value. Limits of functions are essential to [[Calculus|calculus]] and mathematical analysis and are used to define [[Continuous function|continuity]], [[Derivative|derivatives]], and integrals. The existence of a limit is called convergence.

A limit of a function is written lim f(x) = L as x → c, read "the limit of f of x as x approaches c equals L." It means that f(x) can be made arbitrarily close to L by choosing x sufficiently close to c. A sequence of real numbers converges to a limit L if its terms approximate L arbitrarily well after finitely many initial terms are discarded. Formally, for each positive real number ε there must be a positive integer N such that every term aₙ with n > N lies within distance ε of L. Otherwise the sequence is divergent. A sequence that tends to infinity has an infinite limit but does not converge to a real number.

According to Hankel (1871), the modern concept originates from Proposition X.1 of [[Euclid's Elements]], the basis of the method of exhaustion. Grégoire de Saint-Vincent gave the first definition of the limit of a geometric series in 1647. [[Isaac Newton]] defined limits in the Scholium to the *Principia* in 1687. The modern definition goes back to Bernard Bolzano, who in 1817 developed the epsilon-delta technique to define continuous functions. [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] in 1821, followed by [[Karl Weierstrass]], formalized the (ε, δ)-definition. The modern notation of placing the arrow below the limit symbol was invented by John Gaston Leathem in 1905 and popularized by [[G. H. Hardy]]'s 1908 textbook *A Course of Pure Mathematics*.

=== KEY: Limit_of_a_function_10bde4
HEADWORD: Limit of a function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustin-Louis Cauchy | Continuous function | Derivative | G. H. Hardy | Karl Weierstrass
ENTRY:
Value that a function's output approaches as its input nears a given point. The limit of a function f at p is L if f(x) can be made arbitrarily close to L by taking x sufficiently close to p, though the function need not be defined at p. The limit does not exist if inputs very close to p give outputs that stay a fixed distance apart. Limits underpin [[Continuous function|continuity]], which holds when a function's limits agree with its values, and the [[Derivative|derivative]], the limiting slope of secant lines to a graph. The modern epsilon-delta definition was introduced by [[Karl Weierstrass]] in 1861, building on work by Bernard Bolzano in 1817. [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] discussed limits in *Cours d'analyse* (1821). The arrow below the limit symbol is due to [[G. H. Hardy]], who introduced it in *A Course of Pure Mathematics* (1908).

=== KEY: Limit_of_a_sequence_841e3a
HEADWORD: Limit of a sequence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: E (mathematical constant) | Mathematical analysis | Metric space | Sequence | Topological space
ENTRY:
Value that the terms of an infinite [[Sequence|sequence]] tend toward, if one exists. A sequence is convergent if it has a finite limit and divergent otherwise. A number L is the limit if the terms become arbitrarily close to L and stay that close after some point. For example, the sequence 1/n converges to 0. The limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ as n grows is the number [[E (mathematical constant)|e]]. Limits can be defined in any [[Metric space|metric space]] or [[Topological space|topological space]], but are usually first encountered in the real numbers. Bernard Bolzano gave a modern definition in 1816 and Karl Weierstrass in the 1870s. The concept is described as the fundamental notion on which [[Mathematical analysis|mathematical analysis]] rests.

=== KEY: Limnology_023919
HEADWORD: Limnology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Drainage basin | Ecosystem | Lake | Wetland
ENTRY:
Science of inland aquatic [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]], including [[Lake|lakes]], reservoirs, rivers and [[Wetland|wetlands]]. Limnology studies the biological, chemical, physical and geological characteristics of fresh and saline inland waters, including ponds, streams, springs, groundwater and inland salt lakes. It examines the [[Drainage basin|drainage basin]], the movement of water through it and the biogeochemical changes that occur along the way. The name comes from the Greek *límnē*, meaning lake. François-Alphonse Forel coined the term, establishing the field through his studies of Lake Geneva and describing it as "the oceanography of lakes." In 1922 August Thienemann and Einar Naumann co-founded the International Society of Limnology. Landscape limnology considers ecosystems together with their drainage basins, and global limnology studies the role of inland waters in Earth's biogeochemical cycles.

City, prefecture of the Haute-Vienne department in west-central [[France]]. It was the administrative capital of the former Limousin region. === KEY: Limonene_92b038
HEADWORD: Limonene
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bergamot orange | Citrus | Turpentine
ENTRY:
Cyclic monoterpene, the main component of the essential oil of citrus peels. Its name comes from the Italian *limone*, "lemon". The (+)-isomer, d-limonene, is the form found in [[Citrus|citrus]] peel and is used as a food flavoring and as a solvent in cleaning products. The (−)-isomer, l-limonene, has a piny, [[Turpentine|turpentine]]-like odor and occurs in caraway, dill and [[Bergamot orange]]. Limonene oxidizes easily in moist air and is a precursor to carvone.

=== KEY: Limpopo_River_5b0c2d
HEADWORD: Limpopo River
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australopithecus | Indian Ocean | Mozambique | Rudyard Kipling | South Africa | Zambezi
ENTRY:
River of southern Africa, rising in [[South Africa]] and flowing generally east through [[Mozambique]] to the [[Indian Ocean]]. It is about 1,750 km (1,090 mi) long and drains a basin of 415,000 sq km (160,000 sq mi). Its mean discharge at the mouth is 170 m³/s to 313 m³/s, the second-largest of any African river draining to the Indian Ocean, after the [[Zambezi]]. Vasco da Gama anchored off its mouth in 1498 and is credited as the first European to sight the river. The river forms much of the border between South Africa, Botswana and Zimbabwe. [[Rudyard Kipling]] called it the "great gray-green, greasy Limpopo River" in the *Just So Stories*. Rainfall is seasonal and unreliable, and heavy rain from a cyclone caused the catastrophic 2000 Mozambique flood in February 2000. Fossil sites in the Makapans Valley contain [[Australopithecus]] remains from 3.5 million years ago. About 14 million people live in the basin.

Peruvian woman, the youngest confirmed mother in medical history. At the age of five years, seven months and 21 days, she gave birth by [[Caesarean section|caesarean section]] on May 14, 1939. Precocious puberty left her with mature reproductive organs; her menarche had occurred at eight months of age. Her pregnancy was confirmed by specialists in [[Lima]]. City (pop., 2020: 291,082), capital of [[Nebraska]], United States. Founded in 1856 as the village of Lancaster, it was renamed after [[Abraham Lincoln]] and became the state capital in 1869.
