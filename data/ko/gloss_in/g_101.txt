=== KEY: Ozone_depletion_28ada6
HEADWORD: Ozone depletion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bromine | Chlorine | Ozone | Skin cancer | South Pole | Stratosphere
ENTRY:
Decline in the total amount of [[Ozone|ozone]] in Earth's [[Stratosphere|stratosphere]], including the seasonal Antarctic ozone hole. Since the late 1970s two related phenomena have been observed: a lowered total amount of ozone in the upper stratosphere, and a much larger springtime decrease over the [[South Pole]]. The main cause is ozone-depleting substances such as chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), halons and HCFCs, used as refrigerants, solvents, propellants and foam-blowing agents. In the stratosphere they release halogen atoms, mainly [[Chlorine|chlorine]] and [[Bromine|bromine]], which catalyze the breakdown of ozone into oxygen. The ozone layer blocks harmful UVB light, whose increase is linked to [[Skin cancer|skin cancer]], sunburn and cataracts. The 1987 Montreal Protocol banned production of CFCs and halons, and its controls took effect in 1989. Ozone levels stabilized by the mid-1990s and began recovering in the 2000s. In 2019 NASA reported that the ozone hole was the smallest since its discovery in 1982.

=== KEY: PH_a25496
HEADWORD: pH
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acid | Agronomy | Base (chemistry) | Chemistry
ENTRY:
Logarithmic scale for specifying the [[Acid|acidity]] or [[Base (chemistry)|basicity]] of an aqueous solution. pH is the negative base-10 logarithm of hydrogen ion activity. At 25 °C, solutions with a pH below 7 are acidic, those above 7 are basic, and pure water is neutral at 7. The neutral value falls below 7 as temperature rises above 25 °C. The scale is conventionally given as 0 to 14, but very concentrated strong acids can have values below 0, and strong bases can exceed 14. The pH of a solution can be measured with a glass electrode and pH meter or with a color-changing indicator. The concept was introduced in 1909 by Danish chemist Søren Peter Lauritz Sørensen at the Carlsberg Laboratory; the exact original meaning of the letter "p" remains disputed. Measurements are important in [[Chemistry]], [[Agronomy]], medicine and water treatment.

=== KEY: P_value_925c64
HEADWORD: P-value
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Probability
ENTRY:
[[Probability]], assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining test results at least as extreme as those observed. The null hypothesis usually states that a parameter, such as a correlation or a difference between means, is zero. A small p-value means such an extreme result would be unlikely under that hypothesis. A result is statistically significant if it allows rejection of the null hypothesis, and smaller p-values are taken as stronger evidence against it. Ronald A. Fisher introduced the measure in the 1920s, and his 1925 book *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* formalized its use. Misinterpretation is widespread. In 2016 the American Statistical Association stated that p-values do not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true or the size of an effect, but a 2019 task force said that properly applied significance tests increase the rigor of conclusions.

=== KEY: P_versus_NP_problem_b8207a
HEADWORD: P versus NP problem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artificial intelligence | Cryptography | John von Neumann | Kurt Gödel | Millennium Prize Problems | Theoretical computer science
ENTRY:
Unsolved problem in [[Theoretical computer science|theoretical computer science]]. It asks whether every decision problem whose positive answer can be verified quickly can also be solved quickly, where "quickly" means in polynomial time. The class of problems solvable in polynomial time is P, and the class whose answers can be verified in polynomial time is NP. Most researchers believe P ≠ NP, which would mean some problems are harder to solve than to verify. A proof either way would affect [[Cryptography|cryptography]], [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]], economics and many other fields. The problem was formally stated by Stephen Cook in 1971 and independently by Leonid Levin in 1973, after [[Kurt Gödel]] asked in a 1956 letter to [[John von Neumann]] whether theorem-proving could be done in quadratic or linear time. It is one of the seven [[Millennium Prize Problems]], each carrying a US$1,000,000 prize.

Spanish painter, sculptor, printmaker, and ceramicist who spent most of his adult life in France. He co-founded the [[Cubism|Cubist]] movement, invented constructed sculpture, co-invented [[Collage|collage]], and produced about 50,000 artworks in a career of more than 76 years. Among his best-known works are the proto-Cubist [[Les Demoiselles d'Avignon|*Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*]] (1907) and the anti-war painting [[Guernica (Picasso)|*Guernica*]] (1937), a portrayal of the bombing of Guernica during the [[Spanish Civil War]].

His work of the late 1910s and early 1920s is often neoclassical, and that of the mid-1920s shows traits of Surrealism. Later work combines earlier styles. He achieved great renown and fortune. Accounts of his relationships with partners such as Fernande Olivier, Olga Khokhlova, Marie-Thérèse Walter, Dora Maar, and Françoise Gilot describe them as domineering and emotionally abusive, prompting posthumous criticism of misogyny.

=== KEY: Pacific_Ocean_3e3b0e
HEADWORD: Pacific Ocean
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Austronesian peoples | Challenger Deep | Equator | Ferdinand Magellan | International Date Line | Mariana Trench | Ring of Fire | Southern Ocean
ENTRY:
Largest and deepest of Earth's five oceanic divisions, stretching from the Arctic Ocean in the north to the [[Southern Ocean]] or, by some definitions, Antarctica in the south, and bounded by Asia and Australia in the west and the Americas in the east.

With an area of 165,250,000 sq km (defined with a southern Antarctic border), it covers about 46% of Earth's water surface and about 32% of the planet's total surface, more than all land combined. Circulation divides it into the North and South Pacific, which meet at the [[Equator|Equator]]; the [[International Date Line]] informally divides it into east and west. Mean depth is 4,000 m. [[Challenger Deep]], in the [[Mariana Trench]], is the deepest known point in the world at 10,928 m, and the Horizon Deep in the Tonga Trench, at 10,823 m, is the deepest point in the Southern Hemisphere. More than 25,000 islands rise above its surface, many of them the remains of volcanoes, and it is ringed by the [[Ring of Fire]], a belt of volcanoes and earthquake zones driven by subduction of the Pacific Plate.

In 1513 the Spanish explorer Vasco Núñez de Balboa sighted what he named the Mar del Sur. The current name was coined during the Spanish circumnavigation of 1520 by [[Ferdinand Magellan]], who found favorable winds and called it *Mar Pacífico* ("peaceful sea").

Modern humans first reached the western Pacific around 60,000 to 70,000 years ago, crossing at least 80 km of sea between Sundaland and Sahul to reach Australia. The later Neolithic expansion of the [[Austronesian peoples]], from Taiwan c. 3000–1500 BCE, brought outrigger and sailing technology to the first settlement of Remote Oceania, reaching the Marianas by 1500 BCE and Palau and Yap by 1000 BCE.

=== KEY: Pacifism_d6c792

ENTRY:
Philosophy opposing war or violence. The word was coined by French peace campaigner Émile Arnaud and adopted at the Universal Peace Congress in [[Glasgow]] in 1901. A related term, *ahimsa* ("to do no harm"), is central to [[Hinduism]], [[Buddhism]] and [[Jainism]]. In modern times, interest was revived by [[Leo Tolstoy]] in his late works. [[Mahatma Gandhi]] practiced nonviolent resistance, which he called *satyagraha*, in the Indian independence movement, and its example inspired [[Martin Luther King Jr.]] and others in the civil rights movement. Principled pacifists hold that violence is morally wrong, while pragmatic pacifists argue that the costs of war must be avoided. Some pacifists accept force in self-defense. Critics argue that unilateral restraint can enable aggressors.

=== KEY: Painting_2875ec
HEADWORD: Painting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cave painting | Impressionism | Jackson Pollock | Oil painting | Photography | Piet Mondrian | Watercolor painting
ENTRY:
Visual art characterized by applying paint, pigment, color, or another medium to a solid surface. The medium is commonly applied with a brush, but palette knives, sponges, airbrushes, fingers, or dripping may also be used. The term describes both the act and the resulting work. Supports include walls, paper, canvas, wood, glass, lacquer, pottery, leaf, and copper, and paintings may incorporate sand, clay, plaster, gold leaf, or entire objects.

Painting brings together drawing, composition, gesture, narration, and abstraction. Twentieth-century abstraction, as in [[Piet Mondrian]]'s geometries and [[Jackson Pollock]]'s drip methods, favored form, color, and gesture. === KEY: Palaeography_f7cfbc

ENTRY:
Academic discipline concerned with historical [[Writing system|writing systems]], handwriting and manuscripts. It deals with deciphering, reading and dating manuscripts, and with the methods by which books, codices and tracts were produced. Paleography is one of the auxiliary sciences of history and is important for authenticating and dating texts, though script alone rarely pins down exact dates. Geologic period and system, the first of the [[Cenozoic]] Era. === KEY: Paleontology_91d974
HEADWORD: Paleontology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biology | Charles Darwin | Evolution | Extinction | Fossil | Geology | Georges Cuvier
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the life of the past, mainly but not exclusively through [[Fossil|fossils]]. Paleontologists use fossils to classify organisms, measure geologic time, and assess the interactions between prehistoric organisms and their environment. Paleontological observations are known from at least the 6th century BC, but the science's foundation dates to the work of [[Georges Cuvier]] in 1796, who demonstrated evidence for [[Extinction|extinction]] and showed that the life of the past was not necessarily the same as that of the present. The French word *paléontologie* was introduced in 1822. [[Charles Darwin]] later popularized the concept of [[Evolution|evolution]]; evolution and extinction are understood as complementary processes that shaped the history of life.

The field overlaps most with [[Geology|geology]] and [[Biology|biology]], and also with ecology, chemistry, physics, and mathematics. Invertebrate paleontology has traditionally been tied to geology and biostratigraphy, areas with both commercial and academic drivers, while vertebrate paleontology has been closer to biology. Fieldwork, laboratory preparation, and comparative anatomy remain core components. Other subfields include micropaleontology, paleobotany, paleobiology, and paleoecology. Methods include stratigraphy, radiometric dating, and cladistics, and biostratigraphy is used to reconstruct the geologic time scale. Theoretical analysis introduced in the 1950s and 1960s led to more focused fields studying changing geography and climate, phylogenetic relationships, and how fossilization occurs and what biases affect the fossil record.

Paleontology draws attention in mass media comparable to that given to astrophysics and global health. Public interest can be traced to the mythologies of indigenous peoples and to the interpretation of fossils as the bones of dragons or giants. Prehistoric life inspires toys, television, film, computer games, and tourism, with budgets that often exceed funding within the field itself.

=== KEY: Pali_Canon_24d824

Standard collection of Theravada Buddhist scriptures, preserved in [[Pali]]. Known as the Tipiṭaka, or three baskets, it is the most complete extant early Buddhist canon. It is divided into the Vinaya Piṭaka, on monastic discipline; the Sutta Piṭaka, the largest, containing discourses attributed to [[The Buddha|the Buddha]]; and the Abhidhamma Piṭaka, treatises elaborating doctrine, particularly about the mind. The Vinaya and Sutta Piṭakas closely resemble the texts of other early schools, while the Abhidhamma is distinctively Theravada. Tradition holds that the teachings were recited at a council after the Buddha's death and preserved orally before being written down. The earliest surviving canonical Pali fragments date only to the mid-5th to mid-6th century CE, in the [[Pyu city-states]] of Burma. Buddhaghosa, active in the 4th–5th century, wrote commentaries that summarize the traditional interpretation, most fully in his *Visuddhimagga*.

Supercontinent that existed during the late Paleozoic and early Mesozoic eras. Territories on the Italian Peninsula under the direct sovereign rule of the pope, from 756 to 1870. The state was established in the 8th century, when Pepin the Short, king of the [[Franks]], gave Pope Stephen II lands formerly held by the [[Lombards]]. Its territory expanded greatly during the [[Early modern period|early modern period]], and at its zenith it covered most of the modern regions of Lazio, including Rome, Marche, Umbria, Romagna and parts of Emilia. By 1860 much of the territory had been conquered by the Kingdom of Sardinia and incorporated into the [[Kingdom of Italy]] in 1861. Lazio, including Rome, was annexed in 1870. In 1929 [[Benito Mussolini]] negotiated the Lateran Treaty, which acknowledged the sovereignty of the [[Holy See]] over [[Vatican City]], a new city-state within Rome.

Species of flowering plant in the poppy family, also called the opium poppy or breadseed poppy. It is the source of both [[Opium|opium]] and poppy seeds, and it is also grown as an ornamental plant. Its native range is the western Mediterranean, but widespread introduction and cultivation since ancient times have obscured it. [[Carl Linnaeus]] gave it its accepted name in 1753. Grown as an agricultural crop, it is used to produce poppy seeds, opium for the pharmaceutical industry, and other [[Alkaloid|alkaloids]], mainly thebaine and oripavine. All parts of the plant exude white latex when wounded.

PERSON: no

Tropical fruit plant of the genus *Carica*, in the family Caricaceae. It was first domesticated in [[Mesoamerica]], in present-day southern Mexico and Central America, and is now grown in tropical regions worldwide. In 2024 India was the leading producer, accounting for 36% of the world total. The plant usually has a single stem 5 to 10 m (16 to 33 ft) tall, topped by large leaves. Its fruit is a large berry that ripens to an amber to orange skin. Papaya skin, pulp, and seeds contain [[Carotenoid|carotenoids]] and polyphenols. The green fruit contains papain, a cysteine protease [[Enzyme|enzyme]] used to tenderize meat. Spaniards introduced the plant to the Old World in the 16th century.

=== KEY: Paper_d0a662
HEADWORD: Paper
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baghdad | Cai Lun | Cellulose | China | Deforestation | East Asia | Papyrus | Second Industrial Revolution
ENTRY:
Thin sheet of matted [[Cellulose|cellulose]] fibers, largely derived from lignocellulose, made from a pulp dissolved into a slurry that is drained and dried into sheets. The word derives from Latin *papyrus*, but [[Papyrus|papyrus]], a lamination of natural plant fiber, is a separate technology from paper, which is made from fibers changed by maceration.

Papermaking developed in [[East Asia]] at least as early as AD 105 by the Han court eunuch [[Cai Lun]], although paper-like material from the 2nd century BC has been found in [[China]]. Papyrus was replaced because it was limited geographically to Egypt, more expensive and laborious to produce, and more fragile. Popular history credits the Battle of Talas in 751 with spreading papermaking to the Islamic world, but an archive of 76 paper writings found in Panjikent predates the city's capture by Arabs in 722, and paper was being made in [[Baghdad]] by the late 8th century. Knowledge of paper spread to Europe in the 13th century, where the first water-powered paper mills were built.

Before industrialization the most common paper was rag paper. In the 19th century, industrialization greatly reduced costs; Charles Fenerty of Canada and Friedrich Gottlob Keller of Germany independently developed processes for pulping wood fibers, and with the [[Second Industrial Revolution]] pulpwood paper became dominant. Chemical pulping dissolves lignin; the kraft process, invented in the 1870s, is now the most common and recovers and reuses all inorganic reagents. Mechanical pulping yields more than 95%, but the remaining lignin turns paper yellow and brittle over time. Packaging accounts for about 65% of output, and manufacture contributes to [[Deforestation|deforestation]] if forests are unmanaged.

=== KEY: Paper_money_4a857d
HEADWORD: Paper money
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bank | China | French Revolution | Napoleon | Song dynasty | Sweden | Tang dynasty
ENTRY:
Negotiable promissory note payable to the bearer on demand, and a form of currency. The main types are government notes, issued directly by political authorities, and banknotes, issued by [[Bank|banks]] including central banks. Paper money is often, but not always, legal tender. Counterfeiting is an inherent challenge, and fighting it has been a principal driver of security printing methods in recent centuries. The first documented paper money appeared in [[China]] in the 7th century, during the [[Tang dynasty]], as merchants' deposit receipts called "flying money" that spared them the bulk of coinage. The [[Song dynasty]] issued the first generally circulating notes, called *jiaozi*, by the 11th century. In 1661 Stockholms Banco in [[Sweden]] made the first European attempt at issuing banknotes. The [[French Revolution]] brought mass issuance of government notes that collapsed in value, and [[Napoleon]] then established the Bank of France to issue banknotes in the early 1800s.

HEADWORD: Paprika
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christopher Columbus | Ottoman Empire
ENTRY:
Spice made from dried and ground red peppers, *Capsicum annuum*. Sweet paprika is mainly the fruit with most seeds removed, while hotter varieties retain some seeds and stalks. Peppers reached Western Europe via Spain and Portugal in the 16th century, and [[Christopher Columbus]] probably came across them on his first voyage in 1492. Paprika was taken up in the [[Ottoman Empire]], which ruled central Hungary for much of the 16th and 17th centuries. Central European paprika was hot until the 1920s, when a Szeged breeder found a sweet-fruited plant. It is a major ingredient in Hungarian, Serbian and Spanish cooking.
