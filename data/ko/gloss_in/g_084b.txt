HEADWORD: Manchu people

ENTRY:
Tungusic ethnic group native to [[Manchuria]] in Northeast Asia, the people from whom the region takes its name. The Manchus are descended from the Jurchen people who founded the [[Jin dynasty (1115–1234)|Jin dynasty]], a claim disputed by historians. They established the Later Jin (1616–1636) and [[Qing dynasty]] (1636–1912) dynasties of China. In 1635 Hong Taiji adopted Manchu as the official name, replacing Jurchen. About half of the Manchu population lives in Liaoning and one-fifth in [[Hebei]].

=== KEY: Manchukuo_606ee1
HEADWORD: Manchukuo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axis powers | Empire of Japan | Manchuria | Puyi | Second Sino-Japanese War
ENTRY:
Japanese puppet state in [[Manchuria]] (1932–1945). Established by the [[Empire of Japan]] after its invasion of Manchuria in 1931 following the Mukden incident, it received limited diplomatic recognition, mainly from states aligned with the [[Axis powers]], and its existence is widely regarded as illegitimate. Its nominal head was [[Puyi]], the final emperor of China, but Japanese officials made the decisions. More than one million Japanese settled in the state by 1945, and heavy industry expanded using forced labor. Manchukuo was the launching ground for Japan's invasion of China in the [[Second Sino-Japanese War]]. The Soviet invasion of Manchuria in August 1945 ended it.

=== KEY: Manchuria_ab904d
HEADWORD: Manchuria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amur | China | Empire of Japan | Manchu people | Manchukuo | Qing dynasty | Russian Empire
ENTRY:
Historical region in Northeast Asia, encompassing present-day [[China|Northeast China]] and parts of the Russian Far East. In a narrow sense it comprises the Chinese provinces of Heilongjiang, Jilin, and Liaoning, along with parts of Hebei and Inner Mongolia. In a broader sense it includes the [[Amur|Amur River]] basin, parts of which the [[Qing dynasty]] ceded to the [[Russian Empire]] in the Amur Annexation of 1858–1860. The region is home to the [[Manchu people|Manchus]], Mongols, Koreans, Nanai, Nivkhs, and Ulchs, and was the ancestral homeland of the Jurchens. The name is an exonym of Japanese origin, and the [[Empire of Japan]] promoted it to support its puppet state, [[Manchukuo]]. Chinese usage now avoids the term, and the area is commonly called the Northeast.

=== KEY: Mandalay_d42e52
HEADWORD: Mandalay
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Empire | Irrawaddy River | Konbaung dynasty | Myanmar | Yangon | Yunnan
ENTRY:
City (pop., 2014: 1,225,553), second-largest city in [[Myanmar]], on the east bank of the [[Irrawaddy River]], 631 km north of [[Yangon]]. King Mindon founded it in 1857 to replace Amarapura as the royal capital of the [[Konbaung dynasty]]. It was the last royal capital before the kingdom's annexation by the [[British Empire]] in 1885, after which the British shifted commercial and political importance to Yangon. Extensive destruction occurred during the Japanese conquest of Burma in the Second World War. The city became part of the independent Union of Burma in 1948. It remains the economic center of Upper Myanmar and is considered the center of Burmese culture. Since the late 20th century, an influx of Chinese immigrants, mostly from [[Yunnan]], has reshaped its ethnic makeup.

=== KEY: Mandarin_Chinese_03f54d
HEADWORD: Mandarin Chinese
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Singapore | Taiwan | United Nations | Xinjiang | Yunnan
ENTRY:
Largest branch of the Sinitic languages, natively spoken by 71% of Chinese speakers, with nearly one billion speakers as a group. Its varieties stretch from [[Yunnan]] in the southwest to [[Xinjiang]] in the northwest and Heilongjiang in the northeast. Most varieties have four lexical tones, and many retain retroflex initial consonants. Some form of Mandarin served as a lingua franca for officials and courts from at least the 14th century. In the early 20th century a standard based on the Beijing dialect was adopted as the national language. Standard Chinese is the official language of [[China]] and [[Taiwan]], one of four official languages of [[Singapore]], and one of six official languages of the [[United Nations]]. It is the most commonly taught form of Chinese as a foreign language.

=== KEY: Mandarin_orange_973b9b
HEADWORD: Mandarin orange
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Citrus | Pomelo | Vietnam
ENTRY:
Small, rounded [[Citrus]] fruit, *Citrus reticulata*, usually eaten fresh or in fruit salads. The mandarin is oblate, unlike the roughly spherical sweet orange, which is a mandarin-pomelo hybrid. Its thin, loose peel makes it easier to peel and split into segments than most citrus. Ripe fruit is firm to slightly soft, heavy for its size, and pebbly-skinned. The tree is easily damaged by cold and grows in tropical and subtropical areas. Genetic studies indicate that the wild mandarin was one of the original citrus species and is an ancestor of many hybrids. Most commercial strains derive from hybridization with [[Pomelo|pomelo]], which gives them sweet fruit; the ancestral mandarin was bitter. The wild mandarin evolved in a restricted region of South China and [[Vietnam]].

=== KEY: Mandate_of_Heaven_4a59cb
HEADWORD: Mandate of Heaven
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Mencius | Qing dynasty | Shang dynasty | Zhou dynasty
ENTRY:
Chinese political doctrine of divine legitimacy for rulers, used in ancient and imperial [[China]]. Heaven was held to bestow its mandate on a virtuous ruler, the Son of Heaven, and to withdraw it if the ruler proved unworthy. Famine and flood were read as signs of Heaven's displeasure, and revolts often followed major disasters. The mandate did not require noble birth; dynasties such as the Han and Ming were founded by men of common origin but were seen as having succeeded because they gained it. The doctrine implied a right of rebellion against an unjust ruler. The classical statement of that right, in [[Mencius]], was often edited out of the text. The [[Zhou dynasty]] first used the concept to justify overthrowing the [[Shang dynasty]], and later dynasties, including the [[Qing dynasty]], invoked it.

=== KEY: Mandatory_Palestine_57d132
HEADWORD: Mandatory Palestine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Balfour Declaration | Gaza Strip | Israel | League of Nations | Ottoman Empire | United Nations | World War I
ENTRY:
British-administered [[League of Nations]] mandate territory (1920–1948). Britain administered it from 1922 under the Mandate for Palestine, after British forces drove the [[Ottoman Empire]] out of the Levant in 1917–18 during [[World War I]]. The [[Balfour Declaration]] of 1917 promised support for a Jewish "national home"; critics argue that it overrode self-determination for the Palestinian Arab population. Immigration and rising nationalism led to the 1936–1939 Arab revolt and a 1944–1948 Jewish insurgency. In 1947 the [[United Nations]] proposed partition. After the 1948 war the territory was divided among [[Israel]], Transjordan, and Egypt, which established an "All-Palestine Protectorate" in the [[Gaza Strip]].

=== KEY: Mandelbrot_set_1b6bd4
HEADWORD: Mandelbrot set
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benoit Mandelbrot | Fractal | IBM
ENTRY:
[[Fractal]] in the complex plane, named after mathematician [[Benoit Mandelbrot]]. It is the set of complex numbers *c* for which the iteration *z* → *z*² + *c*, starting at *z* = 0, remains bounded. Robert W. Brooks and Peter Matelski first defined and drew it in 1978, and in 1980 Mandelbrot produced high-quality images at [[IBM]]'s research center in Yorktown Heights, New York. Its boundary is a fractal curve showing ever finer detail at higher magnifications, and it is widely cited as an example of mathematical beauty.

=== KEY: Mandolin_c802d3
HEADWORD: Mandolin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonio Vivaldi | Italy | Lute | String instrument | Violin
ENTRY:
Small plucked [[String instrument|stringed instrument]] in the [[Lute|lute]] family, usually with eight strings in four courses tuned in perfect fifths, like a [[Violin|violin]] (G3, D4, A4, E5). Mandolins evolved from European lutes, and the Neapolitan mandolin emerged in [[Italy]]. The three main styles are the round-backed Neapolitan, the archtop, and the flat-backed mandolin. Neapolitan mandolins feature prominently in European classical music, including the works of [[Antonio Vivaldi]]. Archtop instruments are common in American folk, old-time, and bluegrass music, and flat-backed ones in Irish, British, and Brazilian folk music. Metal strings, which exert more pressure than gut, led to the stronger soundboards of modern instruments.
