Scientific study of Earth's climate, regarded as part of the [[Atmospheric science|atmospheric sciences]] and a subdivision of [[Physical geography]]. Climate is often defined as the distribution of weather conditions over a period of at least 30 years. Climatology's main research topics are climate variability, the mechanisms of climate change and modern climate change. Climatology includes some aspects of [[Oceanography]] and biogeochemistry.

=== KEY: Clock_98a892
HEADWORD: Clock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic clock | Babylon | Christiaan Huygens | Harmonic oscillator | Su Song
ENTRY:
Device that measures and displays time.

The clock is one of the oldest human inventions, meeting the need to measure intervals shorter than the day, the lunar month, and the year. The word derives from the medieval Latin *clocca*, 'bell'. A sundial shows time by the position of a shadow; duration timers include candle clocks, incense clocks, and the hourglass. Water clocks are possibly the oldest instruments after sundials: the bowl-shaped outflow clock is known from [[Babylon]] and Egypt around the 16th century BC, and the Chinese had developed advanced water clocks by AD 725. [[Su Song]]'s water-driven clock tower, built in 1088, incorporated an escapement.

The verge escapement made possible the first mechanical clocks, around 1300 in Europe, which kept time with oscillating balance wheels. Spring-driven clocks appeared in the 15th century. Accuracy improved after 1656 with the pendulum clock of [[Christiaan Huygens]]. The electric clock was patented in 1840, and electronic clocks became widespread in the 20th century with small battery-powered semiconductor devices.

The timekeeping element of every modern clock is a [[Harmonic oscillator|harmonic oscillator]], a resonator that vibrates at a particular frequency: a pendulum, balance wheel, tuning fork, quartz crystal, or the vibration of electrons in atoms emitting microwaves. The last is so precise that it defines the second; since 1967 the second has been defined by 9,192,631,770 cycles of cesium-133. [[Atomic clock|Atomic clocks]] provide the standard for Coordinated Universal Time.

Analog clocks show time with a face and moving hands, while digital clocks display numerals in 12-hour or 24-hour notation. Speaking clocks announce the time aloud.

=== KEY: Clothing_efe0bd
HEADWORD: Clothing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Denisovan | Fashion | Georgia (country) | Industrial Revolution | Morocco | Sewing machine
ENTRY:
Items worn on the body, also called clothes, garments, dress, apparel, or attire.

Clothing is typically made from textiles but may also be made from animal skins, furs, other natural materials, and synthetic fibers. It is a feature of every known human society, and its form and use vary with climate, culture, available materials, technology, social norms, and individual preference. It serves protective, physiological, social, and cultural functions: comfort, thermal regulation, protection from environmental hazards, and specialized protection for occupations, sports, and other activities. It also expresses social status, gender, cultural identity, religious affiliation, modesty, and personal style, and is central to [[Fashion|fashion]].

Estimates of when humans began wearing clothes range from 40,000 to as many as 3 million years ago. A 2003 study suggested at least 100,000 years ago, based on the divergence of the body louse, which cannot survive outside clothing, from the head louse; this has been dated to between 40,000 and 170,000 years before present, although later genetic analyses cast doubt on lice as a dating method. In September 2021 scientists reported evidence of clothes being made 120,000 years ago from deposits in [[Morocco]]. Early garments were likely fur, leather, leaves, or grass draped, wrapped, or tied around the body; knowledge of them remains inferential because such materials deteriorate quickly. Bone and ivory sewing needles from about 30,000 BC were found near Kostenki, Russia, and a needle at least 50,000 years old made by [[Denisovan|Denisovans]] was identified at Denisova Cave in Siberia. Dyed flax fibers from 34,000 BC have been found in a cave in [[Georgia (country)|Georgia]]. The oldest known piece of woven clothing is the Tarkhan dress, a linen garment over 5000 years old.

Some cultures, including those in the Arctic Circle, have historically made garments solely from treated animal furs and skins. Others use textiles woven, knitted, or twined from fibers such as linen, wool, cotton, silk, hemp, and ramie. The textile industry was the first to be mechanized, with the powered loom, during the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Garments may be draped from uncut rectangles of cloth, as with the dhoti, sari, Scottish kilt, and Javanese sarong, or cut and sewn by hand or [[Sewing machine|sewing machine]] to the wearer's measurements.

Clothing production has become one of the world's largest industries, employing millions of people and playing an important role in global trade, while facing challenges related to working conditions and sustainability.

=== KEY: Cloud_c8e227
HEADWORD: Cloud
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Climate
ENTRY:
Visible mass of liquid droplets, ice crystals, or other particles suspended in the atmosphere of a planetary body.

In meteorology a cloud is an aerosol composed primarily of water droplets and crystals. On Earth, clouds form when air is cooled to its dew point, or gains enough moisture, usually water vapor, to raise the dew point to the ambient temperature. They occur in the troposphere, stratosphere, and mesosphere. Nephology is the science of clouds. The word derives from the Old English *clud* or *clod*, meaning a hill or mass of stone.

Tropospheric clouds have Latin names under the nomenclature proposed by Luke Howard in 1802, which became the basis of the modern international system of the World Meteorological Organization. It divides clouds into five physical forms and classifies them by altitude to give ten basic genera, including cirrus, altocumulus, cumulus, stratus, nimbostratus, and cumulonimbus. Mid-level types take the prefix *alto-* and high-level types *cirro-*. Very low stratiform clouds reaching the ground are called fog and mist. Clouds above the troposphere have common names and are seen infrequently, mostly in polar regions. Clouds on other planets and moons are often composed of substances such as methane, ammonia, and sulfuric acid, as well as water.

Around 340 BC [[Aristotle]] wrote *Meteorologica*, the first known systematic treatment of meteorological topics. The first truly scientific studies of clouds were made at the beginning of the 19th century by Howard in England and Jean-Baptiste Lamarck in France.

Clouds affect [[Climate]]. They can reflect incoming sunlight, cooling the surface, or trap longwave radiation from the surface, warming it; altitude, form, and thickness determine which effect dominates locally. Clouds are the main uncertainty in climate sensitivity.

=== KEY: Coal_ff8cfc
HEADWORD: Coal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carboniferous | Climate change | Fossil fuel | Industrial Revolution | Peat
ENTRY:
Combustible black or brownish-black sedimentary rock formed as layers called coal seams.

Coal is mostly carbon with variable amounts of hydrogen, sulfur, oxygen, and nitrogen. It is a [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuel]], formed when plants decay into [[Peat|peat]], which the heat and pressure of deep burial over millions of years convert into coal. Grade depends on the maximum temperature and pressure reached: lignite (brown coal) forms under relatively mild conditions, followed in turn by sub-bituminous coal, bituminous coal, and anthracite (hard coal). Temperature matters more than pressure or burial time; anthracite requires at least 180 to 245 °C (356 to 473 °F).

About 90% of all coal beds were deposited in the [[Carboniferous]] and Permian periods, when falling sea level and wide river deltas produced extensive wetlands. Oxygen levels above 30% promoted wildfires and charcoal that decomposers could scarcely digest.

Coal is used primarily as a fuel, and some iron and steel-making burns it. It was known for thousands of years, but use was limited until the [[Industrial Revolution]], and the steam engine increased consumption. In 2020 coal supplied about a quarter of the world's primary energy and over a third of its electricity. China, which mines almost half the world's coal, is the largest consumer and importer, followed by India with about a tenth; Indonesia and Australia export the most.

Extraction and burning of coal damage the environment and human health, and coal is the largest source of carbon dioxide contributing to [[Climate change]]. Over 15 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide were emitted by burning coal in 2024, more than a quarter of total global greenhouse gas emissions. Many countries have reduced or eliminated coal power. Supporters of coal point to the affordable energy it has provided.

=== KEY: Cognition_3f6e3a
HEADWORD: Cognition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Attention | Cognitive development | Latin | Memory | Neuroscience | Perception | Psychology
ENTRY:
Mental processes that deal with knowledge, including the acquisition, storage, retrieval, transformation, and use of information. Cognitive processes are a pervasive part of mental life, and many occur simultaneously. They make individuals aware of their environment and help them plan and carry out appropriate responses.

Processes are typically categorized by function. [[Perception]] organizes and interprets sensory information to construct a coherent experience of objects and events. [[Attention]] prioritizes some aspects of experience while filtering out irrelevant information. [[Memory]] retains, stores, and retrieves information, and includes working memory and long-term memory. Thinking covers reasoning, concept formation, problem solving, and decision-making. Many activities deal with language, including its acquisition, comprehension, and production. Metacognitive processes deal with information about other mental processes. Classifications also distinguish conscious from unconscious processes and controlled from automatic ones. Whether feelings and emotions qualify as cognitions is debated.

Theories differ. Classical computationalism holds that cognitive processes manipulate symbols according to formal rules, much as computers execute [[Algorithm|algorithms]]. Connectionism models the mind as a network of nodes that pass information to one another. Representationalism and anti-representationalism disagree about whether cognitive processes operate on internal representations of the world.

Cognition is studied in [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], cognitive science, and philosophy. Some researchers study [[Cognitive development]], the growth of mental abilities from infancy through adulthood. The field has roots in antiquity and gained interdisciplinary prominence with the cognitive revolution beginning in the 1950s.

The word derives from the Indo-European root *gnō-*, 'to know', through [[Latin]] *cognōscere*, 'to learn, to understand'. It entered Middle English as *cognicioun* in 1447.

=== KEY: Cognitive_development_aebfeb
HEADWORD: Cognitive development
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jean Piaget | Neuroscience | Psychology
ENTRY:
Field of study in [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]] and [[Psychology|psychology]] concerned with a child's development in information processing, conceptual resources, perception and language learning. It is often described in four components: reasoning, intelligence, language and memory, which begin to develop in early infancy. [[Jean Piaget|Jean Piaget]] was a major force in establishing the field, proposing four stages: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational and formal operational. Many of his theoretical claims have since fallen out of favor, though his description of age-related changes in cognition is still broadly accepted. Alternative models include information-processing theory and Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory. Nature versus nurture is now widely regarded as a false dichotomy, because gene activity interacts with experience from the earliest points in development.

=== KEY: Cognitive_dissonance_20bc7f
HEADWORD: Cognitive dissonance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belief | Leon Festinger | Social psychology
ENTRY:
Mental phenomenon in which people hold fundamentally conflicting [[Belief|beliefs]], attitudes or behaviors. The resulting psychological discomfort motivates a person to change a belief, explain something away or take action that reduces the inconsistency. [[Leon Festinger|Leon Festinger]] proposed the theory in *A Theory of Cognitive Dissonance* (1957), arguing that people strive for internal consistency. People may reduce dissonance through rationalization, selective perception or avoidance of contradictory information. Festinger's theory remains one of the most influential in [[Social psychology|social psychology]].

=== KEY: Colonialism_7d1c07
HEADWORD: Colonialism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Decolonization | Indigenous peoples | World War I
ENTRY:
Practice of extending and maintaining political, social, economic, and cultural domination over a territory and its people by another people, in pursuit of interests defined in an often distant metropole. Colonizers differentiate themselves from the colonized and typically organize the territory into colonies separate from the metropole rather than annexing it. In settler colonialism, settlers seek to partly or completely supplant the existing [[Indigenous peoples]]. The term is often used as a synonym for imperialism, though scholars distinguish them.

Analogous practices occurred outside Europe, including Inca forced resettlements, Arab expansion from the 7th century, and Japan's administration of Taiwan from 1895 and Korea from 1910. The concept, however, was developed mainly to describe the European empires of the modern era, which spread from the 15th century to the mid-20th century, covering 35% of Earth's land by 1800 and peaking at 84% by the beginning of [[World War I]]. Expansion was driven by mercantile interests, strategic rivalry, and missionary zeal. It was in some cases justified by a civilizing mission, often rooted in belief in a Christian mission.

Assessments of its effects differ. Documented consequences include widespread violence, genocides, the enslavement of an estimated 12.5 million Africans in the transatlantic trade, and the disruption of Indigenous societies. Scholars have shown that variations in colonial institutions can account for variations in economic development and state capacity. Some analyses credit colonial railways, ports, and public health measures with improving institutions in some regions, while critics stress persistent inequality and resource dependence.

[[Decolonization]] proceeded in waves, with a large wave between 1945 and 1975. Some academics use the term neocolonialism for the continuation of colonial rule through indirect means.

=== KEY: Color_cb5feb
HEADWORD: Color
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ultraviolet | Visible spectrum
ENTRY:
Visual perception produced by the activation of the different types of cone cells in the eye by light. Color is not an inherent property of matter, but perception is related to an object's light absorption, emission, reflection, and transmission. Most humans have three types of cone cells (trichromacy); other animals may have a different number of cone types or sensitivity to other wavelengths, as bees do to [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]]. The brain processes the signals into sensations of hue, colorfulness, and lightness.

The [[Visible spectrum|visible spectrum]] extends approximately from 390 nm to 700 nm. Most light sources emit many wavelengths, and many more spectral combinations exist than color sensations; spectra that give the same sensation are called metamers. Isaac Newton named the colors of the spectrum in 1671. How the continuous spectrum is divided into named colors is a matter of culture and historical contingency; the ROYGBIV mnemonic is disputed, particularly over indigo and cyan.

The physical color of an object depends on how it absorbs and scatters light. Transparent objects transmit almost all light and are perceived as colorless; opaque objects absorb or reflect it; translucent objects appear colored because they scatter or absorb certain wavelengths internally.

Colors mix additively (light) or subtractively (pigments). Color spaces, abstracted as mathematical color models, assign numbers to colors and are essential to reproduction in print, photography, monitors, and television; well-known models include RGB, CMYK, HSL/HSV, CIE Lab, and YCbCr/YUV. In art, color theory governs the harmonious use of color.

Thomas Young proposed his trichromatic theory in 1801, and Johann Wolfgang von Goethe published his *Theory of Colors* in 1810.

=== KEY: Color_theory_fd5167
HEADWORD: Color theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Isaac Newton

Historical body of knowledge describing the behavior of colors in mixing, contrast, harmony and symbolism. It dates back at least to [[Aristotle]]'s treatise *On Colors*, and Bharata's *Nāṭya Shāstra* contains an early functional theory of color. === KEY: Color_vision_6bc5ac

It lies on Fountain Creek, 70 miles (113 km) south of [[Denver]], near the base of Pikes Peak, which rises 14,115 feet (4,302.31 m) on the eastern edge of the [[Rocky Mountains|Southern Rocky Mountains]]. The town was laid out in 1871 and incorporated on June 19, 1886. It is the state's second-most populous city and a key center for U.S. military and aerospace activities, including the U.S. Air Force Academy and Fort Carson.

Cancer of the colon or rectum, also called bowel cancer. Signs and symptoms may include blood in the stool, a change in bowel habits, weight loss, abdominal pain and fatigue. Risk factors include diet, [[Obesity|obesity]], [[Smoking|smoking]] and physical inactivity; red meat, processed meat and alcohol increase risk in the diet. Inherited disorders such as familial adenomatous polyposis account for less than 5% of cases. The cancer typically begins as a benign polyp that becomes cancerous over time. Screening of average-risk adults aged 45–75 effectively prevents the disease and reduces deaths. Treatment may combine [[Surgery|surgery]], [[Radiation therapy|radiation therapy]], [[Chemotherapy|chemotherapy]] and targeted therapy; cancer confined to the colon wall may be curable with surgery, but widespread cancer usually is not. The five-year survival rate in the United States is approximately 65.4% (data from 2016–2022). In 2022, there were 1.93 million new cases and 903,859 deaths worldwide, making it the third-most common cancer.

Species of very large [[Squid|squid]], believed to be the largest by mass. Its confirmed mass is at least 495 kilograms (1,091 lb), and its maximum total length is approximately 4.2 meters (14 ft). *Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni*, the only recognized member of its genus, belongs to the family Cranchiidae. It has the largest eyes of any documented animal, about 27–30 cm (11–12 in) across, and is the only member of its family with hooks on its arms. It inhabits the circumantarctic [[Southern Ocean]], where it is presumed to be an ambush predator and is likely a key prey item of the [[Sperm whale|sperm whale]].
