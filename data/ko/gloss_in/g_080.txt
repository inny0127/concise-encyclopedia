=== KEY: Line_geometry_2354d2
HEADWORD: Line
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axiom | Curve | Determinant | Euclid's Elements | Euclidean plane
ENTRY:
In geometry, an infinitely long straight object with no width, depth, or curvature, and a special case of a [[Curve|curve]]. It idealizes physical objects such as a straightedge, a taut string, or a ray of light. Lines are spaces of dimension one, which may be embedded in spaces of dimension two, three, or higher.

[[Euclid's Elements]] defines a straight line as a "breadthless length" that "lies evenly with respect to the points on itself" and introduces postulates as unprovable properties on which the rest of geometry rests. In modern geometry a line is usually either a primitive notion with properties given by [[Axiom|axioms]] or a set of points obeying a linear relationship. In an axiomatic formulation such as Hilbert's, any two distinct points lie on a unique line, and two distinct lines intersect at most at one point. In the [[Euclidean plane]], lines that do not intersect are parallel; in higher dimensions they are parallel if contained in a plane, and skew if not. In the plane a line may be written y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b the y-intercept.

Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same line. Three points in a plane are collinear if and only if the [[Determinant|determinant]] of the matrix of their coordinates is zero, or equivalently if the slope between one pair equals the slope between another.

With respect to a conic (circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola), lines may be tangent, touching it at a single point; secant, crossing it at two points; or exterior, not meeting it. A transversal intersects two other lines.

=== KEY: Lineage_anthropology_027ffb
HEADWORD: Lineage
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clan | India | Indonesia | Inheritance | Nigeria | Southeast Asia | United States | Western Europe
ENTRY:
Unilineal descent group tracing its ancestry to a demonstrably shared ancestor, known as the apical ancestor. The word derives from Middle English *linage*, from Latin *linea* ("line"). Lineages differ from [[Clan|clans]], which may lack a documented apical ancestor. Three forms are recognized: matrilineal, in which descent runs through the mother, as among the Minangkabau of [[Indonesia]] and the Khasi of [[India]]; patrilineal, which is more common globally and is found across much of [[Southeast Asia]]; and ambilineal, in which individuals choose their mother's or father's group, common in modern societies such as the [[United States]] and [[Western Europe]]. In bilineal, or double, descent, members belong to both lines, as among the Yako of [[Nigeria]]. Lineages shape [[Inheritance|inheritance]] of property and titles, often have religious significance, and provide stability and reciprocal obligations.

=== KEY: Linear_algebra_311c11
HEADWORD: Linear algebra
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthur Cayley | Functional analysis | Gaussian elimination | Matrix (mathematics) | Quaternion | René Descartes | Vector space
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerning linear equations such as a₁x₁ + ⋯ + aₙxₙ = b, linear maps, and their representations in [[Vector space|vector spaces]] and through [[Matrix (mathematics)|matrices]]. It is central to almost all areas of mathematics and is fundamental in modern presentations of geometry, including defining lines, planes, and rotations. [[Functional analysis]] may be viewed as the application of linear algebra to function spaces. It is also used in most sciences and fields of engineering, because linear structures are natural models for many physical phenomena, and in computing, including computer graphics and machine learning.

The procedure now called [[Gaussian elimination]] appears in the ancient Chinese text *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*. Systems of linear equations arose in Europe with the introduction of coordinates in geometry by [[René Descartes]] in 1637. The first systematic methods used determinants, first considered by Leibniz in 1693; in 1750 Gabriel Cramer used them to give explicit solutions, now called Cramer's rule. W. R. Hamilton discovered the [[Quaternion|quaternions]] in 1843, and Hermann Grassmann published his "Theory of Extension" in 1844. In 1848 James Joseph Sylvester introduced the term matrix. [[Arthur Cayley]] introduced matrix multiplication and the inverse matrix in 1856. Peano gave the first modern definition of a vector space in 1888. Linear algebra took its modern form in the first half of the 20th century, as earlier ideas were generalized into abstract algebra, and computers spurred research into efficient algorithms for elimination and matrix decompositions.

A vector space over a field F, often the real or complex numbers, is a set V with two operations: vector addition and scalar multiplication by elements of F. Under addition, V is an abelian group.

=== KEY: Linear_equation_b2b2e9

Equation in the form a₁x₁ + … + aₙxₙ + b = 0, with unknowns x₁,…,xₙ and coefficients that are not all zero. With one variable, it has exactly one solution. With two variables, its solutions form a [[Line (geometry)|line]] in the [[Euclidean plane]], which is the origin of the term "linear"; with n variables they form a hyperplane in [[Euclidean space]]. Linear equations are central to mathematics, [[Physics|physics]] and [[Engineering|engineering]], partly because nonlinear systems are often well approximated by linear ones. Coefficients are usually [[Real number|real numbers]], but the theory applies to any [[Field (mathematics)|field]].

=== KEY: Linear_programming_b712d9
HEADWORD: Linear programming
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Economics | Mathematical optimization
ENTRY:
Method for optimizing a linear objective function subject to linear equality and inequality constraints. The feasible region is a convex polytope, and a linear programming [[Algorithm|algorithm]] finds the point in it where the objective function is largest or smallest. It is a special case of [[Mathematical optimization|mathematical optimization]]. It is widely used in mathematics and, to a lesser extent, in business, [[Economics|economics]] and engineering, with applications in transportation, energy, telecommunications and manufacturing. Systems of linear inequalities were studied by Fourier, who published a solution method in 1827. Leonid Kantorovich and Wassily Leontief explored its applications in the late 1930s, and it became prominent during World War II. From 1946 to 1947, George B. Dantzig independently developed the general formulation for U.S. Air Force planning. Kantorovich and T. C. Koopmans shared the 1975 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.

=== KEY: Linguistic_anthropology_daa0c0
HEADWORD: Linguistic anthropology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Communication | Endangered language | Identity (social science) | Noam Chomsky
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Anthropology|anthropology]] concerned with how language influences social life. It originated in efforts to document [[Endangered language|endangered languages]], especially indigenous languages of North America, and it examines how language shapes [[Communication|communication]], [[Identity (social science)|social identity]] and group membership, and large-scale cultural beliefs and ideologies. It emerged from three paradigms. The first, anthropological linguistics, documents languages and their structure. The second, which Dell Hymes preferred to call linguistic anthropology, studies language use in context, with the speech event as its unit of analysis; Hymes also criticized the cognitive emphasis of [[Noam Chomsky]], arguing for an ethnographic focus on language in use. The third, begun in the late 1980s, uses linguistic tools to study anthropological questions such as identity, shared ideologies and narrative.

=== KEY: Linguistic_typology_f2004d
HEADWORD: Linguistic typology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin | Linguistics
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Linguistics|linguistics]] concerned with classifying languages by their structural features so that they can be compared. Its aim is to describe and explain the structural diversity and common properties of the world's languages. Subdisciplines include phonological typology (sound features), syntactic typology (word order and form), lexical typology (vocabulary) and theoretical typology (universal tendencies). Unlike genealogical linguistics, typology groups languages by formal similarity rather than historical descent, and modern samples are drawn evenly from different language families. In the Middle Ages, [[Latin]] was the model language of linguistics. In 1818 August Wilhelm Schlegel classified languages as lacking grammatical structure, agglutinative or inflectional. Modern typology emerged in the 1960s: at a 1961 conference on language universals at Dobbs Ferry, Joseph Greenberg proposed 45 linguistic universals based on data from 30 languages.

=== KEY: Linguistics_60965a
HEADWORD: Linguistics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comparative linguistics | Ferdinand de Saussure | Historical linguistics | Indo-European languages | Language | Phonetics | Phonology | Syntax
ENTRY:
Scientific study of [[Language|language]] and its structure, meaning, and use. Its core areas are [[Syntax|syntax]] (rules governing the structure of sentences), semantics (meaning), morphology (structure of words), [[Phonetics|phonetics]] (speech sounds and equivalent manual gestures in sign languages), [[Phonology|phonology]] (the abstract sound system of a particular language), and pragmatics (how the context of use contributes to meaning). Subdisciplines such as biolinguistics and psycholinguistics bridge many of these divisions.

Theoretical linguistics seeks to explain the universal nature of language, while applied linguistics uses findings about language for practical purposes, such as improving language education and literacy. Mathematical and computational linguistics apply mathematics and computational modeling to language.

[[Historical linguistics]] studies how languages change. Western work in the field dates to roughly the late 18th century, when it grew out of philology, and it was most widely practiced in the late 19th century. It was long the cornerstone of [[Comparative linguistics|comparative linguistics]], which sorts languages into families and reconstructs prehistoric proto-languages, beginning with the [[Indo-European languages]]. Attempts to link families into super-families such as a hypothetical Nostratic group are still not widely accepted.

Modern linguistics is associated with [[Ferdinand de Saussure]]'s structuralist view of language as a system of signs and with Noam Chomsky's generative grammar, which posits innate linguistic capacities underlying universal grammar. A continuing debate divides generative approaches, which stress formal rules and biological innateness, from functionalist and usage-based approaches, which stress language use, social context, and learning.

=== KEY: Lipid_9241f2
HEADWORD: Lipid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carboxylic acid | Cardiovascular disease | Cell membrane | Cholesterol | Fat
ENTRY:
Broad group of organic compounds that are hydrophobic or amphiphilic and soluble in nonpolar organic solvents. Lipids include [[Fat|fats]], waxes, sterols, fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, and K), monoglycerides, diglycerides, and phospholipids. They store energy, act in signaling, and serve as structural components of [[Cell membrane|cell membranes]]. The amphiphilic nature of some lipids lets them form vesicles, liposomes, or membranes in an aqueous environment. Lipids have applications in the cosmetic and food industries and in nanotechnology.

Biological lipids originate entirely or in part from two types of building blocks, ketoacyl and isoprene groups. On this basis the Lipid MAPS consortium divides them into eight categories: fatty acyls, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, sphingolipids, saccharolipids, and polyketides (from ketoacyl subunits), and sterol lipids and prenol lipids (from isoprene subunits). Fatty acyls are built on a hydrocarbon chain, typically 4 to 24 carbons long, that ends in a [[Carboxylic acid|carboxylic acid]] group. Fats are a subgroup of lipids called triglycerides, and [[Cholesterol|cholesterol]] is a sterol-containing lipid. Elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol is a major risk factor for [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]]. Humans and other mammals synthesize and break down many lipids but must obtain some essential ones from the diet.

In 1815 Henri Braconnot classified lipids into solid suifs and fluid huiles. In 1827 William Prout recognized fat, along with protein and carbohydrate, as an important nutrient. Théophile-Jules Pelouze reported the first synthetic triglyceride, tributyrin, in 1844, and Theodore Gobley discovered phospholipids in 1847. The French pharmacologist Gabriel Bertrand introduced the word *lipide*, from Greek *lipos*, "fat," in 1923.

=== KEY: Liquid_5635bf
HEADWORD: Liquid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boiling point | Crystallization | Gas | Intermolecular force | Mercury (element) | Water
ENTRY:
State of matter with a definite volume but no fixed shape. A liquid adapts to the shape of its container in the direction of a force such as gravity. Liquids are nearly incompressible, and their density is usually close to that of a solid and much higher than that of a [[Gas|gas]].

A liquid consists of atoms or molecules held together by [[Intermolecular force|intermolecular]] bonds of intermediate strength, which let particles move around one another while remaining closely packed. As temperature rises, molecules vibrate more intensely and move farther apart; at the [[Boiling point|boiling point]], cohesive forces no longer hold them together and the liquid becomes a gas. On cooling to the freezing point, molecules typically arrange into an ordered structure by [[Crystallization|crystallization]]. Liquid is the least common state of matter in the known universe, because liquids need a narrow range of temperature and pressure; most known matter is gas or plasma.

Only two elements are liquid at standard temperature and pressure, [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] and bromine. Francium, caesium, gallium, and rubidium melt slightly above room temperature. Other liquids include [[Water|water]], ethanol, gasoline, mayonnaise, paint, and milk. Liquid helium does not solidify even at absolute zero under standard pressure, owing to its quantum properties.

Liquids generally expand when heated; water between 0 °C and 4 °C is a notable exception. Water compresses by only 46.4 parts per million per bar. This incompressibility makes liquids suitable for transmitting hydraulic power but also causes water hammer and cavitation. Pressure in a liquid at rest increases with depth.

=== KEY: Liquid_crystal_display_d3bf2b
HEADWORD: Liquid-crystal display
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculator | Computer monitor | Pixel | Polarizer | Smartphone | Watch
ENTRY:
Flat-panel display that uses the light-modulating properties of liquid crystals combined with [[Polarizer|polarizers]]. Liquid crystals do not emit light, so a liquid-crystal display (LCD) relies on a backlight or reflector to produce images in color or monochrome. Arbitrary images are formed from a matrix of small [[Pixel|pixels]], while simpler devices such as digital clocks show preset digits or words. LCDs appear in [[Computer monitor|computer monitors]], televisions, instrument panels, aircraft cockpit displays and signage, as well as in [[Calculator|calculators]], [[Watch|watches]] and [[Smartphone|smartphones]]. They replaced bulky cathode-ray tube displays in nearly all applications from the late 2000s to the early 2010s. LCDs are not subject to screen burn-in, but they are susceptible to image persistence.

=== KEY: Literacy_4296cd
HEADWORD: Literacy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dyslexia | Mesopotamia | South Asia | Sub-Saharan Africa
ENTRY:
Ability to read and write, understood more broadly as the capacity to understand, use, and communicate information. Traditionally the term meant reading and writing; today it also covers understanding information, using numbers, communicating ideas, and working with digital technology. According to UNESCO, literacy is a lifelong learning process that goes beyond basic reading and writing skills. In 2015 the United Nations Statistics Division defined the youth literacy rate as the percentage of people aged 15 to 24 who can read and write a short, simple statement about everyday life with understanding.

Some researchers treat literacy as the varied ways people use reading and writing in specific situations. Since the 1940s the word has also denoted skill in a particular field, as in computer, scientific, financial, health, media, and visual literacy. Functional illiteracy, which concerns adults, has been defined in several ways, such as the inability to read well enough to manage daily living and employment tasks. It is distinguished from primary illiteracy and from learning difficulties such as [[Dyslexia|dyslexia]], though these categories have been contested.

Script is thought to have developed independently at least five times: in [[Mesopotamia]], Egypt, the Indus civilization, lowland Mesoamerica, and China. Between 3500 BC and 3000 BC the Sumerians of southern Mesopotamia invented writing, initially for managing trade and production. Literacy long remained largely confined to elites. One account puts global adult literacy at below 20% around 1800 and approximately 87% by the early 21st century, with remaining gaps concentrated in [[South Asia]] and [[Sub-Saharan Africa]], where over 750 million adults are affected, disproportionately women. Critics note that self-reported or minimum-threshold measures may overstate functional literacy.

=== KEY: Literature_f1506b
HEADWORD: Literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Homer | Mesopotamia | Rhetoric | Writing
ENTRY:
Body of written work, and more narrowly writing regarded as an art form, especially novels, plays, and poems. The term covers both print and digital writing, and in recent centuries it has expanded to include oral literature, much of which has been transcribed. Literature records, preserves, and transmits knowledge and entertainment, and it can also have a social, psychological, spiritual, or political role.

Definitions have varied over time. In Western Europe before the 18th century, literature denoted all books and writing. The word derives from Latin *literatura* or *litteratura*, "learning, writing, grammar," from *littera*, "letter." A value-judgment definition limits literature to high-quality writing in the *belles-lettres* ("fine writing") tradition; the 1910–1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* called it "the best expression of the best thought reduced to writing." "Literature" is sometimes used synonymously with literary fiction, written with the goal of artistic merit, but it can also include non-fiction genres such as biography, diaries, memoirs, letters, and essays. Literary criticism, one of the oldest academic disciplines, concerns the literary merit or intellectual significance of specific texts, while textual criticism and the history of the book treat texts as artifacts or traditions.

Oral literature is an ancient tradition found in all parts of the world. The earliest poetry is believed to have been recited or sung to remember history, genealogy, and law. Scholars describe the Vedic texts and early Buddhist texts as transmitted orally, though Goody argued that the vast and consistent Vedic literature likely involved both written and oral traditions. All ancient Greek literature was to some degree oral, and the earliest was entirely so; according to Michael Gagarin, [[Homer]]'s epic poetry was largely composed, performed, and transmitted orally. Native North American peoples north of Mesoamerica had no known writing systems before contact with Europeans, and their storytelling traditions served largely as practical moral and social lessons. Gunditjmara oral histories in south-western Victoria, Australia, tell of volcanic eruptions that a 2020 study dated to between 34,000 and 40,000 years ago.

Oratory was long considered a literary art; from ancient Greece to the late 19th century, [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]] was central to Western education. Around the 4th millennium BC the complexity of trade and administration in [[Mesopotamia]] outgrew human memory, and [[Writing|writing]] became a more dependable way to record transactions. In ancient Egypt and Mesoamerica, writing may instead have arisen from the need to record historical and environmental events. Print technology has since allowed ever wider distribution of written works, and the digital era has blurred the lines between electronic literature and other modern media.

=== KEY: Liver_f1a3cb
HEADWORD: Liver
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Glucose | Glycogen | Hepatitis
ENTRY:
Vertebrate organ, the largest internal organ and gland, central to metabolism, digestion, and detoxification. It is found only in vertebrates and has a similar structure in all of them. In humans it lies in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, below the diaphragm and mostly shielded by the lower right rib cage. Estimates of its functions vary but are generally cited as around 500, so it is sometimes called the body's chemical factory.

The liver detoxifies the body, synthesizes proteins and other biochemicals needed for digestion and growth, and carries out carbohydrate metabolism. It produces hormones, converts and stores nutrients such as [[Glucose|glucose]] and [[Glycogen|glycogen]], and breaks down red blood cells. As an accessory digestive organ it produces bile, which emulsifies dietary fat; the gallbladder stores and concentrates the bile. The Greek root *hepat-* gives terms such as hepatology and [[Hepatitis|hepatitis]].

A human liver is a dark reddish-brown, wedge-shaped organ that normally weighs about 1.5 kg and is about 15 cm wide. It has two lobes when viewed from above and four (left, right, caudate, and quadrate) from below, and is functionally divided into eight segments. Its dual blood supply comes from the hepatic artery, carrying oxygen-rich blood, and the portal vein, carrying blood rich in digested nutrients from the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, and pancreas. These vessels lead through sinusoids to hepatic lobules, the functional units, each made up of millions of hepatocytes, the basic metabolic cells.

How to compensate for the absence of liver function in the long term is not known. Liver dialysis can be used in the short term, no artificial liver has been developed for long-term replacement, and as of 2018 liver transplantation is the only option for complete liver failure.

=== KEY: Logarithm_1e62c0
HEADWORD: Logarithm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Leonhard Euler
ENTRY:
Mathematical function giving the exponent to which a fixed base must be raised to produce a given number. For example, the logarithm of 1000 to base 10 is 3, because 1000 = 10³; this is written log₁₀ 1000 = 3. More generally, if x = bʸ, then y = log_b x, so the logarithm to base b is the inverse of exponentiation with base b. Other examples are log₂ 16 = 4, since 2⁴ = 16, and log₁₀ 150 ≈ 2.176.

The base-10 logarithm is the decimal or common logarithm, widely used in science and engineering. The natural logarithm has the number e ≈ 2.718 as its base and is widespread in mathematics and physics because of its simple derivative. The binary logarithm, base 2, is used in computer science, information theory, music theory, and photography. The logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms of the factors: log_b(xy) = log_b x + log_b y.

John Napier introduced logarithms in 1614 to simplify calculation. Henry Briggs introduced base-10 logarithms and published extensive tables in *Arithmetica Logarithmica* (1624), and Adrian Vlacq extended them to numbers up to 100,000 by 1628. The slide rule, first described by William Oughtred in 1622, is also based on logarithms. The present-day notion comes from [[Leonhard Euler]], who connected logarithms to the exponential function in the 18th century and introduced the letter e for the base of natural logarithms.

Logarithmic scales compress wide-ranging quantities: the decibel expresses ratios of signal power and amplitude, pH measures acidity, and the Richter scale measures earthquake intensity. In general settings the logarithm is multi-valued; the discrete logarithm, which inverts the exponential function in finite groups, is used in public-key cryptography.

=== KEY: Logic_35ba12
HEADWORD: Logic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred North Whitehead | Argument | Aristotle | Bertrand Russell | First-order logic | George Boole | Gottlob Frege | Propositional logic
ENTRY:
Study of correct reasoning. Logic includes formal and informal branches. Formal logic studies deductively valid inferences and logical truths, examining how conclusions follow from premises by the structure of [[Argument|arguments]] alone, independent of topic and content. Informal logic is associated with informal fallacies, critical thinking, and argumentation theory, and examines arguments expressed in natural language. Used as a countable noun, "a logic" means a specific formal system. Logic plays a central role in philosophy, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics.

Logic studies arguments, each consisting of premises that lead to a conclusion. An example runs from the premises "it's Sunday" and "if it's Sunday then I don't have to work" to the conclusion "I don't have to work." Premises and conclusions express propositions that can be true or false. An argument is correct if its premises support its conclusion. Deductive arguments give the strongest support: if their premises are true, the conclusion must be true. Ampliative arguments reach new information not found in the premises and divide into inductive arguments, which are statistical generalizations, and abductive arguments, which are inferences to the best explanation. Arguments that fall short of correct reasoning often embody fallacies. A rule of inference, such as modus ponens ("p; if p then q; therefore q"), is a pattern that guarantees validity regardless of what the terms stand for. Formal logic uses formal languages with a limited vocabulary and exact syntactic rules, so natural-language arguments must be translated before their validity can be assessed.

The word comes from Greek *logos*, variously translated as "reason," "discourse," or "language." Logic has been studied since antiquity; early approaches include Aristotelian logic, Stoic logic, Nyaya, and Mohism. [[Aristotle]], in the 4th century BC, developed syllogistic reasoning in works later collected as the *Organon*, and his system was the main logic of the Western world for over two millennia. Modern formal logic has roots in the work of 19th-century mathematicians. [[George Boole]] introduced algebraic approaches in *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847). [[Gottlob Frege]]'s *Begriffsschrift* (1879) established modern quantificational logic. [[Bertrand Russell]] and [[Alfred North Whitehead]] wrote *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), which sought to ground mathematics in logic, and Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) revealed limits of formal systems.

Today the most commonly used system is classical logic, which consists of [[Propositional logic|propositional logic]], concerned with relations between whole propositions, and [[First-order logic|first-order logic]], which also treats predicates and quantifiers. Extended logics apply classical intuitions to questions of necessity, what is or should be, and what is believed or known. Deviant logics, such as intuitionistic logic, which rejects the law of excluded middle, reject certain classical principles. Logic underpins set theory and proof theory, and in computer science it supports programming languages, artificial intelligence, and circuit design.

=== KEY: Logogram_6539e2
HEADWORD: Logogram
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chinese characters | Cuneiform
ENTRY:
Written character that represents a word or morpheme, a semantic component of a language. The term derives from Ancient Greek *logos*, "word," and *gramma*, "that which is drawn or written." [[Chinese characters]], Egyptian hieroglyphs, and characters of [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] script are logograms, and a writing system that primarily uses them is called a logography. Non-logographic systems, such as alphabets and syllabaries, are phonemic: their symbols represent sounds directly and lack inherent meaning.

Some of the earliest recorded writing systems are logographic; the first historical civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica all used some form of logographic writing. All known logographies have a phonetic component, generally based on the rebus principle, in which a symbol stands for a sound-alike word. Adding a phonetic component to pure ideographs is considered a key innovation in enabling a script to encode human language adequately. In cuneiform as used for Akkadian, most glyphs carry sound values rather than word meanings.

Logoconsonantal scripts extend graphemes according to the consonants of the words, ignoring vowels; the main examples are Egyptian hieroglyphic, hieratic, and demotic. Logosyllabic scripts have graphemes for morphemes that, when extended phonetically, represent single syllables; they include cuneiform, Linear B, Chinese characters, Maya script, and Aztec script. Egyptian and Chinese add determinatives or radicals that narrow a character's meaning. Most Chinese characters combine a radical indicating a nominal category with a phonetic element.

Some logograms belong to no single language. The ampersand, a combination of the letters e and t, is a logogram in the Latin script representing "and" in many languages. Unicode, the generally accepted standard for computer character encoding, aims to include every character from every language.

In 774 the Frankish king [[Charlemagne]] conquered it and integrated it into the Frankish Empire. Lombard nobles continued to rule southern Italy until the [[Normans]] conquered them in the 11th century. The region of [[Lombardy]] takes its name from them. Scholars generally accept that the name means "long beards."

=== KEY: London_59ead8
HEADWORD: London
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Capital and largest city of England and the United Kingdom, on the [[River Thames]] in southeast England. Its metropolitan area (15.4 million) is the largest in Western Europe. London stands at the head of a 50-mile (80 km) tidal estuary leading to the North Sea and has been a major settlement for nearly 2,000 years. Since 1965 it has largely comprised the administrative area of [[Greater London]], governed by 33 local authorities and the Greater London Authority. The [[City of London]], its ancient core and financial center, was founded by the Romans as Londinium and retains its medieval boundaries; the City of Westminster, to its west, has been the site of national government for centuries.

The Romans founded the first major settlement around AD 47, about four years after their invasion. It lasted until about AD 61, when the Iceni, led by Queen [[Boudica]], burned it. Londinium had about 60,000 inhabitants in the 2nd century. An Anglo-Saxon settlement, Lundenwic, developed slightly west of the old city from about 500. Viking assaults from the 820s brought decline, and Alfred refounded London in 886, according to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. By the 11th century London was the largest town in England, and [[Westminster Abbey]] had been rebuilt in Romanesque style under King Edward the Confessor. London grew rapidly in the 19th century, becoming the world's largest city at the time.

London is a major global city and Europe's largest city economy, a leading financial center with the London Stock Exchange and the Bank of England. It has more than 50 universities and colleges. Landmarks include the Tower of London, Buckingham Palace, and the Palace of Westminster. The Royal Observatory at Greenwich defines the [[Prime meridian|Prime Meridian]] (0° longitude). The [[London Underground]] is the world's oldest rapid transit system, and in 2012 London became the first city to host three [[Summer Olympic Games|Summer Olympics]], after 1908 and 1948.

=== KEY: London_Bridge_29cde6
HEADWORD: London Bridge

ENTRY:
Series of crossings of the [[River Thames|Thames]] between the [[City of London]] and Southwark since Roman times. The first was a timber bridge built around AD 50 by the Roman founders of London. The medieval stone bridge was replaced in the 19th century by a stone-arched bridge. The current bridge, a concrete and steel box girder structure, was opened by [[Elizabeth II|Queen Elizabeth II]] on March 17, 1973. The 19th-century bridge was sold in 1968, and its granite facing was moved to Lake Havasu City, Arizona. London Bridge appears in the nursery rhyme "London Bridge Is Falling Down" and in *The Waste Land* by [[T. S. Eliot]].

=== KEY: London_Stock_Exchange_aef834
HEADWORD: London Stock Exchange
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brexit | City of London | Elizabeth I | Great Fire of London
ENTRY:
Global stock exchange in the [[City of London]], one of the world's oldest continuously operating exchanges. Founded in 1801, it has been run since 2007 by the London Stock Exchange Group, a constituent of the FTSE 100 index. Its Main Market serves established companies, and the Alternative Investment Market serves smaller, high-growth firms; more than 1,900 companies from over 60 countries are listed. As of mid-2025, its total market capitalization was about US$5.9 trillion. Its origins lie in the Royal Exchange, opened by [[Elizabeth I|Queen Elizabeth I]] in 1571 and rebuilt after the [[Great Fire of London]] in 1669. Barred from the Royal Exchange, stockbrokers traded from coffee houses, where John Castaing began publishing prices in 1698. A 1697 Act of Parliament penalized unlicensed brokering. Despite challenges after [[Brexit]], the exchange regained its position as Europe's largest stock market by total value in 2024 and 2025. As of 2025, London also had the world's largest concentration of mining capital, about US$600 billion, roughly 13% of the global mining market.

=== KEY: London_Underground_26b3d9
HEADWORD: London Underground
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Greater London | Rapid transit | Tunnel
ENTRY:
[[Rapid transit|Rapid transit]] system serving [[Greater London]] and parts of the adjacent home counties. Known as the Tube, it has 11 lines, 272 stations and about 250 miles (400 km) of track, managed by Transport for London. Only 45% of the network is underground; its nickname comes from its deep-level circular [[Tunnel|tunnels]]. The network began with the Metropolitan Railway, which opened on January 10, 1863, as the world's first underground passenger railway, using gas-lit carriages hauled by steam locomotives. The City and South London Railway pioneered underground electric traction when it opened in 1890. The lines were consolidated as London Transport in 1933. The network carried over 1.2 billion passenger journeys in 2024–25. Harry Beck's schematic Tube map, designed in 1931, became a global design icon.

=== KEY: Loneliness_55aa9b
HEADWORD: Loneliness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cardiovascular disease | Emotion | Epic of Gilgamesh | Exercise | Internet | Pet | Substance use disorder
ENTRY:
Unpleasant [[Emotion|emotional]] response to perceived or actual social isolation. Transient loneliness is generally seen as an adaptive response that motivates people to seek connection, much as hunger motivates eating. Chronic loneliness is linked to obesity, [[Substance use disorder|substance use disorder]], depression, [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]], high blood pressure and high cholesterol. Causes include systemic issues, genetic inheritance, cultural factors, lack of meaningful relationships, significant loss and excessive reliance on passive technologies, particularly the [[Internet]]. About one in six people worldwide experiences significant long-term loneliness, and adolescents report the highest levels. Therapy and antidepressants are medical treatments; social approaches include [[Exercise|exercise]], group activities, reconnecting with friends and owning [[Pet|pets]]. Loneliness has been a theme since the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]], and some academics have called it an epidemic, including former U.S. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy.
