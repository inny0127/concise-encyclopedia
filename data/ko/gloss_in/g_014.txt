ENTRY:
Naval battle of the First World War, May 31–June 1, 1916, off the [[North Sea]] coast of Denmark's Jutland Peninsula. Britain's [[Royal Navy]] Grand Fleet under Admiral Sir John Jellicoe fought Germany's High Seas Fleet under Vice-Admiral Reinhard Scheer. === KEY: Battle_of_Marathon_e29eac

ENTRY:
Greek victory over a Persian invasion force in 490 BC, on the plain of Marathon in Attica. === KEY: Battle_of_the_Atlantic_e05e90
HEADWORD: Battle of the Atlantic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Royal Navy | U-boat | Winston Churchill | World War II
ENTRY:
Naval campaign of the [[World War II|Second World War]], from 1939 to Germany's defeat in May 1945, and the longest continuous military campaign of the war. It pitted German [[U-boat|U-boats]], other warships and Luftwaffe aircraft against the [[Royal Navy]], the Royal Canadian Navy and the United States Navy, which protected Allied merchant convoys. Britain needed more than a million tons of imported material each week, making the campaign a tonnage war. The Allies gradually gained the upper hand, overcoming German surface raiders by the end of 1942 and defeating the U-boats by mid-1943. [[Winston Churchill]] wrote, "The only thing that really frightened me during the war was the U-boat peril."

Soviet silent epic film of 1925, directed by [[Sergei Eisenstein]], with Grigori Alexandrov as co-director. *Battleship Potemkin* dramatizes the 1905 mutiny aboard the Imperial Russian [[Battleship|battleship]] *Potemkin*, whose crew rebelled against their officers over maggot-infested meat. === KEY: Battleship_b84637
HEADWORD: Battleship
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aircraft carrier | Battle of Jutland | Battle of Leyte Gulf | Dreadnought | Ship of the line | World War I | World War II
ENTRY:
Heavily armored warship with a main battery of large-caliber guns, designed as a capital ship. Battleships, among the largest and most formidable warships built from the late 1880s, descended from the sailing [[Ship of the line|ship of the line]] through steam and ironclad designs. Dozens of pre-dreadnoughts were built in the 1890s and early 1900s. In 1906 the British launched the all-big-gun [[Dreadnought]], starting a naval arms race. In the only major fleet engagement of [[World War I]], the [[Battle of Jutland]] in 1916, neither side achieved a decisive result. Interwar treaties, beginning with the Washington Naval Treaty, limited construction. The rise of the [[Aircraft carrier|aircraft carrier]] in [[World War II]] relegated battleships to secondary duties, though they fought at the [[Battle of Leyte Gulf]] in 1944. After the war most were placed in reserve, broken up or used as targets. A number of battleships, predominantly American, remain as museum ships.

=== KEY: Bauxite_f5a83a
HEADWORD: Bauxite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aluminium | Basalt | Electrolysis | Gallium | Kaolinite | Provence | Sodium hydroxide
ENTRY:
Sedimentary rock with a high [[Aluminium|aluminum]] content, and the principal commercial source of aluminum and [[Gallium|gallium]]. It consists mostly of aluminum minerals, including gibbsite (Al(OH)₃) and boehmite (γ-AlO(OH)), mixed with iron oxides and the clay mineral [[Kaolinite]]. Bauxite is reddish-brown, white or tan, with a density of 2.7 g/cm³. Its name derives from its discovery in 1821 by French geologist Pierre Berthier near Les Baux in [[Provence]], southern France. Lateritic bauxite forms through weathering of silicate rocks such as [[Basalt]]. Bauxite is processed by the Bayer process, in which it is heated with [[Sodium hydroxide]] solution to dissolve the aluminum, and the resulting alumina is reduced by [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]]. In 2023, global output reached approximately 390 million metric tons, led by Australia, Guinea and China.

Recessed coastal body of water that directly connects to an ocean, lake or larger bay. === KEY: Bayes_theorem_876a76
HEADWORD: Bayes' theorem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bayesian inference | Pierre-Simon Laplace | Pythagorean theorem
ENTRY:
Mathematical rule for inverting conditional probabilities, allowing the probability of a cause to be found from its effect. For example, it gives the probability that a patient has a disease given a positive test, from the probability of a positive test when the disease is present. In symbols, P(A∣B) = P(B∣A)P(A)/P(B). Named after Thomas Bayes, whose essay was published posthumously in 1763, the theorem was developed in the 18th century by Bayes and independently by [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]]. It underlies [[Bayesian inference]], which uses it to update the probability of a hypothesis as new evidence arrives. Harold Jeffreys wrote in 1973 that it "is to the theory of probability what the [[Pythagorean theorem]] is to geometry."

=== KEY: Beauty_7a7f3a
HEADWORD: Beauty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aesthetics | Aristotle | David Hume | Immanuel Kant | John Locke | Plato | Truth
ENTRY:
Quality of objects that makes them pleasurable to perceive, and a central subject of [[Aesthetics]]. Landscapes, sunsets, people, and works of art are typical examples. As a positive aesthetic value, beauty is contrasted with ugliness, and it is often listed with [[Truth]] and goodness as one of three fundamental concepts of human understanding.

A central difficulty is that beauty seems both objective and subjective. It is treated as a property of things, yet it depends on the emotional response of observers, hence the saying that it is in the eye of the beholder. Objectivists hold that beauty is a mind-independent feature of things; subjectivists deny this, an approach influenced by [[John Locke]]'s distinction between primary and secondary qualities. Judgments of beauty rest on feeling yet claim universal correctness, a tension called the antinomy of taste. [[David Hume]] suggested that the sense of taste can be trained and that the verdicts of experts coincide in the long run, implying that standards of beauty are intersubjective. [[Immanuel Kant]] distinguished free beauty from adherent beauty, which depends on a thing's conception or function.

Classical conceptions, associated with [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]], define beauty by proportion, harmony, and the integration of parts into a whole. Hedonist conceptions see beauty as bound to pleasure, so that a thing is beautiful if it causes disinterested pleasure. Others define it through value, a loving attitude, or function. Empirical research has linked facial attractiveness to features such as symmetry, averageness, and signs of health, and some researchers read these as evolved cues of reproductive fitness. Researchers dispute how far biology and culture each shape standards of beauty.

Scientific law describing the absorption of [[Light|light]] by a solution.

=== KEY: Behavioral_modernity_153d28
HEADWORD: Behavioral modernity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Denisovan | Human | Last Glacial Period | Neanderthal | Population bottleneck | Upper Paleolithic
ENTRY:
Suite of behavioral and cognitive traits associated with [[Human|humans]] (*Homo sapiens*), including abstract and symbolic thought, planning depth, cumulative culture, and complex social learning. These traits are inferred from symbolic artifacts, ritualized behavior, music and dance, sophisticated hunting strategies, and advanced stone technologies. It is best understood as a mosaic of traits that emerged gradually and were expressed unevenly across time and populations. Anatomically modern humans had the necessary neural architecture by at least about 300,000 years ago, but symbolic artifacts appear sporadically in Africa between about 150,000 and 75,000 years ago and became widespread only after populations grew denser. The late [[Upper Paleolithic]] model places an abrupt cognitive change in Africa about 40,000–50,000 years ago, while gradualist models locate it earlier, in the African Middle Stone Age. It has been argued that climatic conditions of the [[Last Glacial Period]], causing [[Population bottleneck|population bottlenecks]], contributed to the success of *Homo sapiens* over [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] and [[Denisovan|Denisovans]].

=== KEY: Behaviorism_2054d5
HEADWORD: Behaviorism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Autism | B. F. Skinner | Cognitive psychology | Edward Thorndike | Ivan Pavlov | John B. Watson | Phobia
ENTRY:
Systematic approach to the study of behavior in humans and other animals. It assumes that behavior is either a reflex elicited by antecedent stimuli or a consequence of an individual's history of reinforcement and punishment. Behaviorism emerged in the early 1900s as a reaction to depth psychology and other traditional psychology, which had difficulty making testable predictions. [[Edward Thorndike|Thorndike]] pioneered the law of effect in the late 19th century, and in 1924 [[John B. Watson|John B. Watson]] devised methodological behaviorism, which rejected introspective methods. In 1945 [[B. F. Skinner|B. F. Skinner]] proposed radical behaviorism, arguing that covert events such as cognition and emotion are subject to the same controlling variables as observable behavior. Skinner's analysis of reinforcement became known as operant conditioning, while [[Ivan Pavlov|Pavlov]] studied conditioned reflexes. The late 20th-century cognitive revolution largely replaced behaviorism as an explanatory theory with [[Cognitive psychology|cognitive psychology]]. Applied behavior analysis treats developmental conditions such as [[Autism|autism]], and cognitive-behavioral therapies have proved useful for [[Phobia|simple phobias]], PTSD, and mood disorders.

=== KEY: Belief_f2a543
HEADWORD: Belief
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epistemology | Introspection | Knowledge | Proposition
ENTRY:
Subjective attitude that something is true or that a state of affairs is the case. In [[Epistemology|epistemology]], philosophers use the term for attitudes about the world that can be true or false: to believe that snow is white is comparable to accepting the truth of the [[Proposition|proposition]] "snow is white." Holding a belief does not require active [[Introspection|introspection]]; few people consider whether the sun will rise tomorrow, simply assuming that it will. Beliefs may be dispositional rather than actively thought.

Belief is one necessary condition for [[Knowledge|knowledge]] under the classical analysis of knowledge as justified true belief, a formulation challenged by Edmund Gettier's 1963 cases of justified true beliefs that fall short of knowledge.

Conceptions of belief differ, and there is no consensus. Representationalism, traditionally dominant, treats beliefs as attitudes toward mental representations, typically propositions. Functionalism defines beliefs by their causal role, and dispositionalism, a form of it, identifies them with dispositions to behave in certain ways (Roderick Chisholm). Interpretationism holds that an entity's beliefs depend on someone's interpretation of it (Daniel Dennett, Donald Davidson). Eliminativists such as Paul Churchland argue that nothing in nature corresponds to the folk concept of belief, while formal epistemologists would replace the all-or-nothing notion with credence, a spectrum of degrees of belief.

=== KEY: Bell_s_theorem_3d2f71
