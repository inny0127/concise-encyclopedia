=== KEY: Lake_Baikal_635d5e
HEADWORD: Lake Baikal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belgium | Caspian Sea | Eurasia | Great Lakes | Russia | Siberia | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
Rift lake in southern [[Siberia]], [[Russia]], and the deepest lake in the world. With a maximum depth of 1,642 m, it is also the largest freshwater lake by volume, holding 23,615.39 km³ of water, or 22–23% of the world's fresh surface water, more than all of the [[Great Lakes|North American Great Lakes]] combined. Its surface area of 31,722 km² is slightly larger than [[Belgium]], making it the seventh-largest lake by area and the second-largest in [[Eurasia]] after the [[Caspian Sea]]. At an estimated 25–30 million years old, it is the oldest lake in the world. Its basin lies in a rift zone where the Earth's crust is slowly pulling apart. Lake Baikal holds thousands of species of plants and animals, many of them endemic. UNESCO declared it a [[World Heritage Site]] in 1996.

=== KEY: Lake_Balkhash_166ef0
HEADWORD: Lake Balkhash
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aral Sea | Kazakhstan | Soviet Union | Xinjiang
ENTRY:
Lake in southeastern [[Kazakhstan]]. It is one of the largest lakes in Asia and the 15th largest in the world, covering about 16,400 km². The lake lies in an endorheic basin fed by seven rivers, chiefly the Ili, which carries most of the inflow from mountains in China's [[Xinjiang]] region. Its western part is fresh and its eastern half saline, and a narrow central strait divides the two. Like the [[Aral Sea]], Balkhash is shrinking because of water diversion and extraction from its feeders; it covered an estimated 23,464 km² in 1910 and 15,730 km² by 1946. The largest shore city, also named Balkhash, has about 66,000 inhabitants, and the local economy rests on mining, ore processing and fishing. The lake became part of Kazakhstan after the dissolution of the [[Soviet Union]] in 1991.

=== KEY: Lake_Chad_2e9402
HEADWORD: Lake Chad
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cameroon | Chad | Climate change | Niger | Nigeria
ENTRY:
Endorheic freshwater lake at the junction of [[Nigeria]], [[Niger]], [[Chad]] and [[Cameroon]] in western and central Africa. Its catchment area exceeds 1,000,000 km², and it is one of Africa's important freshwater fish-producing areas. The main source of water is the Chari River. The lake's area changes dramatically with the seasons. Its open water shrank from 25,000 km² in 1960 to just 2,000 km² in 1980, owing to climate change and human water diversion. Rainfall has partly recovered, and the lake is no longer estimated to be shrinking, although it remains threatened by [[Climate change|climate change]]. During a wetter past the basin held Mega-Chad, which covered about 400,000 km² and flowed into the Benue River. Around 900 AD, the Kanem people unified nomadic tribes into the Kanem Empire northeast of the lake.

=== KEY: Lake_Erie_d0c445
HEADWORD: Lake Erie
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Southernmost, shallowest and smallest by volume of the five [[Great Lakes]] of North America. Lake Erie is the fourth-largest of the five by surface area and the eleventh-largest lake in the world, with an area of 26,000 km². It lies on the international boundary between Canada and the United States. One of the five [[Great Lakes]] of North America, shared by the Canadian province of [[Ontario]] and the U.S. state of [[Michigan]]. The name derives from early French explorers, who named it for the indigenous Huron (Wyandot) people of the region. Artificial reservoir on the [[Zambezi]] River, bordering [[Zambia]] and [[Zimbabwe]], and the world's largest artificial lake by volume. It was filled between 1958 and 1963 after completion of the [[Dam|Kariba Dam]] at its northeastern end, flooding the Kariba Gorge. Brackish lake in northwestern [[Venezuela]], connected to the Gulf of Venezuela by a narrow strait. === KEY: Lake_Nicaragua_937f4e

ENTRY:
Freshwater lake in [[Nicaragua]], the largest in Central America. Of tectonic origin, it covers 8,264 km², making it the 19th-largest lake in the world by area and the tenth largest in the Americas. === KEY: Lake_Vostok_b0ace7
HEADWORD: Lake Vostok
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Enceladus | Europa (moon) | Kerosene
ENTRY:
Subglacial lake in central East [[Antarctica]], the largest of the continent's 675 known subglacial lakes. It lies beneath Russia's Vostok Station, under about 4,000 m of ice and roughly 500 m below sea level. Russian geographer Andrey Kapitsa first suggested its existence from seismic soundings made in 1959 and 1964; J. P. Ridley confirmed it in 1993 using ERS-1 laser altimetry. The overlying ice holds a paleoclimatic record of 400,000 years, while the lake water may have been isolated for 15 to 25 million years. The lake's isolated conditions may resemble those of ice-covered oceans on [[Europa (moon)|Europa]] and [[Enceladus]].

=== KEY: Lake_bc0321
HEADWORD: Lake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Canada | Great Lakes | Lagoon | Pond | Reservoir | Sediment | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Relatively large, fixed body of water in a basin on or near the Earth's surface, surrounded by dry land and separate from the ocean. Lakes are part of the [[Water cycle|water cycle]]. Most are fresh water and hold almost all the world's surface freshwater, but some are salt lakes with salinities even higher than seawater. In total they cover approximately 2.5 × 10⁶ sq km, less than 2% of Earth's surface.

The word comes from Middle English *lake*, from Old English *lacu* ("pond, pool, stream"). Lakes are typically larger and deeper than [[Pond|ponds]], but neither term has an internationally accepted definition; proposed minimum sizes range from 2 to 8 hectares, and the ecologist Charles Elton regarded lakes as bodies of 40 hectares or more. Lakes also differ from [[Lagoon|lagoons]], which are shallow tidal pools dammed by sandbars or similar material on coasts. Most lakes are fed by springs, precipitation, and runoff, and most have a natural outflow that maintains the average level. Endorheic lakes have no outflow and lose water only by evaporation or seepage.

Natural lakes are generally found in mountainous areas, dormant volcanic craters, rift zones, and areas of ongoing glaciation. Most lakes lie in the Northern Hemisphere at higher latitudes. [[Canada]] has an estimated 31,752 lakes larger than 3 sq km, and at least 2 million in total; Finland has 168,000 lakes of 500 sq m or more. On a geologic time scale, every lake has a finite lifetime, as it will eventually fill with [[Sediment|sediment]] or spill out of its basin.

Artificially controlled lakes are called [[Reservoir|reservoirs]], built for hydroelectric power, drinking water, industry, agriculture, aquaculture, or recreation. The [[Great Lakes]] of North America together hold about 21% of the world's surface freshwater, roughly 22,671 cubic km.

Major Hindu goddess of fortune, wealth, prosperity, beauty and fertility. Also known as Shri, she forms with Parvati and Sarasvati the goddesses called the Tridevi. Queen consort of the princely state of Jhansi and a leader of the [[Indian Rebellion of 1857]]. === KEY: Land_512ef7
HEADWORD: Land
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Glacier | Pangaea | Plate tectonics
ENTRY:
Solid terrestrial surface of Earth not submerged by the ocean or another body of water, also called dry land or ground. It makes up approximately 29% of Earth's surface and includes all continents and islands. It is almost entirely covered by regolith, a layer of rock, soil, and minerals forming the outer part of the crust. One-third of land is covered in trees, another third is used for [[Agriculture|agriculture]], and roughly one-tenth is covered in glacier ice; the remainder is desert, savanna, and prairie. About 30% has a dry climate. The word may also mean the natural resources land holds, including rivers, lakes, and the biosphere, and human modifications such as agriculture and architecture. The boundary between land and sea is the shoreline.

The word derives from Old English, from Proto-Germanic *landą*, "untilled land." Many place names end in *-land*, as in England, Greenland, and New Zealand; the Indo-Iranian equivalent *-stan* appears in Pakistan, Afghanistan, and elsewhere in Central Asia.

The earliest material found in the Solar System dates to 4.5672 ± 0.0006 billion years ago, and the primordial Earth had formed by 4.54 ± 0.04 billion years ago. Earth's atmosphere and oceans formed through volcanic outgassing, and the crust formed when the molten outer layer cooled. Continents are formed by [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], driven by the loss of heat from Earth's interior. Over hundreds of millions of years, supercontinents have formed and broken apart three times: Rodinia began to break apart roughly 750 million years ago, the continents recombined as Pannotia 600–540 million years ago, and then as [[Pangaea]], which broke apart 180 million years ago.

A continuous area of land surrounded by ocean is a landmass. There are four major landmasses: Africa–Eurasia, America, Antarctica, and Australia. They are subdivided into up to seven continents, which in order of land area are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia. In physical geology, land divides into mountain ranges and relatively flat interiors called cratons, both formed over millions of years. Streams shape the landscape, carve rocks, transport sediments, and replenish groundwater. At high elevations or latitudes, snow compacts into [[Glacier|glaciers]], which can be heavy enough to warp the crust. Terrain affects travel, mapmaking, ecosystems, and surface water flow, and it largely determines suitability for human settlement.

Earth's first cellular life likely originated on land, and biodiversity then evolved over hundreds of millions of years, interrupted by mass extinctions. Survival on land depends on fresh water, which constitutes only 3% of Earth's water. The study of land in general is geography; mineralogy studies minerals, petrology studies rocks, and soil science studies soils, including pedology (soil formation) and edaphology (the relationship between soil and life). In recent decades, scientists and policymakers have emphasized managing land more sustainably by restoring degraded soil, preserving biodiversity, protecting endangered species, and addressing climate change.

=== KEY: Language_4994a8
HEADWORD: Language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ferdinand de Saussure | Immanuel Kant | Jean-Jacques Rousseau | Language family | Linguistics | Ludwig Wittgenstein | Plato
ENTRY:
Structured system of communication consisting of grammar and vocabulary. It is the primary means by which humans convey meaning, in spoken and signed forms and through writing. Human languages are culturally and historically diverse and share the properties of productivity, which allows an infinite number of sentences, and displacement, the ability to refer to objects, events, and ideas not immediately present. Use of language relies on social convention, and it is acquired through learning. A 2026 estimate put the number of spoken or signed languages remaining at around 7,500, half of them described as endangered. Precise counts depend on an arbitrary distinction between languages and dialects. Academic consensus holds that between 50% and 90% of the languages spoken at the beginning of the 21st century will probably be extinct by 2100.

The English word derives from Proto-Indo-European through Latin *lingua*, "language; tongue," and Old French *language*. The term also covers constructed systems such as computer languages. Natural language is modality-independent: it can be spoken, signed, or encoded in writing, whistling, or braille. All languages rely on semiosis, the relating of signs to meanings. Oral, manual, and tactile languages contain a phonological system governing how symbols form words or morphemes, and a syntactic system governing how these combine into phrases and utterances. The Swiss linguist [[Ferdinand de Saussure]] distinguished *langage* (language as a concept), *langue* (a specific language system), and *parole* (concrete speech).

The scientific study of language is [[Linguistics|linguistics]]. Debates about its nature and origin go back to the ancient world: Gorgias argued that language could represent neither objective nor human experience, while [[Plato]] held that communication is possible because language represents ideas that exist independently of it. During the Enlightenment, [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] and Johann Gottfried Herder argued that language originated in the instinctive expression of emotion and was originally closer to music and poetry, whereas rationalists such as [[Immanuel Kant]] and René Descartes held the opposite view. In the 20th century, [[Ludwig Wittgenstein]] and others associated with the linguistic turn argued that philosophy is essentially the study of language.

Language is thought to have diverged gradually from primate communication systems when early hominins acquired the ability to form a theory of mind and shared intentionality, perhaps coinciding with an increase in brain volume. It is processed in many parts of the brain, especially Broca's and Wernicke's areas. Children acquire it through social interaction and generally speak fluently by about three years old. Language and culture are codependent, and language also signifies group identity and social stratification and serves for social grooming and entertainment.

Languages evolve and diversify. A group descended from a common ancestor is a [[Language family|language family]]; a language with no demonstrated relationship to any other is an isolate.

=== KEY: Language_acquisition_f27843
HEADWORD: Language acquisition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: B. F. Skinner | John Locke | Noam Chomsky | Plato | Recursion | Syntax | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Process by which humans acquire the capacity to perceive, understand and produce language. The term usually refers to first-language acquisition in infants, whether the language is spoken or signed. A child must acquire phonology, morphology, [[Syntax|syntax]], semantics and an extensive vocabulary, and [[Recursion|recursion]] allows speakers to produce an effectively infinite number of sentences. Speech perception precedes speech production, and learning builds step by step from individual phonemes. Theories have long disagreed about innate ability. [[Plato|Plato]] suggested that word-meaning mapping may be innate, while empiricists such as [[Thomas Hobbes|Thomas Hobbes]] and [[John Locke|John Locke]] held that knowledge arises from sense experience. Behaviorism, articulated by [[B. F. Skinner|B. F. Skinner]], attributed learning to reinforcement, while nativist theories associated with [[Noam Chomsky|Noam Chomsky]] posit an innate Universal Grammar. Second-language acquisition is often more effortful after early childhood.

=== KEY: Language_death_be2a61
HEADWORD: Language death
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin | Lingua franca | Sanskrit
ENTRY:
Process in which a language loses all its native speakers. A language dies when its last native speaker dies, and it becomes extinct when no one, including second-language speakers, knows it. The forced death of a language is called linguicide. In the modern period, from about 1500, language death has typically resulted from cultural assimilation and language shift to a dominant [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]], largely those of European countries. === KEY: Language_family_5e3ab9

Group of languages related through descent from a common ancestor, called a proto-language. Those accounts place him in the 6th century BC state of Chu during China's [[Spring and Autumn period]] (c. 770–c. 481 BC), describe him as a keeper of archival records at the Zhou court, and remember him as an elder contemporary of [[Confucius]]. Disillusioned with social decay, he is said to have departed westward through the Hao Pass, dictating the *Tao Te Ching* to the gatekeeper Yin Xi before vanishing. [[Sima Qian]] used the name Lao Dan in his *Shiji* (c. 91 BC).

Modern scholars remain divided over his identity and dates. In the 1920s and 1930s many Chinese and Western scholars rejected the traditional dating in favor of the 4th or 3rd century BC. Some regard the traditional figure as largely or wholly legendary, while others consider a historical Lao Dan plausible. The Guodian Chu Slips, buried around 300 BC, contain approximately 2,000 characters corresponding to about forty percent of the received text, though scholars caution that they do not by themselves resolve how it was composed.

Laozi was claimed as an ancestor of the [[Tang dynasty]] (618–907) and honored as a progenitor of the surname Li. In religious Taoism he was increasingly deified and identified with Laojun, a divine manifestation of the Tao.

=== KEY: Laplace_s_equation_3dcbdb

Second-order partial differential equation, named after [[Pierre-Simon Laplace|Pierre-Simon Laplace]], who first studied its properties. === KEY: Laser_d47bda
HEADWORD: Laser
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Crystal | Electromagnetic radiation | Laser cutting | Lidar | Optical disc | Photon | Ruby
ENTRY:
Device that emits coherent light through optical amplification based on the stimulated emission of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]]. The word began as an acronym for light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation; the earlier microwave device was called a maser, and Gordon Gould first published the acronym "LASER" in 1959.

The first laser was built in 1960 by Theodore Maiman at Hughes Research Laboratories, using a synthetic [[Ruby|ruby]] [[Crystal|crystal]] pumped by a flashlamp. It drew on theoretical work by Charles H. Townes and Arthur Leonard Schawlow and the optical amplifier patented by Gould. The principle of stimulated emission was first theorized by [[Albert Einstein]] in 1917.

In a lightbulb or star, photons are emitted at random from many energy levels. In a laser, the release of a [[Photon|photon]] is triggered by the nearby passage of another photon of similar energy, and the emitted photon is identical to the first. Both can trigger further emission in a chain reaction. This requires an active laser medium, a material with metastable states that stay excited for a relatively long time, plus an energy source that continues to "pump" it.

Lasers differ from other sources in their coherence. Spatial coherence lets a beam be focused to a tight spot or stay narrow over great distances, and high temporal coherence permits a very narrow frequency spectrum or ultrashort pulses lasting attoseconds.

Applications include fiber-optic communication, [[Optical disc|optical disc]] drives, laser printers, barcode scanners, semiconductor manufacturing, [[Laser cutting|cutting]] and welding, [[Lidar]], and laser surgery. The laser is regarded as one of the greatest inventions of the 20th century.

Mass extinction at the end of the [[Ordovician]] period, about 445 million years ago, and the first of the Big Five mass extinctions. Extinction of most of the world's [[Megafauna|megafauna]] at the end of the [[Pleistocene]]. Megafauna are typically defined as animals of 45 kg (99 lb) or more. Major extinctions occurred in [[Australia]] and New Guinea from about 50,000 years ago and in the [[Americas]] about 13,000 years ago, coinciding with the arrival of modern [[Human|humans]]. Overall about 65% of megafaunal species became extinct worldwide, rising to 88% in Australia and 83% in South America. Africa and Southeast Asia experienced more moderate losses. Most scholars support at least a contributory role for humans, through hunting, though climate change may also have contributed.

=== KEY: Late_antiquity_12d50c
HEADWORD: Late antiquity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Byzantine Empire | Classical antiquity | Constantine the Great | Justinian I | Middle Ages | Rashidun Caliphate | Sasanian Empire | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Transitional period between [[Classical antiquity|classical antiquity]] and the early [[Middle Ages]], roughly c. 250–750. It saw the end of the [[Western Roman Empire]] and of the [[Sasanian Empire]], the rise of the [[Byzantine Empire]], and the Arab conquests and formation of the [[Rashidun Caliphate]]. Diocletian reorganized the empire, and [[Constantine the Great]] legalized Christianity with the Edict of Milan in 313. The period saw the origins or ascendance of Christianity, Rabbinic Judaism and Islam, and the canonization of the Bible and the Quran. [[Justinian I]] codified Roman law. Historians once accepted the decline narrative of Edward Gibbon, but many now emphasize cultural renewal and adaptation.

=== KEY: Late_modern_period_a18f27
HEADWORD: Late modern period
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Revolution | Cold War | Contemporary history | French Revolution | Industrial Revolution | Information Age | Soviet Union | World War II
ENTRY:
Period of history after the early modern period, generally dated from 1789. Its transition was marked by the [[American Revolution]], the [[French Revolution]] and the start of the [[Industrial Revolution]] around 1760. Historians disagree on its end: some close it with the end of [[World War II]] in 1945, while others extend it to the present as [[Contemporary history|contemporary history]]. The [[Cold War]] is often treated as a subperiod, from the Truman Doctrine of March 12, 1947, to the dissolution of the [[Soviet Union]] on December 26, 1991. Other proposed labels include the [[Information Age]], which began in the mid-20th century, and postmodernity.

=== KEY: Lathe_ed7d5b
HEADWORD: Lathe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Industrial Revolution | Potter's wheel | Warring States period | Woodwind instrument
ENTRY:
Machine tool rotating a workpiece about its axis, with tools applied to shape it. Lathes are used in woodturning, metalworking and glass-working, and can shape pottery, the best-known such design being the [[Potter's wheel|potter's wheel]]. Most metalworking lathes can produce most solids of revolution, plane surfaces and screw threads. The earliest evidence is a depiction in the tomb of Petosiris in the 4th century BC [[Ancient Egypt|Egypt]]. During the [[Warring States period]] in China, around 400 BC, rotary lathes were used to sharpen tools and weapons on an industrial scale. Precision metal-cutting lathes developed during the lead-up to the [[Industrial Revolution]], and the slide-rest, which guides the cutting tool to produce accurate cylindrical or conical surfaces, was a key advance. Lathes make objects such as screws, bowls, table legs and [[Woodwind instrument|woodwind instruments]].

=== KEY: Latin_728057
HEADWORD: Latin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Indo-European languages | Lazio | Norman Conquest | Renaissance | Roman Republic | Romance languages | Rome
ENTRY:
Classical language of the Italic branch of the [[Indo-European languages]], originally spoken in Latium (now [[Lazio]]) around [[Rome]]. Through the expansion of the [[Roman Republic]] it became the dominant language of the Italian Peninsula and then of the Roman Empire. Its grammar is highly fusional, with inflections for case, number, person, gender, tense, mood, voice, and aspect.

Scholars distinguish several phases. Old Latin, spoken until 75 BC, survives in inscriptions and the comedies of Plautus and Terence. Classical Latin, from about 75 BC to AD 200, was a conscious creation of orators, poets, and historians. Vulgar Latin denotes informal registers, which Cicero termed *sermo vulgi* ("the speech of the masses"); regional change in these between the 6th and 9th centuries produced the distinct [[Romance languages]]. Late Latin is the written form from the 3rd century AD onward.

Latin is often called a "dead language," but it did not undergo language death as formally defined. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire it remained the common language of international communication, science, and scholarship in Europe into the early 19th century. Medieval Latin was used across Western and Catholic Europe from the 9th century to the [[Renaissance]], which developed a classicizing form, the basis for Neo-Latin. Latin is now generally studied to be read rather than spoken.

Latin contributed many words to English, particularly after the [[Norman Conquest]], and its roots recur in theology, the sciences, medicine, and law. Ecclesiastical Latin remains the official language of the Holy See and the Roman Rite of the [[Catholic Church]].
