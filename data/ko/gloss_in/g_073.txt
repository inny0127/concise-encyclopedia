=== KEY: Jumping_5f51eb
HEADWORD: Jumping
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: High jump | Kinetic energy | Long jump | Organism | Running | Tendon
ENTRY:
Form of locomotion in which an [[Organism|organism]] or robotic system propels itself through the air along a ballistic trajectory. Jumping is distinguished from [[Running|running]] by the long aerial phase and high angle of launch. Kangaroos use hopping as their primary locomotion, while frogs jump mainly to escape predators. All jumping requires force against a substrate, such as ground or water, and muscles add [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] to the body during launch. Many jumping animals have long legs, and some store energy in elastic [[Tendon|tendons]] before takeoff to release it at a higher power than muscle alone. A running jump adds horizontal velocity, so jumpers travel farther when starting from a run. Jumping is also central to the [[Long jump]], [[High jump]] and show jumping.

=== KEY: Jumping_spider_50ebb7
HEADWORD: Jumping spider
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Species
ENTRY:
Spider of the family Salticidae, the largest spider family, with almost 700 genera and around 7,000 described [[Species|species]]. Jumping spiders have some of the best vision among [[Arthropod|arthropods]] and use sight in courtship, hunting and navigation. Most species move unobtrusively but can make agile jumps, especially when hunting or escaping sudden threats. All have four pairs of eyes, and the large anterior median pair provides detailed vision that helps the spider aim its leaps. They are generally recognized by their eye pattern. Silk safety lines anchor them while they jump, and they build silken "pup tents" for shelter, where they molt and store egg cases. Body length generally ranges from 1 to 25 mm (0.04–0.98 in), and the largest species is *Hyllus giganteus*.

=== KEY: Jungle_4ddf4c
HEADWORD: Jungle
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Land covered with dense forest and tangled vegetation, usually in [[Tropical climate|tropical climates]]. The word derives from the [[Sanskrit]] *jaṅgala*, meaning rough and arid, and entered English in the 18th century through Hindustani. In its strict sense, a jungle is ground-level vegetation so dense that travelers must cut their way through, which distinguishes it from a [[Rainforest|rainforest]], whose understory is usually open. Monsoon forests and [[Mangrove|mangroves]] are often called jungles in this sense. In a looser sense, jungle has meant any humid tropical forest, a usage largely replaced by "tropical forest" and "rainforest" since the 1970s. As a metaphor, the word describes lawless or unruly situations, as in Upton Sinclair's *The Jungle* (1906), about workers at the Chicago Stockyards.

ENTRY:
Genus of coniferous [[Tree|trees]] and [[Shrub|shrubs]] in the cypress family, with between 50 and 67 species, depending on taxonomic authority. Junipers are widely distributed throughout the [[Northern Hemisphere]]. The highest known juniper forest, at 4,900 m (16,100 ft), grows in southeastern [[Tibet]] and the northern [[Himalayas]]. === KEY: Jupiter_89c5c3
HEADWORD: Jupiter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Galilean moons | Gas giant | Great Red Spot | Helium | Hydrogen | Magnetic field | Solar System
ENTRY:
Fifth planet from the Sun and the largest in the [[Solar System]]. A [[Gas giant|gas giant]], it has a mass nearly 2.5 times that of all the other planets combined and slightly less than one-thousandth that of the Sun, and its diameter is 11 times that of Earth. It orbits the Sun at a distance of 5.20 AU (778.5 Gm) with an orbital period of 11.86 years. It is the third-brightest natural object in Earth's night sky, after the Moon and Venus, and was named after the chief deity of ancient Roman religion.

The atmosphere is 76% [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] and 24% [[Helium|helium]] by mass, with traces of carbon, oxygen, sulfur, neon, ammonia, and water vapor. The outer atmosphere is divided into latitudinal bands, with turbulence and storms along their boundaries; the most obvious result is the [[Great Red Spot]], a giant storm recorded since 1831. Rotating once in about ten hours, it is an oblate spheroid, its equatorial radius about 7% larger than its polar radius. Its interior is believed to consist of an outer mantle of fluid metallic hydrogen and a diffuse inner core of denser material. Its [[Magnetic field|magnetic field]], generated by eddy currents within the fluid metallic hydrogen, is the strongest and second-largest contiguous structure in the Solar System.

At least 115 moons orbit Jupiter. The four largest, Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, are the [[Galilean moons]]; they are visible with common binoculars, and Ganymede is larger than the planet Mercury. A faint ring system consists mainly of dust. Since 1973, nine robotic probes have visited the planet: seven flybys and two dedicated orbiters, with two more en route.

=== KEY: Jurassic_b7d583
HEADWORD: Jurassic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cretaceous | Gondwana | Mesozoic | Pangaea | Plesiosaur | Teleost | Triassic–Jurassic extinction
ENTRY:
Geologic period of the [[Mesozoic]] Era, lasting about 58.3 million years, from 201.4 to 143.1 million years ago. It is named after the Jura Mountains, where limestone strata from the period were first identified. The Jurassic began with the [[Triassic–Jurassic extinction|Triassic–Jurassic extinction event]], associated with the eruption of the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province. By its start, the supercontinent [[Pangaea]] had begun rifting into Laurasia and [[Gondwana]], and the climate was warmer than today, with no ice caps. Dinosaurs became the dominant land animals, and the first stem-group birds evolved from theropod dinosaurs. Marine reptiles such as ichthyosaurs and [[Plesiosaur|plesiosaurs]] inhabited the oceans, and modern sharks, rays and [[Teleost|teleost]] fish first appeared. The end of the Jurassic has no clear boundary with the [[Cretaceous]], the only boundary between geological periods to remain formally undefined.

=== KEY: Jurisprudence_d7a5ba
HEADWORD: Jurisprudence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Aristotle | Confucius | Law
ENTRY:
Philosophy of [[Law|law]], also called legal theory or legal science. It applies philosophical methods to questions such as what the law is and how it ought to be. Thinkers including [[Confucius]] and [[Aristotle]] wrote on law, and contemporary academic jurisprudence traces its origin to the early 17th century and the [[Age of Enlightenment]]. Among the oldest conceptions of law are natural law theories, instrumental theories, and law as an expression of social norms and custom. In the 20th century, the legal positivism of H. L. A. Hart and Hans Kelsen became dominant. General jurisprudence asks what law is and what characteristics it has in every jurisdiction, while normative jurisprudence evaluates law philosophically. In civil law jurisdictions, the term refers to case law or precedent.

=== KEY: Jury_c37477
HEADWORD: Jury
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Common law | Middle Ages | Sharia | Trial
ENTRY:
Sworn body of people, called jurors, convened to hear evidence in a [[Trial|trial]] and render an impartial verdict. The jury system developed in England during the [[Middle Ages]] and is a hallmark of [[Common law|common law]] systems. Juries are commonly used in countries with legal systems derived from the British Empire, such as the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia and Ireland. Most other countries, whose legal systems derive from European civil law or [[Sharia|Islamic sharia law]], usually determine guilt with a single professional judge. Most trial juries, called petit juries, have up to 15 members. Grand juries, used mostly in U.S. federal courts and some state jurisdictions, have between 16 and 23 members and decide whether there is enough evidence for a criminal trial to go forward. Under the Fifth Amendment, federal charges for "capital, or otherwise infamous" crimes require a grand jury indictment.

=== KEY: Justice_8cec7d
HEADWORD: Justice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Jean-Jacques Rousseau | John Locke | Nicomachean Ethics | Plato | Punishment | Utilitarianism
ENTRY:
Concept of treating individuals fairly, central to morality, law, and political philosophy. A frequently cited core definition comes from the Institutes of Justinian, a 6th-century codification of Roman law: "the constant and perpetual will to render to each his due."

Early Western theories were developed by Greek philosophers. For [[Plato]], in *The Republic*, justice is balance and harmony: everyone having and doing what they are responsible for or what belongs to them. He compared the three parts of a person, reason, spirit, and desire, with the three parts of a city. [[Aristotle]], in the [[Nicomachean Ethics]], treated justice both as nearly synonymous with virtue and as the particular virtue of giving people what they deserve; his particular justice divides into distributive justice, which allocates goods in proportion to merit, and corrective justice, which restores balance after exchanges or harms. The classical Confucian theory treated justice as a personal and public virtue that did not include freedom or equality and was not part of the law.

Modern Western notions have roots in Christian theology and divine command theory, under which God's commands determine morality and therefore justice. In the 17th century, [[John Locke]] held that justice derives from natural law, while [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] advocated social contract theory, in which justice arises from mutual agreement to be governed. Modern frameworks include distributive justice; [[Utilitarianism|utilitarian]] theories, which look to the future consequences of [[Punishment|punishment]]; retributive justice, which matches punishment to past wrongdoing; and restorative justice, which focuses on the needs of victims, offenders, and society. Procedural justice concerns the impartiality of the processes by which outcomes are reached.

Widely used climate classification dividing Earth's climates into five main groups. The groups are A ([[Tropical climate|tropical]]), B (arid), C (temperate), D (continental) and E (polar), and subgroups are given by letters for seasonal [[Precipitation|precipitation]] and [[Temperature|temperature]]. The Trewartha system modified it in 1966 to create a more refined middle-latitude zone.

Conventional definition of the edge of space, 100 kilometers above [[Sea level|mean sea level]]. === KEY: Karl_Marx_af66a7
HEADWORD: Marx, Karl
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Capitalism | Friedrich Engels | Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel | London | Marxism | Paris Commune
ENTRY:
German philosopher, social and political theorist, journalist, and revolutionary socialist. Born in Trier in the Kingdom of Prussia to a family of Jewish descent that had converted to Christianity, Marx earned a doctorate in philosophy from the University of Jena in 1841. He was influenced by [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel]] and the Young Hegelians, and began a close collaboration with [[Friedrich Engels]] in Paris. With Engels he wrote *The Communist Manifesto* (1848), which lays out a program for revolution. Expelled from Germany after the Revolutions of 1848, he settled in [[London]], where he researched in the British Museum and wrote the *Grundrisse* notebooks and the three volumes of *Das Kapital* (1867–1894). He became a leading figure in the International Workingmen's Association, in which he opposed the anarchists led by Mikhail Bakunin, and celebrated the [[Paris Commune]] of 1871.

Marx developed the theory of historical materialism, holding that societies develop through class conflict. Under [[Capitalism|capitalism]], this is the conflict between the bourgeoisie, who control the means of production, and the proletariat, who sell their labor for wages. Using a dialectical approach, he argued that capital accumulation produces internal contradictions that would lead to the system's self-destruction, and that the working class would then establish a classless, stateless society.

His ideas, collectively called [[Marxism]], inspired revolutionary movements, and Marxism–Leninism became the guiding ideology of states formed in the 20th century. His work is both widely praised and widely criticized. Critics point to the authoritarian outcomes of states founded in his name and to the failure of capitalism to collapse as predicted, while supporters credit his influence on heterodox economics and his role as a principal architect of modern sociology.
