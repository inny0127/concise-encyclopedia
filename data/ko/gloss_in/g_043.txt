=== KEY: Erosion_35b6b7
HEADWORD: Erosion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Appalachian Mountains | Badlands | Deforestation | Landslide | Sediment | Weathering
ENTRY:
Action of surface processes, such as water flow or wind, that removes soil, rock, or dissolved material from one location on the Earth's crust and transports it to another, where it is deposited. Erosion is distinct from [[Weathering|weathering]], which involves no movement. Removal of rock or soil as clastic [[Sediment|sediment]] is physical erosion; removal by dissolution is chemical erosion. Material may travel a few millimeters or thousands of kilometers.

Agents include rainfall, bedrock wear in rivers, coastal erosion by waves, glacial plucking and abrasion, wind abrasion, groundwater processes, and mass movements such as [[Landslide|landslides]]. Physical erosion typically proceeds fastest on steep slopes. Rainfall and runoff produce four main types of soil erosion in increasing severity: splash, sheet, rill, and gully erosion, and extreme gully erosion can form [[Badlands|badlands]]. In rivers, erosion is first mainly downward, producing V-shaped valleys, then lateral, widening the valley floor into a floodplain; most occurs during floods.

Erosion is natural, but human activities have increased the global rate of soil erosion by 10–40 times. At agricultural sites in the [[Appalachian Mountains]], intensive farming has caused erosion at up to 100 times the natural rate. On-site effects include reduced agricultural productivity and ecological collapse from loss of nutrient-rich topsoil, sometimes leading to desertification; off-site effects include sedimentation of waterways, eutrophication, and damage to roads and houses. Water and wind erosion together account for about 84% of the global extent of degraded land. Major human drivers are intensive agriculture, [[Deforestation|deforestation]], roads, anthropogenic climate change, and urban sprawl, though prevention and remediation practices can limit erosion.

ENTRY:
Methods for reliable delivery of digital data over unreliable communication channels. Error detection identifies errors caused by noise during transmission, and error correction reconstructs the original data. Both add redundant check bits to a message. Common detecting codes include the parity bit and cyclic redundancy checks. Hamming codes correct errors; the (7,4) code uses three parity bits to correct single-bit errors in four data bits. Volcanic eruption in [[Campania]], Italy. In 79 AD [[Mount Vesuvius]] ejected a cloud of tephra and gases to a height of 33 km, reaching the [[Stratosphere|stratosphere]]. The eruption released about 100,000 times the thermal energy of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. === KEY: Ethics_0f6805
HEADWORD: Ethics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Buddhism | Confucianism | Consequentialism | Deontology | Hinduism | Socrates | Utilitarianism
ENTRY:
Philosophical study of moral phenomena, also called moral philosophy, concerned with what people ought to do and which behavior is morally right. It examines the principles governing the moral evaluation of conduct, character traits, and institutions, and asks questions such as "How should one live?" and "What gives meaning to life?". It is usually divided into normative ethics, applied ethics, and metaethics.

Normative ethics seeks general principles that determine whether acts are right or wrong. Applied ethics examines concrete problems such as abortion, the treatment of animals, and business practices. Metaethics explores the underlying assumptions of ethics, asking whether there are objective moral facts, how moral knowledge is possible, and how moral judgments motivate people. Descriptive ethics, studied in anthropology, sociology, and history, records the moral codes societies actually hold. Ethics is closely connected to value theory, which studies intrinsic and instrumental value, and to moral psychology, which investigates processes such as moral reasoning and the formation of character.

The three most influential normative theories are [[Consequentialism|consequentialism]], [[Deontology|deontology]], and virtue ethics. Consequentialism, also called teleological ethics, holds that an act is right if it brings the best consequences; the term was coined in the 20th century by G. E. M. Anscombe. Classical [[Utilitarianism|utilitarianism]], formulated in the late 18th century, holds that pleasure is good and the action producing the most overall pleasure is right. Deontologists focus on acts themselves, which must adhere to duties such as telling the truth and keeping promises. Virtue ethics treats the manifestation of virtues such as courage and compassion as the fundamental moral principle. The schools are usually presented as alternatives but can overlap, sometimes recommending the same action for different reasons. Systems with several basic principles may face ethical dilemmas when the principles conflict. Debate between moral realism, which posits objective moral truths, and relativism, which denies universal standards, continues, as do disputes such as the trolley problem, which sets utilitarian sacrifice against prohibitions on harming innocents.

The English word comes from the Ancient Greek *êthos*, 'character', which gave *ēthikós*, translated into Latin as *ethica* and entering English in the 15th century through Old French *éthique*. *Morality* comes from Latin *moralis*, 'manners'. The two terms are usually used interchangeably, though some philosophers distinguish them, and codes in specific areas are usually called ethics, as in business ethics.

The history of ethics began in the ancient period with ethical principles and theories in Egypt, India, China, and Greece. This period saw the emergence of teachings associated with [[Hinduism]], [[Buddhism]], and [[Confucianism]], and the contributions of philosophers such as [[Socrates]] and [[Aristotle]]. Medieval ethical thought was strongly shaped by religious teaching. The modern period shifted to a more secular approach concerned with moral experience, reasons for acting, and the consequences of actions, and metaethics emerged as an influential field in the 20th century.

The church was administratively part of the [[Coptic Orthodox Church|Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria]] until it was granted autocephaly, with its own patriarch, in 1959. It has between 38 million and 51 million adherents in [[Ethiopia|Ethiopia]] and 60 million members worldwide, and it is a founding member of the World Council of Churches. Its name, *Tewahedo*, is a Ge'ez word meaning "united as one", referring to the miaphysite belief in one united nature of Christ. Around 500 bishops refused to accept the two-natures doctrine decreed by the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

=== KEY: Ethnic_conflict_051dde
HEADWORD: Ethnic conflict
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clifford Geertz | Cold War | Federalism | Yugoslav Wars
ENTRY:
Conflict between two or more ethnic groups in which the individuals involved expressly fight for their group's position within society. Political, social, economic or religious sources may underlie it. Academic explanations fall into three schools: primordialist, instrumentalist and constructivist. Primordialists argue that ethnic groups rest on kinship and primordial ties such as territorial location, a view associated with [[Clifford Geertz|Clifford Geertz]]. Other political scientists argue that institutional, political and economic factors cause conflicts, and that the concept of ethnic war is misleading. Primordial accounts struggle to explain why ethnic violence occurs in sporadic outbursts. Scholars debate whether ethnic conflict has become more prevalent since the end of the [[Cold War|Cold War]]. Management approaches include consociationalism and [[Federalism|federalism]]. Although [[Yugoslav Wars|Yugoslavia]] broke up in ethnic violence in the 1990s, it had enjoyed decades of peace before the Soviet Union collapsed.

=== KEY: Ethnicity_8919df
HEADWORD: Ethnicity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Citizenship | Herodotus | Jews | Nation | Oxford English Dictionary | Race (human categorization) | Septuagint
ENTRY:
Group of people who identify with one another on the basis of perceived shared attributes that distinguish them from other groups. The attributes may include language, culture, common ancestry, traditions, society, religion, history, region, or social treatment. Ethnic identities may rest on a narrow or broad spectrum of genetic ancestry, and some groups have mixed ancestry. Ethnicity is sometimes used interchangeably with [[Nation|nation]], and also with [[Race (human categorization)|race]], although not all ethnicities identify as racial groups.

Individuals or groups may shift from one ethnic group to another over time through assimilation, acculturation, amalgamation, language shift, intermarriage, adoption, and religious conversion. Ethnic groups may divide into subgroups or tribes, which can become separate ethnic groups in turn through endogamy or physical isolation. Formerly separate ethnicities may also merge into a panethnicity. The formation of a separate ethnic identity, whether through division or amalgamation, is called ethnogenesis.

The word *ethnic* derives from the Greek *ethnos*, through the adjective *ethnikos*, borrowed into Latin as *ethnicus*. In Early Modern English and until the mid-19th century it meant "heathen" or "pagan," because the [[Septuagint]] used *ta ethne*, "the nations," to translate the Hebrew *goyim*. The sense of "different cultural groups" arose in the 1930s and 1940s, replacing *race*, which was becoming deprecated because of its association with ideological racism. *Ethnic group* was first recorded in 1935 and entered the [[Oxford English Dictionary]] in 1972. *Nationality* may mean either ethnicity or [[Citizenship|citizenship]], depending on context.

Groups can be classified by the source of identity emphasized: ethno-linguistic (French Canadians), ethno-national (Austrians), ethno-racial (African Americans), ethno-regional (South Islanders of New Zealand), ethno-religious (Sikhs, [[Jews]]), ethno-civic (ancient Romans), and ethno-cultural (Travellers). The categories often overlap, and membership is frequently determined by more than one aspect. Armenian ethnicity, for example, can be defined by citizenship, heritage, native use of the Armenian language, or membership of the Armenian Apostolic Church.

[[Herodotus]] gave a famous account of Greek identity as shared descent, shared language, shared sanctuaries and sacrifices, and shared customs. The orator Isocrates, by contrast, wrote that the name Hellenes applied to those who shared Athenian culture rather than a common blood.

Two main theories exist in understanding ethnicities, primordialism and constructivism. Early 20th-century primordialists viewed ethnic groups as real phenomena whose distinct characteristics have endured since the distant past. Perspectives that developed after the 1960s increasingly viewed them as social constructs, with identity assigned by societal rules. Anthropologists such as Fredrik Barth and Eric Wolf do not consider ethnic identity universal, regarding it as a product of specific kinds of inter-group interactions. Other accounts emphasize that ethnic affiliations often correspond to patterns of genetic similarity formed through endogamy and relative isolation, and they dispute the view that ethnicity is purely a social invention.

=== KEY: Ethnocentrism_47289b
HEADWORD: Ethnocentrism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Culture | Discrimination | Racism | Social science | Theodor W. Adorno
ENTRY:
Application of one's own [[Culture|culture]] as the standard for judging other cultures, practices, beliefs and peoples, usually negatively. The term was first applied in the [[Social science|social sciences]] by American sociologist William G. Sumner, who in his 1906 book *Folkways* called it "the technical name for the view of things in which one's own group is the center of everything, and all others are scaled and rated with reference to it." Some people use the term for the belief that one's culture is superior to all others, especially in language, behavior, customs and religion. It is sometimes related to [[Racism|racism]], stereotyping, [[Discrimination|discrimination]] or xenophobia, though it does not necessarily involve a negative view of others' race. Its opposite is cultural relativism, which holds that a culture is best understood from its own perspective. In *The Authoritarian Personality*, [[Theodor W. Adorno]] and colleagues of the Frankfurt School defined it as combining a positive attitude toward one's in-group with a negative attitude toward the out-group.

=== KEY: Ethnography_db6dee
HEADWORD: Ethnography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Herodotus | NASA | Sociology
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Anthropology|anthropology]] devoted to the systematic study of individual cultures from the viewpoint of their members. Ethnographers rely heavily on participant observation, in which the researcher takes part in a setting, often in a marginal role, to document patterns of social interaction and participants' perspectives. Ethnographers mainly use qualitative methods, though they may include quantitative data, and a typical study is holistic, including a brief history and an analysis of the terrain, climate and habitat. The method originated in social and cultural anthropology in the early 20th century and spread notably to [[Sociology|sociology]]. Studied groups include traditional communities, youth gangs, religious cults and organizations. Some studies rely on interviews or documents, as in research on the [[NASA]] Challenger disaster, and online "virtual" settings are studied through netnography. *Histories* by [[Herodotus]] is the usual starting point for ancient ethnographic writing.

=== KEY: Ethnology_a02c2e
HEADWORD: Ethnology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Claude Lévi-Strauss | Ethnography | Human nature | Social anthropology
ENTRY:
Academic discipline that compares and analyzes the characteristics of different peoples and the relationships between them. Unlike [[Ethnography|ethnography]], which studies single groups through direct contact with their culture, ethnology compares and contrasts the research ethnographers have compiled. The term was used and defined by Adam Franz Kollár in a work published in Vienna in 1783. It has been considered an academic field since the late 18th century. Among its goals have been reconstructing human history, identifying cultural invariants such as the incest taboo, and formulating generalizations about [[Human nature|human nature]], a concept criticized since the 19th century. Cultural anthropology became dominant in the United States and [[Social anthropology|social anthropology]] in Great Britain, and the distinction between the three terms is increasingly blurred. The progress of the field, for example through [[Claude Lévi-Strauss]]'s structural anthropology, led to criticism of linear progress models.

=== KEY: Ethology_4ec655

ENTRY:
Branch of [[Zoology|zoology]] that studies the behavior of non-human animals. Its roots lie in the work of [[Charles Darwin]] and of American and German ornithologists of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Wilson's *Sociobiology: The New Synthesis* (1975) shifted the field toward social aspects of behavior.

=== KEY: Etruscan_civilization_254005

=== KEY: Euclid_4877d6
HEADWORD: Euclid
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alexandria | Apollonius of Perga | Archimedes | Euclid's Elements | Euclidean geometry | Plato | Socrates
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek mathematician (fl. 300 BC), active as a geometer and logician in [[Alexandria]]. Considered the "father of geometry," he is chiefly known for the [[Euclid's Elements|*Elements*]], a 13-book treatise that established the foundations of geometry that largely dominated the field until the early 19th century. It deduces theorems from a small set of axioms, and its system, now called [[Euclidean geometry]], synthesized theories of earlier Greek mathematicians, including Eudoxus of Cnidus, Hippocrates of Chios, Thales, and Theaetetus. With [[Archimedes]] and [[Apollonius of Perga]], Euclid is generally considered among the greatest mathematicians of antiquity.

Very little is known of his life, and most information comes from the scholars Proclus and Pappus of Alexandria, writing many centuries later. Proclus placed him during the rule of Ptolemy I (r. 305/304–282 BC), after the followers of [[Plato]] and before Archimedes. His birthplace is unknown. He is often presumed to have been educated by Plato's disciples at the Platonic Academy in Athens, though one historian calls this mere conjecture. Pappus mentions that Apollonius studied with Euclid's students in Alexandria, which suggests that Euclid worked and founded a mathematical tradition there.

He is called "Euclid of Alexandria" to distinguish him from Euclid of Megara, a pupil of [[Socrates]], with whom medieval Byzantine sources conflated him. Renaissance scholars later disproved the confusion on chronological grounds.

Besides the *Elements*, he is accepted as the author of three other mostly extant treatises: *Optics*, an early text in the field, *Data*, and *Phaenomena*. He also wrote on perspective, conic sections, spherical geometry, and number theory. Questions about the fifth postulate, concerning parallel lines, later led mathematicians to non-Euclidean geometries, as in Nikolai Lobachevsky's work in 1829.

=== KEY: Euclid_s_Elements_8e37b1
HEADWORD: Euclid's Elements
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arabic | Euclid | Euclid's theorem | Euclidean algorithm | Euclidean geometry | Pythagorean theorem
ENTRY:
Mathematical treatise by [[Euclid]] (c. 300 BC). The oldest extant large-scale deductive treatment of mathematics, *Elements* is a collection in 13 books of definitions, postulates, constructions and theorems with proofs. It covers plane and solid [[Euclidean geometry|Euclidean geometry]], elementary number theory and incommensurability, including the [[Pythagorean theorem|Pythagorean theorem]], the [[Euclidean algorithm|Euclidean algorithm]] and the proof that infinitely many primes exist, [[Euclid's theorem]]. Drawing on earlier mathematicians such as Hippocrates of Chios, Eudoxus of Cnidus and Theaetetus, it was long used as an introductory geometry textbook and is often called the most successful textbook ever written. Translated into [[Arabic]] and Latin in the medieval period, it strongly influenced mathematics in the medieval Islamic world and Western Europe, and its logical rigor was not surpassed until the 19th century. First printed in 1482, it has had more than 2,000 editions and translations.

=== KEY: Euclid_s_theorem_75c2d5
HEADWORD: Euclid's theorem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclid | Fundamental theorem of arithmetic | Geometric series | Leonhard Euler | Number theory | Prime number
ENTRY:
Fundamental result of [[Number theory|number theory]] that there are infinitely many [[Prime number|prime numbers]]. First proven by [[Euclid]] in *Elements* (Book IX, Proposition 20), it has at least 200 proofs. Euclid's argument shows that the product of any finite list of primes plus one has a prime factor not on the list. Though often described as a proof by contradiction, it is a direct proof by cases. [[Leonhard Euler]]'s proof uses the [[Fundamental theorem of arithmetic|fundamental theorem of arithmetic]] and expands a product over primes as a [[Geometric series|geometric series]], which shows that there are more primes than squares.

=== KEY: Euclidean_algorithm_bea54b
HEADWORD: Euclidean algorithm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Computational complexity theory | Diophantine equation | Euclid | Integer | Internet | Modular arithmetic
ENTRY:
Efficient [[Algorithm|algorithm]] for computing the greatest common divisor of two [[Integer|integers]]. Named after the ancient Greek mathematician [[Euclid]], who described it in his *Elements* (c. 300 BC), it is one of the oldest algorithms in common use. It rests on the principle that the greatest common divisor does not change if the larger number is replaced by its remainder when divided by the smaller. Repeating this produces smaller pairs until the remainder is zero. The number of steps never exceeds five times the number of base-10 digits of the smaller integer, a result proven by Gabriel Lamé in 1844 that marks the beginning of [[Computational complexity theory|computational complexity theory]]. The algorithm reduces fractions, performs division in [[Modular arithmetic|modular arithmetic]], helps solve [[Diophantine equation|Diophantine equations]] and forms part of the cryptographic protocols that secure the [[Internet]].

=== KEY: Euclidean_geometry_d30a3a
HEADWORD: Euclidean geometry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Analytic geometry | Euclid | Non-Euclidean geometry | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Mathematical system attributed to [[Euclid]], deriving theorems from a small set of axioms. Set out in Euclid's *Elements*, it begins with plane geometry, still taught in secondary school as the first axiomatic system, and goes on to solid geometry of three dimensions. Euclid's five postulates, including the parallel postulate, seemed so intuitively obvious that their theorems were considered absolutely true for more than two thousand years. Two self-consistent [[Non-Euclidean geometry|non-Euclidean geometries]], hyperbolic and elliptic, are now known. An implication of [[Albert Einstein]]'s general relativity is that physical space itself is not Euclidean, although Euclidean space approximates it over short distances and weak gravity. Euclidean geometry is synthetic, proceeding logically from axioms, in contrast to [[Analytic geometry|analytic geometry]], introduced almost 2,000 years later by [[René Descartes]], which uses coordinates.

=== KEY: Euclidean_plane_bd6c0a
HEADWORD: Euclidean plane
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cartesian coordinate system | Euclidean space | Pierre de Fermat | Pythagorean theorem
ENTRY:
Two-dimensional [[Euclidean space|Euclidean space]], with positions given by two real numbers. It is an affine space in which parallel lines exist, with metrical properties such as distance, circles and angle measurement. A Euclidean plane with a chosen [[Cartesian coordinate system|Cartesian coordinate system]] is called a Cartesian plane. The real coordinate plane ℝ², with the dot product, is often called the standard Euclidean plane, since every Euclidean plane is isomorphic to it. Books I through IV and VI of Euclid's *Elements* dealt with two-dimensional geometry, including the [[Pythagorean theorem|Pythagorean theorem]]. René Descartes and [[Pierre de Fermat|Pierre de Fermat]] developed coordinates in 1637, but the pair of fixed axes was introduced after Descartes's *La Géométrie* was translated into Latin in 1649. Later the plane was regarded as a field, known as the complex plane and sometimes called the Argand plane after Jean-Robert Argand.

=== KEY: Euclidean_space_79c3a9

Fundamental space of [[Geometry|geometry]], intended to represent physical space. === KEY: Eugenics_574aca

ENTRY:
Largely discredited set of beliefs and practices that aim to improve the genetic quality of a human population. Historically, eugenicists sought to alter the frequency of human traits by inhibiting the fertility of those deemed inferior, often through [[Sterilization (medicine)|forced sterilization]], or by promoting that of those deemed superior. Phylum of mostly [[Flagellate|flagellate]] protists in the supergroup Discoba. Most species are unicellular and around 15–40 μm long, though some euglenids reach 500 μm. The phylum includes free-living species as well as parasites, some of which infect humans. Its four major groups are Kinetoplastea, Diplonemea, Euglenida and Symbiontida. Most species have two flagella. Many euglenids possess [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] and obtain energy through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Reproduction occurs exclusively through cell division, during which the nuclear membrane stays intact during [[Mitosis|mitosis]]. The group is generally accepted as monophyletic and is probably part of the [[Excavata]], a grouping that has been challenged.

=== KEY: Eukaryote_54ad4f
HEADWORD: Eukaryote
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cell nucleus | Chloroplast | Cytoskeleton | Mitochondria | Prokaryote | Protist | Symbiogenesis
ENTRY:
Organism of the domain Eukaryota (or Eukarya), whose cells have a distinct [[Cell nucleus|nucleus]] enclosed by a nuclear membrane, membrane-bound organelles, and a [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeleton]]. Extant eukaryotes are traditionally grouped into four kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, and [[Protist|protists]]. Protists are a basal paraphyletic group that includes all algae, amoebae, flagellates, and ciliates. In the prevailing three-domain system, eukaryotes stand alongside the two domains of [[Prokaryote|prokaryotes]], Bacteria and Archaea, though cladistically they emerged within the archaeal phylum Promethearchaeota.

Eukaryotes first emerged during the Paleoproterozoic. The leading theory holds that they resulted from a [[Symbiogenesis|symbiogenesis]] between an anaerobic archaeon and an aerobic alphaproteobacterium that became the proto-[[Mitochondria|mitochondrion]]. A second symbiogenetic event during the Mesoproterozoic, between a protist host and a cyanobacterial endosymbiont, produced the [[Chloroplast|chloroplast]] and the photosynthetic clade Archaeplastida, which includes green algae, red algae, glaucophytes, and land plants.

Eukaryotic cells are typically much larger than prokaryotic ones. Their organelles include the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, ribosomes, vacuoles, lysosomes, mitochondria, and plastids. Genetic information is divided among multiple linear DNA molecules called chromosomes, held inside the nucleus. Eukaryotes reproduce asexually through mitosis and sexually through meiosis and gamete fusion.

They range from picozoans under 3 micrometers across to the blue whale, weighing up to 190 tonnes, and the coast redwood, up to 120 m tall. Multicellularity has evolved independently at least 25 times within the group. Eukaryotes are a small minority of organisms by number, but their collective biomass of 468 gigatons far exceeds that of prokaryotes at 77 gigatons.

=== KEY: Euler_characteristic_c1af56
HEADWORD: Euler characteristic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Homological algebra | Homology (mathematics) | Leonhard Euler | Platonic solid | Sphere | Topological space | Torus
ENTRY:
Topological invariant, a number describing the shape or structure of a [[Topological space|topological space]] regardless of how it is bent. Denoted χ, it was originally defined for polyhedra as V − E + F, where V, E and F are the numbers of vertices, edges and faces. [[Leonhard Euler]] introduced it for convex polyhedra but did not rigorously prove that it is an invariant. The surface of any convex polyhedron has χ = 2, as does the [[Sphere|sphere]]; toroidal polyhedra have χ = 0, like the [[Torus|torus]]. The concept was used to prove theorems about the [[Platonic solid|Platonic solids]]. In modern mathematics it arises from [[Homology (mathematics)|homology]] and [[Homological algebra|homological algebra]].

=== KEY: Euler_s_formula_01a543
HEADWORD: Euler's formula
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Complex analysis | Euler's identity | Imaginary unit | Leonhard Euler | Richard Feynman
ENTRY:
Mathematical formula in [[Complex analysis|complex analysis]] linking the trigonometric functions with the complex exponential function. For any real number x, eⁱˣ = cos x + i sin x, where e is the base of the natural logarithm and i is the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]]. [[Leonhard Euler]] presented it in his 1748 treatise *Introductio in analysin infinitorum*. The formula remains valid when x is complex, and it is used throughout mathematics, physics, chemistry and engineering. Physicist [[Richard Feynman]] called it "our jewel" and "the most remarkable formula in mathematics". Setting x = π yields [[Euler's identity]].

=== KEY: Euler_s_identity_6e5a6e
HEADWORD: Euler's identity
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Mathematical equation, eⁱπ + 1 = 0, linking five fundamental constants. It is a special case of [[Euler's formula]] evaluated at x = π. The constants are 0, 1, [[Pi|π]], [[E (mathematical constant)|e]] and the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]] i. The equation also contains addition, multiplication and [[Exponentiation|exponentiation]] exactly once each. It is named after [[Leonhard Euler]] and is often cited as an example of mathematical beauty. A 1990 poll of readers by *The Mathematical Intelligencer* named it the "most beautiful theorem in mathematics". Mathematics writer Constance Reid called it "the most famous formula in all mathematics".

=== KEY: Eunuch_50d81a
HEADWORD: Eunuch
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Byzantine Empire | Castration | Ottoman Empire | Sumer
ENTRY:
[[Castration|Castrated]] boy or man. The earliest records of intentional castration to produce eunuchs come from the [[Sumer|Sumerian]] city of Lagash in the 2nd millennium BC. Eunuchs were usually servants or slaves, castrated to make them less threatening at a royal court, where physical proximity to the ruler could bring great influence. They supposedly had no loyalties to the military, the aristocracy or a family of their own, and were therefore seen as more trustworthy. Eunuchs performed many functions, including courtier, espionage, guardian of harem servants, official and soldier. They often held significant power in the [[Byzantine Empire]], Imperial China, the [[Ottoman Empire]] and other cultures in Asia and Africa. Because their status was low, they could be replaced or killed without repercussion.

=== KEY: Euphemism_3609ea
HEADWORD: Euphemism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Death | Disability | Pain | Profanity | Torture
ENTRY:
Mild or indirect word or expression substituted for one considered offensive or unpleasant. Euphemisms soften references to taboo or sensitive topics such as [[Death|death]], [[Disability|disability]], bodily functions, [[Pain|pain]] and violence, and are often used to soften [[Profanity|profanity]]. The term dates from the mid-17th century and derives from the Greek *euphēmismos*, meaning "use of words of good omen." Common strategies include understatement, metaphor and circumlocution, as in "passed away" for death. Linguist Steven Pinker described a "euphemism treadmill," in which neutral substitutes acquire negative connotations and must be replaced. Critics argue that in political and military contexts, euphemisms such as "enhanced interrogation" for [[Torture|torture]] or "collateral damage" for civilian casualties obscure causal realities and moral implications.
