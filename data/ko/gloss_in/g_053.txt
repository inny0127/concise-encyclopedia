=== KEY: Glacial_landform_7777f8
HEADWORD: Glacial landform
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andes | Cirque | Fjord | Glacier | Ice sheet | Sahara
ENTRY:
Landform created by the action of [[Glacier|glaciers]]. Most of today's glacial landforms were created by the movement of large [[Ice sheet|ice sheets]] during the Quaternary glaciations, and some areas, such as Fennoscandia and the southern [[Andes]], have extensive examples. Erosional landforms include [[Cirque|cirques]], arêtes, glacial horns and U-shaped valleys, which become [[Fjord|fjords]] when filled with ocean water. Striations, grooves cut into rock by sediment carried beneath the ice, show the direction in which the glacier was moving. Depositional landforms, left when retreating glaciers drop their glacial drift, include moraines, eskers and kames, and many are reshaped by meltwater into fluvioglacial landforms. Rare, very old fossil glacial landforms occur in the [[Sahara]].

=== KEY: Glacier_4def2a
HEADWORD: Glacier
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Cirque | Climate change | Fjord | Moraine
ENTRY:
Persistent body of natural ice, a form of rock, constantly moving under its own weight. A glacier forms where the accumulation of snow exceeds its ablation over many years, often centuries. It flows and deforms under the stresses of gravity and, as it moves, abrades the underlying rock to create landforms such as [[Cirque|cirques]], [[Moraine|moraines]] and [[Fjord|fjords]]. Distinct from the much thinner sea ice and lake ice, a glacier originates on land. About 99% of glacial ice lies in polar ice sheets, but glaciers occur in mountain ranges on every continent other than the Australian mainland. Glaciers cover about 10% of Earth's land surface, and [[Antarctica]]'s continental glaciers cover nearly 13 million km² (5 million sq mi). Glacial ice holds, together with ice sheets, about 69% of the world's freshwater, and glacial mass changes are considered among the most sensitive indicators of [[Climate change|climate change]].

=== KEY: Glass_aae441
HEADWORD: Glass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amorphous solid | Calcium oxide | Lens | Mesopotamia | Obsidian | Roman Empire | Sodium carbonate | Telecommunications
ENTRY:
[[Amorphous solid|Amorphous]] (non-crystalline) solid, often transparent and chemically inert, used for window panes, tableware, and optics. It is most often formed by rapid cooling (quenching) of the molten form. Some glasses, such as volcanic glass, occur naturally, and [[Obsidian|obsidian]] has been used to make arrowheads and knives since the Stone Age.

Archaeological evidence suggests glassmaking dates back to at least 3600 BC in [[Mesopotamia]], Egypt, or Syria. The earliest known glass objects were beads, perhaps created accidentally during metalworking or the production of faience. Glassblowing, invented around the first century BC in the [[Roman Empire]], allowed faster production of intricate shapes.

Soda–lime glass, made from silica (sand), soda ash ([[Sodium carbonate|sodium carbonate]]), and lime ([[Calcium oxide|calcium oxide]]), contains around 70% silica and accounts for around 90% of modern manufactured glass. Rather than melting at a distinct temperature like crystalline materials, glass undergoes a glass transition from a rigid solid to a viscous liquid on heating. Flat glass is commonly made by the float process, in which molten glass is poured onto a bed of molten tin. Glass has traditionally been used for vessels such as bowls, bottles, and jars, and can be colored by adding metal salts, which led to its use in stained glass windows. Boron gives borosilicate glass, used in laboratory equipment, and chemical tempering strengthens smartphone screens.

The refractive, reflective, and transmissive properties of glass suit it to optical [[Lens|lenses]], prisms, and optoelectronics. Extruded glass fibers serve as optical fibers in [[Telecommunications|telecommunications]] networks, as thermal insulation when matted as glass wool, and in glass-fiber reinforced plastic (fiberglass).

=== KEY: Glider_aircraft_1e8b98
HEADWORD: Glider
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: World War II | Wright brothers
ENTRY:
Fixed-wing aircraft supported in flight by the dynamic reaction of air against its lifting surfaces, without reliance on an engine. Most gliders have no engine, although motor gliders carry small engines to extend their flight. Most exploit meteorological phenomena such as rising air to gain height, and gliders are principally used for gliding, hang gliding and paragliding. Sir George Cayley's gliders achieved brief wing-borne hops from around 1849. Otto Lilienthal made repeated successful flights, eventually over 2,000, and was the first to use rising air to prolong them. The [[Wright brothers]] built three manned gliders before their powered flight. Sport gliding became the main application of gliders, and modern gliders regularly fly hundreds or even over a thousand kilometers in a day when the weather is suitable. Military gliders were developed during [[World War II]] for landing troops.

Satellite-based [[Navigation|navigation]] system owned by the [[United States]] Space Force and operated by Mission Delta 31. It provides geolocation and time information to receivers anywhere on or near Earth where signal quality permits, and it does not require the user to transmit any data. The Department of Defense began the project in 1973, and the full constellation of 24 [[Satellite|satellites]] became operational in 1993. === KEY: Globalization_f507cd
HEADWORD: Globalization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Cold War | Industrial Revolution | International trade | World Trade Organization
ENTRY:
Process of increasing interdependence and integration among the economies, markets, societies, and cultures of different countries worldwide. It is attributed to the reduction of barriers to [[International trade|international trade]], the liberalization of capital movements, the development of transportation infrastructure, and advances in information and communication technologies. The term first appeared in the early 20th century but came into popular use in the 1990s to describe the growing connectivity of the post–[[Cold War]] world.

Large-scale globalization began in the 1820s, and its origins are traced to the 18th and 19th centuries, when transportation and communication advances following the [[Industrial Revolution]] increased global interactions and trade. After World War II, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and its successor, the [[World Trade Organization]], cut average tariffs from over 40% in the 1940s to below 5% by the 2000s. Between 1990 and 2010 globalization progressed rapidly, driven by the information and communication technology revolution, trade liberalization, and the shift of manufacturing to emerging economies, particularly [[China]]. Academic literature commonly divides it into economic, cultural, and political globalization.

Proponents point to economic growth and broader societal development, crediting export expansion and foreign direct investment with lifting hundreds of millions of people, particularly in Asia, out of extreme poverty between 1990 and 2010. Opponents claim that globalization harms social well-being through ethnocentrism, environmental consequences, and other drawbacks. Critics also cite widening income inequality within advanced economies, where offshoring and import competition have depressed wages for low-skilled workers, and job displacement in manufacturing. Others hold that aggregate evidence shows net welfare gains from cheaper goods and the spread of innovation.

[[Chemical element]] (symbol Au, atomic number 79), a bright yellow, dense, soft, malleable, and ductile metal. Its symbol comes from the [[Latin]] *aurum*. Gold is a transition metal and one of the noble metals, and it is one of the least reactive chemical elements. It has a density of 19.3 g/cm³, conducts heat and electricity well, and is the most malleable metal: a single gram can be beaten into a sheet of 1 square meter. Its only stable and only naturally occurring isotope is ¹⁹⁷Au.

Gold often occurs as the free element in nuggets, grains, veins, and alluvial deposits, or alloyed with silver as [[Electrum|electrum]]. It resists most acids but dissolves in [[Aqua regia|aqua regia]] and in alkaline cyanide solutions used in mining and electroplating. Adding copper gives rose gold, and adding palladium or nickel gives white gold.

Gold has been used for coinage, jewelry, and works of art throughout recorded history. Most gold coins ceased to be minted as circulating currency in the 1930s, and the world gold standard was abandoned after the Nixon shock measures of 1971. In 2023 the largest producer was [[China]], followed by Russia and Australia. As of 2020, around 201,296 tonnes existed above ground. === KEY: Goldbach_s_conjecture_84b7bc
HEADWORD: Goldbach's conjecture
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Unproven claim in [[Number theory|number theory]] that every even [[Natural number|natural number]] greater than 2 is the sum of two [[Prime number|prime numbers]]. It is among the oldest unsolved problems in [[Mathematics|mathematics]]. Christian Goldbach raised it in a 1742 letter to [[Leonhard Euler]], who called the two-prime form a completely certain theorem he could not prove. === KEY: Golden_ratio_56a687
HEADWORD: Golden ratio
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Irrational number, approximately 1.618, denoted φ, that is the positive solution of x² − x − 1 = 0. Two quantities are in the golden ratio if their ratio equals the ratio of their sum to the larger quantity. Attempts to apply it to financial markets are sometimes based on dubious fits to data.

=== KEY: Good_and_evil_7421d2
HEADWORD: Good and evil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ahura Mazda | Augustine of Hippo | Confucianism | Democritus | Egypt | Ethics | Plato | Zoroaster
ENTRY:
Philosophical, religious, and psychological dichotomy contrasting what is morally right or desirable with profound immorality. In religions with Manichaean and Abrahamic influence, evil is perceived as the dualistic antagonistic opposite of good, which should prevail while evil is defeated. Evil is also described as a supernatural force. Definitions of evil and analyses of its motives vary, but it is commonly associated with behavior involving expediency, selfishness, ignorance, or negligence. The principal study of good and evil is [[Ethics|ethics]], whose three major branches are normative ethics, applied ethics, and metaethics.

A sense of moral judgment is a cultural universal. [[Zoroaster]] simplified the pantheon of early Iranian gods into two opposing forces, [[Ahura Mazda]] and Angra Mainyu. Ancient [[Egypt]] had Ma'at, the principle of justice and order, and Isfet, the principle of chaos. In Greek thought the absolute moral sense of "good" and "bad" emerged around 400 BC with pre-Socratic philosophy, in particular [[Democritus]], and solidified in the dialogues of [[Plato]]. [[Augustine of Hippo]] classically defined sin as "a word, deed, or desire in opposition to the eternal law of God."

Today good is often associated with life, charity, happiness, love, or justice, and evil with conscious and deliberate wrongdoing, destructiveness, and indiscriminate violence. Explanations of evil differ: some attribute it to the imperfect human condition or to free will, others to ignorance of truth or to tyrannical social structures. Philosophers have also understood evil as a privation of due good. In [[Confucianism]] and Taoism there is no direct analogue to the opposition; Confucianism is concerned with correct social relationships, so evil corresponds to wrong behavior.

=== KEY: Goods_9b2cfa
HEADWORD: Goods
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economics | Information | Service (economics)
ENTRY:
In [[Economics|economics]], things that provide welfare or utility to someone, as opposed to bads, which provide negative value, such as chores or waste products. Economics focuses on economic goods, which are scarce, so that producing them requires effort or resources; free goods such as air have unlimited supply. Goods result from the secondary sector of the economy, which transforms raw materials or intermediate goods into goods.

The change in utility gained by consuming one unit of a good is its marginal utility, which is commonly considered to diminish as more is consumed. Final goods, or consumer goods, are ultimately consumed, as with a microwave oven or a bicycle sold to a consumer. Intermediate goods, such as textiles or transistors, are used to make other goods. Durable goods used to produce further goods are capital goods. Most goods are tangible, like an apple, but some, such as [[Information|information]] and news, are intangible; unlike a [[Service (economics)|service]], an intangible good can be transferred and traded.

Goods are also classified by price elasticity. For an elastic good, a small change in price produces a large change in quantity demanded, as when consumers buy more pencils as pen prices rise. An inelastic good has few or no substitutes, such as insulin. A bad is the opposite of a good: its owner will pay to be rid of it, as when a household pays a waste collector to take away garbage. Goods are further classified by excludability and rivalry into public goods, private goods, common resources, and club goods.

Web mapping platform and consumer application developed by [[Google]]. It offers satellite imagery, street maps, real-time traffic and route planning on foot, by car, by bike and by [[Public transport|public transportation]]. Google acquired the C++ desktop program, developed in [[Australia]], in October 2004, and launched it as a web application in February 2005. By 2013 its mobile app was the world's most popular [[Smartphone|smartphone]] app, used by over 54% of global smartphone owners. In 2018 the desktop version moved from a variant of the [[Mercator projection]] to a 3D globe. As of 2020 it was used by over one billion people every month.
