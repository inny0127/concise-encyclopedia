=== KEY: Lumbar_puncture_d30228
HEADWORD: Lumbar puncture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemotherapy | Meningitis
ENTRY:
Medical procedure in which a needle is inserted into the spinal canal, usually to collect cerebrospinal fluid for testing. Also called a spinal tap, it is typically performed under local anesthesia and yields fluid for biochemical, microbiological, and cytological analysis. Its chief diagnostic use is in suspected [[Meningitis|meningitis]], since no other reliable test can exclude it; it can also help diagnose subarachnoid hemorrhage and detect malignant cells in the cerebrospinal fluid. Post-dural-puncture headache is a common side effect when a small atraumatic needle is not used. The procedure was first introduced in 1891 by the German physician Heinrich Quincke.

SI unit of luminous flux, the perceived power of visible light emitted by a source. === KEY: Luminosity_46aa9f
HEADWORD: Luminosity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Galaxy | Neutrino | Star | Sun
ENTRY:
Radiated electromagnetic energy emitted per unit time, synonymous with radiant power. In astronomy, luminosity is the total energy a [[Star|star]], [[Galaxy|galaxy]], or other object emits per unit time, measured in watts. It is intrinsic to the object, unlike apparent brightness, which depends on distance. The luminosity of the [[Sun]], L☉, is often used as a unit, and the IAU defined a nominal solar luminosity of 3.828 × 10²⁶ W. A star's luminosity can be determined from its size and effective temperature, but for most stars neither can be measured directly. Stars also radiate [[Neutrino|neutrinos]], which carry off about 2% of the Sun's energy.

=== KEY: Lunar_eclipse_c02377
HEADWORD: Lunar eclipse
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmosphere | Rayleigh scattering | Solar eclipse
ENTRY:
Astronomical event in which the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. Lunar eclipses occur during eclipse season, when the Moon's orbit lies approximately in line with Earth and the Sun. Also called blood moons, they are visible from anywhere on the night side of Earth, unlike [[Solar eclipse|solar eclipses]], and often last an hour or longer. They are safe to watch without eye protection. When the Moon passes through the umbra, it appears orange or red, because sunlight reaching it passes through Earth's [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]], where [[Rayleigh scattering]] removes short-wavelength violet and blue light. Penumbral eclipses, in which the Moon lies only in the penumbra, cause a subtle dimming and make up approximately one-third of all lunar eclipses.

=== KEY: Lung_cancer_76371e
HEADWORD: Lung cancer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asbestos | Chemotherapy | Lung | Metastasis | Radiation therapy | Radon | Surgery | Tobacco smoking
ENTRY:
Malignant tumor that originates in the tissues of the [[Lung|lungs]]. Most cases are caused by [[Tobacco smoking|tobacco smoking]], while the rest stem from exposure to hazardous substances such as [[Asbestos|asbestos]] and [[Radon|radon]] gas, or from genetic mutations that arise by chance. Without treatment, tumors spread through the lung and eventually [[Metastasis|metastasize]]. === KEY: Lung_efb2b0
HEADWORD: Lung
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease | Fetus | Lung cancer | Oxygen | Pneumonia | Smoking | Tetrapod
ENTRY:
Primary respiratory organ of many animals, including humans. In mammals and most other [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]], two lungs lie near the backbone on either side of the heart. Their function is gas exchange: they extract [[Oxygen|oxygen]] from the atmosphere into the bloodstream and release [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] from the blood. In early tetrapods, pharyngeal muscles drove air into the lungs by buccal pumping, a mechanism still seen in amphibians; in humans the primary breathing muscle is the diaphragm. The lungs also provide the airflow that makes vocalization, including speech, possible.

Human lungs sit in the thoracic cavity. The right lung is bigger and heavier than the left, which shares space with the heart and has an indentation, the cardiac notch, to accommodate it. The right has three lobes and the left two. Together the lungs weigh approximately 1.3 kg and contain approximately 2,400 km of airways and 300 to 500 million alveoli. Air passes from the trachea into bronchi and bronchioles, which divide until it reaches the microscopic alveoli, where gas exchange takes place. Each lung is enclosed in a pleural sac of two pleurae that slide over each other with little friction during breathing. The blood supply is unusual: deoxygenated blood arrives from the heart through the pulmonary circulation, while a separate bronchial circulation supplies oxygenated blood.

Lung tissue can be affected by diseases such as [[Pneumonia|pneumonia]] and [[Lung cancer]]. Chronic conditions such as [[Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]] and emphysema can be related to [[Smoking|smoking]] or exposure to harmful substances.

In embryonic development the lungs begin as an outpouching of the foregut. The [[Fetus|fetus]] does not breathe, and blood is diverted from the lungs through the ductus arteriosus. At birth air begins to pass through the lungs and the duct closes.

=== KEY: Lungfish_9404f6

Class of freshwater [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] in the [[Sarcopterygii|lobe-finned fish]] group, also called dipnoans. Legendary lawgiver of [[Sparta|Sparta]], credited with political, economic, and social reforms that produced a military-oriented society. === KEY: Lymphatic_system_34f25e
HEADWORD: Lymphatic system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bacteria | Circulatory system | Immune system | Spleen
ENTRY:
Network of vessels, nodes, and lymphoid organs, complementary to the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]] and a vital part of the [[Immune system|immune system]]. Lymph originates as interstitial fluid that leaks from blood into tissues, carrying nutrients to cells and collecting waste, [[Bacteria|bacteria]], and damaged cells before draining into lymphatic vessels. The circulatory system processes about 20 liters of blood daily, and about 3 liters of fluid remain in the tissues, which the lymphatic system returns to the blood. Lymph vessels pass through lymph nodes, which filter out unwanted material and hold the highest concentration of lymphocytes, along with the [[Spleen|spleen]], thymus, and tonsils. Lymph moves by muscle contractions, and its ducts empty into the subclavian veins. The system was first described in the 17th century by Olaus Rudbeck and Thomas Bartholin.

=== KEY: Lysosome_3d53c4

ENTRY:
Membrane-bound organelle found in animal cells, though not in [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]], and rarely in [[Plant cell|plant cells]]. They serve as the cell's degradation center, breaking down proteins, polysaccharides and lipids into amino acids, monosaccharides and free fatty acids. === KEY: Machine_47c954
HEADWORD: Machine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Galileo Galilei | Hero of Alexandria | Industrial Revolution | James Watt | Leonardo da Vinci | Mesopotamia | Simple machine
ENTRY:
Thermodynamic system that uses power to apply forces and control movement to perform an action. The term is commonly applied to artificial devices, such as those employing engines or motors, but also to natural biological macromolecules, called molecular machines. Machines can be driven by animal and human power, by natural forces such as wind and water, and by chemical, thermal, or electrical power, and include a system of mechanisms that shape the actuator input to achieve a specific output of force and movement. They can also include computers and sensors that monitor performance and plan movement. Modern machines are complex systems of structural elements, mechanisms, and control components with interfaces for convenient use; examples include trains, automobiles, boats, airplanes, home and office appliances, farm machinery, machine tools, factory automation systems, and robots.

The English word comes through Middle French from Latin *machina*, itself from Greek *mekhane* ("contrivance, machine, engine"). The modern meaning developed from its use for stage engines in theater and for military siege engines in the late 16th and early 17th centuries.

The elementary devices from which machines are built are the six classic [[Simple machine|simple machines]]: wedge, inclined plane, wheel and axle, lever, pulley, and screw. Renaissance natural philosophers identified them and calculated the ratio of output force to input force, known as mechanical advantage. The wedge, exemplified by the flint hand axe, is the oldest, followed by the inclined plane, used since prehistoric times to move heavy objects. The other four were invented in the ancient Near East: the wheel and axle in [[Mesopotamia]] in the 5th millennium BC, the lever about 5,000 years ago, the pulley in the early 2nd millennium BC, and the screw, the last, during the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BC).

[[Archimedes]] studied the lever, pulley, and screw around the 3rd century BC and discovered the principle of mechanical advantage in the lever. [[Hero of Alexandria]] (c. 10–75 AD) described five mechanisms that can "set a load in motion," though Greek understanding was limited to statics and did not include dynamics or the concept of work. Windmills and wind pumps appeared in the Muslim world by the 9th century AD, and Al-Jazari described programmable automata in 1206. In 1586 Simon Stevin derived the mechanical advantage of the inclined plane, and [[Galileo Galilei]] worked out the complete dynamic theory of simple machines in 1600, recognizing that they transform energy rather than create it. The classic rules of sliding friction were discovered by [[Leonardo da Vinci]], rediscovered by Guillaume Amontons (1699), and developed by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb (1785).

[[James Watt]] patented his parallel motion linkage in 1782, making the double-acting steam engine practical. Steam engines powered locomotives, steamships, and factories during the [[Industrial Revolution]] (1750–1850), which began with the mechanization of the textile industry in Great Britain.

=== KEY: Macroeconomics_f25263
HEADWORD: Macroeconomics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economic growth | Economics | Gross domestic product | Inflation | John Maynard Keynes | Microeconomics | Unemployment | World War II
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Economics|economics]] concerned with the performance, structure, behavior, and decision-making of an economy as a whole, whether regional, national, or global. Macroeconomists study aggregate measures such as output or [[Gross domestic product|gross domestic product]] (GDP), national income, [[Unemployment|unemployment]], [[Inflation|inflation]], consumption, saving, investment, and trade; the three central variables are output, unemployment, and inflation.

Macroeconomics and [[Microeconomics|microeconomics]] are the two most general fields of economics. Microeconomists study markets and decision-making at a smaller level, such as firms or consumers, and the divide is institutionalized because of differences in methods and outcomes of interest. Macroeconomics is further divided by time frame. The short run (a few years) concerns business-cycle fluctuations and changes in aggregate demand, which stabilization policies such as monetary and fiscal policy aim to address. In the medium run (about a decade), output tends toward a level set by supply factors such as capital stock, technology, and labor force, and unemployment reverts to its structural, or "natural," level. The long run (a couple of decades or more) emphasizes the determinants of [[Economic growth|economic growth]].

As a separate field, macroeconomics is generally recognized to have begun in 1936, when [[John Maynard Keynes]] published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, though its intellectual predecessors are older. Swedish economist Knut Wicksell's *Interest and Prices* (1898, translated into English in 1936) is considered pioneering, while Keynes, who introduced national income accounting and related concepts, is called the founding father of the formal discipline. Since [[World War II]], Keynesian, monetarist, new classical, and new Keynesian economists have contributed to mainstream research. Critics argue that reliance on aggregate assumptions can overlook microeconomic foundations and that empirical tests reveal limits in predictive accuracy.

Indian actress and television personality. Born in [[Mumbai]], she made her acting debut in 1984 in *Abodh*. Her breakthrough came with the action drama *Tezaab* (1988), and she became established in top-grossing romantic dramas including *Dil* (1990), *Beta* (1992), *Hum Aapke Hain Koun..!* (1994) and *Dil To Pagal Hai* (1997). She won four Filmfare Awards for Best Actress for these films. In 2008 the Government of India awarded her the Padma Shri, the fourth-highest civilian honor in the country. She has worked with [[UNICEF]] since 2014 to advocate for children's rights and to prevent child labor, and she has appeared in over 70 Hindi films.

Indian philosopher and theologian, chief proponent of the [[Vedanta|Dvaita school of Vedanta]]. Born near Udupi on the west coast of [[Karnataka]], he became a monk as a teenager. He wrote commentaries on the principal [[Upanishads]], the [[Bhagavad Gita]] and the Brahma Sutras, and is credited with 37 works in Sanskrit. He taught that the individual soul and [[Brahman]], identified with God Vishnu, are two different, unchanging realities, and that liberation is achievable only through the grace of God. He criticized the non-dualist teachings of [[Adi Shankara]] and the qualified non-dualism of [[Ramanuja]]. He established the Krishna Mutt at Udupi. His school influenced [[Vaishnavism]] and the Bhakti movement in medieval India.
