=== KEY: Polar_bear_a6a61f
HEADWORD: Polar bear
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arctic | Bear | Brown bear | Climate change | Sea ice
ENTRY:
Large [[Bear|bear]] species, *Ursus maritimus*, native to the [[Arctic]] and nearby areas, closely related to the [[Brown bear]]. Adult males weigh 300–800 kg, making it the largest living bear and land carnivore. Its white or yellowish fur covers black skin and a thick layer of fat, and its large paws let it walk on ice and paddle in water. It feeds mainly on seals, especially ringed seals, typically by ambush, and favors their energy-rich blubber. The International Union for Conservation of Nature classifies the species as vulnerable, with an estimated 22,000 to 31,000 individuals. Its biggest threats include [[Climate change]], pollution and energy development, since climate change reduces [[Sea ice]], limiting access to prey and increasing malnutrition and conflicts with humans.

=== KEY: Polar_coordinate_system_988395
HEADWORD: Polar coordinate system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Cartesian coordinate system | Hipparchus | Isaac Newton | Jacob Bernoulli | Point (geometry) | Spherical coordinate system
ENTRY:
Two-dimensional coordinate system that locates a [[Point (geometry)|point]] in a plane by a distance and an angle. The distance from a reference point called the pole is the radial coordinate, and the angle, measured from a ray called the polar axis, is the angular coordinate. The pole is analogous to the origin in a [[Cartesian coordinate system|Cartesian system]]. Polar coordinates suit phenomena tied to direction and distance from a central point, such as spirals, orbits and circular motion. Extended to three dimensions, the system yields the cylindrical and [[Spherical coordinate system|spherical coordinate systems]]. References indicate that [[Hipparchus]] used polar coordinates to establish stellar positions, and [[Archimedes]] described a spiral whose radius depends on the angle. [[Jacob Bernoulli]] used a pole and polar axis in 1691, and [[Isaac Newton]] is credited with originating the system in its analytic form.

=== KEY: Polar_night_c8931a
HEADWORD: Polar night
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctic Circle | Arctic Circle | Midnight sun | Sirius | Venus
ENTRY:
Phenomenon in which the Sun stays below the horizon for more than 24 hours, occurring only inside the polar circles. Its opposite is the [[Midnight sun]]. Polar night lasts 24 hours just inside the polar circles and up to 179 days at the poles. Atmospheric refraction makes polar day slightly longer than polar night. Twilight can still light the sky during polar night, and [[Venus]] and [[Sirius]] are visible during civil twilight. When it is day in the [[Arctic Circle]], it is night in the [[Antarctic Circle]].

=== KEY: Polar_vortex_0cd3b7
HEADWORD: Polar vortex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jet stream | Ozone depletion | Polar night
ENTRY:
Large region of cold, rotating air encircling each of Earth's polar regions. The stratospheric polar vortex, a band of high-speed winds 15 to 50 km high, forms in autumn as [[Polar night|polar night]] begins and is strongest in winter. A sudden stratospheric warming can break it down in winter and affect surface weather. The tropospheric polar vortex, poleward of the [[Jet stream]], exists all year. When the Arctic tropospheric vortex weakens and splits, cold Arctic air can push toward the equator. A deep freeze in the United States and Canada in late January 2019 was blamed on the vortex, though that use of the term is not scientifically correct. [[Ozone depletion]] is heaviest within the vortices, especially over the Southern Hemisphere, peaking in spring.

=== KEY: Polaris_71f76d
HEADWORD: Polaris
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cepheid variable | Cosmic distance ladder | Ursa Minor | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Yellow supergiant star in the northern constellation [[Ursa Minor]], the current North Star. It lies less than 1° from the north celestial pole, which makes it a stable reference for navigation. Polaris is a triple star system, and its primary, Polaris Aa, is the closest classical [[Cepheid variable]], making it a key part of the [[Cosmic distance ladder]]. Its distance is about 432 light-years according to the Hipparcos parallax. The outer companion, Polaris B, was discovered by [[William Herschel]] in August 1779, while the inner pair was confirmed only in the early 20th century. Its brightness varies with a pulsation period of about four days.

=== KEY: Polarization_waves_ed1eed
HEADWORD: Polarization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Laser | Light | Polarizer | Radar | Sun
ENTRY:
Property of transverse waves that specifies the geometrical orientation of their oscillations. In a transverse wave, the oscillation is perpendicular to the direction of travel, as in a plucked string that vibrates vertically, horizontally or in between. Longitudinal waves, such as sound in a gas, cannot be polarized. Electromagnetic waves such as [[Light|light]] and radio waves can be polarized, with electric and magnetic fields perpendicular to each other. In linear polarization the fields oscillate in one direction, while in circular or elliptical polarization they rotate around the direction of travel. Light from the [[Sun]] or a flame is unpolarized until it passes through a [[Polarizer|polarizer]]. Polarization matters in optics, seismology, radio and microwaves, and for [[Laser|lasers]], optical fiber telecommunications and [[Radar|radar]].

=== KEY: Polarizer_5d718f
HEADWORD: Polarizer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Iodine | Photography | Polarization (waves) | Tourmaline | X-ray
ENTRY:
Optical filter that passes light waves of a specific polarization while blocking others. A polarizer can turn light of mixed polarization into [[Polarization (waves)|polarized light]]. Common types are linear and circular polarizers, and polarizers can also be made for radio waves, microwaves and [[X-ray|X-rays]]. Absorptive polarizers rely on dichroism, the preferential absorption of light polarized in particular directions. The [[Tourmaline]] crystal shows this effect, but its color limits its use. The most common absorptive polarizer is Polaroid, a sheet of polyvinyl alcohol plastic doped with [[Iodine|iodine]] that is durable and inexpensive. It is used in sunglasses, [[Photography|photographic]] filters and liquid crystal displays. Beam-splitting polarizers instead divide an incident beam into two beams of opposite polarization.

=== KEY: Polder_069f9e
HEADWORD: Polder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Netherlands | World War I
ENTRY:
Low-lying tract of land that forms an artificial hydrological unit, enclosed by embankments called dikes. Polders are formed by reclaiming land from a lake or the seabed, by separating flood plains from a river or sea, or by draining marshes, which are called koogs in Germany. Excess water from groundwater, rain and rivers is pumped out or drained through sluices at low tide. Drained peat sinks as it decomposes in contact with air. Polders are common in the [[Netherlands]], where as much as 20 percent of the land area has at some point been reclaimed from the sea. Flooding polders along the Yser River in [[World War I]] created a swamp that allowed Allied armies to stop the Imperial German army.

=== KEY: Pole_vault_91d86a
HEADWORD: Pole vault
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Gymnastics | High jump | Long jump | Olympic Games | Track and field
ENTRY:
[[Track and field]] event in which athletes use a long, flexible pole to vault over a high crossbar. Pole jumping was practiced in [[Ancient Egypt]], Ancient Greece and ancient Ireland, where poles helped people jump over ditches and rivers. Modern pole vaulting, in which height is measured, was established by the German teacher Johann Christoph Friedrich GutsMuths in the 1790s. It is one of the four major jumping events in athletics, alongside the [[High jump]], [[Long jump]] and triple jump. Vaulters often come from [[Gymnastics|gymnastics]] backgrounds. The event has been part of the [[Olympic Games]] for men since 1896 and for women since 2000.

=== KEY: Police_5dac73
HEADWORD: Police
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cesare Beccaria | Gendarmerie | Ireland | Jeremy Bentham | Michel Foucault | Military | State (polity)
ENTRY:
Constituted body of people empowered by a state to enforce the law and protect public order and the public itself. Police powers encompass arrest and the use of force legitimized by the [[State (polity)|state's]] monopoly on violence. Forces are usually public-sector services funded through taxes and are often defined as separate from the [[Military|military]], although the [[Gendarmerie|gendarmerie]] are military units charged with civil policing. Core functions include preventing and detecting crime, maintaining order, investigating offenses, controlling traffic, and responding to emergencies.

The word entered English in the early 15th century from Middle French *police* ("public order, administration, government"), from Latin *politia* and Greek *politeia*. [[Ireland]] uses the terms Garda and Gardaí for its national force and its members.

According to [[Michel Foucault]], the modern concept of police as a paid functionary of the state was developed by German and French legal scholars in the 17th and early 18th centuries, notably in Nicolas Delamare's *Traité de la Police* (1705). Under the German *Polizeiwissenschaft*, police duties extended to public health, urban planning, and the surveillance of prices. Preventive policing gained influence in the late 18th century; [[Jeremy Bentham]] promoted the view of [[Cesare Beccaria]] that preventing crimes is better than punishing them. London's Metropolitan Police, established in 1829, became a model for modern organized policing.

In some societies in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, police developed to maintain the class system and protect private property. Their role remains controversial: they may be involved to varying degrees in corruption, brutality, and the enforcement of authoritarian rule, and debates over accountability, use of force, and the balance between security and civil liberties continue.

=== KEY: Polio_2c59d1
HEADWORD: Polio
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Virus
ENTRY:
Infectious [[Virus|viral]] disease, also called poliomyelitis, caused by poliovirus and found naturally only in humans. Most infections cause no symptoms, and about 25 percent cause minor illness such as sore throat and low fever. In about 1 percent of infections, the virus enters the [[Central nervous system]], and roughly one to five in 1,000 cases progress to paralysis. Polio spreads mainly by the fecal–oral route, through contaminated food or water. There is no specific treatment, so prevention depends on vaccination. Major outbreaks began in Europe and the United States in the late 19th century. After vaccines appeared in the 1950s, incidence declined rapidly, and wild polio cases fell from an estimated 350,000 in 1988 to 30 in 2022. As of October 2023, only Pakistan and Afghanistan remained endemic for wild poliovirus.

=== KEY: Polio_vaccine_76888d
HEADWORD: Polio vaccine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jonas Salk | Polio | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Vaccine used to prevent [[Polio|poliomyelitis]]. Two types are used: an inactivated poliovirus given by injection (IPV), developed by [[Jonas Salk]] and announced as successful in 1955, and a weakened live virus given by mouth (OPV), developed by Albert Sabin and in commercial use from 1961. Hilary Koprowski demonstrated a live vaccine in 1950, though it was not approved in the United States. IPV is very safe, with mild redness or pain at the injection site. OPV is easier to give in mass campaigns and provides intestinal immunity, but it causes about three cases of vaccine-associated paralytic poliomyelitis per million doses, compared with 5,000 cases per million after polio infection. The [[World Health Organization]] recommends full vaccination of all children, and the vaccine appears on its List of Essential Medicines.

=== KEY: Polish_Lithuanian_Commonwealth_e52961
HEADWORD: Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constitutional monarchy | Grand Duchy of Lithuania | Poland
ENTRY:
Aristocratic republic, a union of the Kingdom of [[Poland]] and the [[Grand Duchy of Lithuania]], 1569–1795. Established by the Union of Lublin on July 1, 1569, it was among the largest and most populous countries of 16th- to 18th-century Europe, spanning about 1,000,000 km² at its early 17th-century peak. Its official languages were Polish and Latin, and Catholicism was the state religion, though the Warsaw Confederation Act of 1573 guaranteed religious tolerance by European standards. Power rested with the nobility, who made up about 10 percent of the population and elected the king. Its system, called Golden Liberty, was an early example of [[Constitutional monarchy]]. Austria, Prussia and Russia partitioned the weakened state in the late 18th century. Shortly before its demise, the 3 May Constitution became the first modern codified constitution in European history.

=== KEY: Polish_Lithuanian_Teutonic_War_c923fe
HEADWORD: Polish–Lithuanian–Teutonic War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Grand Duchy of Lithuania | Teutonic Order
ENTRY:
War of 1409–1411 between the [[Teutonic Order|Teutonic Knights]] and the allied Kingdom of Poland and [[Grand Duchy of Lithuania]]. Also called the Great Teutonic War, it began with a Teutonic invasion of Poland in August 1409, inspired by a Samogitian uprising. Wenceslaus IV of Bohemia brokered a nine-month truce. After the truce expired in June 1410, the Teutonic army was decisively defeated at the Battle of Grunwald, one of the largest battles in medieval Europe, where most of the Teutonic leadership was killed or captured. The Knights withstood a siege of their capital at Marienburg and lost only minimal territory under the Peace of Thorn in 1411, but they never regained their former power. The war shifted the balance of power in Central Europe toward the Polish–Lithuanian union.

=== KEY: Polish_Russian_War_1609_1618_4c9d4f
HEADWORD: Polish–Russian War (1609–1618)
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth | Sigismund III Vasa | Tsardom of Russia
ENTRY:
War between the [[Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth]] and the [[Tsardom of Russia]]. It was fought during the Time of Troubles, a period of political instability that followed the death of Tsar Feodor I in 1598 and the extinction of the Rurik dynasty. Polish nobles backed rival claimants to the Russian throne. King [[Sigismund III Vasa]] declared war in 1609, and Polish forces won early victories, including the 1610 Battle of Klushino, and entered Moscow. A popular Russian revolt led by Kuzma Minin and Prince Dmitry Pozharsky expelled the Poles from Moscow in 1612. In 1613 the Zemsky Sobor elected Michael Romanov as tsar, founding the Romanov dynasty. The Truce of Deulino in 1618 gave the Commonwealth some territorial concessions while preserving Russian independence. The war began a long rivalry between Poland and Russia.

=== KEY: Polish_Soviet_War_a34206
HEADWORD: Polish–Soviet War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Józef Piłsudski | Kyiv | Riga | Russian Revolution | Vladimir Lenin | Warsaw
ENTRY:
Conflict fought mainly between the Second Polish Republic and Soviet Russia from 1919 to 1921, following World War I and the [[Russian Revolution]]. [[Vladimir Lenin]] saw an independent Poland as a route for spreading communist revolution into Europe, while [[Józef Piłsudski]] sought to restore Poland's pre-1772 borders. Polish forces captured [[Kyiv]] in May 1920, but a Soviet counteroffensive drove them back to [[Warsaw]]. In the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920, Polish forces won an unexpected victory often called the "Miracle on the Vistula." The Peace of [[Riga]], signed on March 18, 1921, divided disputed territories and placed Poland's eastern border about 200 km east of the Curzon Line. The treaty also recognized the Ukrainian and Byelorussian Soviet Socialist Republics as Soviet states.

=== KEY: Polish_language_46009d
HEADWORD: Polish language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Union | Latin script | Lingua franca | Poland
ENTRY:
West Slavic language of the Lechitic subgroup, official language of [[Poland]]. It is written in the [[Latin script|Latin script]] with 32 letters, nine of them diacritic additions such as ą and ł. More than 39.7 million people were native speakers in 2024, and it ranks sixth among languages of the [[European Union]]. Polish is closely related to Slovak and Czech but differs in pronunciation and grammar. It is a fusional language with seven grammatical cases and fixed penultimate stress. It emerged as a distinct language around the 10th century, and contemporary Polish developed in the 1700s. Historically a [[Lingua franca]] in Central and part of Eastern Europe, it is also spoken by emigrant communities, especially after World War II, in countries including Canada, Argentina, Brazil, Israel, Australia, the United Kingdom and the United States.

=== KEY: Political_campaign_df96e4
HEADWORD: Political campaign
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Barack Obama | Election | Head of government | Head of state | John McCain | Referendum
ENTRY:
Organized effort to influence decision-making within a specific group, often an electoral campaign. In democracies, campaigns often aim to choose representatives or decide [[Referendum|referendums]], and the most high-profile ones focus on general [[Election|elections]] and candidates for [[Head of state|head of state]] or [[Head of government|head of government]]. Campaign messages consist of several talking points about policy issues, repeated to make a lasting impression on voters. Most campaigns keep the message broad to attract the most potential voters. In the 2008 American presidential election, [[John McCain]]'s message moved from "Country First" to "The Original Maverick," while [[Barack Obama]] ran on a consistent message of "change." Funding comes from large donors, direct mail to small donors and interest groups, and through Political Action Committees, which the Supreme Court allowed in Citizens United v. FEC (2010). Modern campaigns combine paid staff with volunteers led by campaign managers, political consultants and activists.

=== KEY: Political_freedom_a79fde
HEADWORD: Political freedom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Civil liberties | Friedrich Hayek | Human rights | Michel Foucault | Milton Friedman
ENTRY:
Concept in political thought, closely tied to [[Civil liberties|civil liberties]] and [[Human rights|human rights]]. Political freedom has been described as freedom from oppression or coercion, and it is often interpreted negatively as freedom from unreasonable external constraints on action. It can also mean the positive exercise of rights, capacities and possibilities for action. Left-wing philosophy generally couples freedom with positive liberty, the enabling of a group or individual to determine its own life, including freedom from poverty and disease. According to [[Friedrich Hayek]], the "socialist argument" defining individual liberty as "freedom from" obstacles confused the aim of securing individual freedom, because it permitted an identification of freedom with power. [[Milton Friedman]] argued that there cannot be political freedom without economic freedom, while Robin Hahnel took issue with Friedman's concept of economic freedom. [[Michel Foucault]] theorized political freedom in opposition to power relations.

=== KEY: Political_party_8e68ea
HEADWORD: Political party
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Communism | Conservative Party (UK) | Democracy | Democratic Party (United States) | Election | Glorious Revolution | Populism | Trade union
ENTRY:
Organization that coordinates candidates to compete in elections and participate in governance. Members commonly hold similar ideas about politics, and parties may promote specific ideological or policy goals. Parties are a major part of the politics of almost every country; most countries have several, while others have one. They matter in autocracies, which often have a single governing party, as well as in [[Democracy|democracies]], and some political scientists consider competition between parties essential to democracy.

A party usually includes a leader, executives who handle administration, and members who may volunteer, donate, and vote for its candidates. Parties may cooperate closely with affiliated organizations such as a [[Trade union|trade union]], a youth organization, or an armed wing. They differ from parliamentary groups, internal factions, and advocacy groups chiefly in focusing on electing candidates, and tend to have larger membership and a deeper connection to the [[Election|electorate]].

Many parties are driven by ideology: liberal, conservative, and socialist parties are common in democratic elections, as are [[Communism|communist]], [[Populism|populist]], nationalist, and Islamist ones. Others have no ideology and engage mainly in patronage or clientelism, or are "big tent" parties seeking voters with varied positions.

Factions are ancient; the late Roman Republic had the Populares and Optimates. Scholars differ on when modern parties emerged. Some point to 17th-century Britain after the [[Glorious Revolution]], when the Whigs backed Protestant constitutional monarchy against absolute rule; others place the rise of mass-based parties in the 1820s–1840s. The [[Conservative Party (UK)|Conservative Party]] of the United Kingdom and the [[Democratic Party (United States)|Democratic Party]] of the United States are both frequently called the world's oldest continuous party. Critics, including James Madison, have warned that factionalism exacerbates social divisions, while defenders credit parties with aggregating interests into coherent platforms.

=== KEY: Political_philosophy_82db2d
HEADWORD: Political philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anarchism | Aristotle | Conservatism | Liberalism | Plato | Socialism | State (polity)
ENTRY:
Study of the theoretical and conceptual foundations of politics, also called political theory. It examines the nature, scope and legitimacy of institutions such as [[State (polity)|states]], and the values that guide political action, such as justice, equality and liberty. As a normative field, it focuses on desirable norms, in contrast to political science, which emphasizes empirical description. Major ideologies include [[Anarchism|anarchism]], which rejects the coercive power of centralized governments; [[Conservatism|conservatism]], which seeks to preserve traditional institutions; [[Liberalism|liberalism]], which advocates individual rights and private property; and [[Socialism|socialism]], which emphasizes collective ownership. The field has roots in ancient Greece, where [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] discussed justice and ideal states, and in ancient China, where Confucianism, Taoism and legalism emerged.

=== KEY: Political_science_22dec2
HEADWORD: Political science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Empiricism | Game theory | International relations | Political philosophy | Positivism | Public administration | Quantitative research
ENTRY:
Academic discipline that studies politics, systems of governance and power, political behavior and thought, and associated constitutions and laws. Unlike [[Political philosophy|political philosophy]], which is primarily normative and judges politics by values, it emphasizes descriptive and explanatory accounts and favors empirical evidence over ethical judgments.

The field emerged as a distinct discipline in the late 19th century, influenced by [[Empiricism|empiricism]] and [[Positivism|positivism]], from history, philosophy, and law amid the professionalization of universities. *Political Science Quarterly* was established in 1886, and its first issue defined political science as "the science of the state." The American Political Science Association (APSA) was founded in 1903 and the *American Political Science Review* in 1906; APSA now serves more than 11,000 members in more than 100 countries. A UNESCO initiative led to the International Political Science Association in 1949, with national associations in France in 1949, Britain in 1950, and West Germany in 1951.

The behavioral revolution of the 1950s shifted emphasis to explaining human behavior from a neutral point of view using [[Quantitative research|quantitative methods]] such as surveys and empirical testing. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, [[Game theory|game-theoretic]] modeling borrowed from economics was applied to institutions such as the U.S. Congress and to voting, led by William H. Riker and colleagues at the University of Rochester. The Perestroika Movement of 2000 reacted against what its supporters called the "mathematicization" of the field and argued for a plurality of methodologies.

Its main subfields are political theory, comparative politics, [[International relations|international relations]], American politics, political methodology, and [[Public administration|public administration]]. Critics have raised concerns about the field's replicability and about the political leanings of its practitioners.

=== KEY: Political_spectrum_0fc279
HEADWORD: Political spectrum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Communism | Conservatism | France | Liberalism | Socialism
ENTRY:
System for classifying political positions in relation to one another, usually placed along one or more geometric axes. The left–right dimension appears in most long-standing spectra. Its terms derive from seating arrangements in the [[France|French]] parliament after the Revolution (1789–1799), where radicals sat on the left and aristocrats on the right. [[Communism]] and [[Socialism|socialism]] are usually regarded internationally as left, while [[Conservatism|conservatism]] and reactionism are usually regarded as right. [[Liberalism]] can fall on either side. Several political scientists have noted that a single left–right axis is too simplistic, so they add other axes, often splitting economic issues from socio-cultural issues. In 1950 Leonard W. Ferguson factor-analyzed ten attitude scales and identified three factors, which he named religionism, humanitarianism and nationalism.

=== KEY: Politics_9258fc
HEADWORD: Politics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Government | Hannah Arendt | Niccolò Machiavelli | Plato | Political science | Sumer | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Activity of settling affairs in an organized society, usually by resolving issues through a [[Government|government]]. People engaged in it are politicians, and the branch of social science that studies politics and government is [[Political science|political science]]. Methods include promoting one's views among people, negotiating, making laws, and exercising internal and external force, including warfare against adversaries. Politics operates at levels from clans and tribes of traditional societies through local governments, companies, and institutions to sovereign states and the international level. In modern states people often form parties to represent their ideas, and an election is usually a competition between parties. A political system is a framework that defines acceptable political methods within a society. The word may be used positively, as in a compromising and non-violent "political solution," or descriptively, as "the art or science of government," but it often carries a negative connotation.

The English word derives from Greek *polis*, "city." Aristotle's *Politiká* ("affairs of the cities") was rendered in the mid-15th century as *Polettiques*, which became Politics; the singular *politic* is attested in English from 1430.

Writers differ on what counts as political. Adrian Leftwich distinguished an extensive view, which finds politics across all human social relations, from a limited view that restricts it to certain contexts such as governance. The feminist slogan "the personal is political" belongs to the extensive view. Robert A. Dahl argued that politics may be defined by the use of power. Political moralists link politics closely to ethics; [[Hannah Arendt]] wrote that for Aristotle, being political meant that everything was decided through words and persuasion rather than violence. Realists, a group that some scholars say includes [[Niccolò Machiavelli]], [[Thomas Hobbes]], and Harold Lasswell, see its true nature in the human desire for rule and self-interest; Lasswell defined it as determining "who gets what, when, and how." Elmer Schattschneider held that conflict lies at the root of all politics, and Carl Schmitt saw its essence in distinguishing friend from foe, while Aristotle and Bernard Crick took more cooperative views. Michael Laver described it as a blend of conflict and co-operation.

Early bands and tribes lacked centralized political structures. Theories of early state formation include voluntary theories, which stress shared rational interest and the pressures that followed the development of agriculture, including the hydraulic hypothesis tying the state to large-scale irrigation; and conflict theories, which stress dominance of one group over others and warfare. The first states were early dynastic [[Sumer]] and early dynastic Egypt, which arose around 3000 BC. Egyptians, Romans, and Greeks were the first known to formulate a political philosophy of the state and analyze political institutions rationally; before that, states were justified through religious myths. The history of political thought reaches back to early antiquity, with seminal works such as [[Plato]]'s *Republic*, Aristotle's *Politics*, Confucius's political manuscripts, and Chanakya's *Arthashastra*. Greek city-states and the Roman Republic produced key innovations, including direct democracy in Athens, where citizenship was combined with directly democratic government.

=== KEY: Pollen_164f5d
HEADWORD: Pollen
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaeology | Gametophyte | Gymnosperm | Paleontology | Sperm
ENTRY:
Powdery substance produced by most types of flowers of seed plants for sexual reproduction. Pollen consists of grains, highly reduced [[Gametophyte|gametophytes]] that produce [[Sperm|sperm]] cells. Each grain has a hard coat of sporopollenin that protects it while it travels from the stamens to the pistil of a flower, or from the male cone to the female cone of a [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperm]]. If pollen lands on a compatible pistil or cone, it germinates and grows a pollen tube that carries sperm to the ovule. The smallest grains, those of forget-me-not, are 2.5–5 μm across, while corn pollen is about 90–100 μm. The study of pollen, palynology, is useful in paleoecology, [[Paleontology|paleontology]], [[Archaeology|archaeology]] and forensics. Pollen is infrequently used as food and is often contaminated by agricultural pesticides.

=== KEY: Pollination_2a972a
HEADWORD: Pollination
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bat | Bee | Bird | Egg cell | Fertilisation | Gymnosperm | Hybrid (biology) | Pollen
ENTRY:
Transfer of [[Pollen|pollen]] from the anther of a plant to the stigma of a plant, which enables [[Fertilisation|fertilization]] and seed production. Pollinating agents include animals such as [[Bee|bees]], beetles and butterflies, [[Bird|birds]] and [[Bat|bats]], as well as water, wind and plants themselves. Self-pollination can occur within a closed flower, while pollination between species can produce [[Hybrid (biology)|hybrid]] offspring. In angiosperms, the pollen tube carries two gametes to the ovule. One nucleus fuses with polar bodies to form the endosperm, and the other fuses with the [[Egg cell|egg cell]] to form the embryo, a process called double fertilization. In [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperms]], the ovule is exposed on a support organ such as a cone scale, so penetration of carpel tissue is unnecessary. Christian Konrad Sprengel first addressed pollination as an interaction between flowers and pollen vectors in the 18th century. Because fruiting depends on fertilization, pollination is important in horticulture and agriculture.

=== KEY: Pollution_c407e8
HEADWORD: Pollution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air pollution | Carbon monoxide | Factory | PFAS | Sulfur dioxide | United Nations | Waste management | Water pollution
ENTRY:
Introduction of contaminants into the natural environment that cause harm. Pollution can take the form of any substance (solid, liquid, or gas) or energy such as radioactivity, heat, sound, or light. Although it can be caused by natural events, the word generally implies a human source, such as manufacturing, extractive industries, poor [[Waste management|waste management]], transportation, or agriculture. It is often classed as point source, coming from a concentrated site such as a [[Factory|factory]], mine, or construction site, or nonpoint source, coming from widespread sources such as microplastics or agricultural runoff.

Major forms include [[Air pollution|air pollution]], [[Water pollution|water pollution]], litter, noise pollution, plastic pollution, soil contamination, radioactive contamination, thermal pollution, light pollution, and visual pollution. Common gaseous air pollutants include [[Carbon monoxide|carbon monoxide]], [[Sulfur dioxide|sulfur dioxide]], chlorofluorocarbons, and nitrogen oxides produced by industry and motor vehicles. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances ([[PFAS]]), informally called "forever chemicals," are a major chemical pollutant.

In 2019, pollution killed approximately nine million people worldwide (about one in six deaths that year); about three-quarters of these deaths were caused by air pollution. Children, the elderly, and marginalized communities bear outsized impacts, a core reason for the environmental justice movement. Many sources went unregulated during 19th- and 20th-century industrialization until environmental regulation emerged in the later half of the 20th century. International efforts are now coordinated by the [[United Nations|UN]] Environmental Program and other treaty bodies.

=== KEY: Polo_edc23e

=== KEY: Polygon_4c0a11
HEADWORD: Polygon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circle | Computer graphics | Euclidean space | Pentagram | Quadrilateral | Triangle
ENTRY:
Plane figure made up of line segments connected to form a closed chain. The segments are its edges or sides, and the points where two edges meet are its vertices or corners. An n-gon has n sides; a [[Triangle|triangle]] is a 3-gon, and a [[Quadrilateral|quadrilateral]] a 4-gon.

A simple polygon does not intersect itself and forms the boundary of a region of the plane called a solid polygon. A chain that crosses itself produces star polygons and other self-intersecting polygons. Some sources also count closed chains in [[Euclidean space]] that do not lie in a single plane (skew polygons). A polygon is the 2-dimensional example of the more general polytope.

Polygons are classified by number of sides, convexity, and symmetry. In a convex polygon, any line through it that is not tangent to an edge or corner meets the boundary exactly twice, and all interior angles are less than 180°. A concave polygon is simple but has at least one interior angle greater than 180°. A regular polygon has equal angles and equal edges. A cyclic polygon has all corners on a single [[Circle|circle]], the circumcircle.

The interior angles of a simple n-gon sum to (n − 2) × 180°, because it can be divided into (n − 2) triangles. The exterior angles of a convex polygon sum to 360°; in general the sum can be any integer multiple of 360°, such as 720° for a [[Pentagram|pentagram]]. Polygons form the basis of polygonal meshes in [[Computer graphics|computer graphics]], of tiling in architecture, and of boundaries in geographic information systems.

=== KEY: Polynesia_653884
HEADWORD: Polynesia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Easter Island | Hawaiian Islands | Melanesia | Navigation | New Zealand | Oceania | Pacific Ocean | Zealandia
ENTRY:
Subregion of [[Oceania]] made up of more than 1,000 islands scattered over the central and southern [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific Ocean]]. Its indigenous inhabitants, the Polynesians, share linguistic relations, cultural practices, and traditional beliefs. The French writer Charles de Brosses first used the term *Polynésie* in 1756 for all the Pacific islands; in 1831 Jules Dumont d'Urville proposed a narrower definition. The region is generally defined as the islands within the Polynesian Triangle, drawn by connecting Hawaii, [[New Zealand]], and [[Easter Island]]. The [[Hawaiian Islands]] lie at its northern vertex. Other main groups inside it include Samoa, Tonga, the Cook Islands, Tuvalu, and French Polynesia. Outliers lie in [[Melanesia]] and Micronesia.

Polynesia has about 300,000 to 310,000 sq km of land, of which more than 270,000 sq km are in New Zealand; the Hawaiian archipelago makes up about half the remainder. Most islands, including the Hawaiian Islands and Samoa, are volcanic islands built by hotspots. New Zealand, Norfolk Island, and Ouvéa are unsubmerged portions of the largely sunken continent of [[Zealandia]], believed to have mostly sunk below sea level 23 million years ago.

Polynesians trace their ancestry to Austronesian-speaking peoples from Island Southeast Asia who reached the Samoa-Tonga-Fiji area around 3,000 years before present as part of the Lapita cultural complex. Settlement of remote outliers such as Hawaii and Rapa Nui was complete by about 1200 CE. Polynesian societies shared chiefly hierarchies and oral genealogies. Their [[Navigation|wayfinding]] drew on stars, ocean swells, winds, and birds, and double-hulled voyaging canoes sustained long-distance exchange between islands.

Small, still, land-based body of water formed by pooling in a depression, naturally or artificially. A pond is smaller than a [[Lake|lake]], and no official criteria distinguish the two. Some definitions treat a pond as less than 5 hectares (12 acres) in area, less than 5 meters (16 ft) deep and with under 30% of its area covered by emergent vegetation. Ponds form by processes such as cutoff river channels on [[Floodplain|floodplains]], glacial action, or [[Beaver|beaver]] activity. === KEY: Pop_music_24255c

ENTRY:
Genre of popular music that originated in its modern form during the mid-1950s in the United States and the United Kingdom. During the 1950s and 1960s it encompassed [[Rock and roll|rock and roll]] and the youth-oriented styles it influenced. Rock and pop remained roughly synonymous until the late 1960s; from about 1967 the term was increasingly used in opposition to [[Rock music|rock music]], which aspired to authenticity and an expansion of the possibilities of popular music, while pop became more commercial, ephemeral, and accessible.

Scholar Timothy Warner describes an emphasis on recording, production, and technology over live performance. Musicologist Simon Frith calls pop music produced "as a matter of enterprise not art," by the [[Music industry|music industry]]. === KEY: Popular_culture_202b94
HEADWORD: Popular culture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hello Kitty | Industrial Revolution | Mass media | Nintendo | Pop music | Postmodernism | Sony
ENTRY:
Set of practices, beliefs, artistic output, and objects that are dominant or prevalent in a society at a given time; also called pop culture or mass culture. [[Mass media]], marketing, and the imperatives of mass appeal within capitalism are the primary engines of Western popular culture, a system the philosopher Theodor Adorno critically termed the "culture industry." It is generally contrasted with folk culture, working-class culture, or high culture, and is defined in conflicting ways, including from [[Postmodernism|postmodernist]] perspectives.

The phrase was coined in the 19th century or earlier and was traditionally associated with poor education and the lower classes, as opposed to the "official culture" of the upper classes. During the [[Industrial Revolution]], Britain experienced rising literacy and growing spending on entertainment such as public houses and sports. The first penny serials were published in the 1830s. After World War II, mass media innovations made the term overlap with mass culture, media culture, and consumer culture. The abbreviated form pop, as in [[Pop music|pop music]], dates from the late 1950s and is narrower, referring to mass appeal rather than mere popularity.

From the 1970s, Japan became a global source of popular culture through [[Sony]] and Matsushita (now Panasonic), the Walkman, and kawaii culture, the "cult of cute," fronted by companies such as Sanrio and [[Nintendo]]. Characters such as [[Hello Kitty]] spread to East Asia and the West.

According to the author John Storey, definitions vary: culture that is widely liked, culture left over once high culture is decided, mass-produced commercial culture, or an "authentic" culture of the people. Neo-Gramscian theory sees it as a site of struggle between subordinate and dominant groups.

=== KEY: Population_ageing_8d1988

ENTRY:
Increase in the median age of a population, usually reflected in rising mean and median ages, a falling share of children and a rising share of older people. Two forces drive it: longer [[Longevity|longevity]] and declining [[Fertility|fertility]], although falling fertility now accounts for most of the aging. === KEY: Population_biology_206ff7
HEADWORD: Population
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ecology | Latin | Species
ENTRY:
Group of individuals of the same [[Species|species]], separated from other groups by characteristics such as living area, genetic attributes or demographic structure. Definitions vary among biologists. The word derives from the [[Latin]] *populatio*, "a people, a multitude." Two main approaches define a population. The [[Ecology|ecological]] view treats its members as interacting and competing in a geographic area, while the evolutionary view treats genes and reproduction as the driving forces, and each population has its own gene pool. Some biologists define a population as individuals that can interbreed, and reproductive isolation separates populations and defines species. Terms such as deme, metapopulation and subpopulation describe other kinds of group. In ecology, the term community is generally used for several species of a region.

=== KEY: Population_bottleneck_204520

Sharp reduction in the size of a [[Population (biology)|population]]. The three main types are hard-paste, soft-paste and bone china. Porcelain originated in China, where the technology matured between approximately 200 and 800 CE, depending on the definition used. It spread to Asia and then Europe, and it was called "china" in English because it was first imported from China in the 17th century. The earliest European porcelain was made at the Meissen factory in [[Saxony]] in the early 18th century. In 2021 the global market for porcelain tableware was estimated at US$22.1 billion.

=== KEY: Porcupine_1af356

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Large [[Rodent|rodent]] with a coat of sharp spines, or quills, that protect it from predators. The name comes from the [[Latin]] *porcus* ("pig") and *spina* ("spine"). Porcupines belong to two families that are not closely related. Old World porcupines (Hystricidae) live in Italy, West and South Asia and most of Africa; they are large, terrestrial and strictly nocturnal. New World porcupines (Erethizontidae) are native to North America and northern [[South America]], and they live in wooded areas and can climb trees. The largest species is the third-largest living rodent, after the [[Capybara|capybara]] and the [[Beaver|beaver]]. Quills are modified hairs made of keratin, and their display advertises that the animal is unsuitable as prey. Most porcupines are about 60–90 cm long. A baby porcupine, or porcupette, has soft quills that harden within days.

=== KEY: Porfirio_D_az_3f34d0
HEADWORD: Díaz, Porfirio
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Benito Juárez | Dictatorship | Mexican Revolution | Paris
ENTRY:
Mexican army general and politician, leader of Mexico, 1876–1911. Born in Oaxaca, he studied law, and [[Benito Juárez]] was among his mentors. He fought in the Reform War and against the French, and in June 1867 he led the forces that retook Mexico City. He then revolted against Juárez and Sebastián Lerdo de Tejada over the principle of no reelection, and he seized power in 1876. In 1884 he abandoned that principle and held office continuously until 1911. His regime ended political instability and brought economic growth. Its technocrats, known as científicos, benefited allies and foreign investors, while large estates grew through violence and legal abuse, provoking repression, strikes and uprisings. The period, the Porfiriato, has been called a de facto [[Dictatorship|dictatorship]]. In 1910 Díaz, then 80, ran for an eighth term despite a 1908 statement favoring democracy. Francisco I. Madero's opposition led to the [[Mexican Revolution]], and in May 1911 Díaz resigned and went into exile in [[Paris]].

=== KEY: Pork_34e3f4

Culinary name for the meat of the [[Pig|pig]]. Pork is eaten both fresh and preserved, as in ham, gammon, bacon and [[Sausage|sausage]]. Charcuterie is the branch of cooking devoted to prepared meats, many of them pork. Pigs were among the earliest domesticated animals, in Mesopotamia and China. Pork accounts for about 34% of meat production worldwide. It is popular in Central Europe and across the Western world, and Chinese cuisine esteems it highly; [[China]] is the world's largest pork consumer. Feijoada, the national dish of [[Brazil]], is traditionally made with pork trimmings. Some religions and cultures prohibit pork, notably Islam and Judaism.

=== KEY: Porpoise_f868d1
HEADWORD: Porpoise
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cetacean | Dolphin | Narwhal | Vaquita
ENTRY:
Small, dolphin-like [[Cetacean|cetacean]] of the family Phocoenidae. Porpoises have flattened, spade-shaped teeth and lack the pronounced beak of most [[Dolphin|dolphins]], and they are more closely related to [[Narwhal|narwhals]] and belugas. They range from the [[Vaquita|vaquita]], about 1.4 m long, to Dall's porpoise, about 2.3 m and 220 kg. They use echolocation as their primary sense and feed mainly on fish and squid. Porpoises live in habitats from rivers to the open ocean. Bycatch in gill nets is a major threat, and the vaquita is nearly extinct, with a predicted population of fewer than a dozen individuals. Since the extinction of the baiji, it has been considered the most endangered cetacean.

=== KEY: Porridge_99cbb4
HEADWORD: Porridge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cholesterol | Maize | Oat | Taro
ENTRY:
Semi-solid food made by soaking, poaching or boiling starchy plants, typically grain, in milk or water. It is a traditional staple in many countries and is often flavored with salt, sugar, honey or fruit, or made savory with spices, meat or vegetables. In British English the word refers specifically to oat porridge, known as [[Oat|oatmeal]] in North America. Other variants include polenta, made from [[Maize|maize]]; poi, made from [[Taro|taro]]; congee, an Asian savory rice porridge; and gruel, a thinner form of oat porridge. Porridge originated over 12,000 years ago, after the domestication of grains. A 2014 review found that at least 3 grams of oat beta-glucan daily lowered total and low-density lipoprotein [[Cholesterol|cholesterol]] by 5–10%. In Charles Dickens's *Oliver Twist*, gruel marks poverty.
