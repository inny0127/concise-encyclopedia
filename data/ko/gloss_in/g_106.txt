=== KEY: Placebo_6f8ce0
HEADWORD: Placebo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consent | Latin | Medicine | Meta-analysis | Pain
ENTRY:
A [[Medicine|medicine]] or treatment intended to appear genuine to its recipient but having no pharmaceutical effect. Common placebos include inert tablets, such as sugar pills, inert injections such as saline, and sham surgery. In randomized clinical trials, the change in a control group given a placebo is the placebo response, and the difference between this and the result of no treatment is the placebo effect. Placebos are used to test treatments while keeping participants and clinicians from knowing who receives the treatment under test. The idea of a placebo effect was discussed in 18th-century psychology but became more prominent in the 20th century. A 2001 [[Meta-analysis|meta-analysis]] of trials in 40 medical conditions found a significant effect only for [[Pain|pain]], and a 2010 Cochrane review likewise found effects apparent only in subjective, continuous measures. Disguising placebos as active treatment raises ethical concerns, because it bypasses [[Consent|informed consent]]. The term derives from the [[Latin|Latin]] *placebo*, meaning "shall be pleasing."

=== KEY: Placoderm_8e013d
HEADWORD: Placoderm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apex predator | Carboniferous | Tetrapod | Tooth | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Extinct group of armored, jawed prehistoric [[Vertebrate|vertebrate]] fishes, known from Silurian and Devonian fossils. Their head and thorax were covered by articulated armored plates, from which the name derives (Greek *plax*, "plate", and *derma*, "skin"), while the rest of the body was scaled or naked. Placoderms were among the first jawed fish and the first vertebrates to have true [[Tooth|teeth]]. They were also the first fish group to develop pelvic fins, the homologous precursor of hindlimbs in [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]]. Fossils of three genera about 380 million years old represent the oldest known examples of live birth. They included some of the first vertebrate [[Apex predator|apex predators]], such as the large *Dunkleosteus terrelli*, 3.5–4.1 meters (11–13 ft) long. All placoderms became extinct at the end-Devonian Hangenberg event 358.9 million years ago, leaving niches for the osteichthyan and chondrichthyan survivors that radiated during the [[Carboniferous|Carboniferous]].

Infectious disease caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis*. Symptoms, which usually begin one to seven days after exposure, include fever, weakness, headache and black lips. === KEY: Planck_constant_89137b
HEADWORD: Planck constant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black-body radiation | Kilogram | Max Planck | Metrology | Photon | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Fundamental physical constant of foundational importance in [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], denoted *h*. A [[Photon|photon]]'s energy is equal to its frequency multiplied by the constant. [[Max Planck]] postulated it in 1900 as a proportionality constant needed to explain experimental [[Black-body radiation|black-body radiation]], and later called it the "quantum of action". In [[Metrology|metrology]] it is used with other constants to define the [[Kilogram|kilogram]]; in SI units it has the exact value *h* = 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J⋅Hz⁻¹. The reduced Planck constant, ℏ (h-bar), equals *h* divided by 2π and is commonly used in quantum physics equations.

=== KEY: Planck_s_law_baeef9
HEADWORD: Planck's law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Black-body radiation | Max Planck
ENTRY:
Formula describing the spectral density of electromagnetic radiation emitted by a [[Black-body radiation|black body]] in thermal equilibrium at a given temperature. At the end of the 19th century, physicists could not explain the observed black-body spectrum, which diverged at higher frequencies from theoretical predictions. In 1900 [[Max Planck]] derived the formula by assuming that an oscillator could change its energy only in minimal increments proportional to frequency. Planck originally saw this as a mathematical artifice, but [[Albert Einstein]] built on his work. Higher temperatures raise the total radiated energy and shift the spectral peak to shorter wavelengths.

=== KEY: Planck_units_eaeb8c
HEADWORD: Planck units
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Max Planck | Natural units | Quantum gravity | Standard Model
ENTRY:
System of [[Natural units|natural units]] defined exclusively in terms of four universal physical constants: c, G, ħ and kB. Expressing one of these constants in Planck units gives a numerical value of 1. The units are defined using properties of free space rather than a prototype object. They were originally proposed in 1899 by German physicist [[Max Planck]] and are relevant to research on unified theories such as [[Quantum gravity|quantum gravity]]. The Planck scale refers to quantities of space, time and energy similar in magnitude to the Planck units, with particle energies of around 10¹⁹ GeV, time intervals of around 10⁻⁴³ s and lengths of around 10⁻³⁵ m. At this scale, the predictions of the [[Standard Model]], quantum field theory and general relativity are not expected to apply. One example is the conditions in the first 10⁻⁴³ seconds of the universe after the [[Big Bang]], approximately 13.8 billion years ago.

=== KEY: Plane_mathematics_34edb0
HEADWORD: Plane
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Complex analysis | Euclidean geometry | Graph theory | Line (geometry) | Point (geometry) | Topology
ENTRY:
Two-dimensional space or flat surface that extends indefinitely. === KEY: Planet_9ffc5d
HEADWORD: Planet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brown dwarf | Dwarf planet | Exoplanet | Kuiper belt | Nebular hypothesis | Solar System
ENTRY:
Large, rounded astronomical body generally required to orbit a star, stellar remnant, or brown dwarf, and not to be one itself. By the most restrictive definition, the [[Solar System]] has eight planets: the terrestrial planets Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, and the giant planets Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union (IAU) adopted a definition placing these eight in the planet category; Ceres, Pluto, and Eris are [[Dwarf planet|dwarf planets]].

The word comes from the Greek *planētai* ('wanderers'). In antiquity it referred to the Sun, Moon, and the five points of light visible to the naked eye that moved against the stars: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn. Earth itself was recognized as a planet when heliocentrism supplanted geocentrism during the 16th and 17th centuries. With the telescope, the term broadened to include Uranus, Neptune, Ceres and other bodies of the asteroid belt, and Pluto, later found to be the largest member of the [[Kuiper belt]].

The prevailing account of formation is the [[Nebular hypothesis|nebular hypothesis]]. A nebula collapses into a thin disk of gas and dust, with a protostar at the core. Through accretion, dust particles steadily accumulate mass to form planetesimals and then protoplanets. The planets of the Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago.

More than 6,000 planets outside the Solar System, called [[Exoplanet|exoplanets]], have been discovered. The discovery of [[Brown dwarf|brown dwarfs]] and planets larger than Jupiter prompted debate over where to draw the line between planet and star. Earth remains the only planet known to support life.

=== KEY: Planetary_nebula_e532f5
HEADWORD: Planetary nebula
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Interstellar medium | Milky Way | Nebula | Red giant | Ring Nebula
ENTRY:
Type of [[Nebula|emission nebula]] consisting of an expanding, glowing shell of ionized gas ejected from [[Red giant|red giant]] stars late in their lives. The name describes the planet-like round shape seen through early telescopes, though these objects are unrelated to planets. Planetary nebulae form at the end of the life of a star of about 1–8 solar masses, and the Sun is expected to form one. They last perhaps a few tens of millennia. Once the red giant's atmosphere has dissipated, ultraviolet radiation from the exposed hot core, the planetary nebula nucleus, ionizes the ejected material, causing it to glow. They probably play a crucial role in the chemical evolution of the [[Milky Way|Milky Way]] by expelling elements into the [[Interstellar medium|interstellar medium]]. About one-fifth are roughly spherical, but the majority are not, and the mechanisms producing their varied shapes are not well understood. The [[Ring Nebula|Ring Nebula]] is a well-known example.

=== KEY: Planetary_system_52a429

ENTRY:
Set of non-stellar bodies gravitationally bound to, and in orbit around, a star or star system. By convention, systems are named after their host star, as with the [[Solar System|Solar System]], named after Sol, Latin for Sun. === KEY: Plankton_52b8f2
HEADWORD: Plankton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bacteria | Jellyfish | Oxygen | Photosynthesis | Pollen | Protozoa
ENTRY:
Organisms that drift in water (or air) but cannot actively propel themselves against currents or wind. Plankton include organisms from all the major biological kingdoms, from microscopic [[Bacteria|bacteria]], archaea and [[Protozoa|protozoa]] to larger organisms such as [[Jellyfish|jellyfish]]. The term describes an ecological niche and level of motility rather than taxonomy, and comes from the Greek *planktos*, meaning "drifter" or "wanderer". Traditionally, plankton were divided into phytoplankton, which make their own food by [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], and zooplankton, which eat other organisms; a third group, mixoplankton, combines both modes. Phytoplankton produce roughly half of Earth's [[Oxygen|oxygen]] and play a major role in carbon sequestration. Marine microorganisms have been estimated to make up 70 to 90 percent of ocean biomass. Airborne plankton include plant spores, [[Pollen|pollen]] and wind-scattered seeds.

=== KEY: Plant_2751e0
HEADWORD: Plant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Botany | Carl Linnaeus | Charles Darwin | Flowering plant | Ordovician | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Eukaryotic organism of the kingdom Plantae, predominantly photosynthetic or descended from photosynthetic ancestors. Plants obtain energy from sunlight by using the green pigment chlorophyll in their chloroplasts to produce sugars from carbon dioxide and water, in the process of [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Some have evolved to be parasitic and take energy from other organisms. Most plants are multicellular, except for glaucophytes, some green algae, and a few red algae.

Historically, as in [[Aristotle]]'s biology, the plant kingdom encompassed all living things that were not animals, including algae and fungi. Aristotle distinguished beings by whether they had a "sensitive soul" or, like plants, only a "vegetative soul." [[Carl Linnaeus]] created the basis of the modern system of scientific classification but retained the animal and plant kingdoms. Current definitions exclude fungi and some algae. In the definition used here, plants form the clade Viridiplantae (green plants), which consists of the green algae and the embryophytes, or land plants: hornworts, liverworts, mosses, lycophytes, ferns, conifers and other gymnosperms, and [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]]. A definition based on genomes also includes the red algae and the glaucophytes, in the clade Archaeplastida.

There are about 382,000 accepted species of plants, of which some 283,000 produce seeds; 85–90% of all species are flowering plants. They range in size from single-celled desmids, about 10 μm across, to the conifer *Sequoia sempervirens*, which reaches up to 120 m in height.

The ancestors of land plants evolved in water. The first land plants appeared in the [[Ordovician]], around 450 million years ago, and primitive land plants began to diversify in the late Silurian, around 420 million years ago. By the end of the Devonian most of the basic features of plants today were present, including roots, leaves, and secondary wood. The Carboniferous saw the development of forests in swampy environments and the appearance of early gymnosperms, the first seed plants. Flowering plants evolved around 200 million years ago, in the Triassic, and underwent an adaptive radiation in the Cretaceous so rapid that [[Charles Darwin]] called it "an abominable mystery." Conifers diversified from the Late Triassic onward and became a dominant part of floras in the Jurassic.

Plant cells have a large water-filled central vacuole, chloroplasts, and a strong, flexible cell wall made mostly of cellulose. Chloroplasts derive from a symbiosis of a non-photosynthetic cell with photosynthetic cyanobacteria. Plant cells differentiate into tissues such as the vascular tissue, with specialized xylem and phloem, and into organs: roots to absorb water and minerals, stems for support and transport, leaves for photosynthesis, and flowers for sexual reproduction. Photosynthesis is summarized by the equation 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, which releases oxygen into the atmosphere. Green plants provide a substantial proportion of the world's molecular oxygen. The sugars they produce provide the energy for most of Earth's ecosystems, and animals and other organisms depend on plant materials. People use plants for food, building materials such as wood, writing materials, ornament, and medicines. The scientific study of plants is called [[Botany|botany]].

=== KEY: Plant_anatomy_a47c00

Study of the internal structure of plants. Originally it included [[Plant morphology|plant morphology]], the description of external form, but since the mid-20th century it has referred only to internal structure. It is now often investigated at the cellular level, using sectioning of tissues and microscopy. Classical divisions cover flower anatomy; [[Leaf|leaf]] anatomy, including the epidermis, [[Stoma|stomata]] and palisade cells; stem anatomy; fruit and seed anatomy; [[Wood|wood]] anatomy; and [[Root|root]] anatomy. British doctor Nehemiah Grew, credited with recognizing plant cells (which he called "vesicles" and "bladders"), published *An Idea of a Philosophical History of Plants* in 1672. Theophrastus had written plant treatises about 300 BC.

=== KEY: Plant_breeding_77a5ea
HEADWORD: Plant breeding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crop | Domestication | Green Revolution | Gregor Mendel | Selective breeding
ENTRY:
Science of changing the traits of plants to produce desired characteristics. It is used to improve the quality of plant products for humans and animals, and aims to produce [[Crop|crop]] varieties with superior traits, such as tolerance to biotic and abiotic stress, grain or biomass yield, and end-use quality. Techniques range from selecting plants for propagation to methods using knowledge of genetics and chromosomes, and to more complex molecular techniques. It is practiced by gardeners and farmers, and by professional breeders at government institutions, universities and research centers. A 2023 study found that without plant breeding, Europe would have produced 20% fewer arable crops over the last 20 years, consuming an additional 21.6 million hectares of land. Plant breeding began with the [[Domestication|domestication]] of the first agricultural plants an estimated 9,000 to 11,000 years ago. [[Gregor Mendel|Gregor Mendel]]'s experiments with plant hybridization led to his laws of inheritance, and [[Selective breeding|selective breeding]] played a crucial role in the [[Green Revolution|Green Revolution]] of the 20th century.

=== KEY: Plant_cell_a75764
HEADWORD: Plant cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cellulose | Chloroplast | Meristem | Photosynthesis | Vacuole
ENTRY:
Eukaryotic cell of green plants. Plant cells have a rigid primary wall of [[Cellulose|cellulose]], hemicelluloses and pectin, built outside the cell membrane. This composition differs from the chitin of fungal walls, the peptidoglycan of bacteria and the pseudopeptidoglycan of archaea. Plastids, most notably [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]], contain chlorophyll and perform [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], converting sunlight into chemical energy. Many cells contain a large central [[Vacuole|vacuole]] that maintains turgor pressure, stores materials and digests waste. Flagella and centrioles are absent except in the motile sperm of bryophytes, pteridophytes, cycads and *Ginkgo*. Cell division builds a cell plate with a phragmoplast, separating the daughter cells. Specialized cells differentiate from [[Meristem|meristematic cells]], which are analogous to animal stem cells.

=== KEY: Plant_hormone_aa5c1a
HEADWORD: Plant hormone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Carbohydrate | Ethylene | Peptide | Vascular tissue
ENTRY:
Signal molecule produced within plants in very low concentrations. Plant hormones control embryogenesis, organ size, defense against pathogens, stress tolerance and reproductive development. Unlike animals, in which specialized glands produce hormones, each plant cell can produce them. The five classical classes are auxins, cytokinins, gibberellins, abscisic acid and [[Ethylene|ethylene]]. Only some cells respond, and they do so at specific points in their growth cycle. Hormones move by cytoplasmic streaming, diffusion and [[Vascular tissue|vascular tissue]], through which sugars travel from leaves to roots and flowers and water and minerals move from roots to foliage. Plants regulate hormone levels by storing or breaking them down, or by binding them to [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]], [[Amino acid|amino acids]] or [[Peptide|peptides]]. Natural and synthetic hormones are used in agriculture as plant growth regulators. Some microorganisms produce similar compounds, though these are better regarded as secondary metabolites.

=== KEY: Plant_morphology_2e1594
HEADWORD: Plant morphology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternation of generations | Cactus | Plant anatomy
ENTRY:
Study of the physical form and external structure of plants. It is usually distinguished from [[Plant anatomy|plant anatomy]], which examines internal structure at the microscopic level, and it is useful for identifying plants visually. Morphologists compare structures across species. Structures that arise from common inherited pathways are homologous; for example, [[Cactus|cactus]] spines are homologous to leaves. The field studies vegetative structures, such as stems, leaves and roots, and reproductive ones, such as flowers, seeds, fern sori and moss capsules. Study of reproductive structures revealed the [[Alternation of generations|alternation of generations]] found in all plants and most algae. The field examines structure from ultrastructure to overall growth habit, and it studies how structures develop. Molecular studies have found transcriptome conservation patterns that mark key transitions in the plant life cycle. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's *Metamorphosis of Plants* proposed an archetypal plant form.

=== KEY: Plant_reproduction_c2e3b7
HEADWORD: Plant reproduction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asexual reproduction | Mutation | Pathogen | Sexual reproduction
ENTRY:
Production of offspring by sexual or asexual means. [[Sexual reproduction|Sexual reproduction]] fuses gametes to produce offspring genetically different from either parent. Vegetative reproduction produces new individuals without gamete fusion, and these clones are genetically identical unless [[Mutation|mutations]] occur. In [[Asexual reproduction|asexual reproduction]] only one parent is involved, and the offspring inherit its characteristics. Asexual methods include budding, fragmentation, spore formation, regeneration and vegetative propagation. Perennial plants often reproduce vegetatively through modified stems or roots, forming clonal colonies that persist and expand. A disadvantage is that [[Pathogen|pathogens]] can pass from parent to offspring. Apomixis, the formation of seeds without fertilization, occurs in dandelions (*Taraxacum*), hawkweeds (*Hieracium*), some *Citrus* species and Kentucky bluegrass (*Poa pratensis*). Rhizomes and runners, or stolons, are important organs of vegetative reproduction.

=== KEY: Plant_reproductive_morphology_b1151f
HEADWORD: Plant reproductive morphology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Flowering plant | Gametophyte | Pollen | Sporophyte
ENTRY:
Study of the form and structure of plant parts involved in sexual reproduction. Among living organisms, flowers, the reproductive structures of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]], are the most physically varied. Non-flowering plants, including green algae, mosses, liverworts, hornworts, ferns and conifers, also have complex reproductive adaptations. The breeding system, which determines how sperm from one plant fertilizes the ovum of another, is the single most important determinant of genetic structure in nonclonal plant populations. Christian Konrad Sprengel studied pollination in 1793, and [[Charles Darwin]] used this work to build his theory of evolution. Plants alternate between a [[Sporophyte|sporophyte]] and a [[Gametophyte|gametophyte]] generation. In bryophytes the gametophyte dominates, while in ferns and seed plants the sporophyte dominates. Flowers are built from sepals and petals, stamens that produce [[Pollen|pollen]], and carpels bearing ovules.

=== KEY: Plant_stem_a65308
HEADWORD: Plant stem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cactus | Onion | Taro
ENTRY:
One of two main structural axes of a vascular plant, the other being the root. The stem supports leaves, flowers and fruits, and transports water and dissolved substances between the roots and shoots. It also carries out photosynthesis, stores nutrients and produces new living tissue. Leaves attach at nodes, which may bear axillary buds that grow into branches, and the segments between nodes are internodes. Stems are usually aerial, though some are underground. Xylem moves water by transpiration pull, capillary action and root pressure, while phloem distributes food from photosynthetic tissue. Cambium lies between them. Stems are often specialized for storage or protection, including [[Onion|onion]] bulbs, [[Taro|taro]] corms and the flattened cladodes of [[Cactus|cactus]] pads.

=== KEY: Plasma_physics_2d9c20
HEADWORD: Plasma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood plasma | Humphry Davy | Ionization | Irving Langmuir | Lightning | Michael Faraday | Star
ENTRY:
State of matter produced when one of the other three classical states, often a gas, undergoes an appreciable degree of [[Ionization|ionization]], so that it contains a significant portion of charged particles, ions or electrons or both. It is called the fourth state of matter after solid, liquid, and gas. While rarely encountered on Earth, an estimated 99.9% of all ordinary matter in the universe is plasma. [[Star|Stars]] are almost pure balls of plasma, and plasma dominates the intracluster and intergalactic medium.

The charged particles make plasma electrically conductive, and the motion of the whole plasma is governed by collective electromagnetic fields and is very sensitive to applied fields. Plasma is typically quasineutral: its overall charge is roughly zero, although its particles are not free of forces. Some neutral particles may be present, in which case the plasma is partially ionized, as in neon signs and [[Lightning|lightning]].

The electric arc was discovered independently by Vasily Petrov and [[Humphry Davy]] in 1803, and in 1831 [[Michael Faraday]] systematically investigated electric glow discharge in rarefied gases. [[Irving Langmuir]] introduced the term plasma for ionized gas in 1928. According to Lewi Tonks and Harold Mott-Smith, who worked with him, Langmuir chose it by analogy with [[Blood plasma|blood plasma]], which carries red and white corpuscles.

Natural plasmas include lightning, the ionosphere, solar wind, and auroras, and artificial ones include fluorescent lights and plasma globes. The response of plasma to electromagnetic fields is used in plasma televisions, plasma etching, semiconductor fabrication, spacecraft propulsion, and the development of fusion energy, which requires plasmas heated to over 100 million degrees Celsius in devices such as tokamaks.

=== KEY: Plasmid_63aa32
HEADWORD: Plasmid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antibiotic | Archaea | Bacteria | Cloning | Insulin | Virus
ENTRY:
Small, usually circular DNA molecule that replicates independently of chromosomal DNA. Plasmids are most commonly found in [[Bacteria|bacteria]] and [[Archaea|archaea]], though some occur in eukaryotes. They often carry genes for [[Antibiotic|antibiotic]] resistance, virulence, secondary metabolism and bioremediation, and they are usually much smaller than chromosomes. Artificial plasmids are widely used as vectors in [[Cloning|molecular cloning]] and are introduced into cells by transformation. Plasmids pass between bacteria mostly by conjugation, a route of horizontal gene transfer. Unlike [[Virus|viruses]], plasmids lack a protein capsid. They range from 1 to over 400 kbp in size, and a cell may hold from one to thousands of copies. Joshua Lederberg coined the term in 1952. In 1973 recombinant plasmids were constructed with restriction enzymes such as EcoRI, enabling the production of [[Insulin|insulin]].

=== KEY: Plastic_1c2301
HEADWORD: Plastic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bakelite | Landfill | Leo Baekeland | Natural gas | Petroleum | Polyethylene | Polymer | Polyvinyl chloride
ENTRY:
Wide range of synthetic or semisynthetic materials composed primarily of [[Polymer|polymers]]. Their defining characteristic, plasticity, allows them to be molded, extruded, or pressed into a diverse range of solid forms. This adaptability, combined with low weight, durability, flexibility, chemical resistance, low toxicity, and low-cost production, has led to widespread use. The word derives from the Ancient Greek *plastikos*, "capable of being shaped or molded." Most plastics are produced from [[Natural gas|natural gas]] and [[Petroleum|petroleum]], while a growing minority come from renewable resources such as polylactic acid.

The first fully synthetic plastic was [[Bakelite]], invented in New York in 1907 by [[Leo Baekeland]], who coined the term plastics. Dozens of types are now produced, such as [[Polyethylene|polyethylene]], widely used in packaging, and [[Polyvinyl chloride]] (PVC), used in construction and pipes for its strength and durability.

An estimated 9.2 billion metric tons of plastic were made between 1950 and 2017, more than half of it since 2004. Preliminary figures indicate that over 400 million metric tons were produced worldwide in 2023 alone, and annual production is projected to exceed 1.3 billion tons by 2060 if demand trends continue. Packaging accounts for about 40% of use and building and construction for about 20%.

Plastics have brought major benefits, from medical devices to lightweight construction materials, and many countries' sewage systems rely on PVC. They are also the basis of widespread environmental concern because of their slow decomposition in natural ecosystems. Most plastic produced has not been reused. Much ends up in [[Landfill|landfills]] or as pollution, including microplastics and marine garbage patches. Of all plastic discarded so far, about 14% has been incinerated and less than 10% recycled.

=== KEY: Plastic_surgery_efff7e
HEADWORD: Plastic surgery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Body dysmorphic disorder | Bologna | World War I
ENTRY:
Surgical specialty concerned with restoring, reconstructing or altering the body. It is divided into reconstructive surgery, which restores or improves the function of body parts, and cosmetic surgery, which aims solely to improve appearance. It overlaps with nearly all other surgical specialties. The word *plastic* derives from the Greek *plastikē tekhnē*, "the art of modeling." The Edwin Smith papyrus (c. 1600 BC) describes treatment of a broken nose, and the *Sushruta Samhita* described nasal reconstruction using skin flaps. === KEY: Plasticity_physics_8e259f
HEADWORD: Plasticity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Concrete | Foam | Metal | Rock (geology)
ENTRY:
Permanent deformation of a solid material in response to applied forces. In engineering, the transition from elastic to plastic behavior is called yielding. Plastic deformation occurs in most materials, including [[Metal|metals]], soils, [[Rock (geology)|rocks]], [[Concrete|concrete]] and [[Foam|foams]], but its physical causes vary. In metals it results mainly from dislocations, and a pure metal deforms by slip or twinning. In brittle materials such as rock, concrete and bone, it is caused mainly by slip at microcracks, while in foams and biological tissues it arises from rearrangements of bubbles or cells. Most metals are more plastic when hot than when cold. Plasticity is directly proportional to the ductility and malleability of a material. Materials hardened by prior deformation, such as by cold forming, need higher stresses to deform further. The von Mises yield criterion predicts yielding in metals.

=== KEY: Plastid_a5a0be
HEADWORD: Plastid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chloroplast | Fat | Meristem | Photosynthesis | Starch
ENTRY:
Membrane-bound organelle found in the cells of plants, algae and some other eukaryotes. Plastids are considered to derive from endosymbiotic cyanobacteria. Types include [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]], which perform [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]; chromoplasts, which synthesize and store pigments; and colorless leucoplasts. A primary endosymbiosis about 1.5 billion years ago gave rise to plastids in the Archaeplastida, including land plants, red algae, green algae and glaucophytes. In land plants, plastids derive from proplastids in [[Meristem|meristematic]] regions and can differentiate into forms such as amyloplasts, which store [[Starch|starch]] and help detect gravity, and elaioplasts, which store [[Fat|fats]]. Each plastid has its own genome, or plastome.

=== KEY: Plate_armour_a66a98

Historical type of personal body armor made of bronze, iron or steel plates. === KEY: Plate_tectonics_4cc4f3
HEADWORD: Plate tectonics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Wegener | Convection | Earthquake | Europa (moon) | Hotspot (geology) | Lithosphere | Mid-Atlantic Ridge | Oceanic trench
ENTRY:
Scientific theory of Earth's [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]] as divided into large tectonic plates that have been slowly moving since 3–4 billion years ago. It grew out of continental drift, proposed by [[Alfred Wegener]] in 1912, and was accepted after seafloor spreading was validated in the mid- to late 1960s.

The lithosphere, the rigid outer shell including the crust and upper mantle, is fractured into seven or eight major plates and many minor ones, riding on the ductile asthenosphere beneath. Relative motion defines three boundary types: convergent, divergent, and transform. Plates move from 10 to 40 mm a year at the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]] to about 160 mm a year for the Nazca plate. Boundaries are geologically active, with [[Earthquake|earthquakes]], volcanic activity, mountain-building, and [[Oceanic trench|oceanic trench]] formation; about 90% of earthquakes occur there. [[Hotspot (geology)|Hotspot]] volcanism produces volcanoes far from boundaries.

At convergent boundaries, subduction carries one plate's edge down under another into the mantle, reducing Earth's surface area. New oceanic crust formed at divergent margins by seafloor spreading balances the loss in a tectonic "conveyor belt." Density variations in the mantle produce [[Convection|convection]] currents, and the sinking of cold, dense oceanic crust at subduction zones is the strongest driver of plate motion; other proposed factors, such as tidal drag of the Moon, are debated.

Earth is the only planet known to have active plate tectonics. Jupiter's moon [[Europa (moon)|Europa]] shows signs of moving ice plates, and Mars and Venus are thought to have had tectonic activity in the past.

=== KEY: Plateau_5cf2a0
HEADWORD: Plateau
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Appalachian Mountains | Deccan Plateau | Erosion | Glacier | Lava | Tibetan Plateau
ENTRY:
Area of highland with flat terrain, raised sharply above the surrounding land on at least one side. Also called a high plain or tableland, a plateau often has steep escarpments or hills on one or more sides. Plateaus form through volcanic magma upwelling, [[Lava|lava]] extrusion, tectonic uplift, and [[Erosion|erosion]] by water and [[Glacier|glaciers]]. Lava plateaus include the [[Deccan Plateau|Deccan Plateau]] in India and the Columbia Plateau in the United States. Plateaus are classed as intermontane, piedmont or continental. The [[Tibetan Plateau|Tibetan Plateau]], the highest and largest in the world, averages over 4,500 meters in elevation, and the Antarctic Plateau covers most of East Antarctica. The Piedmont Plateau lies between the [[Appalachian Mountains|Appalachian Mountains]] and the Atlantic Plain. The Ethiopian Highlands, the highest African plateau, have little of their surface below 1,500 meters (4,921 ft).

=== KEY: Platinum_bf381e

Dense, malleable, precious [[Transition metal|transition metal]] with symbol Pt and atomic number 78. Its name derives from Spanish *platina*, a diminutive of *plata*, meaning silver. === KEY: Plato_3aa3cd
HEADWORD: Plato
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alfred North Whitehead | Aristotle | Classical Athens | Peloponnesian War | Republic (Plato) | Socrates | Solon | Theory of forms
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek philosopher of [[Classical Athens]], most commonly considered the foundational thinker of the Western philosophical tradition. Born into an aristocratic Athenian family, he descended through his mother from [[Solon]], credited with laying the foundations of Athenian democracy. He grew up during the [[Peloponnesian War]] and, according to ancient writers, wrote poetry until he met [[Socrates]] and turned to philosophy.

Socrates became his teacher and greatest source of inspiration. Plato never speaks in his own voice in his dialogues, and every dialogue except the *Laws* features Socrates; how far the Socrates of the dialogues reflects the historical man remains contested among scholars. The prosecution of Socrates by Anytus ended Plato's plans for a political career. He was influenced by Pythagoras, Heraclitus, and Parmenides.

Plato was an innovator of the literary dialogue and dialectic forms and influenced all the major areas of theoretical and practical philosophy. He founded the Academy, a school in Athens, around 387 BC, where he taught the body of philosophical theories later known as Platonism; his students included [[Aristotle]]. His most famous contribution is the [[Theory of forms|Theory of Forms]], which posits immutable ideal archetypes beyond the sensible world and aims to solve the problem of universals. In the [[Republic (Plato)|*Republic*]] he outlined an ideal state ruled by philosopher-kings; admirers have praised its rationalism, while critics have pointed to its censorship of the arts and rigid class structure as potentially authoritarian.

 His complete works are believed to have survived, unlike those of nearly all his contemporaries. [[Alfred North Whitehead]] said that "the safest general characterization of the European philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato."

Any of the five convex regular polyhedra in three-dimensional space. === KEY: Platypus_4bec60

Semiaquatic, egg-laying mammal of eastern [[Australia|Australia]], including [[Tasmania|Tasmania]]. The platypus (*Ornithorhynchus anatinus*) is the sole living member of its family and genus. === KEY: Play_activity_f97be6
HEADWORD: Play
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bird | Game | Jean Piaget | Mammal | Playground | Rubik's Cube | Sport
ENTRY:
Range of intrinsically motivated activities done for recreation. Play is commonly associated with children but may occur at any life stage and among higher-functioning animals, most notably [[Mammal|mammals]] and [[Bird|birds]]. Though often seen as frivolous, it can be intensely focused, particularly when structured and goal-oriented, as in a [[Game|game]]; it ranges from spontaneous to planned or even compulsive.

Johan Huizinga's *Homo Ludens* (1944), the seminal text of play studies, defined play as a free activity standing consciously outside "ordinary" life, not serious yet absorbing the player intensely, connected with no material interest, and proceeding within its own boundaries of time and space according to fixed rules. This "magic circle" notion treats play as a separate sphere of human activity. [[Jean Piaget]] remarked that the many theories of play prove the phenomenon difficult to understand. The National Playing Fields Association defines it as freely chosen, personally directed, intrinsically motivated behavior that actively engages the child.

Play takes the forms of improvisation, pretense, mimicry, games, [[Sport|sports]], and thrill-seeking. Roger Caillois, in *Man, Play and Games* (1961), interpreted many social structures as elaborate forms of games. Free-form play lets children change the rules at will; structured play has defined goals and rules. Designed spaces include sports fields and [[Playground|playgrounds]]. The National Institute for Play describes seven patterns, including attunement, body, creative, object, social, and storytelling play. Another scheme distinguishes challenge play, such as solving a [[Rubik's Cube]], from competitive, construction, cooperative, nurturing, and replica play.

Evolutionary accounts hold that play rehearses skills such as motor coordination and conflict resolution. Researchers report that unstructured play improves impulse control, language, and empathy in children.

Inflammatory condition of the [[Lung|lung]] primarily affecting the alveoli, the small air sacs. Symptoms typically include some combination of productive or dry cough, chest pain, fever, and difficulty breathing; in elderly people confusion may be the most prominent sign. Severity varies.

It is usually caused by infection with viruses or bacteria, and less commonly by fungi and parasites. Common causes include *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, [[Influenza|influenza]] viruses, and [[Respiratory syncytial virus]]; in about half of cases no causative agent is isolated. It is classified by where it was acquired: community-acquired, hospital-acquired, or healthcare-associated. Diagnosis is often based on symptoms and physical examination; chest X-rays, blood tests, and sputum culture may help confirm it. Treatment depends on the cause. Prevention includes [[Vaccine|vaccines]] against certain causes, hand washing, and not smoking.

Each year pneumonia affects about 450 million people globally (7% of the population) and causes about 4 million deaths. Survival has greatly improved since antibiotics and vaccines were introduced in the 20th century, but the disease remains a leading cause of death in developing countries and among the very old, the very young, and the chronically ill.

=== KEY: Poetry_f8ec59
HEADWORD: Poetry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeneid | Aristotle | Epic of Gilgamesh | Iliad | Mahabharata | Odyssey | Ramayana | Sumer
ENTRY:
Form of literary art that uses aesthetic and often rhythmic qualities of language to evoke meanings in addition to, or in place of, literal ones. The word comes from the Greek *poiesis*, "making." Poets use devices such as assonance, alliteration, onomatopoeia, rhythm (via meter), and rhyme schemes to produce musical or other artistic effects, and organize them into structures that may be strict or loose, conventional or invented. Most written poems are set in verse, a series of lines that follow the poetic structure.

Not all traditions depend on rhyme and regular meter: Biblical poetry and alliterative verse use other means, and Somali poetry relies on complex systems of alliteration and meter independent of writing. Much modern poetry forgoes rhyme or set rhythm. Ambiguity, symbolism, and figures of speech such as metaphor and metonymy often leave a poem open to multiple interpretations.

Some scholars believe poetry predates literacy and developed from folk epics and oral genres; others suggest it did not necessarily predate writing. The oldest surviving epic poem, the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]], dates from the 3rd millennium BC in [[Sumer]]. The Egyptian *Story of Sinuhe* dates to c. 1800 BC, and the Pyramid Texts of the 25th century BC are among the earliest written poetry in Africa. Other early poems include the Chinese *Shijing*, the Sanskrit *Rigveda*, the Hebrew Psalms, the [[Iliad]] and the [[Odyssey]], Virgil's [[Aeneid]], and the Indian [[Ramayana]] and [[Mahabharata]]. [[Aristotle]]'s *Poetics* analyzed poetry within the uses of speech in rhetoric, drama, song, and comedy.

=== KEY: Poincar_conjecture_09a72b
HEADWORD: Poincaré conjecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fundamental group | Henri Poincaré | Manifold | Millennium Prize Problems | Science (journal)
ENTRY:
Theorem in geometric topology characterizing the three-dimensional sphere. [[Henri Poincaré]] conjectured it in 1904. It concerns closed, connected three-dimensional [[Manifold|manifolds]] with trivial [[Fundamental group|fundamental groups]], meaning every loop can be contracted to a point, and asserts that such a space is homeomorphic to a three-dimensional sphere. Attempts to resolve it drove much progress in geometric topology during the 20th century. Grigori Perelman completed a program built on Richard S. Hamilton's use of Ricci flow, posting papers on arXiv in 2002 and 2003. The journal [[Science (journal)|Science]] named his proof the 2006 Breakthrough of the Year. The Clay Mathematics Institute included the conjecture among its [[Millennium Prize Problems]] and offered Perelman a US$1 million prize in 2010, which he declined, saying Hamilton's contribution was equal to his own.

=== KEY: Poinsettia_f9bcf3
HEADWORD: Poinsettia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztecs | Christmas | Euphorbiaceae
ENTRY:
Species of flowering plant in the [[Euphorbiaceae|spurge family]], native to Mexico and Central America. Its showy red bracts, which are leaves rather than petals, are widely used in [[Christmas]] floral displays. Europeans first described the plant in 1834, when German scientist Johann Friedrich Klotzsch named it *Euphorbia pulcherrima*. Its common name honors Joel Roberts Poinsett, the first U.S. minister to Mexico, who introduced it to the United States in the 1820s. The [[Aztecs]] cultivated it for use in traditional medicine. Though often described as highly toxic, it is not dangerous to pets or children. Approximately 70 million poinsettias are sold in the United States each year during a six-week period, and Paul Ecke Ranch, which serves half the worldwide market, grows many of them.

=== KEY: Point_geometry_a9f8ff
HEADWORD: Point
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Analytic geometry | Axiom | Euclid | Euclidean plane
ENTRY:
Fundamental object of geometry, an abstract idealization of an exact position without size. Points are zero-dimensional, and curves, surfaces and higher-dimensional objects are built from them. [[Euclid]] defined the point in classical Euclidean geometry as "that which has no part." Modern treatments take the point as a primitive notion, characterized by [[Axiom|axioms]] such as the rule that exactly one straight line passes through two distinct points. A point can be specified by coordinates, an ordered pair (x, y) in the [[Euclidean plane]] or an ordered triple (x, y, z) in three-dimensional space. Since the advent of [[Analytic geometry]], points are often represented numerically, and a space of points is typically treated as a set.

=== KEY: Poison_3a9d18
HEADWORD: Poison
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gastrointestinal tract | Paracelsus | Self-harm | Suicide | Toxicology
ENTRY:
Chemical substance that is harmful or lethal to a living organism. Poisoning, the harmful effect of exposure to a toxic substance, typically occurs through ingestion, inhalation, injection or skin absorption. Its symptoms can mimic other medical conditions and include altered consciousness, abnormal body temperature, irregular heart rate and changes in respiration. Caustic substances can directly injure the mouth, throat, [[Gastrointestinal tract|gastrointestinal tract]] and lungs. Poisoning is sometimes used as a method of [[Self-harm|self-harm]] and [[Suicide|suicide]]. A study in the *Journal of Pediatrics* found that suicide attempts by poisoning among people under 19 doubled between 2000 and 2018, from nearly 40,000 cases to almost 80,000. The 16th-century physician [[Paracelsus]], regarded as the father of [[Toxicology|toxicology]], wrote that "only the dose makes a thing not a poison."

=== KEY: Poison_dart_frog_d65fc2

ENTRY:
Family of small, brightly colored [[Frog|frogs]] native to tropical Central and South America. Their bright coloration warns predators of their toxicity, a strategy called aposematism. Toxicity comes from [[Alkaloid|alkaloids]] obtained from a diet of [[Ant|ants]], [[Mite|mites]] and [[Termite|termites]]; species that eat a wider range of prey are cryptically colored with little or no toxicity. Of over 170 species, only four are documented as having been used to poison blowdart tips, all from the genus Phyllobates. Many species are threatened as human development encroaches on their habitats.

=== KEY: Poisson_distribution_fdf8e6
HEADWORD: Poisson distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: E (mathematical constant) | Expected value | Prussia | Radioactive decay | Siméon Denis Poisson | Variance
ENTRY:
Discrete probability distribution for the number of events in a fixed interval. It applies when events occur at a known constant mean rate and independently of the time since the last event. The single parameter λ is both the [[Expected value|expected value]] and the [[Variance|variance]] of the distribution. The probability of k events is λᵏe⁻λ/k!, where e is [[E (mathematical constant)|Euler's number]]. If a call center receives an average of 3 calls per minute, the probability of 1 to 4 calls in a given minute is about 0.77, while the probability of 0 or at least 5 calls is about 0.23. The distribution is named after [[Siméon Denis Poisson]], who published it in 1837. It describes phenomena such as [[Radioactive decay|radioactive decay]] events and the accidental deaths of soldiers in the [[Prussia|Prussian]] army from horse kicks, which Ladislaus Bortkiewicz studied in 1898.

=== KEY: Poker_cea3a4
HEADWORD: Poker
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Card game | Expected value | Game theory | Probability | Psychology | Texas hold 'em
ENTRY:
Family of [[Card game|card games]] in which players wager on which hand is best under each game's rules. Games vary in deck size and in how many cards are dealt and shared, but all involve one or more rounds of betting. Most begin with a forced bet called the blind or ante. Players match, raise or fold, and when more than one remains after the final round, the hands are revealed. Money enters the pot voluntarily only when a player believes a bet has positive [[Expected value|expected value]] or is bluffing, so long-run results depend on [[Probability]], [[Psychology]] and [[Game theory]] as well as chance. Possible early inspirations include the French game poque and the Iranian game As-Nas, though this origin is debated. Poker was popularized in the American South in the early 19th century, aided by riverboats on the Mississippi during the 1830s. Televising the game helped produce a poker boom between 2003 and 2006. [[Texas hold 'em]] and other community card games later dominated gambling.

=== KEY: Poland_94880b
HEADWORD: Poland
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baltic Sea | European Union | Invasion of Poland | Mieszko I | NATO | Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth | The Holocaust | Warsaw
ENTRY:
Country in Central Europe, with over 37 million people; capital and largest city [[Warsaw]]. Officially the Republic of Poland, it extends from the [[Baltic Sea]] in the north to the Sudetes and Carpathian Mountains in the south and covers 312,696 sq km. It borders Lithuania and Russia to the northeast, Belarus and Ukraine to the east, Slovakia and the Czech Republic to the south, and Germany to the west. It has sixteen voivodeships; other major cities include Kraków, Wrocław, Łódź, Poznań, Gdańsk, and Szczecin.

The West Slavic Polans gave the country its name. A Polan ruler, [[Mieszko I]], converted to Christianity in 966, and the Kingdom of Poland emerged in 1025. In 1569 the union with Lithuania formed the [[Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth]], a great power with an elective monarchy that adopted Europe's first modern constitution in 1791. Russia, Prussia, and Austria partitioned the country at the end of the 18th century, and independence returned in 1918.

In September 1939 the [[Invasion of Poland|invasion]] by Germany and the Soviet Union began World War II, which brought [[The Holocaust|the Holocaust]] and millions of Polish casualties. Poland then entered the Eastern Bloc and signed the Warsaw Pact. The Solidarity movement, which emerged in 1980, helped initiate the fall of the Iron Curtain; the communist government was dissolved and free elections were held in 1989.

Poland is a republic with a bicameral legislature of the Sejm and the Senate. It is a high-income developed country, the 21st largest economy by nominal GDP and the sixth in the European Union. It has 18 UNESCO World Heritage Sites and 23 national parks. A founding member of the United Nations, it joined [[NATO]] in 1999 and the [[European Union]] in 2004.
