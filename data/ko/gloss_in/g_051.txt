=== KEY: Gazelle_98993e
HEADWORD: Gazelle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antelope | Cheetah | Hyena | Indian subcontinent | Lion | Miocene | Savanna
ENTRY:
Any of many [[Antelope|antelope]] species, most in the genus *Gazella*. Gazelles live in herds in the deserts, grasslands and [[Savanna|savannas]] of Africa, and also in southwest and central Asia and the [[Indian subcontinent]]. They are slender and fawn-colored, most standing 60–110 cm high at the shoulder. Some run in bursts of up to 100 km/h (60 mph) and sustain 50 km/h (30 mph). They eat fine, easily digested plants and leaves. Taxonomy is unsettled: *Gazella* is considered to include about 10 species, with two related genera, *Eudorcas* and *Nanger*. One species, the Queen of Sheba's gazelle, is extinct, and most surviving species are threatened to varying degrees. When threatened by predators such as [[Cheetah|cheetahs]], [[Lion|lions]] and [[Hyena|hyenas]], Thomson's gazelle performs stotting, running and jumping high before fleeing. Fossils of *Gazella* date to the [[Miocene]], around 14 million years ago.

=== KEY: Gear_fb8457
HEADWORD: Gear
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antikythera mechanism | Archimedes | Aristotle | Eclipse | Torque | Warring States period
ENTRY:
Rotating toothed machine part that transmits [[Torque|torque]] or rotational motion by meshing with another toothed part. Two or more meshing gears form a gear train. When a small gear drives a larger one, torque increases while rotational speed falls; the reverse happens when a large gear drives a small one, in proportion to the ratio of their tooth counts. Most vehicles use a gearbox to vary the torque applied to the wheels without changing engine speed. Gears range from a few μm in micromachines to over 10 m in mining equipment. The earliest surviving gears date to the 4th century BC in China, during the [[Warring States period]]. [[Aristotle]] described gears around 330 BC, and [[Archimedes]] greatly developed them. The [[Antikythera mechanism]], which predicted astronomical positions and [[Eclipse|eclipses]], contains the earliest surviving European gears, estimated to date from between 150 and 100 BC.

=== KEY: Gecko_d21fd7
HEADWORD: Gecko
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Hispaniola | Lizard | Marseille | Mosquito | Moth | New Caledonia
ENTRY:
Small, mostly carnivorous [[Lizard|lizard]] of the suborder Gekkota, found on every continent except [[Antarctica]]. Geckos are the most species-rich group of lizards, with nearly 2,000 species, and range from 1.6 to 67 cm long. Most are nocturnal, and their low-light color vision is 350 times more sensitive than human eyes. Geckos other than the Eublepharidae lack eyelids; instead, a transparent membrane covers each eye, and they lick it to keep it clean. Many species have specialized toe pads that let them climb smooth vertical surfaces and cross ceilings. House geckos feed on insect pests, including [[Moth|moths]] and [[Mosquito|mosquitoes]]. The largest species, *Gigarcanum delcourti*, which was 60 cm long, is known from a single stuffed specimen in [[Marseille]], France, and was likely endemic to [[New Caledonia]]. The smallest, the Jaragua sphaero, is 16 mm long and was discovered in 2001 off [[Hispaniola]].

=== KEY: Gel_123271

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Semi-solid material, mostly liquid by mass, with a three-dimensional cross-linked network. Gels are defined as dilute cross-linked systems that show no flow in the steady state, though the liquid can still diffuse through them. The network gives a gel its structure, hardness and tack. Gels range from soft and weak to hard and tough. Hydrogels are [[Polymer|polymer]] networks that use water as the liquid, can contain over 90 percent water and are highly absorbent. === KEY: Gel_electrophoresis_57f3fb
HEADWORD: Gel electrophoresis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biomolecule | Carbohydrate | DNA | Electric field | Polymerase chain reaction | Protein | RNA | Seaweed
ENTRY:
Laboratory method for separating [[Biomolecule|biomolecules]] such as [[DNA]], [[RNA]] and [[Protein|proteins]] by size and charge. Molecules migrate through a porous gel matrix, usually agarose or polyacrylamide, under an [[Electric field]]. The gel acts as a sieve: shorter molecules move faster and farther. Proteins are separated mainly by charge in agarose, whose pores are too large to sieve them. Agarose, a [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrate]] from [[Seaweed|seaweed]], suits large DNA fragments, while polyacrylamide gives finer resolution for small DNA, RNA and proteins. DNA gel electrophoresis is usually analytical, often after [[Polymerase chain reaction|PCR]] amplification, but can also prepare samples for mass spectrometry or cloning. Stains such as ethidium bromide make the separated bands visible under ultraviolet light.

=== KEY: Gender_019ec3
HEADWORD: Gender
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Feminism | Intersex | Latin | Middle English | Non-binary | South Asia | Transgender
ENTRY:
Range of social, psychological, cultural, and behavioral aspects of being a man (or boy), woman (or girl), or portraying a third gender. Gender often corresponds to sex, though a [[Transgender|transgender]] person may identify with a gender other than the sex assigned at birth. Most cultures have a gender binary; those outside it may fall under the umbrella term [[Non-binary|non-binary]]. Some societies have third genders, such as the hijras of [[South Asia]] and two-spirit persons native to North America.

The English word comes from [[Middle English]] *gender*, from Anglo-Norman and Middle French *gendre*, from [[Latin]] *genus*, meaning "kind", "type", or "sort". Before the mid-20th century it was uncommon to use it for anything but grammatical categories. A distinction between biological sex and gender then developed in psychology, sociology, sexology, and [[Feminism|feminism]], and in the 1970s feminist scholars adopted it to separate "socially constructed" aspects of male–female differences from "biologically determined" ones. Most contemporary social scientists in Western countries, many biologists, many legal systems, and the WHO make this distinction.

The subject is contested. Critics of the sex-gender distinction argue that it obscures the biological reality of sex, which they define by the two gamete types, and that policies prioritizing gender identity disadvantage women in athletics. Supporters of gender-identity-inclusive policies respond that the distinction reflects real differences between bodily traits and social roles or identity, and that inclusion can be balanced with fairness through eligibility rules. Reported prevalence of gender dysphoria has risen among youth since the early 2010s; proposed explanations include greater social acceptance, peer and online influence, and changes in clinical practice, and their relative weight is disputed. The social sciences often treat gender as a social construct, while the natural sciences investigate how far biology shapes it. Estimates of how common [[Intersex|intersex]] conditions are range from about 0.018% of births to about 1.7%, depending on definition.

=== KEY: Gene_624aa1
HEADWORD: Gene
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Gene expression | Genetic drift | Human genome | Mutation | Natural selection | RNA | Richard Dawkins
ENTRY:
Basic unit of heredity in biology, with two meanings. The Mendelian gene is the classical unit of heredity and refers to any heritable trait; in *The Selfish Gene*, [[Richard Dawkins]] argues that it is the unit of evolution. The molecular gene, the sense more common in biochemistry and molecular biology, is a sequence of nucleotides in [[DNA]] that is transcribed to produce [[RNA]]. There are two types: protein-coding genes and non-coding genes. During [[Gene expression|gene expression]], DNA is first copied into RNA, which can be directly functional or serve as the template for synthesis of a protein.

Genes and their regulatory sequences are mainly responsible for determining an organism's physical traits, or phenotype. A gene can acquire [[Mutation|mutations]], producing variants known as alleles that may be expressed as different traits. Populations evolve when allele frequencies shift, mostly through [[Natural selection|natural selection]] and [[Genetic drift|genetic drift]].

Early work in molecular genetics suggested that one gene makes one protein. Genes producing repressor RNAs were proposed in the 1950s, and by the 1960s textbooks included noncoding genes for functional RNAs such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. Most definitions treat a gene as a transcription unit and regard regulatory sequences as controlling expression without being part of the gene; Kostas Kampourakis emphasizes that a gene must encode a functional product, so transcribed pseudogenes and transcription noise do not qualify. Some publications still restrict the term to protein-coding genes.

The discovery of introns in the 1970s showed that many eukaryotic genes are much larger than their functional products imply. Typical mammalian protein-coding genes are about 62,000 base pairs long in the transcribed region, and since there are about 20,000 of them they occupy about 35–40% of the mammalian genome. In the [[Human genome|human genome]], the approximately 19,000 to 20,000 protein-coding genes make up about 1.5% of the total DNA sequence.

=== KEY: Gene_expression_fae894
HEADWORD: Gene expression
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Eukaryote | Gene | Protein | RNA | Transcription (biology) | Translation (biology)
ENTRY:
Process by which the information in a [[Gene|gene]] is used to produce a functional product, such as a [[Protein|protein]] or a functional [[RNA]] molecule. [[Transcription (biology)|Transcription]] copies the gene's sequence into RNA, which in [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] is performed in the nucleus by three types of RNA polymerase. Protein-coding genes yield messenger RNA that is [[Translation (biology)|translated]] into amino acid chains that fold into proteins, while non-coding genes produce RNA that itself performs a cellular role. Expression is regulated by transcription factors and epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation, and it responds to cellular needs and environmental change, although some genes are expressed continuously. Dysregulated expression underlies diseases including [[Cancer|cancers]] and genetic disorders such as cystic fibrosis.

=== KEY: Genealogy_638f3a
HEADWORD: Genealogy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adoption | DNA | Divorce | Family | Kinship | Lineage (anthropology)
ENTRY:
Study of [[Family|families]], family history and the tracing of [[Lineage (anthropology)|lineages]]. Genealogists use oral interviews, historical records, genetic analysis and other sources to demonstrate [[Kinship|kinship]] and pedigrees, presenting results as charts or narratives. In the narrow sense, a genealogy or family tree traces the descendants of one person, while a family history traces that person's ancestors, although the terms are often used interchangeably. Family history is broader, adding biography, traditions and community history. Amateurs usually research their own ancestry, while professionals may research for others, publish methods books or produce databases. Motives include curiosity about heritage, especially where records were lost through [[Adoption|adoption]] or [[Divorce|divorce]], scholarly or forensic work, and tracing legal next of kin under intestacy laws. Autosomal [[DNA]] analysis now confirms documentary links and identifies unknown relatives through shared genomic segments.

=== KEY: General_relativity_fd1f68
HEADWORD: General relativity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Big Bang | Black hole | Einstein field equations | Isaac Newton | Quantum gravity | Spacetime | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Geometric theory of gravitation published by [[Albert Einstein]] in May 1916. It generalizes [[Special relativity|special relativity]] and refines the law of universal gravitation of [[Isaac Newton]]. It describes gravity as a geometric property of four-dimensional [[Spacetime|spacetime]], in which the curvature of spacetime is related to the energy, momentum and stress of matter and radiation through the [[Einstein field equations]]. Newton's law is a prediction of the theory for the almost flat spacetime around stationary masses. Predictions beyond Newtonian physics include gravitational time dilation, gravitational lensing, the gravitational redshift of light and [[Black hole|black holes]]. Gravitational waves have been observed directly by the LIGO collaboration. The theory underpins modern cosmology, including the [[Big Bang]] and cosmic microwave background radiation. No self-consistent [[Quantum gravity|quantum gravity]] theory has been found, and gravity has not yet been unified with the strong, weak and electromagnetic interactions.

Branch of mathematics, also called point-set topology, that deals with the basic set-theoretic definitions and constructions used in [[Topology|topology]]. Its fundamental concepts are continuity, compactness and connectedness, defined by means of [[Open set|open sets]]. A topology on a set X is a family of subsets containing the empty set and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, and a set with a topology is a [[Topological space|topological space]]. Different choices of open sets yield different notions of continuity. [[Metric space|Metric spaces]], in which a distance is defined between points, form an important class of topological spaces. A basis generates a topology, and subspace, product and quotient topologies extend the construction. General topology emerged from the study of subsets of the real line, the manifold concept and normed spaces, and took its present form around 1940. It is the foundation for [[Algebraic topology|algebraic topology]], differential topology and geometric topology.

=== KEY: Genetic_drift_76e9e7
HEADWORD: Genetic drift
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Evolution | Inbreeding | Mutation | Ronald Fisher
ENTRY:
Change in the frequency of an existing allele in a population due to chance, one of the four primary forces of [[Evolution|evolution]] along with natural selection, mutation and gene flow. Drift may cause alleles to disappear, reducing genetic variation, or cause rare alleles to become frequent, possibly reaching fixation. Its effects are strongest in small populations, where fixation can occur within a few generations. In a model of 20 marbles drawn at random to form each new generation, color proportions fluctuate by chance. [[Ronald Fisher]], who explained natural selection with Mendelian genetics, held that drift played at most a minor role. In 1968 Motoo Kimura's neutral theory of molecular evolution revived the debate, claiming that most genetic changes spreading through populations result from drift acting on neutral [[Mutation|mutations]]. Founder and bottleneck effects are prominent forms, and they increase homozygosity and the risk of [[Inbreeding|inbreeding]] depression.

=== KEY: Genetics_dec501
HEADWORD: Genetics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Epigenetics | Francis Crick | Gene | Gregor Mendel | Human genome | James Watson | Jean-Baptiste Lamarck
ENTRY:
Branch of biology concerned with the study of [[Gene|genes]], genetic variation, and heredity in organisms. The Moravian Augustinian friar [[Gregor Mendel]], working in Brno in the 19th century, was the first to study it scientifically. In a paper presented in 1865 he traced the inheritance of certain traits in pea plants, described the patterns mathematically, and showed that organisms inherit traits through discrete "units of inheritance". His work gained wide understanding only in 1900, when Hugo de Vries and other scientists rediscovered it. William Bateson, a proponent of Mendel's work, coined the word *genetics* in 1905, from the Greek *genesis*, "origin".

Heredity had been used since prehistoric times to improve crop plants and animals through selective breeding. Among earlier theories were blending inheritance, implied by [[Charles Darwin]]'s *On the Origin of Species* (1859), and the inheritance of acquired characteristics, commonly associated with [[Jean-Baptiste Lamarck]] and now known to be wrong. Mendel's results showed that traits are produced by combinations of distinct genes rather than a continuous blend.

[[James Watson]] and [[Francis Crick]] modeled DNA as a double helix in 1953, and the genetic code was cracked in the 1960s. The Human Genome Project (1990–2003) sequenced approximately 99% of the [[Human genome|human genome]] to high accuracy.

Modern genetics studies gene structure, function, variation, and distribution in the cell, the organism, and the population. Its subfields include molecular genetics, [[Epigenetics|epigenetics]], population genetics, and paleogenetics, and the organisms studied span archaea, bacteria, and eukarya. Genetic processes work with an organism's environment and experiences to influence development and behavior, often called nature versus nurture. In a classic example, two genetically identical corn seeds, one grown in a temperate climate and one in an arid climate, differ in height: the plant in the arid climate grows to only half the height of the other because of lack of water and nutrients.

Series of four international treaties of 1949, with three additional protocols, forming the core of international humanitarian law. Founder and first khan of the [[Mongol Empire]] (r. 1206–27), born Temüjin and also known as Chinggis Khan. After uniting the Mongol tribes, he launched military campaigns that conquered large parts of China and [[Central Asia]].

Born between 1155 and 1167, the eldest child of the Borjigin chieftain Yesugei, Temüjin lost his father at age eight, when his family was abandoned by its tribe. Assessments remain divided. He was generous and loyal to his followers but ruthless toward enemies, and the Mongol army under him killed millions; his conquests also facilitated commercial and cultural exchange over a vast area. He is remembered as a tyrant in Russia and the [[Arab world]], while Western scholarship since the 1990s has begun to reassess that view. Mongolians regard him as the founding father of their nation.

=== KEY: Genocide_a30a2d
HEADWORD: Genocide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Armenian genocide | Rwandan genocide | The Holocaust | United Nations
ENTRY:
Intentional partial or total destruction of a human group. In scholarly and legal fields, genocide occurs when the group itself is targeted, not merely when many individuals are killed. Its acts include killing, preventing reproduction within the group, forcibly transferring children to another group, and cultural genocide.

The Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin coined the term between 1941 and 1943, combining the Greek *genos* ('race, people') with the Latin suffix *-caedo* ('act of killing'); his interest was first sparked by the [[Armenian genocide]]. He lobbied for genocide to be outlawed by treaty and brought his proposal to the newly established [[United Nations]] in 1946. Opposition was greater than he expected, as states feared that their own policies, including the treatment of indigenous peoples, colonialism, and racial segregation in the United States, might be labeled genocide. The Genocide Convention, adopted by the UN General Assembly on December 9, 1948, restricted his broad definition to five specific acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as such." Proving this specific intent is the strictest requirement in international criminal law and has led to few convictions, among them those for the 1994 [[Rwandan genocide]]. The definition remains contested across law, history, and sociology.

Genocide has occurred throughout history, most often in wartime and in situations of imperial expansion, and it is associated with colonialism and with both world wars. It is most often a state crime, and perpetrators typically believe themselves to be existentially threatened. The most common pattern is the killing of adult men together with sexual assault and enslavement of women and children; forced displacement and destruction of cultural heritage are also common. Popular understanding is heavily influenced by [[The Holocaust]].

=== KEY: Genocide_denial_ab0bc2
HEADWORD: Genocide denial
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Armenian genocide | Genocide | Rwandan genocide | The Holocaust
ENTRY:
Attempt to deny or minimize the scale and severity of a [[Genocide|genocide]]. Denial is considered the final stage of genocide and a catalyst or indicator of future atrocities, as it includes secret planning, propaganda and destruction of evidence. Prominent examples include denial of the [[Armenian genocide]], [[Rwandan genocide]] and the [[The Holocaust|Holocaust]]. Historians distinguish denial from revisionism by its techniques and motives, which often include evading moral or criminal responsibility and protecting perpetrators' reputations. Common strategies include questioning statistics, denying intent, blaming the victim, claiming self-defense and disputing the victims' group identity. Genocide scholar Adam Jones lists phrases such as "Hardly anybody died" and "It wasn't intentional". Scholars note that denial damages victims and risks future conflict, and that exposure of denial surged in the early 21st century through conspiracy theories spread on social media. Countermeasures include historical research, education and, in some jurisdictions, laws penalizing overt denial.

=== KEY: Geochronology_590104
HEADWORD: Geochronology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coral | Half-life | Zircon
ENTRY:
Science of determining the age of rocks, fossils and sediments using signatures inherent in the rocks themselves. Absolute geochronology relies on radioactive isotopes, while relative geochronology uses tools such as paleomagnetism and stable isotope ratios. Combining several geochronological and biostratigraphic indicators improves the precision of an age. Biostratigraphy places rocks within an interval of time by their fossil assemblages but gives no direct absolute age. Radiometric methods measure the decay of a radioactive parent isotope with a known [[Half-life|half-life]] into its radiogenic product. Uranium–lead dating, often applied to the mineral [[Zircon]] in igneous rocks, is one of the two most commonly used methods for geologic dating, along with argon–argon dating. Uranium–thorium dating is used for [[Coral|corals]], speleothems, carbonates and fossil bones. Geochronology is the prime tool of chronostratigraphy, which seeks absolute ages for fossil assemblages and the geologic history of Earth.

=== KEY: Geodesy_ce5bbc
HEADWORD: Geodesy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Global Positioning System | Surveying
ENTRY:
Science of measuring and representing the geometry, gravity and spatial orientation of Earth in time-varying three-dimensional space. The word derives from [[Ancient Greek]] *geodaisia*, "division of Earth." Geodesy provides the scientific basis for mapping, navigation and positioning, and supports infrastructure development, natural resource management and mineral exploration. === KEY: Geodetic_datum_6cc239
HEADWORD: Geodetic datum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Geodesy | Geoid | Sea level
ENTRY:
Reference frame for representing the position of locations on Earth. Datums are crucial to [[Geodesy|geodesy]], navigation, surveying, geographic information systems, remote sensing and cartography. A horizontal datum measures position across Earth's surface as latitude and longitude, and a vertical datum measures elevation or depth relative to a standard origin such as [[Sea level|mean sea level]]. A standard datum consists of a model of Earth's shape (a reference ellipsoid or [[Geoid|geoid]]), an origin tied to a monumented location, and control points measured from that origin. The relationship between coordinates in different datums is undefined and can only be approximated; the disparity between two datums can reach kilometers, a discrepancy called datum shift. Since the rise of the global positioning system, the WGS 84 datum has supplanted most others in many applications, although local datums such as OSGB36 approximate particular areas more closely.

=== KEY: Geographic_coordinate_system_f34cb4

ENTRY:
Spherical or geodetic system for measuring and communicating positions on Earth as latitude and longitude. It is the simplest, oldest and most widely used type of spatial reference system, and it forms the basis for most others. Its measurements are angles rather than Cartesian coordinates, and a full specification includes a [[Geodetic datum|geodetic datum]], since different datums yield different coordinates for the same location. The system is credited to [[Eratosthenes|Eratosthenes of Cyrene]], who composed his now-lost *Geography* at the [[Library of Alexandria]] in the 3rd century BC. At the International Meridian Conference of 1884, 22 of the 25 nations represented agreed to adopt the longitude of the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, England, as zero. France adopted [[Greenwich Mean Time]] in 1911.

ENTRY:
Integrated computer hardware and software that store, manage, analyze, edit, output and visualize geographic data. A [[Database|spatial database]] is usual but not essential, and in a broader sense a GIS includes human users, procedures and institutions. GIS relates unrelated information through location as the "key index variable," with places recorded by longitude, latitude and elevation. === KEY: Geography_84352d
HEADWORD: Geography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Cartography | Eratosthenes | Human geography | Physical geography | Ptolemy | Remote sensing | Technical geography
ENTRY:
Study of the lands, features, inhabitants, and phenomena of the planet Earth. The name comes from the Ancient Greek *geōgraphía*, combining *gê* ('Earth') and *gráphō* ('write'). Geography seeks to understand not merely where things are but how they have changed and come to be, and it has been called "a bridge between natural science and social science disciplines." Although specific to Earth, many of its concepts can be applied to other celestial bodies in planetary science.

For something to fall within geography, it generally needs a spatial component that can be placed on a map, such as coordinates, place names, or addresses. This has linked the field to [[Cartography]] and place names, though geographers study the spatial and temporal distribution of phenomena, processes, and features, and the interaction of humans and their environment. Because space and place affect economics, health, climate, plants, and animals, the discipline is highly interdisciplinary. Its core concepts across all approaches are space, place, time, and scale. Space is the most fundamental: absolute space is the exact site or coordinates of what is under investigation, though geographers are now trained to see the world as a dynamic space rather than a static image. Place combines the coordinates, the activity and use that occurs there, and the meaning ascribed to the location by individuals and groups. Time includes modeling the movement of people, organisms, and things through space. Scale, in the context of a map, is the ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding distance on the ground; phenomena appear different depending on the scale used. The five themes of geography, established in 1984 by guidelines published jointly by the National Council for Geographic Education and the Association of American Geographers, are location, place, relationships within places, movement, and regions.

The main branches are [[Physical geography]], which focuses on the natural environment; [[Human geography]], which concerns how humans interact with the Earth; and [[Technical geography]], which develops tools for understanding geography. Techniques divide into quantitative and qualitative approaches, with many studies using mixed methods. Common techniques include cartography, [[Remote sensing]], interviews, and surveying.

Geography was developed independently by multiple cultures and spread through trade between them. The earliest example of an attempted world map dates to the 9th century BC in ancient Babylon. Many of its concepts originate with the Greek [[Eratosthenes]] of Cyrene, who may have coined the term "geographia"; the first recorded use of the word was as the title of a book by the Greek scholar [[Ptolemy]]. In the Middle Ages, Islamic scholars such as Muhammad al-Idrisi produced detailed maps of the world. The [[Age of Discovery]] influenced the field as European explorers mapped the New World. Modern developments include geomatics and geographic information science.

=== KEY: Geoid_c6a1e8
HEADWORD: Geoid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Global Positioning System
ENTRY:
Shape that Earth's ocean would take under gravity and rotation alone, without winds or tides. [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]], who first described it, called it the "mathematical figure of the Earth." The geoid results from the uneven distribution of mass within Earth and can be known only through extensive gravitational measurements. It was not defined precisely until satellite geodesy in the mid-20th century, when the mathematician Gladys West first synthesized a high-fidelity geoid from satellite data. The geoid is irregular but considerably smoother than Earth's surface, and its deviation from a reference ellipsoid ranges from +85 m (Iceland) to −106 m (southern India). It serves as a reference surface for heights and is an essential component of satellite-based systems such as the [[Global Positioning System]].

=== KEY: Geologic_time_scale_21225a
HEADWORD: Geologic time scale
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cenozoic | Cretaceous | Geochronology | Mesozoic | Paleozoic | Phanerozoic | Quaternary
ENTRY:
Standardized system relating rock strata to geologic time. It arranges the rock record in chronological order using chronostratigraphy, and uses [[Geochronology|geochronology]], such as radiometric dating, to date the boundaries between units. The scale covers about 4.54 ± 0.05 billion years of Earth history. International units are defined by the International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS), a constituent body of the International Union of Geological Sciences. The [[Phanerozoic]] eon is split into the [[Paleozoic]], [[Mesozoic]] and [[Cenozoic]] eras, each subdivided into periods such as the [[Cretaceous]] and [[Quaternary]]. Boundaries are ratified through Global Boundary Stratotype Sections and Points (GSSPs), which are reference rock layers. The base of the Quaternary was adjusted to 2.58 million years in 2009 on climatic and glacial evidence.

=== KEY: Geological_history_of_Earth_4542ca
HEADWORD: Geological history of Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Geologic time scale | Ice age | Moon | Pangaea | Sun | Supercontinent
ENTRY:
Sequence of major geological events in Earth's past, dated by the [[Geologic time scale|geologic time scale]]. Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago by accretion from the solar nebula, the disk of dust and gas left over from the formation of the [[Sun]]. Initially molten from volcanism and collisions, its outer layer cooled into a solid crust once water began accumulating in the atmosphere. The [[Moon]] may have formed after a protoplanet struck Earth. Outgassing and volcanism produced the primordial atmosphere, and condensing water vapor, augmented by ice from asteroids, produced the oceans. Continents formed, broke apart and recombined into [[Supercontinent|supercontinents]]. The earliest known, Rodinia, began to break apart about 750 million years ago; Pannotia formed 600 to 540 million years ago, and [[Pangaea]] broke apart 200 million years ago. The present pattern of [[Ice age|ice ages]] began about 40 million years ago, and the Last Glacial Period ended about 10,000 years ago.

=== KEY: Geology_55fa61
HEADWORD: Geology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Wegener | Earth science | Igneous rock | Lithosphere | Metamorphic rock | Plate tectonics | San Andreas Fault | Sedimentary rock
ENTRY:
Branch of natural science concerned with the Earth and other astronomical bodies, the rocks of which they are composed, and the processes by which they change over time. The name comes from the Ancient Greek *gê* ('earth') and *-logía* ('study of, discourse'). Modern geology overlaps all other [[Earth science|Earth sciences]], including hydrology, and is integrated with Earth system science and planetary science.

Geology describes the structure of the Earth on and beneath its surface and the processes that have shaped it. Geologists study the mineralogical composition of rocks to gain insight into their history of formation. Geology determines the relative ages of rocks found at a given location, while geochemistry, a branch of geology, determines their absolute ages. By combining petrological, crystallographic, and paleontological tools, geologists chronicle the history of the Earth as a whole, including its age. The discipline provides evidence for [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], the evolutionary history of life, and the Earth's past climates. Its methods include fieldwork, rock description, geophysical techniques, chemical analysis, physical experiments, and numerical modeling. In practical terms it is important for mineral and hydrocarbon exploration, evaluating water resources, understanding natural hazards, remediating environmental problems, and understanding past climate change, and it is central to geological and geotechnical engineering.

Most geological data come from solid Earth materials, though meteorites and other extraterrestrial materials are also studied. Minerals are naturally occurring elements and compounds with a definite homogeneous chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement. They are identified by tests of color, streak, hardness, breakage pattern (fracture or cleavage), luster, specific gravity, effervescence in hydrochloric acid, magnetism, and sometimes taste. A rock is any naturally occurring solid mass or aggregate of minerals or mineraloids. There are three major types: an [[Igneous rock|igneous rock]] solidifies or crystallizes from melt (magma or lava); a [[Sedimentary rock|sedimentary rock]] forms when weathered and eroded material is redeposited and lithified, and falls mainly into four categories (sandstone, shale, carbonate, and evaporite); and a [[Metamorphic rock|metamorphic rock]] forms when heat and pressure change the mineral content of the other types. All three may melt again, forming new magma. Unlithified superficial deposits lying above the bedrock are studied as Quaternary geology.

In the 1960s it was discovered that the Earth's [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]], which includes the crust and the rigid uppermost mantle, is separated into tectonic plates that move across the plastically deforming upper mantle, the asthenosphere. The theory is supported by observations including seafloor spreading and the global distribution of mountain terrain and seismicity. Plate motion is coupled with the convection of the mantle. Mid-ocean ridges are divergent boundaries, where plates move apart; arcs of volcanoes and earthquakes mark convergent boundaries, where one plate subducts under another; and transform boundaries, such as the [[San Andreas Fault]] system, are where plates slide horizontally past each other. Plate tectonics provided a mechanism for [[Alfred Wegener]]'s theory of continental drift and has been called a "grand unifying theory of geology". Seismology has shown a liquid outer core, where shear waves cannot propagate, and a dense solid inner core.

=== KEY: Geometric_distribution_645dd4
HEADWORD: Geometric distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Exponential distribution
ENTRY:
Discrete probability distribution for the number of independent trials needed to get a first success. It takes two forms: the number X of Bernoulli trials until one success, supported on 1, 2, 3, …, and the number Y = X − 1 of failures before the first success, supported on 0, 1, 2, …. If each trial succeeds with probability p, then P(X = k) = (1 − p)ᵏ⁻¹p. It is the only memoryless discrete distribution, the discrete counterpart of the [[Exponential distribution|exponential distribution]]. Rolling a six-sided die until a 1 appears gives a geometric distribution with p = 1/6.

=== KEY: Geometric_series_a984d8
HEADWORD: Geometric series
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Parabola | Zeno's paradoxes
ENTRY:
Sum of the terms of a geometric progression, in which each term is the previous term multiplied by a constant common ratio *r*. The finite sum *a* + *ar* + … + *arⁿ* equals *a*(1 − *r*ⁿ⁺¹)/(1 − *r*) for *r* ≠ 1. For |*r*| < 1, the infinite series converges to *a*/(1 − *r*); for example, ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … sums to 1. === KEY: Geometry_d9c633
HEADWORD: Geometry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Euclid | Euclidean geometry | Fermat's Last Theorem | Non-Euclidean geometry | Omar Khayyam | Pythagorean theorem | Riemannian geometry
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerned with properties of space such as the distance, shape, size, and relative position of figures. With arithmetic, it is one of the oldest branches of mathematics, and a mathematician working in the field is called a geometer. Until the 19th century it was almost exclusively devoted to [[Euclidean geometry]], whose fundamental concepts are point, line, plane, distance, angle, surface, and curve. Originally developed to model the physical world, geometry has applications in almost all sciences and in art, architecture, and other activities related to graphics. It also serves apparently unrelated areas of mathematics: methods of algebraic geometry are fundamental in Wiles's proof of [[Fermat's Last Theorem]], a problem stated in terms of elementary arithmetic.

The earliest recorded beginnings lie in ancient Mesopotamia and Egypt in the 2nd millennium BC, where empirically discovered principles of lengths, angles, areas, and volumes met practical needs in surveying, construction, astronomy, and crafts. The earliest known texts are the Egyptian Rhind Papyrus (2000–1800 BC) and Moscow Papyrus (c. 1890 BC), and Babylonian clay tablets such as Plimpton 322 (1900 BC). In the 7th century BC the Greek mathematician Thales of Miletus used geometry to calculate the height of pyramids and the distance of ships from shore, and is credited with the first use of deductive reasoning in geometry. The Pythagorean School is credited with the first proof of the [[Pythagorean theorem]]. Eudoxus developed the method of exhaustion for areas and volumes of curvilinear figures. Around 300 BC, [[Euclid]]'s *Elements* introduced mathematical rigor through the axiomatic method and is the earliest example of the format still used in mathematics, that of definition, axiom, theorem, and proof; it was known to all educated people in the West until the middle of the 20th century. [[Archimedes]] used the method of exhaustion to find the area under the arc of a parabola by summing an infinite series and gave accurate approximations of pi.

Indian mathematicians also contributed. The Śulba Sūtras contain the earliest extant verbal expression of the Pythagorean theorem, Aryabhata's *Aryabhatiya* (499) includes computation of areas and volumes, and Brahmagupta in 628 stated his theorem on the diagonals of a cyclic quadrilateral. In medieval Islam, al-Mahani conceived of reducing geometrical problems such as duplicating the cube to algebra, and [[Omar Khayyam]] found geometric solutions to cubic equations. Work by Ibn al-Haytham, Khayyam, and Nasir al-Din al-Tusi on quadrilaterals was part of research on the parallel postulate that European geometers continued, leading by the 19th century to hyperbolic geometry. In the early 17th century René Descartes created analytic geometry, geometry with coordinates and equations.

The 19th century dramatically enlarged the field. Carl Friedrich Gauss's *Theorema Egregium* showed that the curvature of a surface is independent of any embedding in Euclidean space, so surfaces can be studied intrinsically; this grew into the theory of manifolds and [[Riemannian geometry]]. Geometries without the parallel postulate, the [[Non-Euclidean geometry|non-Euclidean geometries]], were shown to be free of contradiction, and the geometry underlying general relativity is a prominent application. Since the late 19th century, geometry has split into many subfields, including differential, algebraic, computational, and discrete geometry, algebraic topology, projective geometry, affine geometry, and finite geometry. The word "space" now denotes a mathematical structure on which some geometry is defined.

=== KEY: Geomorphology_1f7046
HEADWORD: Geomorphology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climatology | Geodesy | Geology | Glacier | Moraine | Sediment
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the origin and evolution of topographic and bathymetric features generated by physical, chemical or biological processes at or near Earth's surface. Geomorphologists explain why landscapes look as they do and predict change through field observations, physical experiments and numerical modeling. Surface processes include the action of water, wind, ice, wildfire and life, along with soil-forming reactions and gravity. Geologic processes such as mountain uplift, volcanic growth and the formation of sedimentary basins also shape landscapes, and climate and tectonics can feed back on each other. Subfields include [[Glacier|glacial]] geomorphology, which studies [[Moraine|moraines]] and other deposits, and fluvial geomorphology, which studies how rivers transport [[Sediment|sediment]] and cut bedrock. The field draws on [[Geology]], [[Geodesy]] and [[Climatology]].

=== KEY: Geophysics_432d78

Physical science concerned with the processes and properties of Earth and its surrounding space environment, studied by quantitative and observational methods. It focuses on Earth's shape, its gravitational, magnetic and electromagnetic fields, and its internal structure and dynamics, including [[Plate tectonics|tectonics]] and volcanism. The field also covers the oceans, atmosphere, [[Cryosphere]], ionosphere and magnetosphere, and analogous processes on the [[Moon]] and other planets. === KEY: Georg_Cantor_853f35

German mathematician who helped create [[Set theory|set theory]], now a fundamental theory in mathematics. He established the importance of one-to-one correspondence between sets, defined infinite and well-ordered sets, and proved that the [[Real number|real numbers]] are more numerous than the [[Natural number|natural numbers]]. After a decade of experiments he patented and sold a roll-film camera, making amateur photography accessible to the general public. English novelist, poet, journalist, and translator, born Mary Ann Evans. One of the leading writers of the [[Victorian era|Victorian era]], she wrote seven novels, including *Adam Bede* (1859), *The Mill on the Floss* (1860), and *Silas Marner* (1861). === KEY: George_Frideric_Handel_d1c643
HEADWORD: Handel, George Frideric
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Opera | Westminster Abbey
ENTRY:
German-British Baroque composer. Known for [[Opera|operas]], oratorios, anthems, concerti grossi, and organ concerti, he brought Italian opera to its highest development and created the English oratorio and organ concerto. Born in Halle, he spent time in Hamburg and Italy before settling in London in 1712, becoming a naturalized British subject in 1727. After the success of *Messiah* (1742), he never composed another Italian opera. His music was admired by Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven. His *Water Music* and *Music for the Royal Fireworks* remain popular, and his anthem *Zadok the Priest* has been performed at every British coronation since 1727. He was given a state funeral at [[Westminster Abbey]].

ENTRY:
American composer and pianist of [[Jazz|jazz]], popular, and classical music. His songs included "Swanee" (1919) and "I Got Rhythm" (1930). His orchestral works *Rhapsody in Blue* (1924) and *An American in Paris* (1928) blended jazz idioms with concert forms. He wrote the opera *Porgy and Bess* (1935), which included the hit "Summertime." Initially a commercial failure, it came to be considered one of the most important American operas of the 20th century. His *Of Thee I Sing* (1931) was the first musical to win the Pulitzer Prize for Drama. [[Nadia Boulanger]] refused to teach him, fearing that rigorous study would ruin his jazz-influenced style, and [[Maurice Ravel]] voiced similar objections. He also wrote film scores in Hollywood.

=== KEY: George_III_1c1500
HEADWORD: George III
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic slave trade | Battle of Waterloo | Seven Years' War | United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland
ENTRY:
King of Great Britain and Ireland, 1760–1820, and duke and prince-elector of Hanover, becoming King of Hanover in 1814. He was the first Hanoverian monarch born in Britain, and English was his first language. Early in his reign Britain defeated France in the [[Seven Years' War]], but it lost 13 North American colonies in the American War of Independence. Wars against revolutionary and Napoleonic France ended with Napoleon's defeat at the [[Battle of Waterloo]] in 1815. The Acts of Union created the [[United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland]], and the [[Atlantic slave trade|transatlantic slave trade]] was banned from the British Empire in 1807. In later life he suffered recurrent, then permanent, mental illness, possibly bipolar disorder or porphyria, and his son became Prince Regent in 1811. He married Princess Charlotte of Mecklenburg-Strelitz and had 15 children with her. At his death he was the longest-lived and longest-reigning British monarch, having reigned 59 years and 96 days.

ENTRY:
King of Great Britain and Ireland, 1727–1760, and Elector of [[Hanover]]. Born in Hanover, he was the most recent British monarch born outside Britain. Parliament largely controlled domestic policy, but later scholarship concludes that he influenced foreign policy and military appointments more than earlier historians believed. He took part in the Battle of Dettingen during the [[War of the Austrian Succession]], becoming the most recent British monarch to lead an army in battle. He was succeeded by his grandson, [[George III]].
