=== KEY: Harpsichord_3dedf1
HEADWORD: Harpsichord
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baroque music | Johann Sebastian Bach | Keyboard instrument | Piano
ENTRY:
[[Keyboard instrument]] whose strings are plucked rather than struck. Pressing a key raises a jack holding a small plectrum, usually of quill or plastic, which plucks a string. Because the sound is fixed, a note sounds equally loud however hard the key is pressed. The harpsichord was widely used in Renaissance and [[Baroque music|Baroque]] music, both as a solo instrument and as part of the continuo. Italian makers evolved a design that survived into later centuries, and the Ruckers family of the Southern Netherlands built the first harpsichords with two keyboards. Around 1700 Bartolomeo Cristofori built the first [[Piano|piano]], and by the late 18th century the piano had largely supplanted the harpsichord. Revived in the 20th century, it is used in historically informed performances of music by [[Johann Sebastian Bach|Bach]] and others.

=== KEY: Harpy_eagle_b74669
HEADWORD: Harpy eagle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bird of prey | Carl Linnaeus | Eagle | Greek mythology | Opossum | Rainforest | Sloth
ENTRY:
Large neotropical species of [[Eagle|eagle]], *Harpia harpyja*. It is the largest [[Bird of prey|bird of prey]] throughout its range and usually inhabits tropical lowland [[Rainforest|rainforest]], nesting and hunting in the emergent canopy. It is found from southern Mexico to as far south as Argentina, and preys on [[Sloth|sloths]], [[Opossum|opossums]] and other arboreal mammals. Destruction of its habitat has caused it to vanish from many parts of its former range, and it is nearly extirpated from much of Central America. [[Carl Linnaeus]] first described it in the 1758 *Systema Naturae*, and its name comes from the harpies of [[Greek mythology]].

=== KEY: Harrow_tool_4e97b4

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Agriculture|Agricultural]] implement used for surface [[Tillage|tillage]]. It is used after [[Plough|ploughing]] to break up clods and smooth the soil into a structure called tilth, suitable for planting [[Seed|seeds]]. Coarser harrowing also removes weeds and covers seed after sowing. Unlike a plow, which cuts the upper 12 to 25 cm (5 to 10 in) of soil and leaves furrows, a harrow disturbs the whole surface. Disc harrows suit heavy work such as breaking up sod, while chain harrows suit lighter work such as leveling the tilth. Harrows were once drawn by draft animals but are now almost always attached to a [[Tractor|tractor]].

=== KEY: Harvest_cb49ad

ENTRY:
Process of gathering mature crops from fields. The word comes from Old English *hærfest*, meaning autumn, and "harvest" also refers to the collected crops. Harvesting also collects plants, animals and fish as food, and on small farms with little mechanization it is the most labor-intensive activity of the growing season. On large farms a [[Combine harvester|combine harvester]] does the work, while reaping grain typically used a [[Sickle|sickle]], scythe or reaper. Completion of the harvest marks the end of the growing season and is the focus of seasonal [[Harvest festival|harvest festivals]]. Crop failure, caused by drought, storms, floods or soil degradation, has triggered [[Famine|famines]] and [[Human migration|human migration]] throughout history. The term also applies to fishing and logging, and to harvesting grapes for wine.

Annual celebration around the time of a region's main [[Harvest|harvest]]. Harvest festivals typically feature feasting with foods drawn from the crops. [[Data structure|Data structure]] that maps keys to values using a hash function. The function computes an index into an array of buckets. Most designs use an imperfect hash function, so collisions are handled by separate chaining or open addressing. Major goddess in [[Ancient Egyptian religion|ancient Egyptian religion]]. === KEY: Hatred_07c9e3

ENTRY:
Intense negative emotional response toward certain people, things or ideas. Hatred is closely associated with [[Anger|anger]], contempt and [[Disgust|disgust]], and is regarded as the opposite of [[Love|love]]. As an emotion it can be short-lived or long-lasting, and of low intensity ("I hate broccoli") or high ("I hate the whole world"). Hate speech is perceived to disparage people on similar grounds.

Sixth [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]] of Egypt's Eighteenth Dynasty (r. c. 1479–1458 BC). Hatshepsut was the daughter of Thutmose I and the wife of her half-brother Thutmose II. Confederacy of Iroquoian [[Indigenous peoples|Indigenous peoples]] in northeast [[North America|North America]], also known as the Iroquois. The original Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga and Seneca peoples were joined after 1722 by the Tuscarora, which is why the confederacy was also called the Six Nations. === KEY: Health_605669
HEADWORD: Health
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Disease | Exercise | Public health | Sleep | Smallpox | Smoking | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Condition of physical and emotional well-being, commonly understood as the normal functioning of a living being, free of [[Disease|disease]], pain, or injury.

Definitions of health have varied by purpose and over time. In 1948 the [[World Health Organization]] (WHO) proposed a broader definition: "physical, mental, and social well-being, and not merely the absence of disease and infirmity." Critics called it vague, excessively broad, and unmeasurable. In the 1980s the WHO fostered the health promotion movement, and in 1984 it revised the definition to describe health as "a resource for everyday life, not the objective of living," emphasizing resilience and social and personal resources as well as physical capacities.

Factors known as determinants of health include gender, ethnic background, lifestyle, economic status, and genetics. Observers differ on emphasis: some stress individual choices and high-risk behaviors, while others stress structural determinants, such as unequal access to health care. Health can be promoted by [[Exercise|exercise]] and adequate [[Sleep|sleep]] and by avoiding [[Smoking|smoking]] and excessive stress.

Public health measures such as vaccination, sanitation, and antibiotics have controlled many infectious diseases. The eradication of [[Smallpox|smallpox]] in 1980 was achieved through coordinated global vaccination and surveillance. Contemporary debates include the rise of obesity and metabolic disorders and the relative roles of personal behavior and social conditions; [[Public health]] addresses health at the level of populations.

=== KEY: Health_care_aeebad

Improvement or maintenance of health through the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, amelioration, or cure of disease, illness, injury, and other physical and mental impairments.

=== KEY: Heart_f4a6ce
HEADWORD: Heart
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artery | Blood | Carbon dioxide | Cardiovascular disease | Circulatory system | Smoking | Stethoscope | Ultrasound
ENTRY:
Muscular organ of humans and other animals that pumps [[Blood|blood]] through the blood vessels. The heart and vessels together form the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]], which carries oxygen and nutrients to the tissues and carries metabolic waste such as [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] to the lungs. In humans the heart is roughly the size of a closed fist, has a mass of 250–350 grams (9–12 oz), and lies between the lungs in the mediastinum. It is enclosed in a protective sac, the pericardium, and its wall has three layers: epicardium, myocardium, and endocardium.

The human heart has four chambers: two upper atria, which receive blood, and two lower ventricles, which discharge it. Valves prevent backflow. Deoxygenated blood enters the right atrium and passes to the right ventricle, which pumps it to the lungs, where it gives off carbon dioxide and receives oxygen. Oxygenated blood returns to the left atrium, passes through the left ventricle, and leaves through the aorta into systemic circulation, moving through [[Artery|arteries]], arterioles, and capillaries before returning through venules and veins.

Pacemaker cells in the sinoatrial node set the rhythm, generating an electric current that causes the heart to contract. The adult heart beats at a resting rate close to 72 beats per minute.

[[Cardiovascular disease|Cardiovascular diseases]] are the most common cause of death globally: an estimated 19.8 million people died from them in 2022, about 32% of all deaths worldwide. Risk factors include [[Smoking|smoking]], being overweight, little exercise, high cholesterol, high blood pressure, and poorly controlled diabetes. Diagnosis relies on medical history, listening with a [[Stethoscope|stethoscope]], ECG, and echocardiogram, which uses [[Ultrasound|ultrasound]].

=== KEY: Heat_7d4863
HEADWORD: Heat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calorie | Electromagnetic radiation | Francis Bacon | Friction | Joule | Robert Boyle | Robert Hooke | Thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Energy in transfer between a body and its surroundings, other than through thermodynamic work or transfer of matter. In [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]], heat is defined by exclusion of those other modes of transfer, to keep a strict logical distinction. Heat is not a state variable or state function but a process quantity, and a thermodynamic system does not contain heat.

Heat can enter or leave a body by thermal conduction, by [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]], or through [[Friction|friction]], electrical resistance (Joule heating), or repeated magnetization and demagnetization. It is not necessarily conserved in a transfer: it can be generated, though not destroyed, by friction. Calorimetry measures heat by its effect on the states of interacting bodies, for example by the amount of ice melted.

In the International System of Units the unit of heat is the [[Joule|joule]] (J); traditional units such as the British thermal unit and the [[Calorie|calorie]] remain in use in engineering. The unit for the rate of heating is the watt (W), one joule per second. The symbol Q was introduced by Rudolf Clausius and Macquorn Rankine in c. 1859. By convention, heat released by a system is negative (Q < 0) and heat absorbed is positive (Q > 0).

In the 17th century, [[Francis Bacon]] (1620), [[Robert Hooke]], and [[Robert Boyle]] described heat as the motion of the small particles of bodies, though their "heat" may have meant what is now called temperature; no clear distinction was made until the mid-18th century. The caloric theory, which treated heat as a substance, was replaced around the end of the 18th century by the mechanical theory, which is accepted today.

=== KEY: Heat_capacity_f7d386
HEADWORD: Heat capacity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Joule | Phase transition | Specific heat capacity
ENTRY:
Property of matter equal to the heat that must be supplied to an object to raise its temperature by one unit. Its SI unit is the [[Joule|joule]] per kelvin (J/K), and it measures the ability of a material or system to store thermal energy. It is an extensive property. Dividing it by mass gives the [[Specific heat capacity]], and dividing it by the amount of substance in moles gives the molar heat capacity. It usually varies with starting temperature and pressure, changing sharply during a [[Phase transition|phase transition]] such as melting or vaporization. For a block of iron weighing one pound, the value is about 204 J/K near 25 °C and 1 atm, adequate for temperatures between 15 °C and 35 °C. In architecture and civil engineering, a building's heat capacity is often called its thermal mass.

=== KEY: Heat_equation_3dc5ca

ENTRY:
Parabolic [[Partial differential equation|partial differential equation]] of heat transfer, modeling how the temperature of a body changes over time. Joseph Fourier developed it in 1822 to model how heat diffuses through a region, and it is fundamental in pure and applied mathematics. For a homogeneous, isotropic medium with thermal diffusivity α, the temperature u(x,y,z,t) obeys ∂u/∂t = α∇²u. === KEY: Heat_transfer_d7b1e3

Branch of thermal engineering concerned with the generation, use, conversion and exchange of thermal energy between physical systems. Family of philosophical views that give priority to [[Pleasure|pleasure]].
