Capital and largest city of the [[Northern Territory]], Australia. === KEY: Das_Kapital_8e8f83
HEADWORD: Das Kapital
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Capital (economics) | David Ricardo | Friedrich Engels | Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel | Interest (economics) | Karl Marx | Profit (economics)
ENTRY:
Three-volume work of political economy by [[Karl Marx]], foundational to Marxist theory. Its full title is *Capital: A Critique of Political Economy*. Only the first volume appeared in Marx's lifetime, in 1867; [[Friedrich Engels]] published the second and third from Marx's manuscripts in 1885 and 1894. Its central argument is that capitalism depends on the exploitation of labor, whose unpaid work is the source of surplus value and [[Profit (economics)|profit]]. Marx held a labor theory of value and argued that the rate of profit tends to fall, leading to economic crises. Volume I covers production, Volume II the circulation of [[Capital (economics)|capital]], and Volume III the distribution of surplus value into profit, [[Interest (economics)|interest]] and rent. The work drew on [[Adam Smith]], [[David Ricardo]] and [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel|Hegel]]. Its association with 20th-century communist states has made it a subject of continuing debate.

=== KEY: Data_and_information_visualization_7e5f50

ENTRY:
Practice of creating graphic representations of quantitative and qualitative [[Data|data]] and [[Information|information]] using static, dynamic or interactive visual items. Data visualization presents mainly quantitative data in schematic form, using charts, graphs, geospatial maps, correlation matrices and percentage gauges. Information visualization handles large, complex datasets, including abstract information, with tools such as Sankey diagrams, [[Flowchart|flowcharts]] and timelines. === KEY: Data_communication_65d1e8
HEADWORD: Data communication
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coding theory | Information theory | Infrared | Microwave | Optical fiber | Radio wave
ENTRY:
Transfer of messages over a point-to-point or point-to-multipoint communication channel. It comprises transmission and reception and is classified as analog or digital. Analog systems convey voice, images or video as a continuous signal that varies in amplitude or phase, while digital transmission carries a digitized analog signal or a born-digital bitstream. Channels include copper wire, [[Optical fiber|optical fibers]], [[Radio wave|radio]] and [[Microwave|microwave]] links, [[Infrared|infrared]] signals and computer buses. Electrical telegraphy (1809) and teletypewriters (1906) were early digital applications. The theory is covered by [[Information theory|information theory]] and [[Coding theory|coding theory]], while courses address modulation and error-correcting codes at the physical layer and media access and error detection at the data link layer.

=== KEY: Data_compression_7f0140
HEADWORD: Data compression
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bit | Claude Shannon | Entropy (information theory) | GIF | Information | JPEG | MP3
ENTRY:
Encoding of [[Information|information]] using fewer [[Bit|bits]] than the original representation, to reduce file size. Lossless compression removes statistical redundancy so the original can be reconstructed exactly, with typical ratios of 2:1 to 4:1. Lossy compression discards less perceptible detail for higher ratios and is used for images, audio and video, as in [[JPEG]] and [[MP3]]. Techniques include run-length encoding, Huffman coding (David Huffman, 1952) and dictionary methods such as LZW, developed by Jacob Ziv and Abraham Lempel in 1977–1978 and used in ZIP and [[GIF]] files. The theory traces to [[Claude Shannon]], who established [[Entropy (information theory)|entropy]] as the theoretical minimum for lossless encoding.

=== KEY: Data_storage_dc21b0
HEADWORD: Data storage
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Barcode | Computer | DNA | Energy | Information | Magnetic tape | Optical disc | RNA
ENTRY:
Recording of [[Information|information]] on a storage medium, such as handwriting, [[Magnetic tape|magnetic tape]] or [[Optical disc|optical discs]]. Recording may use virtually any form of [[Energy|energy]]. Biological molecules such as [[DNA]] and [[RNA]] are considered by some to be data storage. Data stored in a digital, machine-readable form is called digital data, and electronic storage requires electrical power. === KEY: Data_structure_37e19e
HEADWORD: Data structure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic structure | Computer | Computer science
ENTRY:
Way of organizing and storing data in a [[Computer|computer]] that is usually chosen for efficient access. In [[Computer science|computer science]], a data structure is the physical implementation of a data type, specifying data organization, storage format and the operations performed on it. Data structures are closely related to abstract data types, which describe the logical form or [[Algebraic structure|algebraic structure]] of a type without specifying how its operations are implemented. Linear structures such as arrays and linked lists arrange elements sequentially, while nonlinear structures such as trees and graphs support hierarchical or interconnected relationships. Choice involves trade-offs between storage space and speed, analyzed with measures such as Big-O notation.

=== KEY: Database_e307db

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computing | Data | File system | SQL
ENTRY:
Organized collection of [[Data|data]] in [[Computing|computing]], usually managed by a database management system (DBMS). The DBMS is the software that interacts with users, applications and the database to capture and analyze data, and it provides facilities to administer the database. The term is often used loosely for the DBMS, the wider database system or an associated application. Small databases may be kept on a [[File system|file system]], while large ones run on computer clusters or cloud storage. Relational databases, which store data in tables of rows and columns, became dominant in the 1980s, and most use [[SQL]] for writing and querying data. Non-relational databases, collectively called NoSQL, became popular in the 2000s and use different query languages.

Flowering palm (*Phoenix dactylifera*) of the family [[Arecaceae]], cultivated for its sweet fruit, the date. === KEY: Day_03727a
HEADWORD: Day
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calendar | Circadian rhythm | Coordinated Universal Time | Earth | Jupiter | Mercury (planet) | Time zone | Venus
ENTRY:
Unit of time equal to one full rotation of the [[Earth]] with respect to the Sun, averaging 24 hours (86,400 seconds). The daily cycle drives [[Circadian rhythm|circadian rhythms]] in many organisms.

Days are organized into [[Calendar|calendars]], grouped into weeks, months, and years. In common usage a day starts at midnight. Because the time of midnight varies between locations, [[Time zone|time zones]] provide a uniform standard time; they began to be adopted in the middle of the 19th century with railroads, and as of 2015, 40 were in use, all defined as offsets from [[Coordinated Universal Time]]. The Jewish religious calendar counts days from sunset, and in astronomy a day begins at noon.

Several definitions serve specific purposes. The SI day is exactly 86,400 seconds. A fictitious mean Sun moving at constant speed defines the mean solar day, removing the yearly variation caused by Earth's orbit and axial tilt. A sidereal day, measured against the celestial background, lasts 23 hours, 56 minutes, and 4 seconds. Tidal interactions with the Moon are lengthening Earth's day by about 2.3 milliseconds per century.

On other bodies a day is a full rotation with respect to the star. The solar day on [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]] is 176 Earth days, longer than its year of 88 days; on [[Venus]] it is 117 Earth days, shorter than its year of 225 days. [[Jupiter]] has a day of approximately 10 hours, and a solar day on Mars is about 24 hours and 37 minutes.

=== KEY: Daylight_saving_time_4ce67f

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benjamin Franklin | Canada | United States
ENTRY:
Practice of advancing clocks by one hour in spring and setting them back in autumn. The first local implementation was in Port Arthur and Fort William, Ontario, [[Canada]], in 1908, and the first nationwide ones were by the German and Austro-Hungarian Empires, starting April 30, 1916. === KEY: Death_6097f8
HEADWORD: Death
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Afterlife | Brain death | Cardiovascular disease | Funeral | Natural selection | Suicide
ENTRY:
Irreversible cessation of the biological functions that sustain a living organism. After death, organisms stop exhibiting behavior and generally begin to decompose. Death can occur at any stage of development, and entropy, the tendency toward disorder, can contribute to the breakdown of living systems. Clinical death, the stopping of breathing and heartbeat, may in some cases be reversed, sometimes producing a near-death experience.

Defining death is difficult, partly because there is little consensus on how to define life and because life functions do not cease simultaneously across organ systems. Death was originally defined as the end of breathing and heartbeat, but cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) meant that this state was no longer strictly irreversible. For organisms with a brain, death can instead be focused on that organ. Some hold that all brain functions must cease; others hold that a person whose personality and identity are irretrievably lost should be considered dead even if the brainstem is alive. [[Brain death]] is sometimes used as a legal definition. It is diagnosed through clinical examination, apnea testing, and exclusion of confounding factors such as hypothermia or drug effects. Controversies include the rare reports of recovery and the ethics of withdrawing life support.

In the natural world, organisms die through predation, parasitism, competition, starvation, dehydration, natural disasters, disease, or old age. Some organisms, such as the immortal jellyfish, are biologically immortal yet can still die from other causes. Selective pressures cause organisms with unfavorable traits to die off, and [[Natural selection]] drives living things to avoid death. When all members of a clade have died, it is extinct; extinction events are periods of mass death in many species worldwide that cause a global loss of biodiversity.

As of the early 21st century, 56 million people die per year. As of 2022, an estimated almost 110 billion humans had died, roughly 94% of all humans to have ever lived. The most common reason is aging, and the most common cause is [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]]. An autopsy can be done to determine cause of death. Biogerontology seeks ways to eliminate death by natural aging, and humans attempt to reach their maximum lifespan through lifestyle changes such as calorie reduction, diet, and exercise. Humans also die through [[Suicide|suicide]], homicide, punishment for crimes, and war.

Because humans are aware of death, it is a central part of the human condition and universal to human culture. It has motivated religious, spiritual, ethical, and legal responses. Many cultures hold that individuals survive death in an [[Afterlife|afterlife]], as spirits, or through reincarnation, while monist forms of thought such as materialism and physicalism take other views. Customs for honoring the body include the [[Funeral|funeral]], cremation, and sky burial, and family and friends usually go through a process of grieving. The study of death is known as thanatology.

Large igneous province of west-central India, among the largest volcanic features on Earth. === KEY: Decimal_937504

Numeral system using ten as its base (radix). Its most common form is the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], a positional system with ten digits, 0 to 9, which made very large numbers far easier to write and calculate with and was later extended to represent decimal fractions. === KEY: Declaration_of_the_Rights_of_Man_and_of_the_Citizen_b1c6d0

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: French Revolution | Jean-Jacques Rousseau | Marquis de Lafayette | Montesquieu | Thomas Jefferson
ENTRY:
Human and civil rights document of the [[French Revolution]]. Its last article was adopted by the National Constituent Assembly on August 26, 1789, as the first step toward a constitution for the Kingdom of France. === KEY: Decolonization_63b3a9
HEADWORD: Decolonization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algerian War | American Revolution | British Empire | Cold War | Frantz Fanon | Haitian Revolution | Spanish American wars of independence | United Nations
ENTRY:
Process by which colonized territories gain independence or self-determination from imperial powers. Its meanings and applications are disputed; some scholars focus on independence movements and the collapse of global colonial empires.

Decolonization began in 1775 with the [[American Revolution]] against the [[British Empire]]. During the Napoleonic Wars the French colonial empire, the Spanish Empire, and Portugal faced the [[Haitian Revolution]], the [[Spanish American wars of independence]], and the independence of Brazil. A further wave followed World War I, another followed World War II, and a last came after the [[Cold War]] with the collapse of the Soviet Union, the independence of Palau, and the handovers of Hong Kong and Macau. Over 80 new states emerged after World War II through negotiated handovers, armed struggles such as the [[Algerian War]], and swift independences such as that of 17 African nations in 1960. Seventeen territories remain on the [[United Nations]] list of non-self-governing territories.

The UN holds that the right to self-determination is the core requirement and can be exercised with or without political independence; a 1960 General Assembly resolution characterized colonial foreign rule as a violation of human rights. Many scholars trace the ideological roots of independence movements to the Age of Enlightenment, though decolonial scholars have criticized its failure to extend liberty and dignity to Indigenous and subaltern peoples.

Early studies appeared in the 1960s and 1970s, notably *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) by [[Frantz Fanon]]. Decolonization often left arbitrary borders, resource-reliant economies, and weak institutions, which contributed to conflicts and uneven development.

=== KEY: Decomposition_b8204a
HEADWORD: Decomposition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biosphere | Carbon dioxide | Earthworm | Microorganism | Odor
ENTRY:
Process by which dead organic matter is broken down into simpler organic or inorganic substances, such as [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], water, simple sugars and mineral salts. It is part of the nutrient cycle and recycles finite matter within the [[Biosphere]]. Abiotic decomposition involves chemical or physical processes such as hydrolysis, while biotic decomposition, or biodegradation, is the metabolic breakdown of materials by living organisms, typically [[Microorganism|microorganisms]]. Decomposers, or detritivores, include bacteria, fungi and animals such as [[Earthworm|earthworms]]. In animals, decay begins at death through autolysis, the breakdown of tissues by the body's own enzymes, and putrefaction by bacteria, which releases cadaverine and putrescine, the chief source of the [[Odor|odor]] of decaying tissue. Rates depend on temperature, moisture, oxygen and soil pH.

=== KEY: Deductive_reasoning_535676
HEADWORD: Deductive reasoning
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Inductive reasoning | Psychology
ENTRY:
Form of reasoning in which a conclusion follows logically from its premises. An inference is valid if the premises cannot be true while the conclusion is false, and an argument is sound if it is valid and all its premises are true. A classic example infers "Socrates is mortal" from the premises "all men are mortal" and "Socrates is a man." Deductive logic studies when an argument is valid, using rules of inference such as modus ponens and modus tollens; invalid deductive arguments are called formal fallacies. Unlike [[Inductive reasoning|inductive]] reasoning, deduction gives certainty within its premises but adds no information beyond them. Research in the [[Psychology|psychology]] of reasoning finds that people draw valid inferences more successfully from modus ponens than from modus tollens, and that concrete cases are easier than abstract ones. Natural deduction is a proof system built on self-evident rules of inference.

=== KEY: Defecation_f967c2

Biological process by which organisms eliminate solid, semisolid or liquid waste, known as [[Feces|feces]], from the digestive tract through the [[Anus|anus]] or [[Cloaca|cloaca]]. === KEY: Deflation_03b2ca
HEADWORD: Deflation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Debt | Great Depression | Inflation
ENTRY:
Decline in the general price level of goods and services, measured by broad price indices. Deflation is a monetary phenomenon, explained by changes in the money supply and velocity relative to spending on output. It is distinct from [[Inflation|disinflation]], a slowdown in inflation that remains positive. Because deflation increases the real value of [[Debt|debt]], a large unexpected deflationary shock is generally considered a problem in a modern economy. When prices are falling, consumers may delay purchases, which idles productive capacity and reduces investment in a deflationary spiral. Deflation also results when rising productivity lowers prices. Some analyses of 17 countries from 1870 to 1999 find no strong causal link between deflation and depressions, while during the [[Great Depression]] prices in the United States fell by about 25%.

=== KEY: Deforestation_3103c9
HEADWORD: Deforestation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Biodiversity | Wildfire
ENTRY:
Removal and destruction of forest for conversion to non-forest use, such as farms, ranches or urban development. Forests cover about 31% of Earth's land surface, one-third less than before the expansion of [[Agriculture|agriculture]]. Agriculture directly causes nearly 90% of global deforestation as of 2025, mainly through cropland expansion and pasture creation, and plantations of coffee, palm oil and rubber also drive clearing. Livestock grazing, logging, urbanization, mining and [[Wildfire|wildfires]] contribute. Deforestation causes habitat destruction and [[Biodiversity|biodiversity]] loss, extinction, and displacement of indigenous people, and it reduces the uptake of carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Since 1990, about 420 million hectares of forest have been lost, although the estimated rate fell from 16 million hectares per year in the 1990s to 10 million hectares per year in 2015–2020.
