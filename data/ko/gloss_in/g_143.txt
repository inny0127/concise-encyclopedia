=== KEY: Video_34e2d1
HEADWORD: Video
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blu-ray | DVD | Film | Latin | Magnetic tape | Smartphone | Sony | Television
ENTRY:
Electronic medium for recording, copying, playback, transmitting, and display of moving visual images, with or without accompanying audio. It was first developed for live transmission and later expanded to recording and storage in analog formats such as [[Magnetic tape|magnetic tape]]. Since the late 20th century, digital video has become the dominant form. The word was coined in 1935 as a visual equivalent of "audio", from the [[Latin]] *video*, "I see".

Video developed from facsimile systems of the mid-19th century. Mechanical scanners such as the Nipkow disk were patented as early as 1884. Unlike [[Film|film]], which records small photographic images, video encodes images electronically. It was first developed for mechanical [[Television|television]], which cathode-ray tube systems quickly replaced. In 1951, a team at Ampex led by Charles Ginsburg produced one of the first practical video tape recorders, which captured live images from television cameras onto magnetic videotape. In 1971, [[Sony]] began selling videocassette recorder decks and tapes to consumers.

Digital video offers higher quality and eventually much lower cost. After the commercial introduction of the [[DVD]] in 1997 and the [[Blu-ray]] Disc in 2006, sales of videotape and recording equipment fell. Inexpensive computers and [[Smartphone|smartphones]] can now capture and edit digital video. Since 2013, the use of digital cameras in Hollywood has surpassed that of film cameras.

Frame rate ranges from six or eight frames per second for older mechanical cameras to 120 or more for new professional cameras. PAL and SECAM specify 25 frames per second, NTSC 29.97, and film is shot at 24. Interlacing, which captures alternate scan lines as two fields per frame, was invented to reduce flicker without increasing the number of complete frames per second.

=== KEY: Video_camera_e061ee
HEADWORD: Video camera
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bell Labs | Charge-coupled device | MOSFET | Philo Farnsworth | Smartphone | Surveillance | Television | Video
ENTRY:
Optical instrument that captures [[Video|video]], unlike a movie camera, which records images on film. Video cameras were first developed for the [[Television|television]] industry and are now used for [[Surveillance|surveillance]], military and industrial work, and consumer recording. Live feeds show images for immediate viewing, while recorded video has moved from videotape to optical discs, hard disks and flash memory. Electronic camera tubes, including [[Philo Farnsworth|Philo Farnsworth]]'s image dissector, replaced mechanical Nipkow disks by the 1930s and stayed in wide use until the 1980s. Solid-state image sensors, such as the [[Charge-coupled device|charge-coupled device]] and later CMOS sensors, eliminated problems such as image burn-in and made digital video practical. These sensors rest on metal–oxide–semiconductor technology, which originates from the [[MOSFET|MOSFET]], invented at [[Bell Labs|Bell Labs]] in 1959. Modern forms include studio and field cameras, camcorders, pan–tilt–zoom surveillance cameras, webcams and the cameras built into many [[Smartphone|smartphones]].

=== KEY: Video_game_37a57c
HEADWORD: Video game
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Esports | Game Boy | Massachusetts Institute of Technology | Nintendo | Oscilloscope | Sega | Touchscreen | Video game console
ENTRY:
Electronic game involving interaction with a user interface or input device, such as a joystick, controller, keyboard, or motion-sensing device, to generate visual feedback on a display device. The display may be a television set, computer monitor, flat-panel display, [[Touchscreen|touchscreen]], or virtual reality headset. Games are categorized by hardware platform (arcade, console, computer, and more recently mobile, virtual and augmented reality, and cloud gaming) and by genre.

An early example is a "cathode-ray tube amusement device", patented by Thomas T. Goldsmith Jr. and Estle Ray Mann (filed January 25, 1947; issued December 14, 1948). Others include *OXO* (1952), *Tennis for Two* (1958), which William Higinbotham engineered with an [[Oscilloscope|oscilloscope]] display, and *Spacewar!* (1962), written by [[Massachusetts Institute of Technology]] students. The first consumer video game was the arcade game *Computer Space* in 1971. In 1972 came *Pong* and the first home console, the Magnavox Odyssey. The "golden age" of arcade games ran from the late 1970s to the early 1980s, but loss of publishing control and market saturation led to the crash of the North American video game market in 1983.

By the late 1980s, [[Video game console|video game consoles]] were dominated by Japanese companies such as [[Nintendo]], [[Sega]], and later Sony, while Microsoft Windows and MS-DOS came to dominate computer gaming, and multiplayer online games helped establish [[Esports|eSports]]. Nintendo achieved dominance in handheld gaming with the [[Game Boy]] line. By the 2000s Sega had exited the console market, leaving Nintendo, Sony, and Microsoft as the "big three". Independent "indie" games gained prominence in the 2010s. As of 2020, the global market had estimated annual revenues of US$159 billion, three times the global music industry and four times the film industry in 2019. Some reviews have estimated addiction prevalence among heavy users at 1-10%, a subject of controversy.

=== KEY: Vietnam_fdef6d
HEADWORD: Vietnam
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ASEAN | First Indochina War | French Indochina | Han dynasty | Hanoi | Ho Chi Minh City | Mainland Southeast Asia | Vietnam War
ENTRY:
Socialist republic (pop. over 102 million) on the eastern edge of [[Mainland Southeast Asia]]. Its area is about 331,000 sq km, and it is the world's 16th-most populous country. Vietnam is bordered by China, Laos, and Cambodia and lies along the South China Sea. The capital is [[Hanoi]]; the largest city is [[Ho Chi Minh City]].

States arose in the first millennium BC on the Red River Delta. The [[Han dynasty]] annexed northern and central Vietnam, which remained under Chinese rule from 111 BC until the first dynasty emerged in 939. Successive dynasties expanded south to the Mekong Delta, conquering Champa. The last imperial dynasty, the Nguyễn, surrendered to France in 1883, and in 1887 the territory was integrated into [[French Indochina]]. The communist-led Viet Minh declared independence in 1945. France was defeated in the [[First Indochina War]] in 1954, and the country was divided in two. The [[Vietnam War]] followed between communist North Vietnam and South Vietnam, supported by the United States. After the North Vietnamese victory in 1975, the country reunified in 1976 under the Communist Party of Vietnam (CPV).

A planned economy, a Western trade embargo, and wars with Cambodia and China crippled the country until the CPV launched reforms in 1986 creating a socialist-oriented market economy. Vietnam is now an upper-middle-income economy and a one-party state. Observers cite corruption, censorship, and restrictions on political pluralism and expression. Vietnam belongs to [[ASEAN]] and the WTO.

=== KEY: Viking_Age_e82007
HEADWORD: Viking Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Battle of Stamford Bridge | Cnut | Iceland | Kievan Rus' | Middle Ages | Normandy | Saga | Vikings
ENTRY:
Period of the [[Middle Ages]], about 800–1050, of large-scale raiding, colonizing, conquest, and trading by Norsemen, known as [[Vikings]]. Traditional accounts place the beginning at the attack of June 8, 793, that destroyed the abbey on Lindisfarne, off Northumberland, and the end at the defeat of the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada at the [[Battle of Stamford Bridge]] in 1066.

Voyaging from Denmark, Norway, and Sweden, the Norse settled in the British Isles, Ireland, the Faroe Islands, [[Iceland]], Greenland, [[Normandy]], and along the Dnieper and Volga trade routes. They briefly settled in Newfoundland, becoming the first Europeans to reach North America; the voyage is dated to 1021. They founded kingdoms and earldoms including York, Dublin, and [[Kievan Rus']]. In the 11th century the Norse homelands were unified into larger kingdoms, and the short-lived North Sea Empire of [[Cnut]] included large parts of Scandinavia and Britain.

Proposed causes of expansion include wealthy towns and monasteries overseas, weak kingdoms, overpopulation, lack of good farmland, political strife arising from the unification of Norway, and innovations in sailing.

Knowledge comes largely from texts written by those the Vikings encountered, from archaeology, and from the Icelandic [[Saga|sagas]]. Regarded as barbaric by the late 18th century, the age was reassessed by scholars outside Scandinavia from the 1890s, who recognized the Vikings' artistry, technological skills, and seamanship.

=== KEY: Village_abc634
HEADWORD: Village
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Human settlement | India | Indonesia | Industrial Revolution | Rural area | Subsistence agriculture | Town | Urbanization
ENTRY:
[[Human settlement]] larger than a hamlet but smaller than a [[Town|town]], with dwellings fairly close together rather than dispersed across the landscape. Some geographers describe villages as having between 500 and 2,500 inhabitants. Villages are often in [[Rural area|rural areas]] and are normally permanent.

Villages originated with the shift from nomadic hunter-gatherer life to sedentary life about 12,000 years ago in the Levant, where groups such as the Natufians built permanent stone houses. They were long a usual form of community for societies practicing [[Subsistence agriculture|subsistence agriculture]] and for some non-agricultural societies. Homes were grouped for sociability and defense, with the surrounding land farmed. In Great Britain, a hamlet earned the right to be called a village when it built a church. The [[Industrial Revolution]] drew people to mills and factories and turned many villages into towns and cities, and [[Urbanization|urbanization]] continues.

Usage and administrative status vary widely. In [[India]], 69% of the population (around 833 million people) lived in villages at the 2011 census, which counted 649,481 villages; 236,004 had fewer than 500 inhabitants and 3,976 had 10,000 or more. In Kazakhstan, 42.7% of citizens (7.5 million people) lived in 8172 villages (*auyl*) in 2009. In China and Taiwan, villages are divisions under townships or towns. Japan has 183 villages, a number that is decreasing through mergers. In Southeast Asia the *kampung* is the traditional village; in [[Indonesia]], villages are called *kampung* or *desa*, or *kelurahan* where urban, and a *desa* is administered by customary law (*adat*).

=== KEY: Virgil_3b28ee

ENTRY:
Roman poet of the Augustan period, whose full name was Publius Vergilius Maro; also spelled Vergil. Born in the village of Andes, near Mantua in Cisalpine Gaul, he composed three of the most famous poems in [[Latin literature]]: the *Eclogues* (or *Bucolics*), the *Georgics*, and the epic *[[Aeneid]]*.

The *Eclogues* are ten pastoral poems modeled on Theocritus, exploring rural life, love, and politics. The *Georgics*, composed around 29 BC, is a didactic poem in four books on agriculture, viticulture, animal husbandry, and beekeeping. Virgil was close to [[Horace]]. According to tradition, he asked on his deathbed at Brundisium that the *Aeneid* be burned, but his executors Varius and Tucca published it with minimal changes.

Biographical details come from later ancient *vitae* by Donatus, Servius, and others, drawing on a lost work by Suetonius; some rest on inferences from his poetry and are considered problematic.

Virgil was acclaimed in his lifetime and became a standard school text, remaining the most popular Latin poet through late antiquity, the [[Middle Ages]], and early modernity. In Dante's [[Divine Comedy]] he appears as the author's guide through Hell and Purgatory. [[T. S. Eliot]] began his lecture "What Is a Classic?" by asserting that any definition of a classic must reckon with Virgil.

=== KEY: Virus_a94d49
HEADWORD: Virus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Microbiology | Plasmid | RNA | Tobacco mosaic virus | Vaccine
ENTRY:
Submicroscopic infectious agent that replicates only inside the living cells of an organism. Viruses infect all life forms, from animals and plants to bacteria and archaea, and are found in almost every ecosystem on Earth. The first virus discovered, in the 1890s, was the [[Tobacco mosaic virus|tobacco mosaic virus]]; since then more than 16,000 of the millions of virus species have been described in detail. The study of viruses is virology, a subspecialty of [[Microbiology|microbiology]].

Outside a host cell, a virus exists as a viral particle, or virion, consisting of genetic material (long molecules of [[DNA]] or [[RNA]]); a protein coat, the capsid; and in some cases an outer envelope of lipids. Most virions are too small to be seen with an optical microscope, and are one-hundredth the size of most bacteria. An infected host cell is often forced to produce thousands of copies.

The origins of viruses are unclear, since they do not form fossils. Hypotheses include descent from small parasitic cells that lost genes, and escape of genetic material from larger organisms, such as from [[Plasmid|plasmids]]. Viruses are an important means of horizontal gene transfer. Some biologists consider them a life form because they carry genetic material, reproduce, and evolve through natural selection, though they lack characteristics such as cell structure; they have been described as "organisms at the edge of life."

Transmission routes include insect vectors such as aphids; coughing and sneezing, as with influenza, smallpox, and measles; the faecal–oral route, as with norovirus; and sexual contact and infected blood, as with HIV. Animal immune responses usually eliminate the infection, and [[Vaccine|vaccines]] produce immunity artificially.

=== KEY: Visual_arts_32a88f

ENTRY:
Art forms perceived chiefly through sight, including [[Painting|painting]], drawing, printmaking, sculpture, ceramics, photography, video, filmmaking, design, crafts, and [[Architecture|architecture]]. The applied arts, such as industrial, graphic, fashion, and interior design and decorative art, are also included, and disciplines such as performing arts, conceptual art, and textile arts involve aspects of the visual arts.

Current usage covers fine art as well as applied arts and crafts, but this was not always so. Before the [[Arts and Crafts movement]] at the turn of the 20th century, the term "artist" had for centuries often been restricted to practitioners of the fine arts such as painting and sculpture, and art schools maintained that a craftsperson could not be considered an artist. Both Western and East Asian art have tended to privilege painting, and to a lesser degree sculpture, as the arts that rely most on imagination and are furthest removed from manual labor. In Chinese painting, the valued "scholar-painting" was in theory practiced by gentleman amateurs.

Training began as apprentice and workshop systems, typically lasting 2 to 6 years; in many European and North American nations, academies later grew into universities and institutes. Museums and galleries serve as informal education.

Drawing makes a two-dimensional image by marking a surface with tools such as graphite pencils, pen and ink, charcoal, pastels, and markers, or with digital tools. Upper Paleolithic art includes figurative art beginning at least 40,000 years ago, with cave sites at Lascaux, Altamira, Maros, and Gabarnmung. Ancient Egyptians drew in ink on potsherds and limestone ostraca, and Greek vase painting developed black-figure pottery in the 6th century BC. With paper brought by Islamic traders from China, drawing on paper increased in the Middle East and Europe in the 14th century, and artists such as Raphael, Michelangelo, and [[Leonardo da Vinci]] treated it as an art in its own right rather than a preparatory stage.

Painting applies pigments suspended in a medium with a binder to a surface such as walls, canvas, or paper. The earliest evidence is natural pigments of ochre, chalk, and charcoal applied to stone; tools for processing pigments for body painting date to about 200,000 years ago in Zambia, and cave paintings to at least 50,000 years ago. Later traditions developed fresco, tempera (egg yolk, animal glue, or milk), and encaustic (hot wax), while ink wash painting became a major East Asian tradition. Oil paint on wood panels, later on stretched canvas, permitted shading and glazing and the use of chiaroscuro. In the 19th century the box easel and collapsible paint tube allowed *plein air* painting outdoors.

Movements since the Renaissance include Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Impressionism, Cubism, Abstract Expressionism, and Pop Art. Contemporary practice often incorporates performance, installation, and new media.

=== KEY: Visual_impairment_a81c49

=== KEY: Vitamin_8331ee
HEADWORD: Vitamin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Enzyme | Metabolism | Royal Navy | Scurvy | Vitamin C | Vitamin D
ENTRY:
Organic molecules, or sets of closely related molecules called vitamers, essential in small quantities to an organism for proper [[Metabolism|metabolic]] function, such as [[Vitamin C]]. They cannot be synthesized in sufficient quantities for survival and must be obtained through consumption. Most vitamins are groups of vitamers; vitamin E has eight, four tocopherols and four tocotrienols.

Major health organizations list thirteen vitamins: the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K, and the water-soluble vitamin C and eight B vitamins. Their functions are diverse. Vitamin A regulates cell and tissue growth. [[Vitamin D]] acts like a hormone, regulating mineral metabolism for bones. The B complex vitamins serve as [[Enzyme|enzyme]] cofactors or their precursors. Vitamins C and E are antioxidants, and vitamin K aids blood clotting. Both deficiency and excess can cause illness, and there is little evidence that supplements protect healthy people against heart disease or cancer.

All vitamins were discovered between the 1900s and 1930s, but the effects of deficiency were recognized earlier. Long ocean voyages made [[Scurvy|scurvy]] common among ships' crews. In 1747 the Scottish surgeon James Lind ran a controlled experiment on 12 sailors showing that citrus foods helped prevent it, and his *Treatise on the Scurvy* (1753) recommended lemons and limes, which the British [[Royal Navy]] adopted. In 1884 Takaki Kanehiro of the Imperial Japanese Navy observed that beriberi struck low-ranking crew who ate mostly rice, but not officers with a Western-style diet.

Casimir Funk and Max Nierenstein coined "vitamine" in 1912 from "vital amine"; Jack Cecil Drummond proposed dropping the final "e" in 1920. Commercial vitamin tablets appeared in 1935. Some governments mandate adding vitamins to staple foods such as flour or milk.

=== KEY: Volcano_c1258d
HEADWORD: Volcano
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Holocene | Iceland | Magma chamber | Mauna Loa | Mid-Atlantic Ridge | Mount Fuji | Plate tectonics | Ring of Fire
ENTRY:
Vent or fissure in the crust of a planetary-mass object through which hot lava, volcanic ash, and gases escape from a [[Magma chamber|magma chamber]] below. On Earth, volcanoes lie mostly where tectonic plates diverge or converge, and because most plate boundaries are underwater, most volcanoes are too. The word derives from Vulcano, an island of Italy, and ultimately from Vulcan, the Roman god of fire.

According to [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], most volcanic activity takes place along plate boundaries. At divergent boundaries such as the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]], rising mantle rock partly melts and creates new oceanic crust; these volcanoes are usually nonexplosive. Where a ridge rises above sea level, islands such as [[Iceland]] form. At convergent boundaries, one plate subducts beneath another, and released water lowers the melting temperature of the overlying mantle, producing extremely viscous magma. These volcanoes cause violent eruptions and form volcanic arcs, as around the Pacific [[Ring of Fire]], which holds about 75% of the world's active volcanoes. Volcanoes also form above hotspots, thought to be fed by mantle plumes rising from 3,000 km deep, as in the Hawaiian island chain.

By frequency of eruption, volcanoes are called active, dormant, or extinct. Dormant volcanoes have not erupted since the start of the [[Holocene]], about 12,000 years ago, but may erupt again. About 1,350 potentially active volcanoes exist on land. Principal types include shield volcanoes, broad and gently sloping, such as [[Mauna Loa]]; stratovolcanoes, steep cones of alternating lava and pyroclastic layers, such as [[Mount Fuji]]; cinder cones; and lava domes.

Hazards include lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash falls, and lahars, or volcanic mudflows. Large eruptions can cool the troposphere, and have been followed by volcanic winters causing catastrophic famines. Volcanic soils support dense populations, and volcanoes supply geothermal energy, pumice, and sulfur. Venus has very numerous volcanoes.

Small [[Rodent|rodent]] related to lemmings and hamsters, with about 155 [[Species|species]]. Voles grow to 8–23 cm (3–9 in), and most do not exceed 16 months of age in the wild. Litters of two to nine offspring are produced monthly during the breeding season, so populations can grow very large quickly. Voles eat small plants, and they can girdle small trees and ground cover. Many predators eat them, including [[Owl|owls]], [[Weasel|weasels]], [[Fox|foxes]] and [[Snake|snakes]].

=== KEY: Volga_72c06e
HEADWORD: Volga
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caspian Sea | Europe | Moscow | Russia | Turkic peoples
ENTRY:
River in [[Russia]], the longest in [[Europe]], flowing into the [[Caspian Sea]]. It is 3,531 km (2,194 mi) long and drains 1,360,000 sq km (530,000 sq mi), the largest drainage basin in Europe. It is also Europe's largest river by average discharge at its delta, between 8,000 and 8,500 cubic meters per second. Because it drains into the endorheic Caspian Sea, the Volga does not naturally connect to any ocean. Five of Russia's ten largest cities, including [[Moscow]], lie in its basin. The name derives from a Proto-Slavic word meaning "wetness", and [[Turkic peoples|Turkic peoples]] formerly called it Itil. Russian literature often calls it "Volga-Matushka" (Mother Volga).

=== KEY: Volga_Bulgaria_15c10c
HEADWORD: Volga Bulgaria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Golden Horde | Islam | Kazan | Khazars | Russia | Sviatoslav I | Volga
ENTRY:
Medieval Bulgar state around the confluence of the [[Volga]] and Kama rivers in what is now European [[Russia]], existing between the 9th and 13th centuries. The Bulgars, an Oghuric people, had come under the overlordship of the [[Khazars]], and the state gained independence after Khazaria was destroyed by [[Sviatoslav I]] in the late 10th century. Volga Bulgaria adopted [[Islam]] as its state religion in 922, 66 years before the Christianization of Kievan Rus'. Its capital, Bolghar, lay about 160 km south of modern [[Kazan]]. The state dominated trade on the Volga, exchanging northern furs and slaves for silks, spices and metals, and it minted silver dirhams. Mongol forces under Batu Khan razed major cities in 1236–1237, and the remnants were integrated into the [[Golden Horde]].

=== KEY: Volgograd_1b76b0

ENTRY:
City on the western bank of the [[Volga]], administrative center of Volgograd Oblast, Russia. Founded as the fortress of Tsaritsyn in 1589, it grew into an important river port and commercial center by the 19th century. It came under Bolshevik control in November 1917 at the start of the [[Russian Civil War]], and in 1925 it was renamed Stalingrad in honor of [[Joseph Stalin]]. During [[World War II]], the [[Battle of Stalingrad]], one of the largest and bloodiest battles of the war, ended in a Soviet victory widely held to be its turning point. [[Nikita Khrushchev]]'s administration renamed the city Volgograd in 1961 as part of de-Stalinization. The city is the site of The Motherland Calls, an 85 m (279 ft) statue honoring the battle's heroes, and it was one of the host cities of the 2018 FIFA World Cup.

Small family car produced by [[Volkswagen]] from 1938 to 2003. Conceived in the 1930s when Adolf Hitler called for a people's car, its bug-like design became a global cultural icon. With 21.5 million units produced at twenty locations, it was the best-selling car of a single platform. ENTRY:
German automobile brand, commonly abbreviated VW, based in Wolfsburg, [[Lower Saxony]]. Established in 1937 by the German Labour Front, a [[Nazi Party]] organization, to produce a low-cost car for German families, the company was revived after World War II by British Army officer Ivan Hirst. The name derives from the German words Volk ('people') and Wagen ('wagon'). As the Volkswagen Group, the firm now encompasses 12 brands, including [[Audi]], Porsche and Bentley, and employs approximately 672,000 people. Its Beetle, which sold over 21 million units by 1974, was among the best-selling cars in history. In 2015 the company admitted equipping about 11 million diesel vehicles with software that reduced emissions only during laboratory tests, a scandal that led to over $30 billion in fines, recalls and settlements. The company has since invested heavily in battery-electric vehicles.

=== KEY: Volleyball_c26e25
HEADWORD: Volleyball

ENTRY:
Team sport in which two teams of six players are separated by a net. Teams score points by grounding the ball on the opponent's court under organized rules. William G. Morgan, a [[YMCA]] physical education director in Holyoke, Massachusetts, invented the game in 1895 as a less physically demanding alternative to [[Basketball]], originally calling it *mintonette*. The international governing body, the Fédération Internationale de Volleyball (FIVB), was established in 1947. Volleyball has been part of the [[Summer Olympic Games]] since Tokyo 1964, and [[Beach volleyball]] was added at Atlanta in 1996. Sitting volleyball is the adapted version played at the [[Paralympic Games]]. In standard indoor play, matches are the best of five sets; the first four are played to 25 points and the deciding set to 15.
