=== KEY: Dye_276524
HEADWORD: Dye
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Georgia (country) | Neolithic | Pigment | Tyrian purple
ENTRY:
Soluble colored substance, natural or synthetic, used to color textiles and other materials. Unlike [[Pigment|pigments]], which are insoluble, dyes are usually soluble in a solvent and can chemically bind to the material they color. Textile dyeing dates back to the [[Neolithic]] period, and dyed flax fibers from a prehistoric cave in the [[Georgia (country)|Republic of Georgia]] date to 36,000 BP. Scarce natural dyes such as [[Tyrian purple]] were prized luxury items in the ancient and medieval world. The first synthetic dye, *mauveine*, was discovered by William Henry Perkin in 1856, starting a surge in synthetic dyes and in organic chemistry. Most dyes used in the modern world are synthetic, made from petrochemicals, while natural dyes come mainly from plants.

=== KEY: Dynamical_system_7c3d9e
HEADWORD: Dynamical system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chaos theory | Dynamical systems theory | Ordinary differential equation | Pendulum | Real number
ENTRY:
Mathematical model of the time dependence of a point in an ambient space, such as in a parametric curve. Examples include the swinging of a clock [[Pendulum|pendulum]], the flow of water in a pipe and the number of fish in a lake each spring. A dynamical system has a state, often given by a tuple of [[Real number|real numbers]] or by a vector in a manifold, and an evolution rule that describes which future states follow from the current state. The rule is often deterministic, though some systems are stochastic. The most general definition unifies concepts such as [[Ordinary differential equation|ordinary differential equations]] and ergodic theory. The study of dynamical systems is the focus of [[Dynamical systems theory]], and dynamical systems are a fundamental part of [[Chaos theory]].

=== KEY: Dynamical_systems_theory_f0c549
HEADWORD: Dynamical systems theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chaos theory | Classical mechanics | Dynamical system
ENTRY:
Area of mathematics describing the long-term qualitative behavior of complex [[Dynamical system|dynamical systems]], usually by employing differential equations or difference equations. When differential equations are used, the theory is called continuous dynamical systems; when difference equations are used, it is called discrete. From a physical point of view, continuous dynamical systems generalize [[Classical mechanics]], in which the equations of motion are postulated directly and need not derive from a least action principle. The field studies systems such as planetary orbits, electronic circuits, and systems in biology and economics. Its central goals include finding the fixed points, or steady states, of a system and determining whether long-term behavior depends on initial conditions. Much modern research focuses on chaotic systems, and the branch that investigates chaos is called [[Chaos theory]].

Explosive made of [[Nitroglycerin|nitroglycerin]], sorbents such as powdered shells or clay, and stabilizers. Swedish chemist [[Alfred Nobel]] invented it in 1866 and [[Patent|patented]] it the following year. It was the first safely manageable explosive stronger than traditional black powder-based explosives, and it was widely adopted as a safer and more robust alternative. Today it remains in use for controlled blasting in mining and demolition, though it has largely been supplanted by safer alternatives such as [[Ammonium nitrate|ammonium nitrate-based explosives]].

=== KEY: E_mathematical_constant_219c09
HEADWORD: E
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christiaan Huygens | Jacob Bernoulli | Leonhard Euler | Natural logarithm
ENTRY:
Mathematical constant that is the base of the natural logarithm and the exponential function, approximately 2.718281828459045. It plays important and recurring roles across mathematics, comparable in significance to π or the imaginary unit i. Like π, it is irrational (not a ratio of integers) and transcendental (not a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients); [[Leonhard Euler]] is credited with proving irrationality in 1737 and Charles Hermite transcendence in 1873. It is sometimes called Euler's number.

The constant is the limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ as n approaches infinity, the sum of the infinite series 1/0! + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + …, and the unique positive number a such that the graph of y = aˣ has a slope of 1 at x = 0. It is the base of the [[Natural logarithm|natural logarithm]], and the function eˣ equals its own derivative.

The first calculations involving it appeared in 1618 in a table in an appendix to a work on logarithms by John Napier; the table listed natural logarithms without recognizing the base. In 1661 [[Christiaan Huygens]] calculated a quantity that is, in retrospect, the base-10 logarithm of e. [[Jacob Bernoulli]] introduced the constant itself in 1683 while studying continuous compounding of interest: $1.00 at 100% annual interest becomes $2.00 if credited once a year, $2.25 twice, and $2.718281828… with continuous compounding. Euler began using e in 1727 or 1728, and it first appeared in print in his *Mechanica* (1736); why he chose the letter is unknown.

The constant also arises in probability: if a gambler plays n times a slot machine that pays out with probability one in n, the probability of losing every bet approaches 1/e, about 36.79%, as n grows.

=== KEY: Ear_2fe8f2
HEADWORD: Ear
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Embryo | Hearing | Hearing loss | Sound | Tinnitus | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
[[Vertebrate]] organ of [[Hearing|hearing]] and, in mammals, of balance through the vestibular system. In humans it has three parts. The outer ear consists of the auricle, the visible outer part, and the ear canal, which stretches for about 2.5 cm and ends at the eardrum; skin in the canal produces earwax, which migrates outward and so keeps the canal clean. The middle ear is an air-filled tympanic cavity containing the three ossicles, the malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), and stapes (stirrup), which transmit vibrations from the eardrum to the inner ear and amplify sound by nearly 15–20 times. The stapes is the smallest named bone in the body. The middle ear connects to the throat at the nasopharynx through the Eustachian tube.

The inner ear sits in the bony labyrinth. The semicircular canals enable balance and eye tracking when moving, the utricle and saccule enable balance when stationary, and the cochlea enables hearing. Its sensitivity spans about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz in young adults. Because the two ears are arranged symmetrically, the brain can localize [[Sound|sound]] by comparing arrival times and intensities from each ear.

The ear develops in the early [[Embryo|embryo]] from the first pharyngeal pouch and six small swellings called otic placodes, derived from the ectoderm. Infection and traumatic damage may affect it, and disease of the ear may lead to [[Hearing loss|hearing loss]], [[Tinnitus|tinnitus]], and balance disorders such as vertigo. Human ears have been adorned with earrings and other jewelry in numerous cultures for thousands of years and have been subjected to surgical and cosmetic alterations.

=== KEY: Early_modern_period_d9a376
HEADWORD: Early modern period
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Atlantic slave trade | Late modern period | Middle Ages | Reformation | Renaissance | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
Historical period, c. 1500–c. 1800, with divisions based primarily on the history of Europe and the broader concept of modernity. No exact date marks its beginning or end, and its extent varies with the area of history studied. It is generally considered to have started at the beginning of the 16th or 17th century and to have ended in the 18th or 19th. In a European context it follows the [[Middle Ages]] and precedes the advent of modernity, though there is no universal agreement on these boundaries. Proposed starting points include the start of the [[Renaissance]], the printing revolution of the 1440s, the Fall of Constantinople in 1453, the [[Age of Discovery]], and the start of the [[Reformation]]. The transition into the [[Late modern period]] in the late 18th century was marked by the Industrial Revolution, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution, along with Napoleon's rise to power.

The concept grew from the traditional European division into ancient, medieval, and modern periods, made since the time of Petrarch. The term was first proposed to denote a distinct period by the medieval historian Lynn Thorndike in his 1926 work *A Short History of Civilization*; it spread through economic history in the 1940s and 1950s and became widely known among scholars during the 1990s. Attempts to apply it beyond Europe began by the 1980s, with scholars using it for societies such as Tokugawa Japan. Historian Jerry H. Bentley defined the early modern world as the era of about 1500 to 1800 in which cross-cultural interactions increasingly linked peoples throughout the world. He identified the connection of the world by sea, global biological exchanges, and the creation of an "early capitalist global economy" as its three main processes. Some historians have criticized the periodization as Eurocentric and as underemphasizing contemporaneous developments in non-Western regions, such as the Mughal and Ottoman empires.

Feudalism declined in Europe, and religious unity under the Roman Catholic Church ended. The Reformation provoked a backlash that expanded the Inquisition and led to the European wars of religion, including the Thirty Years' War (1618–48), which ended with the Peace of Westphalia. European colonization of the Americas began, and with it the [[Atlantic slave trade]]. The period also saw the Commercial Revolution, the [[Scientific Revolution]], the Golden Age of Piracy, and the rise of mercantilism. Voyages included those of Christopher Columbus in 1492 and Ferdinand Magellan from 1519 to 1522; Nicolaus Copernicus's heliocentric model dates to 1543 and Isaac Newton's *Principia Mathematica* to 1687. Russia reached the Pacific coast in 1647, and Western Europe greatly surpassed China in technology and per capita wealth, a development known as the Great Divergence.

=== KEY: Earth_5cdd21
HEADWORD: Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Great Oxidation Event | Moon | Ocean | Old English | Plate tectonics | Solar System | Sun
ENTRY:
Third planet from the [[Sun]] and the only astronomical object known to harbor life, at a mean distance of 149.6 million km. It is the only ocean world in the [[Solar System]] sustaining liquid surface water; the [[Ocean|ocean]] covers 70.8% of Earth's crust, and the remaining 29.2% is dry land. Earth is rounded into an ellipsoid with a circumference of about 40,000 km and an equatorial diameter of 12,756 km. It is the densest planet in the Solar System and the largest and most massive of the four rocky planets. Its mass is 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg.

The crust consists of slowly moving tectonic plates whose interaction produces mountain ranges, volcanoes, and earthquakes (see [[Plate tectonics]]). A liquid outer core generates a magnetosphere that deflects most of the solar wind and cosmic radiation. The atmosphere, composed primarily of nitrogen and oxygen, protects the surface from most meteoroids and ultraviolet light. Water vapor and other greenhouse gases, particularly carbon dioxide (CO₂), maintain the current average surface temperature of 14.76 °C.

Earth takes about 365.25 days to orbit the Sun and rotates in about 23 hours and 56 minutes. Its axis is tilted, producing seasons. It has one permanent natural satellite, the [[Moon]], which orbits at 384,400 km, is roughly a quarter as wide as Earth, helps stabilize the axis, causes tides, and gradually slows Earth's rotation. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago from gas and dust in the early Solar System. The leading hypothesis holds that the Moon formed after a Mars-sized object named Theia collided with Earth. The ocean formed, and life developed, during the first billion years. Life has altered the atmosphere and surface, leading to the [[Great Oxidation Event]] two billion years ago. Humans emerged 300,000 years ago in Africa and have spread across every continent; they depend on the biosphere and natural resources, and their activity has increasingly affected the planet's environment, including its climate and biosphere, with widespread extinctions.

The Modern English word *earth* developed, via Middle English, from the [[Old English]] noun *eorðe*, which has cognates in every Germanic language and translated the many senses of Latin *terra* and Greek *gē*. The name *Terra* is occasionally used in scientific writing.

=== KEY: Earth_s_crust_6d8792
HEADWORD: Earth's crust
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basalt | Feldspar | Granite | Lithosphere | Plate tectonics | Pyroxene | Quartz | Rock (geology)
ENTRY:
Outermost shell of [[Rock (geology)|rock]], comprising less than one percent of Earth's radius and volume. It is the top component of the [[Lithosphere]], which is broken into [[Plate tectonics|tectonic plates]] and lies on the denser mantle. Temperature rises with depth, by as much as 30 °C (54 °F) per kilometer in the upper crust. Continental crust is 25–70 km (about 15–44 mi) thick and composed of less dense, more felsic rocks such as [[Granite]]. Oceanic crust is 5–10 km thick and composed primarily of denser, more mafic rocks such as [[Basalt]]. The most abundant minerals in continental crust are [[Feldspar]], about 41% by mass, followed by [[Quartz]] at 12% and [[Pyroxene]] at 11%.

=== KEY: Earth_s_inner_core_0fb591
HEADWORD: Earth's inner core
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Earth's magnetic field | Moon | Oxygen | Sulfur | Sun
ENTRY:
Innermost geologic layer of [[Earth]], a solid ball of iron–nickel alloy. It has a radius of about 1,230 km (760 mi), roughly 20% of Earth's radius and 70% of the [[Moon|Moon's]] radius. Its surface temperature is estimated at about 5,700 K (5,430 °C; 9,800 °F), similar to the surface of the [[Sun]], and it remains solid because of the high pressure at its depth. Lighter elements such as [[Oxygen|oxygen]] and [[Sulfur|sulfur]] are thought to be present along with iron and nickel. The inner core was deduced in 1936 by the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann from seismograms of earthquakes in New Zealand. Its rigidity was confirmed in 1971, and in 2005 shear waves were detected passing through it. It grows by about 1 millimeter per year as material from the outer core solidifies at its boundary, and its interaction with the convecting outer core helps sustain the geodynamo that generates [[Earth's magnetic field]].

=== KEY: Earth_s_magnetic_field_474a5c
HEADWORD: Earth's magnetic field
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Compass | Earth | Earth's outer core | Mid-ocean ridge | Van Allen radiation belt
ENTRY:
Magnetic field extending from [[Earth|Earth's]] interior into space, also called the geomagnetic field. It is generated by electric currents in the convecting molten iron and nickel of the [[Earth's outer core|outer core]], a process called a geodynamo. In a 2019 survey its strength at the surface ranged around 30,000 to 60,000 nT (0.30 to 0.60 G). The field approximates a magnetic dipole tilted about 11° from Earth's rotational axis. The magnetic poles usually lie near the geographic poles and drift slowly, but at irregular intervals averaging several hundred thousand years the field reverses. Reversals are recorded in igneous rocks as stripes on [[Mid-ocean ridge|mid-ocean ridges]], which helps paleomagnetists track the motion of continents. The magnetosphere deflects most of the solar wind, which would otherwise strip away the ozone layer, and traps charged particles in the [[Van Allen radiation belt|Van Allen radiation belts]]. Humans have used [[Compass|compasses]] for direction-finding since the 11th century.

=== KEY: Earth_s_mantle_768015
HEADWORD: Earth's mantle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crust (geology) | Mid-ocean ridge | Plate tectonics
ENTRY:
Layer of silicate rock between Earth's [[Crust (geology)|crust]] and outer core. It makes up about 67% of Earth's mass and 84% of its volume, and it is about 2,900 km (1,800 mi) thick. Predominantly solid, it behaves on geologic time scales as a viscous fluid, sometimes compared to caramel. The mantle is divided into the upper mantle, a transition zone at depths of about 410 to 660 km, and the lower mantle. The upper mantle is composed mainly of peridotite. Partial melting at [[Mid-ocean ridge|mid-ocean ridges]] produces oceanic crust, and partial melting at subduction zones produces continental crust. Convection within the mantle drives [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]] and volcanism.

Path of [[Earth]] around the [[Sun]], an ellipse with the Earth–Sun barycenter as one focus and an eccentricity of 0.0167. === KEY: Earth_s_outer_core_9ccf4a
HEADWORD: Earth's outer core
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's magnetic field | Iron | Nickel | Oxygen | Sulfur
ENTRY:
Fluid layer of mostly [[Iron|iron]] and [[Nickel|nickel]], about 2,260 km (1,400 mi) thick, lying between Earth's inner core and mantle. It begins about 2,889 km (1,795 mi) beneath the surface and ends at 5,150 km (3,200 mi) at the inner core boundary. The core remains liquid because there is not enough pressure to solidify it. Its density is 5 to 10 percent lower than that of iron at Earth's core temperatures and pressures, so lighter elements such as [[Sulfur|sulfur]] and [[Oxygen|oxygen]] are thought to be present. As of 2023, estimates put sulfur at 1.7 percent and oxygen at 0.8 to 5.3 percent of the outer core by weight. Convection in the fluid generates [[Earth's magnetic field]] through a geodynamo.

=== KEY: Earth_s_rotation_a29598
HEADWORD: Earth's rotation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Moon | Sun
ENTRY:
Spin of [[Earth]] around its axis. Earth turns once in about 24 hours relative to the [[Sun]], and once in 23 hours, 56 minutes and 4 seconds relative to distant stars. Tidal effects of the [[Moon|Moon]] have slowed the rotation, so the day has lengthened by about 2.3 milliseconds per century since the 8th century BCE. Scientists reported in 2020 that Earth had begun to rotate faster. At the equator the rotational speed is about 1,674.7 km/h (1,040.6 mph), decreasing with latitude to zero at the poles.

=== KEY: Earth_science_a5e3f8
HEADWORD: Earth science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climatology | Earthquake | Geology | Hydrology | Meteorology | Oceanography | Plate tectonics | Volcano
ENTRY:
Collective term for the fields of natural science related to planet Earth, also called geoscience. It deals with the physical, chemical, and biological constitution of, and linkages among, Earth's four spheres: the biosphere, hydrosphere/cryosphere, atmosphere, and geosphere (lithosphere). It can be generalized into planetary science.

[[Geology]] is broadly the study of Earth's structure, substance, and processes, largely of the lithosphere. Its subfields include geochemistry, geophysics, paleontology, geomorphology, structural geology, mineralogy, and petrology. The mantle beneath the crust is heated by the radioactive decay of heavy elements, and its convection moves the lithospheric plates, a process known as [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Plate boundaries are divergent, convergent, or transform. [[Earthquake|Earthquakes]] result from plate movement, often near convergent boundaries, and [[Volcano|volcanoes]] result primarily from the melting of subducted crust. In 1906, seismic wave analysis revealed Earth's molten core.

Atmospheric science developed in the late 19th century as a means to forecast weather through [[Meteorology|meteorology]]; atmospheric chemistry developed in the 20th century to measure air pollution and expanded in the 1970s in response to acid rain. [[Climatology]] studies climate and climate change. The atmosphere has five layers, with 75% of its mass in the lowest, the troposphere, and consists of about 78.0% nitrogen, 20.9% oxygen, and 0.92% argon. Water vapor and CO₂ hold the Sun's energy through the greenhouse effect, making the surface warm enough for liquid water and life. The magnetic field, created by internal motions of the core, produces the magnetosphere, which protects the atmosphere from the solar wind.

[[Hydrology]] studies the hydrosphere and the movement of water, with subdisciplines including [[Oceanography|oceanography]], hydrogeology, ecohydrology, and glaciology. The earliest exploitation of groundwater dates back to 3000 BC, and hydrogeology as a science was developed beginning in the 17th century.

=== KEY: Earthquake_5143a0
HEADWORD: Earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1556 Shaanxi earthquake | 1960 Valdivia earthquake | 1976 Tangshan earthquake | Fault (geology) | Lithosphere | Modified Mercalli intensity scale | Richter scale | Tsunami
ENTRY:
Shaking of the Earth's surface resulting from a sudden release of energy in the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]] that creates seismic waves. Also called a quake, tremor, or temblor, an earthquake ranges from events too weak to be felt to those violent enough to destroy entire cities. The initial point of rupture is the hypocenter, or focus; the point at ground level directly above it is the epicenter.

Most earthquakes are caused by the rupture of geological [[Fault (geology)|faults]]; volcanic activity, landslides, mine blasts, and nuclear tests can also produce them, and human activities such as mining and fluid injection can induce smaller ones. Under the elastic-rebound theory, the sides of a fault lock at irregularities, strain builds as the plates keep moving, and the stored energy is released suddenly when the stress breaks through. Over 90% of earthquakes occur in the circum-Pacific seismic belt or along other convergent and transform margins.

Size is measured on the moment magnitude scale, which supersedes the [[Richter scale]] for larger events; shaking intensity is assessed with scales such as the [[Modified Mercalli intensity scale]]. Effects include ground shaking, soil liquefaction, and landslides, and an offshore event may displace the seabed enough to cause a [[Tsunami|tsunami]].

The [[1960 Valdivia earthquake|1960 earthquake]] in Chile, at 9.5 magnitude, is the largest measured on a seismograph. The [[1976 Tangshan earthquake]] in China, which killed between 240,000 and 655,000 people, was the deadliest of the 20th century. The [[1556 Shaanxi earthquake]] killed more than 100,000 people, many in collapsing hillside dwellings. Precise prediction remains elusive; risk reduction relies on preparedness, seismic retrofitting, and earthquake engineering.

=== KEY: Earthworm_b65000
HEADWORD: Earthworm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algae | Annelid | Bacteria
ENTRY:
Soil-dwelling [[Annelid|annelid]] invertebrate, the largest members of the class Oligochaeta. Earthworms live in moist, compost-rich soil and feed on detritus, protozoa, nematodes, [[Bacteria|bacteria]], fungi and [[Algae|algae]], making them among the most important detritivores in nature. Adults range from 10 mm to 3 m in length depending on species. The body is a segmented tube within a tube, usually with setae on all segments, and it lacks a true skeleton, relying instead on fluid-filled coelom chambers that act as a hydrostatic skeleton. Earthworms breathe through the skin. Most are hermaphrodites that exchange sperm when mating, but many species, particularly in the family Lumbricidae, reproduce by parthenogenesis. Their excreta also serve as food, and their burrowing and castings help maintain soil fertility.

=== KEY: East_Asia_f74e1c

Geocultural region of Asia comprising [[China]], [[Japan]], [[Mongolia]], [[North Korea]], [[South Korea]], and [[Taiwan]], plus China's special administrative regions of [[Hong Kong]] and Macau. It borders North Asia to the north, Southeast Asia to the south, South Asia to the southwest, and Central Asia to the west, with the Pacific Ocean to its east. About 1.65 billion people live in East Asia, nearly 33% of the population of continental Asia and 20% of the global population. Major religions include Buddhism (mostly Mahayana), Confucianism, Taoism, and Chinese folk religion; Shinto in Japan; and Christianity and Musok in Korea. The major languages are Mandarin Chinese, Japanese, and Korean. The region has 76 officially recognized minority or indigenous ethnic groups, 55 of them native to mainland China.

The Battle of Stalingrad, the bloodiest battle of the war, was a key turning point, and the Battle of Kursk crippled German offensive capabilities. Historian Geoffrey Roberts noted that more than 80 percent of all combat in the Second World War took place on the Eastern Front. Nazi military operations included mass deportations, starvation and massacres. The United States and United Kingdom provided substantial material aid to the Soviet Union through Lend-Lease, though they sent no ground troops.

=== KEY: Eastern_Orthodox_Church_610bd0
HEADWORD: Eastern Orthodox Church
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Second-largest Christian body in the world after the [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic Church]], officially the Orthodox Catholic Church and also known as the Greek Orthodox Church, with an estimated 260 to 300 million adherents.

It is a communion of autocephalous churches, each governed by its bishops through local synods, with no central authority analogous to the [[Pope|pope]]. The Ecumenical Patriarch of [[Constantinople]] is recognized as *primus inter pares* ("first among equals"), a title held by the patriarch of Rome before the [[East–West Schism]]. Its theology rests on the Scriptures and holy tradition, which includes the decrees of the seven ecumenical councils and the teaching of the Church Fathers. The church holds that it is the one, holy, catholic, and apostolic church established by Jesus Christ, with bishops who are successors of the apostles. The churches of Constantinople, Alexandria, Jerusalem, and Antioch shared communion with the Church of Rome until the schism of 1054, the culmination of theological, political, and cultural disputes, particularly over the authority of the pope. Since 2018 an ongoing schism has divided Constantinople and [[Moscow]], which are not in full communion.

Eastern Orthodoxy is the primary confession in [[Russia]], Ukraine, Romania, Greece, Belarus, Serbia, Bulgaria, Georgia, Moldova, North Macedonia, Cyprus, and Montenegro. Roughly half of its adherents live in post-Eastern Bloc countries, mostly in Russia; communities in North America, Western Europe, and Australia arose through diaspora, conversion, and missionary activity.

=== KEY: Eastern_philosophy_b37396
HEADWORD: Eastern philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhist philosophy | Chinese philosophy | Confucius | Hindu philosophy | Indian philosophy | The Buddha | Upanishads | Vedas
ENTRY:
Collective term, also Asian or Oriental philosophy, for the philosophies that originated in East, Southeast, and South Asia. It includes [[Chinese philosophy]], Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese philosophy, which are dominant in East Asia, and [[Indian philosophy]], which is dominant in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Tibet, Japan, and Mongolia.

Indian philosophy comprises the ancient traditions (*darshana*, "world views") of the Indian subcontinent, including [[Hindu philosophy]], Jain philosophy, and [[Buddhist philosophy]]. Among the earliest surviving philosophical texts are the [[Upanishads]] of the later [[Vedas|Vedic]] period (1000–500 BC). Central concepts include dharma, karma, samsara, moksha, and ahimsa. Indian thinkers developed systems of epistemology and logic and wrote on political philosophy, as in the *Arthashastra* (c. 4th century BC).

Schools are classified as orthodox or heterodox depending on whether they accept the Vedas as a valid source of knowledge, the premises of Brahman and Atman, or an afterlife. The six major orthodox schools are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta; the five major heterodox schools are Jain, Buddhist, Ajivika, Ajnana, and Carvaka. Samkhya is a dualist tradition holding that the universe consists of *purusha* (consciousness) and *prakriti* (matter); the closely related Yoga school emphasized meditation and liberation and, unlike most Samkhya thinkers, allowed for the existence of God. Buddhism mostly disappeared from India after the Muslim conquest, surviving in the Himalayan regions and south India.

Chinese philosophy arose chiefly in the Zhou dynasty. Along with Indian thought, it flourished in the Axial Age of the 6th to 5th centuries BC, when figures such as [[The Buddha|the Buddha]] and [[Confucius]] articulated paths to self-realization and social harmony.

Astronomical event in which an object is temporarily obscured by passing into another body's shadow or between it and the viewer. === KEY: Ecliptic_813794

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Celestial sphere | Eclipse | Solar System | Sun
ENTRY:
Apparent path of the [[Sun|Sun]] on the [[Celestial sphere|celestial sphere]], resulting from Earth's orbit around the Sun. === KEY: Ecological_niche_41f15b
HEADWORD: Ecological niche
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Competition (biology) | Ecology | Habitat
ENTRY:
Concept in ecology describing the specific environmental conditions a species or population occupies. The term was coined in 1910 by Roswell Hill Johnson. Joseph Grinnell first used it in research in 1917, defining a niche by the [[Habitat|habitat]] a species lives in and its accompanying behavioral adaptations. In 1927 Charles Sutherland Elton emphasized the role a species plays in its biotic environment and its use of resources. G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined the niche in 1957 as the set of environmental variables and their ranges within which a species can persist. In niche theory, species with overlapping niches should [[Competition (biology)|compete]], and coexistence requires differences between their niches. The concept is central to ecological biogeography, which studies spatial patterns of [[Ecology|ecological]] communities.

=== KEY: Ecology_879605
HEADWORD: Ecology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biodiversity | Biology | Biome | Biosphere | Ecosystem | Ernst Haeckel | Habitat | Natural selection
ENTRY:
Natural science that studies the interactions between organisms and their environments. It considers organisms at the community, ecosystem, and biosphere levels and overlaps with biogeography, evolutionary biology, genetics, and ethology.

Ecology is a branch of [[Biology|biology]] concerned with the abundance, biomass, and distribution of organisms in their environmental context. It encompasses life processes, interactions, and adaptations; the movement of materials and energy through living communities; the successional development of ecosystems; cooperation, competition, and predation within and between species; and patterns of biodiversity and their effect on ecosystem processes. The term, from Ancient Greek *oîkos* ("house") and *-logía* ("study of"), was coined in 1866 by the German scientist [[Ernst Haeckel]]. The science as it is known today began with a group of American botanists in the 1890s, and concepts of adaptation and [[Natural selection|natural selection]] are cornerstones of modern ecological theory.

To make the field manageable, the biological world is organized into a hierarchy running from organisms to populations, guilds, communities, [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]], [[Biome|biomes]], and the [[Biosphere|biosphere]]. The hierarchy exhibits non-linear behavior: small changes to critical variables, such as the number of nitrogen fixers, can lead to disproportionate and perhaps irreversible changes in the system. The two main subdisciplines differ in paradigm. Population (or community) ecology focuses on the distribution and abundance of organisms, while ecosystem ecology focuses on fluxes of materials and energy. Ecosystems, in turn, vary from tiny to vast: several generations of an aphid population can live and die on a single leaf, and each aphid supports diverse bacterial communities.

Several concepts are central to the field. [[Biodiversity]] describes the diversity of life from genes to ecosystems and includes species, ecosystem, and genetic diversity. A species' [[Habitat|habitat]] is the environment over which it occurs and the type of community that forms there. The niche concept dates to 1917; in 1957 G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined it as the set of biotic and abiotic conditions in which a species can persist and maintain stable population sizes, and it is divided into fundamental and realized niches. Organisms also modify their habitats, from a beaver pond to decaying logs, a process called niche construction. Biomes categorize regions of the Earth's ecosystems mainly by the structure and composition of their vegetation and include tropical rainforest, temperate forests, taiga, tundra, hot desert, and polar desert.

Ecology has practical applications in conservation biology, wetland management, natural resource management, and human ecology. Studies of fishery collapses caused by overexploitation exceeding reproductive rates have informed resource management.

=== KEY: Econometrics_b3019f

ENTRY:
Application of statistical methods to economic data to give empirical content to economic relationships. A basic tool is the multiple linear regression model, still the most frequently used starting point for an analysis. Econometric theory develops estimators with desirable properties such as unbiasedness, efficiency and consistency, while applied econometrics uses data to assess economic theories, develop models, analyze economic history and forecast. === KEY: Economic_growth_dbbb0f
HEADWORD: Economic growth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gross domestic product | Industrial Revolution
ENTRY:
Increase in the quantity and quality of the goods and services a society produces, usually measured as the inflation-adjusted growth of [[Gross domestic product|gross domestic product]] or GDP per capita. Growth from more efficient use of inputs is called intensive; growth from larger inputs, such as a bigger population or new territory, is extensive. Increases in labor productivity have historically been the most important source of real per capita growth. Before the [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]], technological progress raised population rather than income, a condition known as the Malthusian trap.

=== KEY: Economic_inequality_09a125

Distribution of income, wealth or consumption among individuals or groups within or between nations. === KEY: Economic_policy_0a3810

Set of government actions that set taxation, budgets, the money supply, interest rates and labor market regulation. Most instruments are divided into fiscal policy, which concerns government revenue and spending, and monetary policy, which concerns central bank actions on the money supply and interest rates. === KEY: Economics_7e1e3f
HEADWORD: Economics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Hesiod | Inflation | John Stuart Mill | Macroeconomics | Microeconomics | Nobel Prize | Social science
ENTRY:
[[Social science]] that studies the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. It examines the behavior and interactions of economic agents and how economies work. [[Microeconomics]] analyzes individual agents, such as households, firms, buyers, and sellers, and markets; [[Macroeconomics|macroeconomics]] analyzes economies as systems in which production, distribution, consumption, savings, and investment interact, along with the factors of production (labor, capital, land, and enterprise), [[Inflation|inflation]], economic growth, and public policy. Other broad distinctions are between positive economics, which describes "what is," and normative economics, which advocates "what ought to be"; between economic theory and applied economics; between rational and behavioral economics; and between mainstream and heterodox economics. Economic analysis is applied to business, finance, health care, government, and such subjects as crime, education, the family, law, politics, religion, war, and the environment.

The earlier term was "political economy"; "economics" has been common since the late 19th century. It derives from Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, the "way (*nomos*) to run a household (*oikos*)." Some economic historians have called the Boeotian poet [[Hesiod]] the "first economist" for his writings on the distribution of resources.

Definitions have varied. [[Adam Smith]] (1776) described political economy as "an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations," Jean-Baptiste Say (1803) as the science of the production, distribution, and consumption of wealth, and [[John Stuart Mill]] (1844) as the science tracing the laws of social phenomena arising from the combined operations of mankind for the production of wealth. Thomas Carlyle (1849) coined "the dismal science" as an epithet for classical economics. Alfred Marshall (1890) called economics "a study of man in the ordinary business of life." Lionel Robbins (1932) defined it as "the science which studies human behavior as a relationship between ends and scarce means which have alternative uses," a formulation that has been called perhaps the most commonly accepted. Because it focuses on a form of behavior rather than a subject matter, it allowed economic analysis to extend to areas such as war; from the 1960s, rational-choice modeling, advanced by Gary Becker among others, broadened the field further. Critics have called the definition overly broad or noted that scarcity does not account for the macroeconomics of high unemployment. Several [[Nobel Prize|Nobel Prize]] winners, including James M. Buchanan and Ronald Coase, rejected Robbins's method-based definition in favor of subject-matter definitions like Say's, and Ha-Joon Chang has argued that no other science defines itself by a single method.

Schools of thought include the classical, neoclassical, Keynesian, and Austrian, which offer differing explanations of growth, business cycles, and the effects of policy. Economics informs policy on trade, money, and regulation, and economists continue to debate its predictive record, including its failure to anticipate the 2008 crisis, and the influence of ideology on the field.

=== KEY: Economy_8afc1f
HEADWORD: Economy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Babylonia | Currency | Gross domestic product | Industrial Revolution | Stock exchange | Sumer | Supply and demand
ENTRY:
Area of the production, distribution, and trade, as well as consumption, of goods and services. An economy is a set of interrelated human practices and transactions whose main factors include culture, values, education, technology, history, social organization, political structure, legal systems, and natural resources. Transactions occur when two parties agree on the price of a good or service, commonly expressed in a [[Currency|currency]], though monetary transactions account for only a small part of the economic domain.

The word derives from Middle French *yconomie*, Medieval Latin *oeconomia*, and Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, from *oikos* ("house") and *nemein* ("to manage").

[[Sumer]] developed a large-scale economy based on commodity money, and the [[Babylonia|Babylonians]] and neighboring city-states developed rules and laws on debt, contracts, and private property. In the Middle Ages most exchange occurred within social groups, but voyages by Marco Polo, Christopher Columbus, and Vasco da Gama led to a first global economy. The first [[Stock exchange|stock exchange]] was founded in Antwerp in 1513.

[[Adam Smith]] is regarded as the first economist in the modern sense. He held that goods are offered at a natural price generated by competition, or [[Supply and demand|supply and demand]], and by the division of labor, and that self-interest is the basic motive for free trade. The [[Industrial Revolution]] of the 18th and 19th centuries brought major changes in agriculture, manufacturing, mining, and transport.

Economies are classified as market systems, in which decentralized decisions coordinated by prices allocate resources; command systems, dominated by central planning; and mixed systems. Output is commonly measured by [[Gross domestic product|gross domestic product]] (GDP), though critics note that it overlooks unpaid household labor and environmental degradation.

Socialist market economy of the People's Republic of China, the second-largest by nominal [[Gross domestic product|GDP]]. Market reforms introduced under [[Deng Xiaoping|Deng Xiaoping]] in 1978 opened the economy to foreign investment. Growth has faced challenges in the 2020s from a property crisis.

=== KEY: Ecosystem_47e317
HEADWORD: Ecosystem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biome | Climate | Decomposition | Invasive species | Photosynthesis | Pollination | Pollution | Sustainable development
ENTRY:
Community of living organisms together with the nonliving components of their environment, linked through nutrient cycles and energy flows. Ecosystem processes are the transfers of energy and materials from one pool to another and take place across a wide range of scales.

The term was first used in 1935 by the British ecologist Arthur Tansley, who devised the concept to draw attention to transfers of materials between organisms and their environment; it was coined by Arthur Roy Clapham at Tansley's request.

Ecosystems are controlled by external and internal factors. External factors, including [[Climate|climate]], control an ecosystem's structure but are not influenced by it; climate determines the [[Biome|biome]] in which an ecosystem is embedded. Internal factors, including [[Decomposition|decomposition]], the species present, disturbance, and succession, both control and are controlled by ecosystem processes. An ecosystem's tendency to remain near equilibrium is termed resistance, and its capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganize while retaining the same function, structure, and identity is termed ecological resilience.

Plants bring energy into the system through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]; animals move matter and energy by feeding on plants and one another; and decomposers return carbon to the atmosphere and make nutrients available again to plants and microbes.

Ecosystems provide goods such as water, food, fuel, and medicinal plants, and services such as cleaning air and water, maintaining atmospheric oxygen, and crop [[Pollination|pollination]]. Many are degraded by human impacts including soil loss, [[Pollution|pollution]], habitat fragmentation, and [[Invasive species|invasive species]]; an ecosystem that has lost its defining features is considered "collapsed." Restoration can contribute to achieving the [[Sustainable development|Sustainable Development]] Goals.

Complication of pregnancy in which the [[Embryo|embryo]] attaches outside the [[Uterus|uterus]], most often in a fallopian tube. Symptoms classically include abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding, and the [[Fetus|fetus]] cannot survive. Risk factors include [[Chlamydia|chlamydia]] infection, [[Smoking|smoking]] and [[Endometriosis|endometriosis]]. Diagnosis relies on blood tests for human chorionic gonadotropin and on ultrasound. === KEY: Ecumenical_Patriarchate_of_Constantinople_1e20ec

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Autocephalous [[Eastern Orthodox Church|Eastern Orthodox]] church headed by the Ecumenical Patriarch of [[Constantinople]]. The see holds special honor as the former capital of the Eastern Roman Empire and mother church of most modern Eastern Orthodox churches, and its Ecumenical Patriarch holds the status of *primus inter pares* (first among equals) among the world's Eastern Orthodox primates. In 330 AD, [[Constantine the Great]] moved his residence to the city, renaming it Nova Roma, and at the First Council of Constantinople in 381 the see was recognized as the fourth ancient patriarchate, after [[Antioch]], [[Alexandria]] and [[Rome]]. Its 2019 grant of autocephaly to the Orthodox Church of Ukraine led to schisms, including a break in communion with the [[Russian Orthodox Church]], which disputes the see's appellate authority. The reopening of the Theological School of Halki, closed by Turkish authorities in 1971, is a prominent 21st-century issue.

=== KEY: Ecumenism_a58553
HEADWORD: Ecumenism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | League of Nations
ENTRY:
Principle that Christians of different denominations should cooperate toward closer relations and greater unity. The adjective *ecumenical* applies to any interdenominational initiative that promotes such cooperation, and ecumenists regard baptism and faith in Jesus as the basis of Christian unity, citing John 17:20–23. In 1920, the Ecumenical Patriarch of the Eastern Orthodox Church, Germanus V of Constantinople, urged closer cooperation among separated Christians and proposed a "League of Churches" parallel to the newly founded [[League of Nations]]. In 1937, Christian leaders resolved to establish the World Council of Churches, which today includes most major Christian traditions as full members, while the [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic Church]] participates as an observer. Critics argue that ecumenism risks doctrinal compromise and prioritizes institutional unity over confessional distinctives.
