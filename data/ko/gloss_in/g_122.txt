=== KEY: Sex_education_b334f4
HEADWORD: Sex education
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of consent | Birth control | Puberty | Sexually transmitted infection | United States
ENTRY:
Instruction on issues relating to human sexuality. It covers human sexual anatomy, sexual activity, [[Birth control|birth control]], safe sex, reproductive rights, emotional relationships and the [[Age of consent|age of consent]]. Comprehensive sexuality education covers all of these issues, whereas abstinence-only education focuses solely on promoting sexual abstinence and is often favored in socially conservative regions, including parts of the [[United States]]. Programs may be delivered in schools, through public health campaigns, or by parents. Many governments see it as beneficial to provide education before or at the beginning of [[Puberty|puberty]], to improve public health, limit the spread of [[Sexually transmitted infection|sexually transmitted infections]] and avoid teenage pregnancy. In the late 19th century, progressive educators introduced sex education in North American schools as "social hygiene". The first sex education curriculum in Singapore, from 1966 to 1973, emphasized birth control as a way to avoid overpopulation.

=== KEY: Sexism_d1c42c
HEADWORD: Sexism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conscription | Gender | Misogyny | Oxford English Dictionary | Rape | Sexual harassment | Stereotype | Talcott Parsons
ENTRY:
Prejudice or discrimination based on one's sex or gender. Sexism can affect anyone but primarily affects women and girls. It has been linked to [[Gender|gender]] roles and [[Stereotype|stereotypes]] and may include the belief that one sex or gender is intrinsically superior to another. Extreme sexism may foster [[Sexual harassment]], [[Rape|rape]], and other forms of sexual violence. It may arise from social or cultural customs and norms.

Definitions differ by field. Peter Glick and Susan Fiske coined the term ambivalent sexism for stereotypes that can be both hostile and benevolent. The feminist author bell hooks defines it as a system of oppression that disadvantages women, and philosopher Kate Manne sees it as the branch of a patriarchal order that makes patriarchal arrangements seem natural or inevitable. Functionalist sociologists such as [[Talcott Parsons]] understood gender inequality as the natural outcome of a dimorphic model of gender.

According to legal scholar Fred R. Shapiro, the term was most likely coined on November 18, 1965, by Pauline M. Leet at Franklin and Marshall College. The [[Oxford English Dictionary]] cites a print appearance in 1866. Historical examples include written laws barring women from the political process in ancient Rome,. Some scholars suggest sexism and [[Misogyny|misogyny]] helped fuel the witch trials of the 15th to 18th centuries.

Some commentators stress that men also face sexism, citing legal biases, social expectations, and [[Conscription|conscription]]. Whether disparities such as earnings gaps mainly reflect discrimination, or occupational choice and hours worked, is disputed.

=== KEY: Sextant_032bc0
HEADWORD: Sextant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Greenwich Mean Time | Latitude | Longitude | Moon | Polaris | Sun
ENTRY:
Doubly reflecting navigation instrument that measures the angle between two visible objects. Its primary use is to measure the altitude of a celestial body above the horizon, such as the [[Sun]] at noon or [[Polaris]] at night, to estimate [[Latitude|latitude]]. Measuring the lunar distance between the [[Moon]] and another body gives [[Greenwich Mean Time]] and hence [[Longitude|longitude]]. Its frame is a sector of about one-sixth of a circle (60°), hence the name from the Latin for "one sixth". Unlike the backstaff, it allows direct observation of stars, and because it does not depend on electricity or GPS, it is a practical back-up for ships. The principle was first implemented around 1731 by John Hadley and Thomas Godfrey. In 1922, Carlos Viegas Gago Coutinho adapted it for aeronautical navigation.

=== KEY: Sexual_dimorphism_811ca2

Difference in form, color or behavior between the sexes of a species. It occurs in most dioecious species, which consist of most animals and some plants. === KEY: Sexual_orientation_cfa5d8
HEADWORD: Sexual orientation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Asexuality | Bisexuality | Heterosexuality | Homosexuality | Psychology | Sociology
ENTRY:
Enduring personal pattern of romantic or sexual attraction to persons of the opposite sex or gender, the same sex or gender, or more than one gender. Patterns are generally categorized as [[Heterosexuality|heterosexuality]], [[Homosexuality|homosexuality]], and [[Bisexuality|bisexuality]]. [[Asexuality|Asexuality]], experiencing no sexual attraction to others, is sometimes identified as a fourth category, though there is significant debate over whether it is a sexual orientation.

Orientation is distinguished from sexual identity, a person's conception of themselves, and from behavior, the sexual acts actually performed. According to the American Psychological Association, it is also distinct from biological sex, gender identity, and social gender role. The term "sexual preference" largely overlaps with it but may suggest voluntary choice, whereas orientation is not a choice.

No single theory of cause has gained widespread support, but scientists favor biological theories, and the evidence for nonsocial causes is considerably stronger than for social ones, especially for males. A major hypothesis implicates the prenatal environment, specifically the organizational effects of hormones on the fetal brain. There is no substantive evidence that parenting or early childhood experiences play a role. Heritability estimates of 30–50% have been reported, and longitudinal studies show some fluidity, particularly in women, with 10–20% of individuals reporting shifts over time. Across cultures most people are heterosexual; in Western nations surveys find 90–95% of adults predominantly heterosexual, about 2–4% homosexual, and 1–3% bisexual.

The subject is studied primarily within biology, [[Anthropology|anthropology]], and [[Psychology|psychology]], including sexology, and also within [[Sociology|sociology]], history, and law.

=== KEY: Sexual_reproduction_b3593e
HEADWORD: Sexual reproduction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Bacteria | Egg cell | Eukaryote | Meiosis | Mutation | Prokaryote | Sperm
ENTRY:
Type of reproduction involving a life cycle in which two haploid gametes, such as a [[Sperm|sperm]] and an [[Egg cell|egg cell]], fuse to form a zygote with two sets of chromosomes. It is the most common life cycle in multicellular [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]], including animals, fungi and plants. It does not occur in [[Prokaryote|prokaryotes]] such as [[Bacteria]] and [[Archaea]], though some bacterial gene-transfer processes resemble it. In eukaryotes, [[Meiosis]] produces haploid cells, and genetic recombination increases diversity among offspring. The process is believed to have developed in an ancient eukaryotic ancestor. Its evolution is considered paradoxical, because asexual populations can grow faster; each sexual organism passes on only 50% of its genes. However, sexual reproduction increases genetic diversity and impedes the accumulation of harmful [[Mutation|mutations]].

=== KEY: Sexually_transmitted_infection_32d677
HEADWORD: Sexually transmitted infection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chlamydia | Condom | Gonorrhea | HIV/AIDS | Herpes | Infertility | Syphilis | Vaccination
ENTRY:
Infection spread by sexual activity, especially vaginal intercourse, anal sex, oral sex, or sometimes manual sex; also called sexually transmitted disease (STD) or venereal disease. The term "infection" is generally preferred because it includes cases without symptomatic disease. Many STIs cause no initial symptoms, which creates a risk of transmission. Symptoms may include vaginal or penile discharge, genital ulcers, and pelvic pain, and some STIs can cause [[Infertility|infertility]].

Bacterial STIs include [[Chlamydia|chlamydia]], [[Gonorrhea|gonorrhea]], and [[Syphilis|syphilis]]. Viral STIs include genital warts, genital [[Herpes|herpes]], hepatitis B, human papillomavirus (HPV), and [[HIV/AIDS]]. Parasitic STIs include trichomoniasis, scabies, and pubic lice, and a fungal ringworm strain is an emerging infection. Most bacterial and parasitic STIs are treatable and curable with antibiotics, although gonorrhea faces growing antimicrobial resistance. Viral infections such as HIV and genital herpes are not curable but can be managed. HPV and hepatitis B can be prevented by [[Vaccination|vaccination]]. Using [[Condom|condoms]], having fewer sexual partners, mutual monogamy, and comprehensive sex education also reduce risk.

Diagnostic tests are usually easily available in the developed world but often not in the developing world. In 2015, STIs excluding HIV caused 108,000 deaths worldwide, and about 1.1 billion people had STIs other than HIV/AIDS. About 500 million had syphilis, gonorrhea, chlamydia, or trichomoniasis, at least 530 million had genital herpes, and 290 million women had HPV. Documentation of STIs dates back to at least the Ebers Papyrus (c. 1550 BC).

=== KEY: Seyfert_galaxy_c00df9
HEADWORD: Seyfert galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edwin Hubble | Milky Way | Quasar | Supermassive black hole
ENTRY:
Class of active galaxy with a quasar-like nucleus. Seyfert galaxies are one of the two largest groups of active galaxies, along with quasar host galaxies. Unlike [[Quasar|quasars]], their host galaxies are clearly detectable. They account for about 10% of all galaxies, and their nuclei are thought to be powered by [[Supermassive black hole|supermassive black holes]] surrounded by accretion discs. Seen in visible light most resemble normal spirals, yet their cores can be as luminous as whole galaxies the size of the [[Milky Way]]. The first Seyfert galaxies were detected in 1908 by Edward A. Fath and Vesto Slipher, and [[Edwin Hubble]] classified such objects as extragalactic in 1926. The class is named after Carl Seyfert, who described it in 1943.

ENTRY:
Epic poem by [[Ferdowsi]], the national epic of Greater Iran. Written between c. 977 and 1010 CE, its some 50,000 couplets make it one of the world's longest epic poems and the longest created by a single author. It tells the mainly mythical but partly historical past of Iran from the creation of the world to the Muslim conquest in the seventh century. Ferdowsi based much of it on a prose work, the Khwadāy-Nāmag, a late [[Sasanian Empire|Sasanian]] compilation of the kings of Persia. Traditional historiography holds that it was meant to preserve Persia's golden days for a new generation. Written in Early New Persian, it is central to Persian language and identity, and it is celebrated in [[Iran]], [[Afghanistan]], [[Tajikistan]] and the wider region, including [[Turkey]] and [[Uzbekistan]].

One of the major [[Hinduism|Hindu]] traditions, worshipping [[Shiva]] as the supreme being. Its followers, called Shaivas, number about 385 million, mostly in [[India]], [[Sri Lanka]] and [[Nepal]]. === KEY: Shamanism_06181d
HEADWORD: Shamanism
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Spiritual practice in which a practitioner, the shaman, interacts with the spirit world through altered states of consciousness such as trance. The usual goal is to direct spirits or spiritual energies into the physical world for healing, divination, or other help to people.

The word derives from Russian *shaman*, which comes from a Tungusic term, possibly from the Evenki or Manchu language. It is sometimes connected to a Tungus root meaning "to know", a link the linguist Juha Janhunen questions. Mircea Eliade suggested the [[Sanskrit]] *śramaṇa*, a wandering monastic figure whose word spread across Central Asia with [[Buddhism]], as a possible ultimate origin. Anthropologists have no single agreed definition. Western anthropologists later applied the term broadly to unrelated practices in Asia, Africa, Australasia, and the Americas. Critics say this misapplies the words to the varied spiritual practices of [[Indigenous peoples]], each of which has its own terms. Studies report trance-related brain changes similar to those in meditation or hypnosis and psychological benefits such as reduced anxiety, but claims of literal contact with spirits lack verifiable evidence beyond subjective experience.

Domesticated ruminant mammal (*Ovis aries*) kept as [[Livestock|livestock]] for wool, meat, and milk. There were around 1.3 billion domestic sheep as of 2021. Sheep's wool is the most widely used animal fiber and is usually harvested by shearing. Lamb is meat from younger animals and mutton from older ones. Ewes range from 20–41 kg in the Soay to 77–113 kg in the Suffolk, and rams are typically 50 to 60 percent heavier than ewes. Sheep figure in many mythologies, such as the Golden Fleece, and in the Abrahamic religions, and are used as sacrificial animals. Australia, New Zealand, and the British Isles are most associated with sheep production, while China and India host among the largest flocks by count.

=== KEY: Shen_Kuo_2e1b03
HEADWORD: Shen Kuo
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Camera obscura | Compass | Fossil | Geomorphology | Hangzhou | Song dynasty | Surveying
ENTRY:
Chinese polymath, scientist, and statesman of the Northern [[Song dynasty]]. Born in Qiantang, modern [[Hangzhou]], he passed the imperial examinations and became a finance minister, state inspector, head of the Bureau of Astronomy, Assistant Minister of Imperial Hospitality, and academic chancellor. Politically he belonged to the Reformist New Policies Group led by Chancellor Wang Anshi. Political setbacks led to his retirement in 1085.

His main work, the *Dream Pool Essays* (*Mengxi Bitan*, 1088), made him the first to describe the magnetic needle [[Compass|compass]] used for navigation. He recognized true north through magnetic declination. In [[Astronomy|astronomy]] he improved the armillary sphere, gnomon, and sighting tube and invented a new type of inflow water clock. With his colleague Wei Pu he planned a five-year project of daily observations to map the orbits of the Moon and planets, which political opponents thwarted.

He proposed a hypothesis of land formation, an early account of [[Geomorphology|geomorphology]], based on inland marine [[Fossil|fossils]], soil erosion, and silt deposition. From petrified bamboos preserved underground in a dry northern region, he proposed gradual climate change. He was the first literary figure in China to mention the drydock. Though not the first to invent the [[Camera obscura|camera obscura]], he noted the relation between the focal point of a concave mirror and that of the pinhole. He wrote extensively on the movable type of Bi Sheng, and his writings preserved its legacy. An ancient crossbow mechanism he unearthed proved to be a Jacob's staff, a [[Surveying|surveying]] tool not known in Europe until 1321.

He also wrote on divination and the supernatural. Much of his other writing has not survived.

=== KEY: Shia_Islam_de83d4
HEADWORD: Shia Islam
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Second-largest branch of [[Islam]], defined by the belief that [[Muhammad]] designated his cousin and son-in-law [[Ali|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] (r. 656–661) as his successor.

Shia Muslims hold that Ali was the rightful political successor (caliph) and the divinely guided spiritual leader (imam) of the Muslim community. They maintain that his right was unjustly usurped at the meeting of Saqifa, where [[Abu Bakr]] (r. 632–634) was chosen as caliph. Sunni Muslims accept Abu Bakr, [[Umar]] (r. 634–644), and [[Uthman]] (r. 644–656). Shia Muslims cite Muhammad's sermon at Ghadir Khumm as a clear designation of Ali; Sunni scholars tend to read it as a general affirmation of his merit. The Shia emerged as a distinct movement during the First Fitna (656–661). Scholars debate their origins: some Western historians describe early Shia Islam as merely a political faction, while others call that view anachronistic.

Shia Muslims believe the imamate continued through Ali's sons, Hasan and Husayn, after whom various branches emerged. Central to Shia devotion is reverence for the ahl al-bayt, the family of Muhammad, regarded as infallible inheritors of his knowledge and spiritual authority. Holy sites include the shrines of Ali in Najaf and Husayn in Karbala; mourning rituals commemorate Husayn's death in 680. The principal branches are Twelverism, Isma'ilism, and Zaydism. Twelvers, estimated at 85–90% of Shia Muslims, hold that twelve imams succeeded Ali. The [[Fatimid Caliphate]] (909–1171) was a Shia state.

Shia Muslims are estimated at 10–13% of the world's Muslims, roughly 200–260 million. They form a majority in [[Iran]], Iraq, and Azerbaijan and almost half the Muslim population of Lebanon and Bahrain. Iran is the only country where Shia Islam officially underlies both the legal framework and the system of governance.

=== KEY: Shinto_e2b288
HEADWORD: Shinto
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amaterasu | Buddhism | Divination | Empire of Japan | Japan | Kojiki | New religious movement | World War II
ENTRY:
Religion of [[Japan]] centered on the veneration of kami, supernatural entities believed to inhabit all things.

Shinto is often regarded by its practitioners as Japan's indigenous religion and as a nature religion. It has no central authority, single creator, or specific doctrine. The kami are worshipped at household shrines (kamidana) and public shrines (jinja). Priests called kannushi staff the public shrines and oversee offerings to the kami, intended to cultivate harmony and solicit blessing. Other rituals include kagura dances, rites of passage, and kami festivals; shrines also offer [[Divination|divination]]. Purity, pursued through ritual washing, is a major concern. Little emphasis falls on moral codes or afterlife beliefs, although the dead can become kami.

Historians debate when Shinto became a distinct religion. Kami veneration has been traced to Japan's Yayoi period (300 BC–AD 250). [[Buddhism]] entered Japan at the end of the Kofun period and spread rapidly; the two became functionally inseparable in a process called shinbutsu-shūgō. Kami worship was first recorded in the 8th-century [[Kojiki]] and *Nihon Shoki*.During the Meiji era (1868–1912), nationalist leaders expelled Buddhist influence and formed State Shinto, which some historians regard as the origin of Shinto as a distinct religion; citizens were encouraged to worship the emperor as a kami, though the emperor's descent from [[Amaterasu]] was a longstanding tradition. Shinto spread to other parts of East Asia with the [[Empire of Japan]]. After Japan's defeat in [[World War II|World War II]], it was formally separated from the state.

Shinto is numerically Japan's largest religion, with Buddhism second. Most Japanese take part in both Shinto and Buddhist activities, and aspects of Shinto appear in various [[New religious movement|new religious movements]].

=== KEY: Ship_038783
HEADWORD: Ship
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Large watercraft designed for travel across the surface of a body of water, carrying cargo or passengers, or supporting specialized tasks such as warfare, oceanography, and fishing.

Ships are generally distinguished from [[Boat|boats]] by size, shape, load capacity, and purpose, but there is no universally accepted distinction. A common notion holds that a ship can carry a boat but not the reverse. Some large vessels are traditionally called boats, notably [[Submarine|submarines]]. The word has meant either simply a large vessel or, from the mid-18th century, specifically a full-rigged ship with three or more masts, all square-rigged.

The earliest historical evidence of boats is found in Egypt in the 4th millennium BC. Model ships from [[Mesopotamia]] date to the same period. The [[Austronesian peoples]], who originated in what is now [[Taiwan]], used catamarans, outriggers, and crab claw sails to colonize Pacific islands and [[Madagascar]]. For most of history, transport by ship was cheaper, safer, and faster than overland travel, a principle changed only by railways in the mid-19th century and commercial aviation in the second half of the 20th. Rome consumed about 150,000 tons of Egyptian grain a year in the first three centuries AD, delivered by sailing and oared ships.

Ship transport carries the largest portion of world commerce. In 2024, ships had a global cargo capacity of 2.4 billion tons; the three largest classes were dry bulk carriers (43%), [[Tanker (ship)|oil tankers]] (28%), and container ships (14%).

=== KEY: Shirt_3f1f22
HEADWORD: Shirt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American English | Egypt | Giuseppe Garibaldi | Linen | Underwear
ENTRY:
Cloth garment for the upper body, from the neck to the waist. Originally an [[Underwear|undergarment]] worn only by men, it became a catch-all term in [[American English]] for upper-body garments. In British English a shirt has a collar, cuffed sleeves and a full front opening with buttons. The oldest preserved garment in the world is a [[Linen|linen]] shirt from a First Dynasty [[Egypt|Egyptian]] tomb at Tarkan, dated to about 3000 BC. In the Middle Ages it was a plain, undyed garment worn next to the skin. In 1860 European and American women began wearing shirts, when the red Garibaldi shirt, worn by followers of [[Giuseppe Garibaldi]], was popularized by Empress Eugénie of France. Types include the aloha shirt, the camp shirt and the dress shirt, which has a formal collar and a full-length front opening.

=== KEY: Shitao_80b9d6
HEADWORD: Shitao
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Beijing | Calligraphy | Kangxi Emperor | Ming dynasty | Nanjing | Qing dynasty
ENTRY:
Chinese painter, [[Calligraphy|calligrapher]] and Buddhist monk of the early [[Qing dynasty]], born into the imperial clan of the [[Ming dynasty]]. He narrowly escaped the fall of the Ming in 1644 and became a Buddhist monk by 1651. He lived in [[Nanjing]] and Yangzhou in the 1680s and moved to [[Beijing]] in 1690 to seek patronage, without success. He converted to Daoism in 1693 and returned to Yangzhou, where he is said to have greeted the [[Kangxi Emperor]] during the emperor's visit. His landscapes broke with the rigid conventions of the time, using washes, bold brushstrokes and open white space to suggest distance. He stressed a single brushstroke, or primordial line, as the root of all painting. His treatise *Sayings on Painting from Monk Bitter Gourd* repeats these ideas, and his poetry and calligraphy accompany his landscapes.

=== KEY: Shiva_3fbff0
HEADWORD: Shiva
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bali | Brahma | Ganesha | Hinduism | Java | Parvati | Shaivism | Yoga
ENTRY:
Major deity in [[Hinduism]]. In [[Shaivism]], one of the major traditions within Hinduism, Shiva is the Supreme Lord who creates, protects and transforms the universe. He is known as the Destroyer within the Trimurti, the Hindu trinity that also includes [[Brahma]] and Vishnu. Shiva has benevolent and fierce aspects: he is depicted as an omniscient yogi and as a householder with his wife [[Parvati]] and their sons [[Ganesha]] and Kartikeya, and in fierce forms he slays demons. He is regarded as the patron god of [[Yoga|yoga]], meditation and the arts. Though associated with the Vedic storm god Rudra, he may have non-Vedic roots. He is usually worshiped in the aniconic form of the lingam and is revered across India, Nepal, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka and Indonesia, especially in [[Java]] and [[Bali]].

=== KEY: Shivaji_70244d
HEADWORD: Shivaji
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Aurangzeb | Bal Gangadhar Tilak | Brahmin | Deccan sultanates | Maratha Empire | Mughal Empire
ENTRY:
Indian ruler, Chhatrapati of the Marathas from 1674 to 1680. He inherited a fiefdom from his father, a general who served the [[Deccan sultanates]], and it later formed the genesis of the [[Maratha Empire|Maratha kingdom]]. Shivaji offered his services to the Mughal emperor [[Aurangzeb]] to invade the declining Sultanate of Bijapur. After a defeat at the Battle of Purandar, he entered vassalage with the [[Mughal Empire]] and was later conferred the title of Raja. He was crowned Chhatrapati at Raigad Fort in 1674, despite opposition from local [[Brahmin|Brahmins]]. He employed people of all castes and religions, including Muslims and Europeans, and his forces expanded Maratha influence, built forts and created a Maratha navy. Jyotirao Phule revived his legacy about two centuries after his death. Nationalists such as [[Bal Gangadhar Tilak]] celebrated him, and Hindutva activists later adopted him.
