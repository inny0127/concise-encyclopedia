True datum about one or more aspects of a circumstance or occurrence in the real world. The word derives from Latin *factum*, meaning "a thing done". Scientific facts are verified by careful, repeatable observation or measurement, including [[Experiment|experiments]]. In [[Philosophy|philosophy]], a fact is the worldly correlate of a true proposition, a state of affairs that makes the proposition true. In 1870, [[Charles Sanders Peirce]] described four ways people fix belief: tenacity, authority, a priori reasoning and the [[Scientific method|scientific method]].

=== KEY: Factorial_012b11
HEADWORD: Factorial
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Combinatorics | Exponential function | Gamma function | Integer | Leonhard Euler | Permutation | Stirling's approximation
ENTRY:
Product of all positive [[Integer|integers]] less than or equal to a non-negative integer *n*, written *n*!. For example, 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120, and by convention 0! = 1. In [[Combinatorics|combinatorics]], *n*! counts the [[Permutation|permutations]] of *n* distinct objects. Factorials appear in the power series of the [[Exponential function|exponential function]], and [[Stirling's approximation|Stirling's approximation]] approximates them accurately for large numbers. [[Leonhard Euler]] and Daniel Bernoulli extended the factorial to complex numbers, except at negative integers, through the [[Gamma function|gamma function]]. The concept arose independently in several ancient cultures, including Jain literature and the Hebrew mystical text *Sefer Yetzirah*.

Industrial facility, often a complex of buildings filled with machinery, where workers make or process [[Goods|goods]]. === KEY: Fahrenheit_d11904
HEADWORD: Fahrenheit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ammonium chloride | Brine | Cayman Islands | Celsius | Kelvin | United States
ENTRY:
Temperature scale proposed in 1724 by Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit. Its lower reference point, 0 °F, was the freezing temperature of a [[Brine|brine]] of water, ice and [[Ammonium chloride|ammonium chloride]]; the upper point was his estimate of average human body temperature, first set at 90 °F and later at 96 °F. For much of the 20th century the scale was defined by water freezing at 32 °F and boiling at 212 °F at sea level and standard atmospheric pressure, a 180-degree interval. It is now defined through the [[Kelvin]] scale. Fahrenheit remains in use in the [[United States]] and its territories, Palau, the Federated States of Micronesia, the Marshall Islands and the [[Cayman Islands]]. Several other territories and nations use it alongside [[Celsius]], which was developed 18 years later and was called centigrade until 1948.

=== KEY: Falcon_dc5264

ENTRY:
Bird of prey in the genus *Falco*, which includes about 40 species. Falcons are found on every continent except [[Antarctica]]. Adults have thin, tapered wings that allow high speed and rapid changes of direction. Falcons kill prey with a tomial tooth on the beak, while hawks, [[Eagle|eagles]] and other birds of prey of the family Accipitridae use talons. The largest species is the gyrfalcon, up to 65 cm (26 in) long; the smallest is the Seychelles kestrel, at 15–24 cm. As with hawks and owls, females are typically larger than males. The [[Peregrine falcon]] has been recorded diving at 320 km/h (200 mph). The genus was introduced by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758, and falcons are not closely related to other birds of prey; their nearest relatives are [[Parrot|parrots]] and [[Songbird|songbirds]].

=== KEY: Fall_of_the_Western_Roman_Empire_f813e4
HEADWORD: Fall of the Western Roman Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edward Gibbon | Goths | Huns | Late antiquity | Theodosius I | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Loss of central political control in the [[Western Roman Empire]], conventionally dated to 476. In 376 large numbers of [[Goths]] and other non-Roman peoples, fleeing the [[Huns]], entered the empire, and Roman forces could not expel or subjugate them. After [[Theodosius I]] died in 395, the empire was divided between the ministers of his two sons, and the Western armed forces became few and ineffective. In 476 the Germanic king Odoacer deposed the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, and sent the imperial insignia to the Eastern emperor Zeno. The Eastern Roman, or Byzantine, Empire survived for centuries. Historians cite army strength, economic decline, imperial competence, internal struggles, religious change, disease and climate. [[Edward Gibbon]]'s 1776 history of the decline and fall of Rome shaped how the period is written about, and the period is also described as [[Late antiquity|late antiquity]], which emphasizes cultural continuity.

=== KEY: Family_cefc4f
HEADWORD: Family
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Affinity (law) | Consanguinity | Nuclear family | Social order
ENTRY:
Group of people related by [[Consanguinity|consanguinity]] (recognized birth) or [[Affinity (law)|affinity]] (marriage or other relationship). The word derives from Latin *familia*. The family forms the basis for [[Social order|social order]]. Ideally it offers predictability, structure, and safety as members mature and learn to participate in the community, and historically most human societies have treated it as the primary setting for attachment, nurturance, and socialization.

Anthropologists classify most family organizations as matrifocal (a mother and her children), patrifocal (a father and his children), conjugal (a married couple with children, also called the [[Nuclear family|nuclear family]]), avuncular (a man, his sister, and her children), or extended (parents, spouse, and children, plus grandparents, aunts, uncles, or cousins). Historically the most common type was the multigenerational family, in which grandparents, parents, and children live as one unit. In the U.S. this arrangement declined after World War II to a low in 1980, when about one in eight people lived in such a family, and had risen to one in five by 2016; in Canada the figure was about 6% in 2016. A single-parent family consists of one parent and children, the parent being widowed, divorced, or never married.

One primary function of the family is to provide a framework for the biological and social production and reproduction of persons, through shared food, care and nurture, legal rights and obligations, and moral and sentimental ties. From a child's perspective it is a "family of orientation," which shapes enculturation; from a parent's perspective it is a "family of procreation." In societies with a sexual division of labor, marriage is also necessary for forming an economically productive household. Early Western anthropologists tied family to "blood" relations, but later research found that many societies instead understand family through living together and sharing food and care. Steven Ruggles's analysis of census data suggests that 19th-century Northwest Europe and North America did not have exceptionally simple or nuclear family structures.

Who counts as family is disputed. C. C. Harris notes that the Western conception is ambiguous and confused with the household, and a survey asking Americans about 11 household structures found a wide variety of answers. As of 2015 the total fertility rate ranged from 6.76 children per woman in Niger to 0.81 in Singapore, with rates below replacement in all Eastern and Southern European countries and particularly high in Sub-Saharan Africa. Some researchers link stable two-parent biological families to better child outcomes; redefinitions of the family beyond biological ties remain contested.

=== KEY: Famine_4fa41e
HEADWORD: Famine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Democracy | England | Malnutrition | Netherlands | Soviet Union | Subsistence agriculture | United Nations
ENTRY:
Widespread scarcity of food, usually accompanied or followed by regional [[Malnutrition|malnutrition]], starvation, epidemic, and increased mortality. Causes include war, natural disasters, crop failure, poverty, and government policies. Every inhabited continent has experienced famine; the Americas have been among the least affected. In the 19th and 20th centuries, [[China]], India, Europe, and the Middle East suffered the greatest number of famine deaths. Deaths declined sharply from the 1970s and have fallen further since 2000, though Haiti, Afghanistan, Sudan, Yemen, Gaza, and Syria are among places that have experienced famine.

Under the Integrated Food Security Phase Classification, famine (Phase 5) exists when three conditions coincide: at least 20% of households in an area face extreme food shortages with limited ability to cope; acute malnutrition in children exceeds 30%; and the death rate exceeds two people per 10,000 per day. A declaration carries no binding obligations on the [[United Nations]] or member states but focuses global attention.

Famine recurred cyclically in societies practicing [[Subsistence agriculture|subsistence agriculture]]. As the feudal system broke down in the 16th and 17th centuries, commercialized farming and taxes paid in money pushed production toward the market. By the 1590s the province of Holland, in the [[Netherlands]], could withstand a famine that struck Western Europe, and in [[England]] the last peacetime famine was in 1623–24.

Analysts differ on emphasis. Economist Amartya Sen's entitlement approach holds that famines arise from breakdowns in people's rights to obtain food, not merely from shortfalls in supply, and he observed that no substantial famine has afflicted a functioning [[Democracy|democracy]] with a free press. One account attributes the deadliest famines of the 20th century to state-driven collectivization in the [[Soviet Union]] and China, with estimates of 5–10 million and 30–45 million deaths respectively.

=== KEY: Farad_979747

SI unit of electrical capacitance, equal to one coulomb per volt (C/V). It is named after [[Michael Faraday]], an English physicist. === KEY: Faraday_cage_9957ff

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's magnetic field | Michael Faraday
ENTRY:
Enclosure of conductive material, usually mesh, that blocks some electromagnetic fields. Named after [[Michael Faraday]], who first built one in 1836, it works because an external field redistributes charges in the conductor so that the field cancels inside. === KEY: Fascism_a4bc86

Far-right, authoritarian, and ultranationalist political ideology and movement that rose to prominence in early 20th-century Europe. Scholars generally describe it as characterized by a dictatorial leader, centralized autocracy, [[Militarism|militarism]], forcible suppression of opposition, belief in a natural social hierarchy, subordination of the individual to the perceived interest of the nation or race, and strong regimentation of society and the economy. Defenders of Italian fascism point to public works and reduced unemployment, while critics stress the murder of the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti in 1924, press censorship, and the 1938 racial laws. === KEY: Fault_geology_9ad2bf

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crust (geology) | Earthquake | Plate tectonics
ENTRY:
Planar fracture or discontinuity in a volume of rock across which significant displacement has occurred. Large faults in Earth's [[Crust (geology)|crust]] result from plate tectonic forces, with the largest forming the boundaries between [[Plate tectonics|plates]], such as the megathrust faults of subduction zones and transform faults. Rapid movement on active faults releases the energy that causes most [[Earthquake|earthquakes]], although some faults slip slowly by aseismic creep. A fault zone is a cluster of parallel faults or the zone of crushed rock along a single fault.

=== KEY: Fear_85caab
HEADWORD: Fear
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrenaline | Anxiety | Brainstem | Cortisol | Emotion | Evolutionary psychology | Natural selection | Phobia
ENTRY:
[[Emotion|Emotional]] state aroused by threatening situations, usually regarded as negative and unpleasant. Fear can result directly from sensory stimulation, such as sounds resembling screaming, from anticipating that undesirable events may occur, or from imagining threats that may not be real.

In humans, fear is correlated with more rapid breathing and heart rate, dilation of the pupils, spikes in [[Cortisol|cortisol]] levels, and elevated electrodermal activity, changes that overlap considerably with other negative, arousing emotions. Behavioral reactions include the startle, fight-or-flight, and freeze responses. The amygdala serves as a central hub for threat detection, interfacing with the hypothalamus and [[Brainstem|brainstem]] to trigger release of [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]]. Fear is distinguished from [[Anxiety|anxiety]], a diffuse, future-oriented apprehension, by its focus on specific, proximate stimuli. Its subjective experience is shaped by individual differences, situational factors, and culture, and it may be pleasurable and deliberately sought in thrilling activities and horror films. Maladaptive levels are associated with specific [[Phobia|phobias]] and with depression, anxiety, and suicidality.

Although many fears are learned, the capacity to fear is part of human nature, and certain fears, such as of animals and heights, are far more common and easier to induce than others, such as of flowers or clouds. This phenomenon, called preparedness, is theorized to be a genetic effect of [[Natural selection|natural selection]]. From an [[Evolutionary psychology|evolutionary psychology]] perspective, fear of heights may be common to all mammals and fear of snakes to all simians.

Fear conditioning was first studied in John B. Watson's Little Albert experiment in 1920, in which an 11-month-old boy was conditioned to fear a white rat; the fear generalized to other white, furry objects, including a rabbit, a dog, and a Santa Claus mask.

Range of socio-political movements and ideologies that aim to establish the political, economic, personal, and social equality of the sexes. Feminism holds that modern societies are patriarchal, prioritizing the male point of view, and that women are treated unjustly in them. Originating in late 18th-century Europe, feminist movements have campaigned for the right to vote, run for office, work, earn equal pay, own property, receive education, and enter into contracts, as well as for access to contraception and legal [[Abortion|abortion]] and protection from sexual assault, sexual harassment, and domestic violence.

Many scholars consider feminist campaigns a main force behind historical changes in women's rights, particularly in the West, where they are widely credited with achieving [[Women's suffrage|women's suffrage]], reproductive rights, and the right to own property. The movement is commonly described in "waves": the first focused on suffrage and property rights, the second on employment discrimination and reproductive autonomy from the mid-20th century, the third on identity and representation from the 1990s, and the fourth on digital activism since the 2010s. Liberal, socialist, and radical feminism are sometimes called the "Big Three" schools. Some newer forms have been criticized for reflecting only white, middle-class, or heterosexual perspectives, prompting black and intersectional feminism. Critics argue that feminism underappreciates biological sex differences, a claim supporters dispute.
