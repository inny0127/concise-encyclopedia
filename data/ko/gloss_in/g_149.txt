HEADWORD: Yosemite National Park
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
National park in the [[Sierra Nevada]] of [[California]], United States. Designated a [[World Heritage Site]] in 1984 for its [[Granite|granite]] cliffs, waterfalls, giant sequoia groves and biological diversity, it is almost 95 percent designated wilderness. It is one of the largest and least fragmented habitat blocks in the range. The Sierra Nevada was uplifted about 10 million years ago, and later glaciers carved the U-shaped Yosemite Valley. Native Americans inhabited the region for nearly 4,000 years before European Americans arrived by 1833. The Yosemite Grant of 1864, signed by [[Abraham Lincoln]], declared Yosemite federally preserved land. In 1890 [[John Muir]] led a successful movement to establish Yosemite Valley and its surrounding areas as a national park. It draws about four million visitors annually.

=== KEY: Young_adult_ad9331

ENTRY:
Phase of the human life span at the onset of adulthood, following [[Adolescence|adolescence]]. Definitions vary. The United States Census Bureau defines young adults as people aged 18 to 34, while cancer researchers use 18 to 49. [[Erik Erikson]]'s stages of human development significantly shaped the concept, describing this stage as focused on [[Intimate relationship|intimate relationships]] and personal identity. === KEY: Young_s_modulus_224b0f
HEADWORD: Young's modulus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hooke's law | Leonhard Euler | Thomas Young (scientist)
ENTRY:
Mechanical property of solid materials measuring tensile or compressive stiffness. It is the ratio of stress (force per unit area) to axial strain in the linear elastic region of a material, similar to the spring constant in [[Hooke's law]] but with dimensions of pressure. It is named after the 19th-century British scientist [[Thomas Young (scientist)|Thomas Young]], although the concept was developed in 1727 by [[Leonhard Euler]]. Values are usually given in gigapascals (GPa); aluminum has a high value and rubber a low one. The term derives from the Latin modus, meaning measure.

=== KEY: Ytterbium_66c140
HEADWORD: Ytterbium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brazil | Chemical element | Lanthanide | Stainless steel
ENTRY:
Metallic [[Chemical element|chemical element]] (symbol Yb, atomic number 70) of the [[Lanthanide|lanthanide]] series. Swiss chemist Jean Charles Galissard de Marignac separated it in 1878 from the rare earth erbia and named it for Ytterby, the Swedish village near where the component was found. Pure metal was first obtained in 1953. Natural ytterbium is a mixture of seven stable isotopes, present at an average of 3 parts per million in the Earth's crust. It is mined mainly in China, the United States, [[Brazil]] and India, chiefly as the mineral monazite. Ytterbium is used mainly as a dopant in [[Stainless steel|stainless steel]] and in laser media, and less often as a gamma-ray source. Prepared ytterbium is somewhat hazardous as an eye and skin irritant, and the metal is a fire and explosion hazard.

=== KEY: Yttrium_7c439b

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Silvery [[Transition metal|transition metal]] (symbol Y, atomic number 39) chemically similar to the [[Lanthanide|lanthanides]]. It is almost always found with lanthanide elements in rare-earth [[Mineral|minerals]] and never occurs free in nature. Its only stable isotope, ⁸⁹Y, is the only one found in the Earth's crust. It was named after the mineral ytterbite, first identified in 1787 in the Swedish village of Ytterby. Its most important present use is in phosphors, especially for LEDs; it was once widely used in red phosphors in [[Cathode ray tube|cathode ray tubes]] of television sets. It also goes into electrodes, electrolytes, electronic filters, [[Laser|lasers]], superconductors and medical applications. Yttrium has no known biological role, but exposure to its compounds can cause lung disease.

=== KEY: Yucca_b760da

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Plant genus of the agave subfamily, native to North America from Panama to southern Canada, with 50 accepted species. Species range from small shrubs to tree-like forms such as the Joshua tree, which can reach 15 meters (49 feet). Yuccas are xerophytes with rosettes of pointed leaves and large, cream-white flowers. Their relationship with the yucca moths that pollinate them is a well-known example of evolutionary [[Pollination|mutualism]]. The plants provide shelter in North American deserts, and some species grow in the [[Rocky Mountains]] and [[Great Plains]]. Flower petals are eaten in local cuisine in Central America and Mexico, and fibers were historically woven into cords, baskets, mats and sandals. Native peoples still use the plant to make traditional soaps.

Highland region of southwest [[China]], spread mainly over [[Yunnan]] and Guizhou provinces. Mountain range in West Asia. The Zagros Mountains extend about 1,600 km (990 miles) from southeastern [[Turkey]] through northern [[Iraq]] and western [[Iran]] to the [[Strait of Hormuz]]. The highest point is Mount Dena, at 4,409 m (14,465 ft). The range formed through the collision of the Eurasian and Arabian plates, mainly during the [[Miocene]], about 25 to 5 million years ago, which folded sedimentary rocks deposited from the [[Paleozoic]] onward. The folded rocks trap large petroleum deposits, and the Zagros is an important area for oil production.

Indigenous pre-Columbian civilization of the Valley of [[Oaxaca]] in [[Mesoamerica]]. Its culture originated at least 2,500 years ago. === KEY: Zero_sum_game_46bfe4

Competitive situation in [[Game theory|game theory]] in which one participant's gain equals another's loss, so the net change in benefit is zero. === KEY: Zoology_feb0fc
HEADWORD: Zoology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander von Humboldt | Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Aristotle | Biology | Carl Linnaeus | Charles Darwin | DNA | Galen
ENTRY:
Scientific study of animals, a primary branch of [[Biology|biology]]. It covers the structure, embryology, classification, habits, and distribution of all animals, living and extinct, and how they interact with their ecosystems. The term derives from Ancient Greek *zôion* (animal) and *lógos* (study of).

Formal zoology is usually said to originate with [[Aristotle]], who in the fourth century BC studied animals as living organisms, examined their structure and development, and divided them into those with blood, roughly the vertebrates, and those without, the invertebrates. The Roman physician [[Galen]] dissected animals to study anatomy because human dissection was prohibited; some conclusions were false, but challenging his views was considered heretical for centuries.

During the Renaissance and early modern period, renewed empiricism and the discovery of new organisms revolutionized European zoology. Andreas Vesalius and William Harvey used experiment and observation in physiology, while [[Carl Linnaeus]], Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, and Buffon began to classify the diversity of life. [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] pioneered microscopy and revealed microorganisms, laying the groundwork for cell theory. [[Alexander von Humboldt]] studied how organisms relate to their environment, laying foundations for ecology and ethology. These strands were synthesized in [[Charles Darwin]]'s 1859 theory of evolution by natural selection.

Modern zoology addresses form and function, adaptation, relationships among groups, behavior, and ecology, and has divided into disciplines such as classification, physiology, biochemistry, and evolution. The 1953 discovery of the structure of [[DNA]] opened the field of molecular biology, leading to advances in cell biology, developmental biology, and molecular genetics.

=== KEY: Zoroastrianism_d2bae0
HEADWORD: Zoroastrianism
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Iranian religion centered on the [[Avesta]] and the teachings of Zarathushtra Spitama, better known by the Greek form of his name, [[Zoroaster]]. Also called Mazdayasna and Behdin, it is among the world's oldest organized faiths. Its adherents exalt [[Ahura Mazda]], an uncreated, benevolent, and all-wise deity regarded as the supreme being of the universe.

The oldest and most central part of the Avesta is the Gathas, regarded as Zoroaster's own teachings; they belong to the Yasna, the core of Zoroastrian liturgy. In the Gathas the primary dualism is ethical: a choice between Asha (truth, order) and Druj (falsehood, deceit). Spenta Mainyu, the Bountiful Spirit, is opposed by Angra Mainyu, the Destructive Spirit. Scholars disagree on whether the religion is monotheistic, polytheistic, henotheistic, or a combination. The central moral precepts are good thoughts, good words, and good deeds. Fire is held sacred as a symbol of Ahura Mazda.

The religion began in the Avestan period, possibly as early as the 2nd millennium BC, and was first recorded in the mid-6th century BC. For about a millennium it was the official religion of successive Iranian polities, from the [[Achaemenid Empire]] to the [[Sasanian Empire]]. The 7th-century [[Muslim conquest of Persia]] began its decline, and persecution prompted much of the community to migrate to the Indian subcontinent, where they became the ancestors of the [[Parsis]].

A 2012 survey estimated the worldwide population at between 111,691 and 121,962, concentrated in India, Iran, and diaspora communities.

King of the Anglo-Saxons from 924 and King of the English from 927 to 939, regarded by modern historians as the first King of England. He was the son of King Edward the Elder. In 927 he conquered York, the last remaining Viking kingdom, making him the first Anglo-Saxon ruler of the whole of England. In 934 he invaded Scotland and forced Constantine II to submit, and his victory over a Scottish and [[Vikings|Viking]] invasion at the Battle of Brunanburh in 937 gave him great prestige. He centralized government, increasing control over the production of charters and summoning leaders from distant areas to his councils. More legal texts survive from his reign than from any other tenth-century English king, and his legal reforms built on those of his grandfather, [[Alfred the Great]]. A devout king who collected relics and founded churches, he never married, and he arranged marriages for several of his sisters to continental rulers. He was succeeded by his half-brother Edmund I.

Hittite king (r. c. 1350–1322 BC) who extended Hittite authority across [[Anatolia]] and northern Syria. Before becoming king he served as military commander to his predecessor, Tudhaliya III, recovering Hittite territories. His victories over [[Mitanni]] brought a number of petty kingdoms in northern Syria under Hittite authority, and he installed his younger sons as local viceroys at [[Aleppo]] and Carchemish. The rump of the Mittanian state became a dependency of the Hittite kingdom. Relations with Egypt turned to hostility after the Zannanza Affair, in which he sent one of his sons to marry the widowed queen of Egypt and the prince was murdered. Captives he had taken caused a plague that ravaged Hittite society for at least two decades. His son Muršili II, who composed the Deeds of Šuppiluliuma, blamed the misfortune on his father's cruelty.

French mathematician and political activist whose work laid the foundations of [[Galois theory]] and [[Group theory|group theory]], two major branches of [[Abstract algebra|abstract algebra]]. While still in his teens, he determined a necessary and sufficient condition for a [[Polynomial|polynomial]] to be solvable by radicals, solving a problem that had been open for 350 years. A staunch Republican, he was arrested repeatedly and served one jail sentence of several months. After twice failing the entrance examination to the École Polytechnique, he submitted early papers that [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] refereed but refused to accept for publication, for reasons that remain unclear. Shortly after his release from prison he fought a [[Duel|duel]], for reasons that remain obscure, and died of his wounds. The night before, he hurriedly wrote letters outlining his mathematical insights. His complete works, just over 60 pages, were published posthumously in 1846.

Port city on the Aegean coast of [[Anatolia]], capital of İzmir Province. Turkey's third most populous city, after [[Istanbul]] and [[Ankara]], it is the largest metropolitan area on the [[Aegean Sea]] and has a [[Mediterranean climate]]. The city is home to around 3.5 million inhabitants. The site has been settled since the [[Neolithic]] period, and its recorded urban history exceeds 3,000 years. In classical antiquity it was called Smyrna, a name that remained in English until around 1930, when government efforts led to the Turkish form İzmir being adopted internationally. Until the 1923 population exchange it had a very large Greek-speaking population. Today it is an important port and hosts the annual İzmir International Fair.
