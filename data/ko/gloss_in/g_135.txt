=== KEY: The_Prince_39fe3a
HEADWORD: The Prince
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cesare Borgia | Lorenzo de' Medici | Niccolò Machiavelli
ENTRY:
16th-century political treatise by Italian diplomat and political philosopher [[Niccolò Machiavelli]], written as an instruction guide for new princes. Its worldview treats practical effect as more important than any abstract ideal, and it includes positive references to the career of [[Cesare Borgia]]. Many commentators view immoral acts as sometimes necessary to achieve political glory. Printed in 1532, five years after Machiavelli's death, it is dedicated to [[Lorenzo de' Medici]], Duke of Urbino. It is sometimes claimed to be one of the first works of modern political philosophy, and it conflicted with the dominant Catholic and scholastic doctrines of its time. It is the most remembered of Machiavelli's works and the source of the pejorative "Machiavellian."

=== KEY: The_Raven_12cbd8
HEADWORD: The Raven
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Dickens | Edgar Allan Poe | Student
ENTRY:
Narrative poem by American writer [[Edgar Allan Poe]], first published in January 1845. A distraught lover, often identified as a [[Student|student]], mourns the loss of Lenore when a raven visits him and repeatedly says "nevermore." Poe stated that he composed the poem in a logical and methodical manner, as he elaborated in his 1846 essay "The Philosophy of Composition." The poem was partly inspired by a talking raven in [[Charles Dickens]]'s 1841 novel *Barnaby Rudge*. It was first attributed to Poe in print in the *New York Evening Mirror* on January 29, 1845, and made him popular in his lifetime, though it brought him little financial success. The poem is often noted for its musicality and internal rhyme, and critical opinion on its literary status remains divided.

ENTRY:
1913 ballet and orchestral concert work by [[Igor Stravinsky]]. ENTRY:
Series of paintings, pastels and lithographs by Norwegian artist [[Edvard Munch]], first created in 1893 and later repeated in different versions. Its agonized central figure has become one of the most iconic images in art, seen as representing existential dread. Munch recalled a sunset walk above Oslo when the clouds turned "a blood red" and he sensed an "infinite scream passing through nature." === KEY: The_Structure_of_Scientific_Revolutions_30e53d
HEADWORD: The Structure of Scientific Revolutions
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Paradigm
ENTRY:
1962 book by the philosopher Thomas S. Kuhn, published by the University of Chicago Press. Kuhn challenged the view that science progresses through the steady accumulation of accepted facts and theories. He argued instead that periods of "normal science" are interrupted by revolutions, in which accumulating anomalies lead to new [[Paradigm|paradigms]] that ask new questions and change the rules of research. He illustrated this with the Copernican revolution, which at first did not predict planetary positions more accurately than the Ptolemaic system. His claim that paradigm shifts are not logically determinate caused considerable controversy, and he addressed critics in a 1969 postscript. The book launched the word "paradigm" into widespread use, and it is a landmark in the history, philosophy and sociology of science.

ENTRY:
Classic work of Japanese literature written by [[Murasaki Shikibu]], a noblewoman and lady-in-waiting, around the early 11th century. It is the first novel by a woman to win global recognition, and in Japan it has a stature similar to that of Shakespeare's works in English. The work depicts the lifestyles of high courtiers in the [[Heian period]]. It is written mostly in [[Hiragana|hiragana]] rather than the [[Kanji|kanji]] used for prestigious literature. Arthur Waley's English translation, made between 1925 and 1933, omitted only one chapter. Complete translations have since been made by Edward Seidensticker, Royall Tyler and Dennis Washburn.

=== KEY: The_arts_323486
HEADWORD: Arts, The
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Architecture | Ceramic art | Conceptual art | Literature | Marcel Duchamp | Performing arts | Visual arts
ENTRY:
Broad range of human practices involving creative expression, storytelling, and cultural participation, carried out with skill, creativity, and imagination across cultures and history.

The arts encompass diverse modes of thought and action in an extensive range of media. They have developed into increasingly stylized and intricate forms through sustained study, training, or theorizing within a tradition, across generations, and even between civilizations. They serve as a means of cultivating social, cultural, and individual identities and of transmitting values, ideas, spiritual meanings, and experiences across time and space. They can refer to common, everyday practices as well as sophisticated or institutionalized ones, and they can stand alone or combine, as when comics join artwork with the written word. From prehistoric cave paintings of the Upper Palaeolithic to modern films, they have registered the changing relationships of humans with each other and the world.

The arts are divided into three main branches: [[Visual arts|visual arts]], [[Literature|literature]], and [[Performing arts|performing arts]]. Visual forms include [[Architecture|architecture]], [[Ceramic art|ceramic art]], drawing, filmmaking, painting, photography, sculpture, printmaking, and video; the applied arts, such as industrial, graphic, fashion, and interior design, are also included. Literature includes fiction, drama, poetry, and prose, and the performing arts include dance, music, and theater. Architecture is the art and science of designing buildings and structures, and some definitions extend it to urban planning and landscape architecture. Ceramic art is made from ceramic materials, which may take forms such as pottery, tiles, figurines, sculptures, and tableware.

Classifications have changed over time. In ancient Greece, art and craft were both called techne. Byzantine and Gothic art of the Middle Ages expressed Christian themes, while Islamic art avoids representing living beings in religious contexts, using calligraphy and geometrical designs instead. In medieval European universities, the liberal arts comprised the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, and logic) and the quadrivium, or "mathematical arts" (arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy). One traditional scheme lists seven arts: painting, architecture, sculpture, literature, music, theater, and filmmaking. Francophone scholarship sometimes adds television as the "eighth" and comics as the "ninth" art. In modern academia, the arts are grouped with, or treated as a subset of, the humanities.

What counts as art is disputed. [[Conceptual art]], which gives precedence to ideas over traditional aesthetic and material concerns, emerged as a term in the 1960s. Classificatory disputes in the 20th century have involved Cubist and Impressionist paintings, [[Marcel Duchamp]]'s *Fountain*, the movies, J. S. G. Boggs' imitations of banknotes, conceptual art, and video games. The arts are open to continual redefinition.

=== KEY: Theatre_46abaf
HEADWORD: Theater
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeschylus | Aristophanes | Aristotle | Dionysus | Opera | Performing arts | Sophocles
ENTRY:
Collaborative form of [[Performing arts|performing art]] in which live performers, usually actors, present a real or imagined event before a live audience, often on a stage, through gesture, speech, song, music, and dance. The word derives from the Greek *théatron*, "a place for viewing",.

Theater is the oldest form of drama, though live performance has been joined by recorded forms. Modern Western theater draws on that of ancient Greece for terminology, genres, themes, and stock characters. Modern theater includes plays and musical theater; ballet and [[Opera|opera]] also use theatrical conventions such as acting, costumes, and staging.

Western theater originated in Athens, where attendance at the City Dionysia was part of citizenship. According to [[Aristotle]], the first theorist of theater, its origins lie in festivals honoring [[Dionysus]]. Performances were given in semicircular auditoria cut into hillsides that seated 10,000-20,000 people; actors, always men, wore masks and might play several parts. Greek drama had three types: tragedy, comedy, and the satyr play. Tragedy emerged in the 6th century BC and flowered in the 5th; of more than a thousand tragedies performed in that century, only 32 survive, with complete texts by [[Aeschylus]], [[Sophocles]], and Euripides. Aeschylus's *The Persians* (first prize, 472 BC) is the earliest surviving drama on recent history. Aristotle's *Poetics* (c. 335 BC) is the oldest surviving work of dramatic theory.

Athenian comedy is divided into Old, Middle, and New periods. Old Comedy survives in the eleven plays of [[Aristophanes]], and New Comedy is known mainly from fragments of Menander. Parallel traditions include Sanskrit drama in India and Chinese opera from as early as the Tang dynasty.

=== KEY: Theft_40f03b

Taking of another person's [[Property|property]] or services without [[Consent|consent]] and with intent to deprive the rightful owner of it. The English word derives from Old English *þeofð*, cognate with "thief".

"Theft" is also used informally for crimes against property such as larceny, [[Robbery|robbery]], embezzlement, extortion, and blackmail. It is a statutory offense in California, Canada, England and Wales, Hong Kong, and parts of Australia.

Its actus reus is usually an unauthorized taking, keeping, or using of another's property, accompanied by the mens rea of dishonesty and intent to permanently deprive the owner. Religious traditions condemn theft. One of Buddhism's five precepts prohibits it, as do cheating and forgery, and [[Fraud|fraud]]. The Hebrew Bible's [[Ten Commandments]] include "Thou shalt not steal". In parts of the world governed by [[Sharia]], the punishment is amputation of the right hand if the thief does not repent, a ruling derived from surah 5, verse 38 of the [[Quran]].

=== KEY: Theology_29bf39
HEADWORD: Theology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Christianity | Deity | Metaphysics | Plato | Religion | Tertullian | Thomas Aquinas
ENTRY:
Study of [[Religion|religious]] belief from a religious perspective, focused on the nature of divinity and the history behind religion. It is taught as an academic discipline, typically in universities and seminaries.

Theology analyzes the supernatural, religious epistemology, and the question of revelation: whether a [[Deity|deity]] is not only transcendent but also willing and able to interact with the natural world. Theologians use experiential, philosophical, ethnographic, and historical analysis to understand, explain, test, critique, defend, or promote religious topics. The study may deepen understanding of a theologian's own or another tradition, or explore divinity without reference to any tradition. It may be used to propagate, reform, or justify a tradition, or to compare, challenge (as in biblical criticism), or oppose it. Within [[Christianity]], the discipline is typically divided into biblical, historical, systematic, and practical theology.

The term derives from Greek *theologia*, from *theos* ("god") and *logia* ("utterances, sayings, oracles"), and had evolved into its current English form by 1362. [[Plato]] used *theologia* to mean "discourse on God" in *The Republic*. [[Aristotle]] divided theoretical philosophy into mathematike, physike, and theologike, the last corresponding roughly to [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]]. The Latin writer Varro distinguished mythical, rational, and civil theology, a usage followed by Christian authors such as [[Tertullian]] and Augustine. In scholastic Latin sources, the term came to denote the rational study of Christian doctrine, as in the work of [[Thomas Aquinas]]. From the 17th century it was extended to non-Christian ideas, as in natural theology, which reasons from natural facts. Some hold that the term suits only religions that worship a deity and presuppose that it can be reasoned about, and less well those without a single deity.

=== KEY: Theory_of_forms_f8a7b6

Philosophical theory, credited to [[Plato]], that timeless, unchanging Forms are the basis of reality. === KEY: Theory_of_relativity_b4b05b
HEADWORD: Relativity, theory of
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Black hole | General relativity | Gravitational wave | Michelson–Morley experiment | Special relativity | Time dilation
ENTRY:
Two interrelated physics theories of [[Albert Einstein]]: [[Special relativity|special relativity]], published in 1905, and [[General relativity|general relativity]], developed between 1907 and 1915 and published in final form in 1916.

Special relativity applies to all physical phenomena in the absence of gravity. It rests on two postulates: the laws of physics are the same for all observers in any inertial frame of reference, and the speed of light in vacuum is the same for all observers regardless of the motion of the source. The second postulate explains the result of the [[Michelson–Morley experiment]]. Consequences include relativity of simultaneity, [[Time dilation|time dilation]], length contraction, a finite maximum speed, and the equivalence of mass and energy, E = mc².

General relativity is a theory of gravitation. It began with the equivalence principle, under which accelerated motion and rest in a gravitational field are physically identical, so that free fall is inertial motion. Einstein proposed that spacetime is curved and, with mathematician Marcel Grossmann, formulated the theory in Riemannian geometry. The Einstein field equations, devised in 1915, relate spacetime curvature to matter and energy. The theory accounts for the precession of Mercury's orbit and the bending of starlight, confirmed in 1919, and predicts [[Black hole|black holes]] and [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]], first directly detected in 2015 by LIGO.

By the 1920s physicists had accepted special relativity, but general relativity became central to physics and astronomy only around 1960, as it explained quasars (1963), the microwave background (1965), pulsars (1967) and the first black hole candidates (1981). Relativity transformed 20th-century physics. General relativity remains incompatible with quantum mechanics, motivating research on quantum gravity.

=== KEY: Theravada_03e701
HEADWORD: Theravada
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Oldest existing school of [[Buddhism]], dominant in Sri Lanka and mainland Southeast Asia. The name is Pali for "Doctrine of the Elders". Its adherents have preserved their version of the Buddha's teaching, or Dhamma, in the Pali Canon for over two millennia. As of 2010, 36% of Buddhists belonged to Theravada, the second largest branch, compared with 53% to Mahayana.

The Pali Canon is the most complete Buddhist canon surviving in a classical Indian language. [[Pali]], the school's sacred language, serves as its [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]]. Theravada tends to be conservative in the study of doctrine and in monastic discipline. It rejects the authenticity of the [[Mahayana]] sutras, which appeared from about the 1st century BC, and so generally does not recognize Buddhas and bodhisattvas such as Amitabha and Vairocana. Its practice centers on the arhat ideal of personal enlightenment through ethical conduct, concentration, and wisdom.

The school derives from the Indian Sthavira nikaya and developed significantly in India from the 3rd century BC, with patronage from Mauryan kings such as [[Ashoka]]. From India and [[Sri Lanka]], its principal center of development, it spread to Southeast Asia, where it became the dominant form of Buddhism. It is the official religion of Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and Cambodia and the main Buddhist variant in Laos and [[Thailand]]. Minorities practice it in India, Bangladesh, Nepal, and elsewhere.

The term Theravada became its official designation only in the modern period, at the World Fellowship of Buddhists in 1950. Earlier scholars called it Southern Buddhism or labeled it Hinayana, a pejorative term found in Mahayana sutras. Modern developments include Buddhist modernism, the Vipassana movement, and the Thai Forest Tradition, which re-emphasized forest monasticism.
