=== KEY: Neural_network_machine_learning_e974f3
HEADWORD: Neural network
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Graphics processing unit | Large language model | Machine learning | Neuron
ENTRY:
Computational model used in [[Machine learning|machine learning]], loosely inspired by biological neural networks. It consists of connected units called artificial [[Neuron|neurons]], which pass real-number signals along weighted connections. Networks with at least two hidden layers are typically called deep neural networks. Training is compute-intensive and is often accelerated by [[Graphics processing unit|graphics processing units]] (GPUs). Convolutional neural networks improved computer vision, while transformer architectures, which introduced attention mechanisms, have been the basis of [[Large language model|large language models]]. Neural networks are used for chatbots, text, image and video generation, and robotics.

=== KEY: Neurology_3505fe
HEADWORD: Neurology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epilepsy | Migraine | Multiple sclerosis | Neuroscience | Parkinson's disease | Stroke
ENTRY:
Branch of medicine concerned with diagnosing and treating disorders of the nervous system, which comprises the brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves. Neurologists treat conditions including [[Stroke|stroke]], [[Epilepsy|epilepsy]], [[Parkinson's disease]], [[Multiple sclerosis]], [[Migraine|migraine]] and dementias such as Alzheimer's disease. Neurology is a nonsurgical specialty; its surgical counterpart is neurosurgery. Clinical practice relies heavily on [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], the scientific study of the nervous system. In the United States and Canada, neurologists typically complete residency training after medical school, and total training takes eight to ten years. Neurologists may also take part in clinical research and trials. The academic discipline began between the 17th and 18th centuries.

=== KEY: Neuron_26d4a0
HEADWORD: Neuron
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Nervous system | Neurotransmitter
ENTRY:
Electrically excitable nerve cell, the primary cell of the [[Nervous system|nervous system]]. Neurons are the main components of nervous tissue in all animals except sponges and placozoans. Most receive signals through dendrites and the cell body, then send signals down a long axon, usually releasing [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitters]] at synapses. When voltage changes enough, the neuron generates an all-or-nothing electrochemical pulse called an action potential. Neurons are typically classified by function as sensory, motor or interneurons. Most are generated during brain development and childhood, and neurogenesis largely ceases in adulthood in most brain areas. The ability to generate electric signals first appeared some 700 to 800 million years ago.

=== KEY: Neuroscience_993c8c
HEADWORD: Neuroscience
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer science | Galen | Hippocrates | Luigi Galvani | Medicine | Neuron | Psychology | Santiago Ramón y Cajal
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the nervous system, its functions and its disorders. The field is multidisciplinary, combining physiology, anatomy, molecular biology, [[Psychology|psychology]], physics, [[Computer science|computer science]], chemistry, [[Medicine|medicine]] and statistics to study [[Neuron|neurons]], glia and neural circuits. Eric Kandel called the biological basis of learning, memory, behavior, perception and consciousness the "epic challenge" of the biological sciences. The study of the nervous system dates to ancient Egypt, and [[Hippocrates]] argued that the brain was the seat of intelligence. [[Galen]] later observed that brain damage impaired mental faculties. [[Luigi Galvani]]'s work in the late 1700s set the stage for studying electrical excitability. Camillo Golgi's staining method, developed in the late 1890s, was used by [[Santiago Ramón y Cajal]] to reveal individual neurons.

=== KEY: Neurotransmitter_299478
HEADWORD: Neurotransmitter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Carbon monoxide | Depression (mood) | Dopamine | Neuron | Nitric oxide | Parkinson's disease | Serotonin
ENTRY:
Signaling molecule released by a [[Neuron|neuron]] to affect another cell across a synapse. The target cell may be another neuron, a gland or a muscle cell. Neurotransmitters are usually stored in synaptic vesicles and released into the synaptic cleft, where they bind to receptors. The receptor determines whether the effect is excitation, inhibition or modulation. Many are synthesized from simple precursors such as [[Amino acid|amino acids]], and monoamines such as [[Dopamine|dopamine]] and [[Serotonin|serotonin]] are formed by altering a single amino acid. Metabolic gases such as [[Nitric oxide|nitric oxide]] and [[Carbon monoxide|carbon monoxide]] also act like neurotransmitters. More than 100 have been identified, and common ones include glutamate, GABA, acetylcholine, glycine and norepinephrine. They are therapeutic targets for disorders such as [[Parkinson's disease]] and [[Depression (mood)|depression]].

=== KEY: Neutrino_fc3cec
HEADWORD: Neutrino
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beta decay | Sun | Weak interaction | Wolfgang Pauli
ENTRY:
Electrically neutral elementary particle with an extremely small mass. It interacts with other matter only through the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]] and gravity, so it usually passes through matter undetected. Neutrinos are created in weak decays such as [[Beta decay|beta decay]], and most of those detected on Earth come from nuclear reactions inside the [[Sun]]. [[Wolfgang Pauli]] postulated the particle in 1930 to explain how beta decay conserved energy, momentum and angular momentum. Neutrinos come in three flavors (electron, muon and tau) and oscillate between them in flight, which shows that they have mass. Their individual masses are not yet known, but their sum is less than 0.120 eV/c².

=== KEY: Neutron_88fac4
HEADWORD: Neutron
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Ernest Rutherford | Isotope | James Chadwick | Neutron star | Nuclear fission | Proton | Quark
ENTRY:
Subatomic particle, symbol n or n⁰, with no electric charge and a mass slightly greater than that of a [[Proton|proton]]. Neutrons are found, with a similar number of protons, in the [[Atomic nucleus|nuclei]] of atoms, and atoms of an element differing only in neutron number are called [[Isotope|isotopes]]. A neutron is not an elementary particle but a baryon composed of three [[Quark|quarks]], one up and two down. A free neutron decays into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino, with a mean lifetime of about 15 minutes.

In 1920 [[Ernest Rutherford]] suggested that the nucleus contained neutral particles, then imagined as a proton and electron bound together, and in 1921 W. D. Harkins named the hypothetical particle a neutron. This model proved inconsistent with quantum theory and observed nuclear spins. In 1931 Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker found that alpha particles from polonium striking beryllium, boron, or lithium produced an unusually penetrating radiation, which they took to be gamma rays. [[James Chadwick]] then demonstrated that it consisted of uncharged particles with about the proton's mass. The discovery, made in 1932, won him the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics. Werner Heisenberg and others then developed a proton–neutron model of the nucleus.

The discovery led to rapid developments: [[Nuclear fission]] was discovered in 1938, the first self-sustaining nuclear reactor, Chicago Pile-1, followed in 1942, and the first nuclear weapon, the Trinity test, in 1945. Neutrons are essential to nuclear power and contribute to the nucleosynthesis of elements in stars. [[Neutron star]]s consist of neutrons at nuclear density. Free neutrons do not directly ionize atoms but indirectly cause ionizing radiation, so they can be a biological hazard.

=== KEY: Neutron_star_88a479
HEADWORD: Neutron star
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Gamma-ray burst | Gravitational wave | Milky Way | Pulsar | Solar mass
ENTRY:
Collapsed core of a massive star, left after a supernova. Neutron stars are the second-smallest and second-densest known class of stellar objects, surpassed only by [[Black hole|black holes]]. They have a radius on the order of 10 km (6 miles) and a mass of about 1.4 [[Solar mass|solar masses]]. Estimates put the number in the [[Milky Way]] at around one billion, and at a minimum several hundred million, although many have cooled considerably. Most detected neutron stars are [[Pulsar|pulsars]] or part of a binary system. In 2017 the LIGO and Virgo observatories detected [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]] from the merger of two neutron stars, GW170817, the first direct detection of such waves from that kind of event. The merger is associated with kilonovae and short [[Gamma-ray burst|gamma-ray bursts]].

=== KEY: New_Deal_ee9b19

ENTRY:
Series of economic, social and political reforms introduced in the [[United States]] by President [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] in response to the [[Great Depression]]. The First New Deal (1933–35) aimed at relief, recovery and reform of the financial system. Its measures included the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Securities and Exchange Commission and the Civilian Conservation Corps, which employed young men on government land. The Second New Deal (1935–38) created the National Labor Relations Act, which guaranteed the right to organize [[Trade union|trade unions]], and the Social Security Act. Historians still debate its effectiveness, though most accept that full employment was not achieved until the United States entered [[World War II]] in 1941. Some econometric studies argue that its cartel-style policies prolonged the Depression.

ENTRY:
Period of colonial expansion by the major Western powers and Japan in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. The participants included the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Portugal, Russia, the [[United States]] and Japan. The European powers and Japan conquered almost all of [[Africa]] and parts of [[Asia]], motivated by rivalry among the great powers, the economic desire for new resources and markets, and a civilizing mission. Many colonies gained independence during the era of [[Decolonization|decolonization]] that followed [[World War II]].

=== KEY: Newton_s_law_of_universal_gravitation_04b8b9
HEADWORD: Newton's law of universal gravitation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coulomb's law | General relativity | Henry Cavendish | Isaac Newton | Mercury (planet) | Sun
ENTRY:
Law of physics stating that any two particles attract each other with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. [[Isaac Newton]] published it in *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* on July 5, 1687. In the equation F = Gm₁m₂/r², F is the force, m₁ and m₂ are the masses, r is the distance between their centers, and G is the gravitational constant, 6.674×10⁻¹¹ m³⋅kg⁻¹⋅s⁻². The law resembles [[Coulomb's law]] for electric forces, and both are inverse-square laws. [[Henry Cavendish]] first tested it in a laboratory in 1798. The law was superseded by [[General relativity|general relativity]], but it remains an excellent approximation in most applications. Relativity is needed for extreme accuracy, very strong gravitational fields or small distances, such as [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]]'s orbit around the [[Sun]].

=== KEY: Newton_s_laws_of_motion_d47e8c
HEADWORD: Newton's laws of motion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acceleration | Classical mechanics | Inertia | Isaac Newton | Momentum | Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica | Quantum mechanics | Theory of relativity
ENTRY:
Three physical laws describing the relationship between the motion of a body and the forces acting on it, forming the basis of Newtonian mechanics. They were first stated by [[Isaac Newton]] in *[[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica]]* (Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), published in 1687, and he used them to investigate and explain the motion of many physical objects and systems.

The first law, often called the law of [[Inertia|inertia]], states that a body remains at rest, or in motion at constant speed in a straight line, unless acted upon by a force. The second law states that at any instant the net force on a body equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration, F = ma, or, equivalently, the rate at which its [[Momentum|momentum]] changes with time. The third law states that when two bodies exert forces on each other, the forces have the same magnitude and opposite directions. No force therefore exists in isolation.

The laws are usually stated for point masses, bodies whose volume is negligible, a reasonable approximation when the separation between bodies is much larger than their size. Their mathematical description rests on calculus. Velocity is the derivative of position with respect to time, and [[Acceleration|acceleration]] is the derivative of velocity. Position, velocity, acceleration, and force are vector quantities, having both magnitude and direction.

They hold for speeds much less than the speed of light and for scales larger than the atomic. Their limitations led to new theories, namely [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] and the [[Theory of relativity|theory of relativity]], which address extreme cases. New insights, especially around energy, built the field of [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] on Newton's foundations.

Root-finding algorithm for real-valued functions, also called the Newton–Raphson method after [[Isaac Newton]] and Joseph Raphson. === KEY: Niccol_Machiavelli_48d837
HEADWORD: Machiavelli, Niccolò
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Cesare Borgia | Florence | Jean-Jacques Rousseau | Political philosophy | Political science | The Prince
ENTRY:
Florentine statesman, diplomat, and political theorist of the Italian Renaissance. Born in [[Florence]], he served the Florentine Republic as secretary to the second chancery from 1498 to 1512, with responsibilities in diplomatic and military affairs. He rose in importance under the gonfaloniere Piero Soderini and represented Florence at the courts of powerful rulers. When the Medici retook power in 1512, he was expelled from his duties, soon afterward mistakenly suspected of treason, and sent into exile. A year later he took up the life of a political writer.

His most famous work is [[The Prince]], written around 1513 but not published until 1532, five years after his death. It advises rulers on acquiring and keeping power, drawing on observations of figures such as [[Cesare Borgia]], and argues that rulers must sometimes adopt unsavory policies when political necessity requires, in contradiction to classical political thought. His *Discourses on Livy* (composed c. 1517) favors republican government modeled on ancient Rome and has been said to have paved the way for modern republicanism.

The Prince has been controversial since its publication. Many commentators read it as a manual for would-be tyrants, and scholars such as Leo Strauss restated the traditional view of Machiavelli as a "teacher of evil"; others stress his empirical analysis of power, built on the concepts of *virtù* and *fortuna*. His name came to evoke unscrupulous acts. He is often called the father of modern [[Political philosophy|political philosophy]] and [[Political science|political science]], and influenced Enlightenment authors such as [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] and James Harrington.

=== KEY: Nicolaus_Copernicus_533ee4
HEADWORD: Copernicus, Nicolaus
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | De revolutionibus orbium coelestium | Kraków | Nuremberg | Scientific Revolution | Sun | University of Bologna
ENTRY:
Renaissance polymath and astronomer from Royal Prussia who formulated a model of the universe placing the [[Sun]] rather than Earth at its center. He was born in Thorn (Toruń), in Royal Prussia, a semi-autonomous, multilingual region of the Crown of the Kingdom of Poland, to German-speaking parents. His father was a merchant from [[Kraków]].

Copernicus studied mathematics and astronomy at the University of Kraków from 1491 to 1495, then canon law at the [[University of Bologna]], medicine at Padua, and took a doctorate in canon law at Ferrara. He was a mathematician, astronomer, physician, classics scholar, translator, governor, diplomat, and economist. From 1497 he was a canon of the Warmian cathedral chapter, managing church estates at Frombork. In 1517 he derived a quantity theory of money, and in 1519 he formulated an economic principle later called Gresham's law.

His heliocentric model held that Earth and the other planets revolve around a stationary Sun, with Earth also rotating daily on its axis. A similar model had been developed eighteen centuries earlier by Aristarchus of Samos, an [[Ancient Greek|ancient Greek]] astronomer, but Copernicus likely arrived at his independently. He set it out in [[De revolutionibus orbium coelestium]] (*On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres*), published in [[Nuremberg]] in 1543, just before his death and dedicated to Pope Paul III. The book triggered the Copernican Revolution and made a pioneering contribution to the [[Scientific Revolution]].
