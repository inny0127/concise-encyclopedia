=== KEY: Helicopter_2ab8b4
HEADWORD: Helicopter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fixed-wing aircraft | Unmanned aerial vehicle
ENTRY:
Type of rotorcraft in which horizontally spinning rotors supply lift and thrust. Helicopters can take off and land vertically, hover, and fly in any direction, so they operate where [[Fixed-wing aircraft|fixed-wing aircraft]] need a runway. The Focke-Wulf Fw 61 was the first successful, practical and fully controllable helicopter, in 1936. Igor Sikorsky's VS-300 became the basis for modern helicopters with a single main rotor and a tail rotor, the most common configuration. The Sikorsky R-4 was the first helicopter to reach full-scale production, in 1942. Quadcopters were pioneered in France as early as 1907, and multicopters are now widely used as [[Unmanned aerial vehicle|drones]]. The term was coined in 1861 by Gustave Ponton d'Amécourt.

=== KEY: Heliosphere_cdea6b
HEADWORD: Heliosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Solar System | Solar wind | Sun | Voyager 1 | Voyager 2
ENTRY:
Astrosphere of the [[Sun]], a bubble-shaped region of space. It is inflated by the outward flow of the [[Solar wind|solar wind]], which is bounded by the interstellar medium. The heliosphere shields the [[Solar System]] from much cosmic ionizing radiation, though uncharged gamma rays pass through. Its outer boundary, the heliopause, lies far beyond the orbit of Pluto. [[Voyager 1]] encountered the heliopause on 25 August 2012, and [[Voyager 2]] traversed it on 5 November 2018.

=== KEY: Helium_3_1268de
HEADWORD: Helium-3
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fermion | Isotope | Moon | Nuclear fusion | Solar wind
ENTRY:
Light, stable [[Isotope|isotope]] of helium with two protons and one neutron. Its nucleus is called a helion. It contrasts with the more common helium-4, which has two neutrons. Helium-3 atoms are [[Fermion|fermions]] and become a superfluid at 2.491 mK. It was discovered in 1939. Helium-3 is thought to be more abundant on the [[Moon]] than on Earth, deposited there by the [[Solar wind|solar wind]] over billions of years. Its [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]] has long been viewed as a desirable energy source, because the fusion of two of its atoms would be aneutronic.

=== KEY: Helium_baa8b7
HEADWORD: Helium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hydrogen | Noble gas | Superconductivity
ENTRY:
Chemical element with atomic number 2 and symbol He. It is a colorless, odorless, inert monatomic [[Noble gas|noble gas]] with the lowest boiling point of any element, and it has no melting point at standard pressures. Helium is the second-most abundant element in the observable universe, after [[Hydrogen]], and makes up about 24% of its elemental mass. It was first detected as an unknown yellow spectral line in sunlight in 1868, and isolated on Earth in 1895 by William Ramsay, Per Teodor Cleve and Nils Abraham Langlet. Cryogenics consumes about a quarter of production, and liquid helium cools the [[Superconductivity|superconducting]] magnets in MRI scanners. Helium also serves as a lifting gas in balloons and airships.

Art of the Hellenistic period, roughly 323–30 BC. Common iron oxide mineral, Fe₂O₃, found in rocks and soils. It is mined as an important [[Iron|iron]] ore and is harder than pure iron but much more brittle. === KEY: Hematology_0eb49b

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anemia | Blood | Hemoglobin | Leukemia | Lymphoma | Multiple myeloma | Spleen
ENTRY:
Branch of medicine concerned with diseases of the [[Blood|blood]]. It covers the study of the cause, prognosis, treatment and prevention of disorders affecting blood cells, platelets, clotting factors and [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]], as well as the bone marrow and [[Spleen|spleen]] that produce these components. Examples include [[Anemia|anemias]], bleeding and clotting disorders, and blood cancers such as [[Leukemia|leukemia]], [[Lymphoma|lymphoma]] and [[Multiple myeloma]]. Physicians who specialize in the field, called hematologists, diagnose and treat both noncancerous and cancerous disorders in hospitals and clinics, while laboratory-based hematopathologists interpret blood films and bone marrow slides. The term first appeared in the medical literature in 1743, in the work of German physician Thomas Schwenke.

=== KEY: Hemiptera_78f3da

Order of [[Insect|insects]] commonly called true bugs, with more than 100,000 species. Members include [[Cicada|cicadas]], [[Aphid|aphids]], planthoppers, leafhoppers, assassin bugs, [[Bed bug|bed bugs]] and shield bugs, which range from 1 mm to around 15 cm (6 in) in size. All share piercing-sucking mouthparts. Most feed on plant sap, some are bloodsucking, and others prey on insects or small invertebrates. Some species are significant agricultural pests, and some kissing bugs transmit Chagas disease. A few have been cultivated for cochineal and carmine dyes and for [[Shellac|shellac]], and cicadas have been eaten as food.

Division of the globe into two equal halves. Hemispheres are typically divided into northern and southern halves by the [[Equator]], and into western and eastern halves by the [[Prime meridian]]. They can also be divided culturally, by religion, or by prominent geographic features, such as the land-water division. === KEY: Hemoglobin_172a9f
HEADWORD: Hemoglobin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Claude Bernard | Iron | Nitric oxide | Oxygen | Red blood cell | Respiration (physiology) | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
[[Iron]]-containing protein that carries [[Oxygen|oxygen]] in [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]]. Almost all [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] have hemoglobin, with the sole exception of the fish family Channichthyidae. It carries oxygen from the lungs or gills to the body's tissues, where it is released for [[Respiration (physiology)|aerobic respiration]]. In mammals hemoglobin makes up about 96% of a red blood cell's dry weight, and it can bind and transport up to four oxygen molecules. Its oxygen-binding capacity of 1.34 mL of O₂ per gram increases the total blood oxygen capacity seventy-fold compared with dissolved oxygen in plasma alone. It also carries about 20–25% of the body's respiratory [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and the regulatory molecule [[Nitric oxide|nitric oxide]]. In 1959 Max Perutz determined its molecular structure, and he shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with John Kendrew. The role of hemoglobin in the blood was elucidated by the French physiologist [[Claude Bernard]].

PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Dutch theoretical physicist. He shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics with Pieter Zeeman for their discovery and theoretical explanation of the Zeeman effect. He derived the [[Lorentz transformation]] of the [[Special relativity|special theory of relativity]], and the [[Lorentz force]], which describes the force on a charged particle in an electromagnetic field. He also developed the Lorentz oscillator model of anomalous dispersion in [[Dielectric|dielectric]] materials. In 1878 he was appointed to the newly established chair of theoretical physics at Leiden University, where he was primarily interested in electromagnetic theory, refining the ideas of [[James Clerk Maxwell|Maxwell]]. In 1912 he became curator of the Physical Cabinet at Teylers Museum in Haarlem. From 1925 until his death he chaired the International Committee on Intellectual Cooperation, the forerunner of [[UNESCO]]. He had first asked [[Albert Einstein]] to succeed him at Leiden, but Einstein declined because he had just taken a position at [[ETH Zurich]].

=== KEY: Henri_Becquerel_f3a398

French experimental physicist. He shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics with [[Marie Curie]] and [[Pierre Curie]] for the discovery of radioactivity. After [[Wilhelm Röntgen|Röntgen]]'s discovery of [[X-ray|X-rays]] in late 1895, he looked for a link between them and the phosphorescence he had been studying. He placed [[Uranium|uranium]] salts on photographic plates wrapped in black paper, and the plates showed images even when left in a dark cabinet. He noted the physiological effects of radioactivity, which sparked its medical use in radiotherapy.

French philosopher. === KEY: Henri_Poincar_4f0212
HEADWORD: Poincaré, Henri
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic topology | Chaos theory | Hendrik Lorentz | Karl Popper | Lorentz transformation | Poincaré conjecture | Special relativity | Three-body problem
ENTRY:
French mathematician, theoretical physicist, engineer and philosopher of science. Often described as a polymath, he is called "The Last Universalist" in mathematics, having excelled in every field of the discipline as it existed in his lifetime. In his research on the [[Three-body problem|three-body problem]], he became the first to discover a chaotic deterministic system, laying foundations of modern [[Chaos theory|chaos theory]]. He is regarded as the creator of [[Algebraic topology|algebraic topology]], and early in the 20th century he formulated the [[Poincaré conjecture]], which was solved in 2002–2003 by Grigori Perelman. He was the first to present the [[Lorentz transformation|Lorentz transformations]] in their modern symmetrical form, and he helped lay the foundations of [[Special relativity|special relativity]]. In a 1905 letter to [[Hendrik Lorentz]] he recorded the remaining relativistic velocity transformations. Philosopher [[Karl Popper]] regarded him as the greatest philosopher of science of all time. He served as president of the French Academy of Sciences in 1906.

=== KEY: Henri_Rousseau_6ed8b9

ENTRY:
French post-Impressionist painter in the naive or primitive manner. Known as Le Douanier (the customs officer), a humorous description of his job as a toll and tax collector in Paris, he began painting seriously in his early forties and retired from his job at 49 to paint full-time. Critics ridiculed him during his lifetime, but he came to be recognized as a self-taught artist of high quality. His painting *The Hungry Lion Throws Itself on the Antelope* was exhibited in 1905 near works by [[Henri Matisse]] and other younger avant-garde artists, in what is seen as the first showing of the Fauves. In 1908 [[Pablo Picasso]] held a banquet in his studio in honor of Rousseau, now considered one of the most notable social events of the twentieth century. His work influenced several generations of [[Avant-garde|avant-garde]] artists.

Norwegian playwright, regarded as the father of modern [[Drama|drama]]. From 1864 he lived for 27 years in Italy and Germany, mainly in [[Rome]], [[Dresden]] and [[Munich]], before moving to Christiania (now [[Oslo]]) in 1891. He pioneered theatrical realism and, after *Peer Gynt*, abandoned verse for realistic prose. Several of his later dramas were considered scandalous in an era when European theater was expected to uphold strict morals of family life. Critics often rate *The Wild Duck* and *Rosmersholm* as his best works, while he regarded *Emperor and Galilean* as his masterpiece. [[Sigmund Freud]] placed him on a par with Shakespeare and [[Sophocles]], and [[George Bernard Shaw]] argued that he had surpassed Shakespeare as the world's pre-eminent dramatist.

=== KEY: Henry_Cavendish_13cc11

ENTRY:
English experimental and theoretical chemist and physicist. He is noted for his discovery of [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]], which he termed "inflammable air." In an 1766 paper, *On Factitious Airs*, he described the density of this gas, which formed water on combustion. [[Antoine Lavoisier]] later reproduced the experiment and gave the element its name. Reclusive and publishing few papers, Cavendish conducted research notable for its accuracy and precision. His work included the composition of the atmosphere, the properties of different gases, the synthesis of water, the law governing electrical attraction and repulsion, and a mechanical theory of heat. His experiment to measure the density of the [[Earth]], which informs the calculation of the [[Gravitational constant|gravitational constant]], is known as the Cavendish experiment. He entered the [[University of Cambridge]] in 1748 but left without a degree, and in 1773 he was elected a trustee of the [[British Museum]].

[[Inflammation|Inflammation]] of the [[Liver|liver]] tissue, which may cause [[Jaundice|jaundice]], fatigue or no symptoms at all. === KEY: Heraldry_51c066

ENTRY:
Discipline concerned with the design, display, and study of armorial bearings. A full heraldic achievement may include a coat of arms on a [[Shield|shield]], a helmet and crest, and supporters, badges, banners, and mottoes, formally described in a blazon. Devices signifying individuals and groups date back to antiquity, but regular hereditary designs did not develop until the [[Middle Ages|High Middle Ages]]. Early emblems, such as Egyptian royal symbols topped by the falcon of [[Horus]], are regarded as precursors, since no distinct symbolic language existed. Heraldry survived the abandonment of armor on the battlefield during the seventeenth century and is still used by individuals, organizations, and cities to symbolize heritage and aspirations. Related disciplines include vexillology, [[Genealogy|genealogy]], and the study of ceremony and rank. Heraldry has been described as the "handmaid of history."

Savory or aromatic plant, excluding vegetables, used for flavoring food, for medicine, or for fragrance. In [[Botany|botany]], an herb is a plant without a woody stem whose aerial parts die back each season, and the adjective "herbaceous" derives from Latin for "grassy." Under this strict definition, sage, [[Rosemary|rosemary]], and lavender would be excluded because they have woody stems. Theophrastus divided the plant world into trees, shrubs, and herbs. The [[Talmud]] refers to approximately fifteen herbs used for seasoning. Herbs were grouped as pot herbs, sweet herbs such as [[Thyme|thyme]], and salad herbs. In the seventeenth century, selective breeding changed pot herbs so much that they came to be called [[Vegetable|vegetables]].

Study and use of medicinal plants, which are a basis of traditional medicine, also called herbalism or phytotherapy. === KEY: Herbivore_8d89a4

Animal anatomically or physiologically adapted to feed mainly on plants, especially on vascular tissues such as foliage, fruits, and seeds. === KEY: Herd_604700
HEADWORD: Herd
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ecology | Ungulate
ENTRY:
Social group of animals of the same species, usually mammals, most particularly grazing [[Ungulate|ungulates]]. Herds, whether wild or domestic, tend to act together without planning or coordination, each individual responding to the others or to the same external circumstances. The collective behavior is called herding, and the animals are gregarious. Birds form flocks, and large groups of carnivores are called packs, though herd is used generically in theoretical discussions of [[Ecology|behavioral ecology]]. === KEY: Herding_dog_fbee15

=== KEY: Heredity_e20562
HEADWORD: Heredity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | DNA | Francis Galton | Gene | Genetics | Gregor Mendel | Natural selection
ENTRY:
Passing on of genetic information from parents to offspring, also called inheritance or biological inheritance. Heredity occurs through sexual reproduction, in which two cells combine genomes, or asexual reproduction, in which new cells arise from the division of individual cells. Through heredity, genetic variations accumulate in populations and can cause species to evolve by [[Natural selection|natural selection]]. Its study is the field of [[Genetics|genetics]].

Heritable traits pass between generations via [[DNA]], a long polymer that encodes genetic information in the sequence of four types of bases. A portion of DNA specifying a single functional unit is a [[Gene|gene]]. Within cells, DNA forms condensed structures called chromosomes. Different forms of a sequence at a particular locus are called alleles, and mutations produce new ones. An organism's complete set of genes is its genotype, and the observable traits of its structure and behavior are its phenotype. Phenotype arises from the interaction of genotype with environment, so many aspects of it are not inherited: a suntan, for example, is not passed to children. Most traits are controlled by multiple interacting genes.

Some heritable changes cannot be explained by DNA alone. These epigenetic inheritance systems include DNA methylation, gene silencing by RNA interference, and the conformation of proteins such as prions. Inheritance also operates above the gene, through ecological inheritance and cultural traits.

When [[Charles Darwin]] proposed his theory of evolution in 1859, it lacked a mechanism of heredity. He held a mix of blending inheritance and the inheritance of acquired traits. His cousin [[Francis Galton]] modified Darwin's model and laid the framework for the biometric school of heredity. The foundational principles of heredity were established by [[Gregor Mendel]] in the mid-1860s through experiments on pea plants, which showed that traits are inherited as discrete factors, now called alleles.

Ceremonial county in the East of England, bordering [[Greater London]] to the south. Its largest settlement is Watford, and it has ten districts. The county covers 634 square miles (1,640 km2) and had an estimated population of 1,236,191 in 2024. Much of its undeveloped land is agricultural, and much of the county lies within the Metropolitan green belt. Letchworth has served as the prototype garden city since 1903, and Stevenage became the first town to expand under the New Towns Act 1946. St Albans includes remains of the Roman town of Verulamium. The name Hertford derives from an Anglo-Saxon term meaning deer crossing.

=== KEY: Hertz_f37352
HEADWORD: Hertz
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Heinrich Hertz | International System of Units
ENTRY:
[[International System of Units|SI]] unit of frequency, equal to one cycle or event per second. The hertz (symbol Hz) is used only for periodic events, and its base-unit form is 1/s. It is named after [[Heinrich Hertz]], who provided the first conclusive proof of the existence of electromagnetic waves. Common multiples include kilohertz, megahertz, gigahertz and terahertz. The unit describes periodic waveforms and musical tones, particularly in radio and audio, and the clock speeds of computers. A disc rotating at 60 revolutions per minute has a frequency of 1 Hz. The name replaced "cycles per second" and was adopted by the General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1960.

=== KEY: Hertzsprung_Russell_diagram_6e2eb5
HEADWORD: Hertzsprung–Russell diagram
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Main sequence | Red giant | Stellar evolution | Sun | Supergiant | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between their absolute magnitudes or luminosities and their stellar classifications or effective temperatures. It was created independently in 1911 by Ejnar Hertzsprung and in 1913 by Henry Norris Russell, and it represented a major step toward an understanding of [[Stellar evolution|stellar evolution]]. Luminosity increases toward the top of the diagram and surface temperature increases toward the left. Most stars lie along the [[Main sequence]], where stars such as the [[Sun]] spend most of their lives. [[Red giant|Red giants]] and [[Supergiant|supergiants]] lie above it, and [[White dwarf|white dwarfs]] lie below. The diagram is also called a color–magnitude diagram, and observers often use it for stars in clusters, whose members share a single distance.

=== KEY: Hesiod_04e26d
HEADWORD: Hesiod
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Greek mythology | Homer | Muses | Zeus
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek poet of the archaic period, traditionally associated with the Boeotian town of Ascra. His surviving works include the Theogony, which traces the origins of the gods and the events that led to the rise of [[Zeus]] to power, and Works and Days, a didactic poem addressed to his brother Perses. Works and Days offers farming advice and moral wisdom, describes the five Ages of Man and includes the myth of Pandora's box. Hesiod's account of being given a laurel staff by the [[Muses]] on Mount Helicon, while pasturing sheep, is among the few personal details in his work. Ancient authors credited him and [[Homer]] with establishing Greek religious customs, and modern scholars regard him as a major source on [[Greek mythology]], farming, early economic thought, [[Astronomy|Archaic Greek astronomy]] and ancient timekeeping.
