=== KEY: Mountain_range_8e1ea1
HEADWORD: Mountain range
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andes | Himalayas | Mount Everest | Mountain pass | Plate tectonics | Ring of Fire
ENTRY:
Series of mountains or hills arranged in a line and connected by high ground. A mountain system or belt is a group of ranges with similar form, structure and alignment that arose from the same cause, usually an orogeny. Most significant ranges on Earth result from [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Ranges are usually segmented by highlands, [[Mountain pass|mountain passes]] and valleys. Most young ranges on the land surface are associated with the [[Ring of Fire|Pacific Ring of Fire]] or the Alpide belt. The [[Andes]], often considered the world's longest mountain system, are 7,000 kilometers (4,350 mi) long. The [[Himalayas]] contain the highest mountains, including [[Mount Everest]], which is 8,848 meters (29,029 ft) high. Air rising over a range cools and drops its moisture, leaving a rain shadow on the leeward side, and erosion gradually wears mountains down into low hills and plains.

=== KEY: Mountaineering_bcc22c
HEADWORD: Mountaineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climbing | Mont Blanc | Skiing
ENTRY:
Outdoor activity involving the ascent of mountains, including traditional [[Climbing|climbing]], [[Skiing]] and via ferratas. In everyday language the term is synonymous with mountain climbing or alpinism. Unlike most sports, mountaineering lacks widely applied formal rules and governance; practitioners follow a variety of techniques and philosophies, and local alpine clubs provide resources and social activities. The International Climbing and Mountaineering Federation (UIAA) is the world organization for the sport and is recognized by the International Olympic Committee. In 1492 Antoine de Ville climbed Mont Aiguille using ropes, ladders and iron hooks, an ascent widely recognized as the birth of mountaineering. In 1786 Jacques Balmat claimed a reward offered by Horace-Bénédict de Saussure for the first ascent of [[Mont Blanc]].

=== KEY: Mouse_f2a47c
HEADWORD: Mouse
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dog | Feces | Fox | House mouse | Rat | Rodent
ENTRY:
Small [[Rodent|rodent]] with a pointed snout, small rounded ears and a body-length scaly tail. The best-known species is the [[House mouse|common house mouse]] (*Mus musculus*). Mice are typically distinguished from [[Rat|rats]] by size, although the name is not confined to the genus *Mus*. The white lab mouse, with its more uniform traits, is widely used in research. Cats, [[Dog|wild dogs]], [[Fox|foxes]], birds of prey, snakes and some large lizards prey on mice, yet mouse populations remain plentiful. Mice spread disease through their [[Feces|feces]] and are often carriers of parasites; in North America, breathing dust contaminated with mouse excrement has been linked to hantavirus. As primarily nocturnal animals, mice compensate for poor eyesight with a keen sense of hearing and rely on smell to locate food. In the wild they build intricate burrows with long entrances and escape tunnels.

=== KEY: Moustache_9afcaf
HEADWORD: Mustache
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolf Hitler | Beard | Celts | Middle Ages
ENTRY:
Growth of facial hair above the upper lip and below the nose, mostly on men. Mustaches have been worn in many styles throughout history. The word is French, derived from Italian, and ultimately from a Greek word meaning "upper lip" or "facial hair." One of the earliest documents of mustaches without beards comes from Iron Age [[Celts]], according to the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus, whose account describes Gallic nobles letting their mustaches grow until they covered the mouth. Mustaches remained common through the [[Middle Ages]], and their popularity in the West peaked in the 1880s and 1890s. In many 20th-century Arab countries, mustaches were associated with power, while [[Beard|beards]] were associated with Islamic traditionalism. In Islam, trimming the mustache is considered a recommended practice. After [[Adolf Hitler]] adopted the narrow toothbrush mustache, it became stigmatized in post-1945 Western fashion.

=== KEY: Mouth_d39f27
HEADWORD: Mouth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anus | Bilateria | Ontogeny | Tetrapod | Tongue | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Body opening through which many animals ingest food and vocalize. The oral cavity behind it is the first part of the alimentary canal, which leads to the pharynx and gullet. In [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]], the mouth is bounded by the lips and cheeks, and the oral cavity, from Latin bucca, meaning "cheek," is also called the buccal cavity. It contains the [[Tongue]]. Except for some groups such as birds and lissamphibians, [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] usually have teeth, although some fish have pharyngeal teeth instead of oral teeth. Most [[Bilateria|bilaterian]] animals, including arthropods, mollusks and chordates, have a gut tube with a mouth at one end and an [[Anus|anus]] at the other. Which end forms first in [[Ontogeny|ontogeny]] is used to classify bilaterians as protostomes or deuterostomes. Sponges have no mouth or gut and instead capture food by endocytosis.

=== KEY: Movie_theater_7b9eed
HEADWORD: Movie theater
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Candy | Fast food | Lumière brothers | Popcorn
ENTRY:
Venue containing auditoriums for the public viewing of films. Most are commercial operations that sell tickets to audiences. Films are projected onto a large screen at the front of each auditorium, and sound plays through wall-mounted speakers, with subwoofers used for low-pitched sounds since the 1970s. In some jurisdictions they may be licensed to sell alcoholic drinks. The first commercial film screenings took place in Paris in 1895, presented by the [[Lumière brothers]].

=== KEY: Mozambique_Channel_40cc2e
HEADWORD: Mozambique Channel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comoros | Indian Ocean | Madagascar | Mozambique | Southern Africa
ENTRY:
Arm of the [[Indian Ocean]] between [[Madagascar]] and [[Mozambique]] in southeastern Africa. The channel is about 1,700 km (900 nmi; 1,100 mi) long and 419 km (226 nmi; 260 mi) across at its narrowest point. It reaches a depth of 3,292 m (10,801 ft) about 230 km (124 nmi; 143 mi) off the coast of Mozambique. A warm current, the Mozambique Current, flows southward through the channel and leads into the Agulhas Current off the east coast of [[Southern Africa|southern Africa]]. Its warm waters support coral reefs, mangroves and seagrass beds, and its fisheries provide livelihoods for coastal populations in Mozambique, Tanzania and the [[Comoros]]. Offshore areas hold natural gas reserves, which have driven exploration and development.

Country in southeastern Africa on the Indian Ocean, with its capital at [[Maputo]] (pop., 2024: 34,777,605). It borders [[Tanzania]], [[Malawi]], [[Zambia]], [[Zimbabwe]], Eswatini and [[South Africa]], and it is separated from Madagascar by the [[Mozambique Channel]]. Swahili port towns developed on its coast between the 7th and 11th centuries. Portuguese colonization began in 1505, after [[Vasco da Gama]] reached the area in 1498. Mozambique gained independence in 1975, and a civil war lasted from 1977 to 1992. The economy is based chiefly on fishing and agriculture, with growing food, chemical, aluminum and oil industries. Portuguese is the only official language, and Christianity is the most popular religion. Despite rich natural resources, it remains one of the world's poorest and least developed countries.

Thai martial art and full-contact combat sport, also called Thai boxing or the Art of Eight Limbs. [[Amphibian|Amphibious]] goby fish of the subfamily Oxudercinae, able to survive long periods out of water. Mudskippers can grow up to 30 cm (12 in) long, and most are brownish green. === KEY: Mughal_Empire_fb6e95
HEADWORD: Mughal Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Akbar | Aurangzeb | Babur | Humayun | Nader Shah | Shah Jahan | Sher Shah Suri | Taj Mahal
ENTRY:
Early modern empire in South Asia, 1526–1857.

At its peak it stretched from the fringes of the Indus River Basin in the west and northern Afghanistan in the northwest to Kashmir in the north, the highlands of Assam and Bangladesh in the east, and the Deccan Plateau in the south.

It is conventionally said to have been founded in 1526 by [[Babur]], a Timurid prince from the Fergana Valley, who defeated Ibrahim Lodi, Sultan of Delhi, at the First Battle of Panipat, using firearms and cannons. His son [[Humayun]] was driven into exile in Persia by [[Sher Shah Suri]], returned in 1555, and died the next year. [[Akbar]] (r. 1556–1605) consolidated the empire. The imperial structure is sometimes dated from Akbar's rule in 1600 and lasted until 1720, shortly after the death of [[Aurangzeb]] (r. 1658–1707), under whom the empire reached its greatest extent. It was reduced to the area around Old Delhi by 1760, and the British Raj formally dissolved it after the Indian Rebellion of 1857.

Though created by warfare, the empire did not vigorously suppress the cultures it ruled, instead governing through new administrative practices and diverse ruling elites. Its wealth rested on agricultural taxes introduced by Akbar, amounting to well over half a peasant cultivator's output and paid in a well-regulated silver currency. Akbar's policy of *sulh-i-kul* ('universal peace') abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims. Critics argue that Aurangzeb's reimposition of the jizya and stricter application of Islamic law alienated non-Muslim subjects and fueled rebellions by Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, and Rajputs. [[Nader Shah]] attacked Delhi in 1739.

Relative peace in the 17th century aided India's economic expansion, and court wealth patronized painting, literature, textiles, and architecture, especially under [[Shah Jahan]]. Its UNESCO World Heritage Sites include the Red Fort and the [[Taj Mahal]].

=== KEY: Muhammad_0b2531
HEADWORD: Muhammad
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Druze | Gabriel | Hadith | Islam | Islamic calendar | Mecca | Medina | Quran
ENTRY:
Arab religious, military, and political leader, founder of [[Islam]]. According to Islam, he was the final prophet of God who confirmed the monotheistic teachings of earlier prophets including Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. Muslims believe him to be the Seal of the Prophets, and his teachings and normative examples, along with the [[Quran]], form the basis of Islamic religious belief.

According to the traditional account, he was born in [[Mecca]] to the aristocratic Banu Hashim clan of the Quraysh and was orphaned at six, raised by his grandfather and his uncle Abu Talib. In c. 610, at age 40, he reported being visited by [[Gabriel]] in a cave and receiving his first revelation. From 613 he preached publicly that "God is One" and that complete "submission" (*Islām*) to God is the right way of life. His followers were persecuted by Meccan polytheists for 13 years. In 622 they migrated to [[Medina]] (then Yathrib), an event, the Hijrah, that marks the beginning of the [[Islamic calendar]]. In December 629, after eight years of intermittent fighting with Meccan tribes, he marched on Mecca with 10,000 converts and seized it with minimal casualties. He died in 632, a few months after the Farewell Pilgrimage; by then most of the Arabian Peninsula had converted to Islam.

The revelations he reported form the verses of the Quran, which Muslims regard as the verbatim word of God. His teachings and practices, recorded in transmitted reports known as [[Hadith|hadith]] and in his biography (*sīrah*), are also sources of Islamic law. He also holds a significant role in the [[Druze]] faith as one of seven main prophets.

=== KEY: Mule_4de09e
HEADWORD: Mule
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Death Valley | Donkey | Hittites | Horse | Tractor
ENTRY:
Domestic equine hybrid, offspring of a male [[Donkey|donkey]] and a female [[Horse|horse]]. Mules are more patient, hardier and longer-lived than horses, and are perceived as less obstinate and more intelligent than donkeys. Horses have 64 chromosomes and donkeys 62, so a mule's 63 cannot pair properly, and mules are typically sterile. Mules were probably bred in Anatolia and Mesopotamia before 1000 BC, and the [[Hittites]] valued them more highly than their best horses. In the 19th century mules became favored draft animals on farms, and from 1883 to 1889 teams of 18 mules and two horses hauled borax out of [[Death Valley]], California. Their use declined in industrialized countries in the second half of the 20th century, as [[Tractor|tractors]] replaced them.

=== KEY: Muscle_36143f
HEADWORD: Muscle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Heart | Mitochondria | Neurotransmitter | Sarcomere | Skeletal muscle | Tendon
ENTRY:
Specialized soft tissue, one of the four basic types of animal tissue, whose cells contract to produce force and movement. In vertebrates there are three types: [[Skeletal muscle|skeletal]], cardiac, and smooth muscle. All contain the contractile proteins actin and myosin, which interact to cause movement, along with the regulatory proteins troponin and tropomyosin.

Skeletal muscle is striated and made of elongated, multinucleate cells called muscle fibers, 10 to 100 micrometers wide, containing contractile units called [[Sarcomere|sarcomeres]]. Anchored to bone by [[Tendon|tendons]], it contracts voluntarily under the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]] and moves the body and maintains posture. It makes up 42% of the body mass of an average adult man and 36% of an average adult woman. Cardiac muscle, found only in the walls of the [[Heart|heart]], is striated but involuntary. Smooth muscle, non-striated and involuntary, lies in the walls of organs and structures including the esophagus, stomach, intestines, uterus, bladder, and blood vessels.

Skeletal muscle fibers are classified as type I (slow-twitch), rich in capillaries, [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]], and myoglobin and suited to sustained aerobic activity, or type II (fast-twitch), which contracts more quickly and with greater force but sustains only short anaerobic bursts. Mammalian skeletal muscle has a density of about 1.06 kg/liter, roughly 15% denser than fat. Muscle types differ in their response to [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitters]] and hormones such as acetylcholine, adrenaline, and nitric oxide. In all types, contraction depends on calcium ions triggering interaction between actin and myosin filaments. The word derives from Latin *musculus*, a diminutive of *mus*, "mouse", because a flexed biceps resembles the back of a mouse.

=== KEY: Muscle_cell_fceea7
HEADWORD: Muscle cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Esophagus | Sarcomere | Stomach | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Mature contractile cell in animal muscle, also called a myocyte. Humans and other [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] have three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac. Skeletal muscle cells are long and threadlike with many nuclei and are called muscle fibers; they form by fusion of embryonic precursor cells called myoblasts, a process known as myogenesis. A single human biceps brachii contains around 253,000 muscle fibers. Cardiac muscle cells form the walls of the heart chambers, have a single central nucleus and are joined by intercalated discs. Smooth muscle cells have a single nucleus and no [[Sarcomere|sarcomeres]], and control involuntary movements such as peristalsis in the [[Esophagus]] and [[Stomach]].

=== KEY: Muscle_contraction_7cfd5a
HEADWORD: Muscle contraction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Muscle cell | Nervous system | Protein
ENTRY:
Activation of tension-generating sites within [[Muscle cell|muscle cells]]. Contraction does not necessarily mean shortening: an isometric contraction produces tension without a change in length, as when holding a heavy object still. Thin filaments of actin and thick filaments of the motor [[Protein|protein]] myosin form myofibrils, the basic functional organelles of skeletal muscle. In vertebrates, skeletal muscle contraction is neurogenic and requires input from motor neurons. According to the sliding filament theory, the filaments slide past each other to shorten the fiber, producing a twitch, summation or tetanus depending on the frequency of action potentials. Smooth and cardiac muscle contract myogenically, although the [[Nervous system|autonomic nervous system]] can modulate them. Calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum initiates contraction, and [[Adenosine triphosphate|ATP]] powers the cross-bridge cycling that drives sliding.

=== KEY: Muscular_system_f6c3fe
HEADWORD: Muscular system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Blood | Calcium | Hormone | Muscle | Nervous system | Sarcomere
ENTRY:
Organ system of skeletal, smooth and cardiac [[Muscle|muscle]]. It permits movement, maintains posture, circulates [[Blood|blood]] and generates heat. Together with the skeletal system it forms the musculoskeletal system. An adult male human body has more than 600 muscles, and skeletal muscle makes up about 40% of body mass. === KEY: Museum_68d282

Institution dedicated to displaying and preserving culturally or scientifically significant objects. Some collections are kept from display for researchers. Collections often focus on a theme such as the arts, science, natural history, or local history.

=== KEY: Music_47dcbd
HEADWORD: Music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Charles Darwin | Muses | Music industry | Musical instrument | Origin of language | Plato | Ugarit
ENTRY:
Art of arranging sound to create some combination of form, harmony, melody, rhythm, or other expressive content. Music is generally agreed to be a cultural universal, present in all human societies, though definitions vary widely and scholars have reached no consensus on which elements are necessary. Its making is often divided into composition, improvisation, and performance. It may be performed on a wide variety of [[Musical instrument|musical instruments]], including the human voice, or produced mechanically or electronically.

Music plays a key role in social events and religious ceremonies, and its techniques are often transmitted as part of a cultural tradition. It is performed at festivals and concerts and used in the soundtracks of films, television shows, operas, and video games. Listening is a common means of entertainment, and the culture around music extends to academic study, journalism, philosophy, psychology, and therapy. The [[Music industry|music industry]] includes songwriters, composers, performers, producers, and publishers of sheet music and recordings. Recording technology has included microphones, phonographs, and tape machines, with digital playback common on smartphones.

The word entered English in the 1630s, from Middle English *musike*, Old French *musique*, and Latin *mūsica*, ultimately from the Greek *mousiké technē*, "art of the [[Muses]]". Concepts of music vary across cultures. Before Western contact, neither Japan nor China had a single word covering music in a broad sense; the Chinese *yue* shares a character with *le*, "joy". The Songye of the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the Tiv of Nigeria have a broad conception of music but no corresponding word.

Origins are debated. Many scholars see a relationship with the [[Origin of language|origin of language]], and disagree on whether music arose before, after, or alongside it, and on whether it was a direct result of natural selection or a byproduct of evolution. [[Charles Darwin]] proposed in 1871 that music arose through sexual selection, perhaps via mating calls; the theory has been heavily criticized, though some 21st-century scholars have promoted it. Other theories hold that music arose to organize labor, aid communication, build community cohesion, or scare off predators. The disputed Divje Babe flute, a perforated cave bear femur, is at least 40,000 years old. The earliest widely accepted instruments are bone flutes from the Swabian Jura, Germany, with three from Geissenklösterle dated oldest, c. 43,150–39,370 BP.

In [[Ancient Egypt]], harps, flutes, and double clarinets are securely attested in the Old Kingdom, and percussion instruments, lyres, and lutes joined orchestras by the Middle Kingdom. The "Hurrian Hymn to Nikkal", found on clay tablets in the Syrian city of [[Ugarit]] and dating to approximately 1400 BC, is the oldest surviving notated work of music. In ancient Greece music was one of the main subjects taught to children, with boys beginning at age six, and musical education was considered important for the development of the soul, as in [[Plato]]'s *Republic*.

=== KEY: Music_genre_a19cd1
HEADWORD: Music genre
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Country music | Jazz | Music
ENTRY:
Conventional category that groups pieces of [[Music|music]] sharing a tradition or set of conventions. Genre is distinguished from musical form and style, though the terms are sometimes used interchangeably. Music is divided into genres in many ways, such as popular versus art music, or religious versus secular. Classification is often subjective and controversial, since genres evolve and novel music is sometimes lumped into existing categories. A subgenre adopts the basic characteristics of its genre while distinguishing itself within it; the proliferation of popular music in the 20th century has produced over 1,200 definable subgenres. Fusion genres combine two or more genres, as [[Jazz|jazz]] fusion combines jazz and rock and [[Country music|country rock]] combines country music and rock music. In 1982, Franco Fabbri proposed a definition now considered normative: "musical genre is a set of musical events (real or possible) whose course is governed by a definite set of socially accepted rules."

=== KEY: Music_of_Africa_5e25a5

Diverse musical traditions of the African continent, shaped by distinct regional and national cultures. African music is highly rhythmic and often uses polyrhythm, in which one rhythm is played against another. Percussion is central, and sub-Saharan traditions rely on [[Xylophone|xylophones]], [[Djembe|djembes]], [[Drum|drums]] and tone-producing instruments such as the mbira. === KEY: Music_of_China_c8b23b
HEADWORD: Music of China
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Confucius | Flute | Yin and yang | Zhou dynasty
ENTRY:
Traditions of music in China, including classical court forms, folk music and modern popular music. Archaeological finds show a developed musical culture as early as the [[Zhou dynasty]] (1122–257 BC), when a formal system of court and ceremonial music later termed yayue was established. Bone [[Flute|flutes]] dated to 9,000 years ago have been found at Jiahu Village in Henan. In ancient China, musicians had lower social status than painters. Since the early 20th century, Chinese composers have integrated Western orchestration and notation, and popular genres now draw on global markets, though state oversight shapes lyrical content.

=== KEY: Music_theory_91ec63
HEADWORD: Music theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Counterpoint | Harmony | Musicology | Scale (music)
ENTRY:
Study of the theoretical frameworks for understanding the practices and possibilities of music. The term has three related uses: the rudiments needed to read notation, such as key signatures, time signatures and rhythmic notation; the study of scholars' views on music from antiquity to the present; and a subtopic of [[Musicology|musicology]] that seeks to define processes and general principles in music. Music theory covers tuning systems, [[Scale (music)|scales]], consonance and dissonance, and rhythmic relationships, as well as [[Harmony|harmony]] and [[Counterpoint|counterpoint]]. Teaching as a tenure-track music theorist at a university in the United States or Canada typically requires graduate study to the MA or PhD level.

=== KEY: Musical_instrument_69a981
HEADWORD: Musical instrument
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric guitar | Flute | Mesopotamia | Neanderthal | Slovenia | Southeast Asia | Synthesizer
ENTRY:
Device created or adapted to make musical sounds. In principle any sound-producing object can be a musical instrument; purpose makes it one. Early instruments may have served rituals, such as a horn signaling a hunt, before cultures developed melodies for entertainment.

The date and origin of the first instrument are widely disputed. The oldest object identified by scholars as an instrument is a simple [[Flute|flute]] dated 50,000–60,000 years ago. Many historians consider dating the first impossible, as most early instruments were made of perishable materials. The Divje Babe flute a perforated bone found in 1995 in northwest [[Slovenia]] by Ivan Turk, is estimated at 43,400 to 67,000 years old; many argue that carnivores chewing the bone produced it, while Turk and others argue it is a [[Neanderthal]]-made flute. Mammoth bone and swan bone flutes of 30,000 to 37,000 years ago from the Swabian Alps of Germany are more commonly accepted as the oldest known instruments.

Instruments developed independently in many regions, but contact among civilizations spread and adapted most of them. By the post-classical era, instruments from [[Mesopotamia]] were in maritime [[Southeast Asia]], and Europeans played instruments originating from North Africa. Many new instruments appeared between roughly 1750 and 1900, and electricity led to new electric and electronic instruments such as the [[Electric guitar|electric guitar]], the [[Synthesizer|synthesizer]], and the theremin.

The most common academic classification, the Hornbostel–Sachs system, groups instruments by how they produce sound. Developed in 1914, it divides them into idiophones, membranophones, chordophones, and aerophones; electrophones were added later. The academic study of instruments is called organology.

=== KEY: Musical_notation_b0ff86
HEADWORD: Musical notation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Babylonia | Baroque | Cuneiform | Guitar | Papyrus | Parchment
ENTRY:
System of symbols for representing music visually, recording the elements considered important for performance in a given tradition. Ancient cultures used symbols for melodies and rhythms, but none was particularly comprehensive. The earliest known notation is a [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] tablet from Nippur in [[Babylonia]], dating to about 1400 BCE. The direct ancestor of the modern Western system emerged in medieval Europe, where it was developed to standardize the performance of plainsong melodies. Notation developed further during the Renaissance and [[Baroque]] periods and continued to evolve with instrumental technology in the Classical and Romantic eras. Since the 20th century some composers have used graphical notation, and computer-based scorewriter programs have been used since the 1980s. Classical performers mostly use staves, time signatures, key signatures and noteheads, the Nashville Number System is the main method in professional country music, and [[Guitar|guitar]] players often use tablature. Notation has been written on stone, clay tablets, [[Papyrus|papyrus]] and [[Parchment|parchment]], and later printed.

=== KEY: Musical_theatre_6fa4c5

=== KEY: Musicology_46f8fa

ENTRY:
Academic, research-based study of music, as distinct from composition or performance. Musicology draws on fields including [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Sociology|sociology]], [[Acoustics|acoustics]], neurology, the natural sciences and computer science. It is traditionally divided into three branches. Historical musicology studies the history of musical traditions, the origins of works and the lives of composers. === KEY: Muskrat_c674c9

Medium-sized semiaquatic [[Rodent|rodent]] native to North America and the only living species in the genus *Ondatra*. Adults weigh 0.6–2 kg (1¼–4½ lb) and measure 20–35 cm (8–14 in) in body length, excluding the tail. Their short, thick fur is medium to dark brown, and their scaly tails are laterally compressed and mainly help with directional stability in the water. === KEY: Muslim_conquest_of_Persia_b44f30
HEADWORD: Muslim conquest of Persia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caspian Sea | Rashidun Caliphate | Sasanian Empire | Umar | Zagros Mountains | Zoroastrianism
ENTRY:
Arab conquest of the [[Sasanian Empire]] by the [[Rashidun Caliphate]] between 633 and 651 CE, as part of the early Muslim conquests. The Sasanian state was weakened by the Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628 and by a civil war that followed the execution of Khosrow II in 628. The first Rashidun invasion, in 633, took parts of Asoristan, the Sasanian center in Mesopotamia. A second invasion began in 636, and a decisive victory at the Battle of al-Qadisiyyah ended Sasanian control west of modern Iran. For six years the [[Zagros Mountains]] marked the boundary. In 642 the caliph [[Umar]] ordered a full-scale invasion of the rest of the empire. By 651 most urban centers in Iranian lands had come under Muslim domination, except provinces along the [[Caspian Sea]], though many cities rebelled before reinforcements suppressed the uprisings. Islamization was gradual over centuries, and [[Zoroastrianism]] declined. Iran remained mostly Sunni until the Safavids converted it to Shia Islam in the 16th century.

=== KEY: Muslim_conquests_in_the_Indian_subcontinent_3a2f6d
HEADWORD: Muslim conquests in the Indian subcontinent
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Delhi Sultanate | East India Company | Indian subcontinent | Mahmud of Ghazni | Mughal Empire | Muhammad of Ghor | Umayyad Caliphate
ENTRY:
Military campaigns and territorial expansion by Muslim rulers across the [[Indian subcontinent]], mainly between the 13th and 18th centuries. Earlier [[Umayyad Caliphate|Umayyad]] campaigns reached India in the 8th century, and [[Mahmud of Ghazni]] invaded Punjab and Gujarat in the 11th century. [[Muhammad of Ghor]] laid the foundations of Muslim rule in India in 1192, and Bengal fell in 1202. The [[Delhi Sultanate]] was founded in 1206, and under Alauddin Khalji Muslim rule extended to Gujarat, Rajasthan and the Deccan. Timur's invasion in 1398 led to several regional sultanates. The Delhi Sultanate was replaced by the [[Mughal Empire]] in 1526. Under Akbar, who stressed religious tolerance, non-Muslim subjects were integrated into the bureaucracy and military. The empire peaked under Aurangzeb at the end of the 17th century and declined after his death in 1707. The conquests halted as the [[East India Company|British East India Company]] took control of much of the subcontinent, and British rule lasted until 1947. Historian K.S. Lal inferred 60–80 million excess deaths among Hindus from 1000 to 1525 CE, and historians debate the scale of such losses.

Arab Muslim [[Hadith|hadith]] scholar, known as Imam Muslim. He was born in Nishapur in the Abbasid province of [[Khorasan]], in what is now northeastern [[Iran]], and was of Arab origin. After studying across the Arabian Peninsula, in [[Egypt]], Iraq and [[Syria]], he settled in Nishapur, where he became a lifelong friend of al-Bukhari. His collection *Sahih Muslim* is one of the six major hadith books of [[Sunni Islam]] and, with *Sahih al-Bukhari*, is considered one of the two most authentic. Muslim's collection shares 2,326 traditions with al-Bukhari's, according to al-Jawzaqi. Estimates of the number of hadiths in it range from three to twelve thousand, depending on whether duplicates are counted. His students included al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Khuzayma.

Adherents of [[Islam]], a monotheistic religion of the Abrahamic tradition. Muslims regard the [[Quran]] as the verbatim word of God as revealed to Muhammad. === KEY: Mustafa_Kemal_Atat_rk_44ad02
HEADWORD: Atatürk, Mustafa Kemal
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Abdul Hamid II | Gallipoli campaign | Ottoman Empire | Surname | Thessaloniki | Turkish War of Independence
ENTRY:
Turkish field marshal and revolutionary statesman, founder of the Republic of Turkey and its first president, 1923–38. Born in [[Thessaloniki|Salonica]] in the [[Ottoman Empire]], he joined opposition movements against Sultan [[Abdul Hamid II]] and took part in the Young Turk Revolution. He rose to prominence in the defense of [[Gallipoli campaign|Gallipoli]] during World War I. After the war he led the Turkish National Movement in the [[Turkish War of Independence]], whose victory was confirmed by the Treaty of Lausanne. His government abolished the sultanate in 1922 and proclaimed the republic in 1923. As president he pursued secularist, Western-oriented reforms, replacing the semi-secular *Mecelle* with a fully secular civil code, introducing a Latin-based alphabet and granting Turkish women equal civil and political rights. Opponents were suppressed, and a one-party regime was established. Supporters praise his military, nation-building and reform record. Critics condemn his dictatorship and assimilationist policies toward minorities such as the Kurds. In 1934 the Turkish Parliament granted him the [[Surname|surname]] Atatürk, meaning "Father of the Turks."

=== KEY: Mustard_condiment_ebf706
HEADWORD: Mustard
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brassicaceae | Condiment | Hamburger | Hot dog | Sandwich | Zhou dynasty
ENTRY:
[[Condiment|Condiment]] made by mixing ground mustard seeds with water, vinegar, wine or other liquids, usually with salt and other flavorings. The seeds come from white or yellow mustard (*Sinapis alba*), brown mustard (*Brassica juncea*) or black mustard, all members of the [[Brassicaceae|mustard family]]. The paste ranges in color from bright yellow to dark brown, and in taste from sweet to spicy. Mustard is commonly served with meats, vegetables and cheeses, especially on [[Sandwich|sandwiches]], [[Hamburger|hamburgers]] and [[Hot dog|hot dogs]]. The earliest evidence of human use of mustard as food is a seed cake from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic site of Jerf el Ahmar in Syria, dated to between 9224 and 8753 BC. Yellow mustard paste originated in China during the [[Zhou dynasty]] (1046–256 BC). Dijon, France, had become a recognized center of mustard making by the 13th century, and Dijon mustard was granted an *Appellation d'origine contrôlée* in 1937.

=== KEY: Mustelidae_0b8e04
HEADWORD: Mustelidae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Badger | Ferret | Otter | Sea otter | Weasel | Wolverine
ENTRY:
Family of carnivoran mammals that includes [[Weasel|weasels]], [[Badger|badgers]], [[Otter|otters]], [[Sea otter|sea otters]], polecats, martens and [[Wolverine|wolverines]]. The family has between 66 to 70 species in nine subfamilies and is the largest family in the suborder Caniformia. Mustelids vary greatly in size. The least weasel can be under 20 cm (8 in) long, the giant otter of South America can measure up to 1.7 m (5 ft 7 in), and sea otters can exceed 45 kg (99 lb). Most have elongated bodies and short legs, and they are adapted to terrestrial, arboreal or aquatic lives. Most are solitary and nocturnal, and all but the sea otter have anal scent glands. Most mustelids undergo embryonic diapause, which can extend the normal gestation to sometimes up to a year. Only one mustelid, the [[Ferret|ferret]], has been domesticated, while the mink is often raised for its fur. Mustelid-like forms appeared about 40 million years ago.

=== KEY: Mutapa_Empire_7e0c1d
HEADWORD: Mutapa Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Great Zimbabwe | Indian Ocean | Kalahari Desert | Limpopo River | Zambezi | Zimbabwe
ENTRY:
Shona empire on the northern part of the Zimbabwean Plateau, also called Mwenemutapa or Munhumutapa. The name derives from the Shona royal title Mwene we Mutapa, from *Mwene*, meaning "lord," and *Mutapa*, meaning "conquered land." The plateau is bordered by the [[Zambezi]] and [[Limpopo River|Limpopo]] rivers, the [[Indian Ocean]] and the [[Kalahari Desert]]. Shona oral tradition attributes the demise of [[Great Zimbabwe]] to a salt shortage, and Karanga migrants settled the plateau under an elephant hunter named Mutota. Early-15th-century Angoche traders had opened a route along the Zambezi, and the Karanga increased their power through alliances and succession disputes. Matope, a successor, conquered Tavara and Tonga territory and established his own [[Zimbabwe]] at Bedza. Historians differ on how far these traditions reflect events; one has suggested that Mutota may have been a symbolic figure.

=== KEY: Mutation_590163
HEADWORD: Mutation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA replication | Ionizing radiation | Meiosis | Mitosis | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Alteration in the nucleic acid sequence of the genome of an organism, virus or extrachromosomal DNA. Mutations result from errors during [[DNA replication]], [[Mitosis|mitosis]] or [[Meiosis|meiosis]], or from damage to DNA. They may also result from insertion, deletion or substitution of DNA segments by mobile genetic elements. Mutations may or may not change observable characteristics. Mutations play a part in evolution, cancer and the development of the immune system, and they are the ultimate source of all genetic variation. A 2007 study of *Drosophila* suggested that if a mutation changes a protein, the result is likely to be harmful, with an estimated 70% of amino acid polymorphisms having damaging effects. Unlike DNA damage, a mutation is replicated when the cell replicates and is not ordinarily repaired. Causes include [[Ionizing radiation|ionizing radiation]], [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet light]] and chemicals. Germline mutations can be inherited, while somatic mutations contribute to aging and diseases such as cancer. In humans the mutation rate is approximately 1.2 × 10⁻⁸ per base pair per generation.

=== KEY: Muthuswami_Dikshitar_47e464
HEADWORD: Dikshitar, Muthuswami
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Carnatic music | Hindustani classical music | Sanskrit | Syama Sastri | Tamil Nadu | Temple | Tyagaraja | Varanasi
ENTRY:
South Indian Hindu poet, singer, veena player and composer of [[Carnatic music]]. He was the youngest member of the Trinity of Carnatic music, alongside [[Tyagaraja]] and [[Syama Sastri]]. He is credited with around 500 compositions, widely recognized for elaborate poetic descriptions of Hindu deities and detailed depictions of [[Temple|temples]]. His music captures the essence of ragas in the veena style, which highlights gamakas, and his works are usually composed in a slower tempo. Unlike the predominantly Telugu compositions of his contemporaries, most of his works are in [[Sanskrit]], although he also composed a few kritis in Manipravalam, a style combining Sanskrit and Tamil. Born into a Tamil Brahmin family in Tiruvarur, in present-day [[Tamil Nadu]], he was educated by his father in the Vedas, poetry, music and astronomy. He later studied in [[Varanasi]], where he was exposed to [[Hindustani classical music|Hindustani music]]. According to tradition, the deity Murugan at Tiruttani inspired his career and his signature mudra, Guruguha.
