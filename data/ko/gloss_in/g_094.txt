=== KEY: Nation_7a71eb
HEADWORD: Nation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred the Great | Cicero | Ethnicity | Nationalism | State (polity)
ENTRY:
Large community whose members share a collective identity based on features such as language, history, ethnicity, culture, territory, and society. Some nations are constructed around ethnicity (ethnic nationalism), while others are bound by political constitutions (civic nationalism). A nation is generally more overtly political than an [[Ethnicity|ethnic group]]; according to Thomas Hylland Eriksen, what distinguishes it from other collective identities is its relationship with the state.

Benedict Anderson defined a nation as "an imagined political community," imagined because even members of the smallest nation will never know most of their fellow members. Anthony D. Smith defined nations as cultural-political communities that have become conscious of their autonomy, unity, and particular interests. Black's Law Dictionary describes a community inhabiting a defined territory under an independent government, so that nation can be synonymous with [[State (polity)|state]] or country. Others stress that a nation emphasizes sociocultural cohesion, which may not align with political boundaries, producing multinational states and stateless nations.

The consensus among scholars is that nations are socially constructed, historically contingent, and a distinctly modern phenomenon. [[Nationalism]], the belief that state and nation should align, did not become a prominent ideology until the end of the 18th century. Some historians trace nations to the medieval period or antiquity. Smith argued that only Egypt and Judah, at certain periods, approximated true nations in the ancient world. Adrian Hastings traced English nationhood to [[Alfred the Great]]'s struggle against Norse invaders, a view John Breuilly criticized.

The English word comes from Middle English *nacioun*, from Old French and from Latin *natio*, from *nasci* ("to be born"). [[Cicero]] used *natio* for "people."

=== KEY: National_security_7973b7
HEADWORD: National security
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Diplomacy | Economic inequality | Organized crime | Sovereign state | Terrorism | Walter Lippmann
ENTRY:
Security and defense of a [[Sovereign state|sovereign state]], including its citizens, economy and institutions, regarded as a duty of government. Originally conceived as protection against military attack, national security now also includes non-military dimensions, such as protection from [[Terrorism|terrorism]], economic security and cyber security. Risks include actions by other states, violent nonstate actors, narcotic cartels, [[Organized crime|organized crime]], multinational corporations and natural disasters. === KEY: Nationalism_b5dccf
HEADWORD: Nationalism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conservatism | French Revolution | Hugo Grotius | Nation | Sovereignty
ENTRY:
Ideology or movement holding that the [[Nation|nation]] should be congruent with the state. As a movement, it tends to promote the interests of a particular nation, especially to gain and maintain its [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]] over a perceived homeland and create a nation-state. It holds that the nation should govern itself free from outside interference and is the only rightful source of political power. It also seeks a single national identity built on shared culture, ethnicity, language, religion, traditions, or belief in a shared history, and promotes national unity.

Definitions of "nation" differ, producing different types of nationalism. The two main forms are ethnic nationalism and civic nationalism, though this dichotomy has been criticized as an oversimplification. Nationalism can combine with diverse ideologies, such as [[Conservatism|conservatism]] or socialism. Its moral value, its relation to patriotism, and its compatibility with cosmopolitanism are subjects of philosophical debate.

In the early 17th century [[Hugo Grotius]] refined the terminology of nations and sovereignty. The term nationalism dates to around 1798 and became more negative in its connotations after 1914. Benedict Anderson linked its rise to print capitalism and printed media in vernacular languages. From the late 18th century, particularly with the [[French Revolution]], the principle of popular sovereignty spread. Modernization theory, currently the most commonly accepted explanation, holds that industrialization, urbanization, and mass education made national consciousness possible. Primordialism, which held that nations have always existed, has been rejected by most scholars.

Supporters point to nationalism's role in movements for freedom and justice, in cultural revivals, and in national pride. Critics note that it has been used to legitimize racial, ethnic, and religious divisions, suppress minorities, and start wars, and it is frequently cited as a cause of both world wars.

=== KEY: Natural_disaster_386890
HEADWORD: Natural disaster
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Drought | Earthquake | Flood | Tropical cyclone | Wildfire
ENTRY:
Severely harmful impact on a society or community caused by a natural phenomenon or hazard. Natural hazards include avalanches, [[Drought|droughts]], [[Earthquake|earthquakes]], [[Flood|floods]], heat waves, landslides, [[Tropical cyclone|tropical cyclones]], volcanic activity, and [[Wildfire|wildfires]], as well as blizzards, dust storms, hail, sinkholes, thunderstorms, tornadoes, and tsunamis. Disasters can cause loss of life, property damage, and economic damage. The extent depends on how well people are prepared and how strong buildings, roads, and other structures are.

A hazard is the threat of an event likely to have a negative impact; a disaster is the harm that follows when a hazard significantly affects a community. The U.S. Federal Emergency Management Agency illustrates the distinction with the 1906 San Francisco earthquake: the earthquake was the hazard, and the resulting damage the disaster. Hazards are commonly grouped as geophysical, meteorological, hydrological, climatological, and, in some classifications, biological.

Many scholars argue that the term "natural disaster" is a misnomer, a criticism made as early as 1976, and should be replaced by "disaster" with the type of hazard specified. In this view a disaster arises when a hazard meets a vulnerable community, and human choices shape the outcome. Inadequate building norms, marginalization of people, poor land-use planning, and overexploitation of resources can worsen disasters. Critics of the term say that calling disasters natural suggests the results are inevitable and obscures responsibility for risk reduction, compensation, and insurance. [[Climate change]] also affects how often extreme-weather hazards occur.

Many developing countries lack proper disaster risk reduction systems and are more vulnerable than high-income countries. The Asia-Pacific region is the most disaster-prone in the world. In 2012 there were 905 natural disasters worldwide, 93% of them weather-related, with overall costs of US$170 billion.

=== KEY: Natural_environment_9725a4
HEADWORD: Natural environment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropocene | Atmosphere | Biosphere | Climate | Climate change | Hydrosphere | Lithosphere
ENTRY:
Living and nonliving things on Earth that occur naturally, without artificial human intervention. The term encompasses the interaction of all living species, [[Climate|climate]], weather and natural resources that affect human survival and economic activity. It is contrasted with the built environment, where humans have fundamentally transformed landscapes, as in urban settings and agricultural land conversion. No environment on Earth is absolutely natural, and naturalness varies along a continuum. Human changes in the [[Anthropocene]] have affected all natural environments, including through [[Climate change|climate change]], biodiversity loss and pollution from plastic and other chemicals. Earth science generally recognizes four spheres, corresponding to rocks, water, air and life: the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]], [[Hydrosphere|hydrosphere]], [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]] and [[Biosphere|biosphere]]. Some scientists add the cryosphere and pedosphere.

=== KEY: Natural_gas_02ca5f
HEADWORD: Natural gas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fossil fuel | Greenhouse gas | Methane | Petroleum | Pipeline
ENTRY:
Gaseous [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuel]] occurring naturally in geological formations, also called gas, methane gas, or fossil gas. It is a mixture of hydrocarbons, primarily [[Methane|methane]], with small amounts of higher alkanes and traces of carbon dioxide, nitrogen, hydrogen sulfide, and helium. Methane is colorless and odorless, so a commercial odorizer such as methanethiol, which smells of rotten eggs, is added to make leaks detectable.

Natural gas forms when layers of organic matter, primarily marine microorganisms, are thermally decomposed without oxygen under intense heat and pressure over millions of years. It is often found alongside coal and [[Petroleum|petroleum]] and is frequently a byproduct of oil production, where it may be vented, flared, or collected. Before use it must be processed to remove impurities such as water, hydrogen sulfide, and carbon dioxide; the processing yields ethane, propane, butanes, and pentanes. It travels by [[Pipeline|pipeline]], and inter-country transport and long-term storage became widely available only with liquefied natural gas.

The gas was called "natural" in the early 1800s to distinguish it from manufactured coal gas. Chinese drillers were using gas from brine wells by about 400 BC, carrying it through bamboo pipes to boil salt water in Sichuan. In the United States, William Hart dug the first commercial well in 1821 at Fredonia, New York, leading in 1858 to the Fredonia Gas Light Company. Long-distance pipelines grew from the 1920s onward.

Burning natural gas emits fewer toxic air pollutants, less carbon dioxide, and almost no particulate matter than other fossil fuels. Critics note that venting and fugitive methane emissions can give it a similar or greater carbon footprint overall, since methane is the second-greatest [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]] after carbon dioxide. Some organizations therefore prefer the terms "fossil gas" or "methane gas." Supporters stress its role in displacing coal and in supplying dispatchable energy.

=== KEY: Natural_logarithm_77e1eb
HEADWORD: Natural logarithm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: E (mathematical constant) | Exponential function | Half-life
ENTRY:
Logarithm to the base of the mathematical constant [[E (mathematical constant)|e]], an irrational number approximately equal to 2.718. The natural logarithm of x, written ln x, is the power to which e must be raised to equal x; for example, ln 7.5 is 2.0149..., because e raised to that power equals 7.5. It is the inverse of the [[Exponential function|exponential function]] and turns multiplication into addition. It can be defined as the area under the curve y = 1/x from 1 to x for positive numbers. It is used to solve problems of exponential decay, such as [[Half-life|half-life]] calculations.

=== KEY: Natural_number_217533
HEADWORD: Natural number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Addition | Integer | Multiplication | Number theory
ENTRY:
Number used for counting, for indexing positions in a sequence, and for labeling. The natural numbers are 1, 2, 3, and so on, or, depending on convention, begin with 0; set theory often includes 0 and elementary arithmetic often excludes it. Other terms include positive integers, non-negative integers, whole numbers, and counting numbers. The set is commonly denoted by a bold N or a blackboard bold ℕ.

Natural numbers express two related ideas. As cardinal numbers they give the size of a collection, as in "there are seven days in a week." Two finite collections have the same cardinality if their objects can be put in one-to-one correspondence. As ordinal numbers they give position in a sequence, as in "the third day of the month." They are written with ten digits (0 to 9), and numerals can also serve as nominal numbers, identifiers with no mathematical properties, such as jersey numbers.

Natural numbers can be compared by size and arranged in a total order. Two basic operations, [[Addition|addition]] and [[Multiplication|multiplication]], are defined on them and are closed, commutative, and associative, with multiplication distributing over addition. The inverse operations, subtraction and division, only sometimes give natural-number results: subtracting a larger number from a smaller one yields a negative number, and division commonly leaves a remainder. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every natural number greater than 1 factors uniquely into primes.

The natural numbers can be formalized with the Peano axioms, in which an initial element and a successor function generate each subsequent number, together with the principle of mathematical induction. Giuseppe Peano axiomatized them in 1889. The [[Integer|integers]], rational numbers, real numbers, and complex numbers are defined successively from them. Arithmetic, [[Number theory|number theory]], and combinatorics build on the natural numbers.

=== KEY: Natural_philosophy_a0ed22
HEADWORD: Natural philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Isaac Newton | Supernatural
ENTRY:
Systematic, research-based study of nature and the physical universe, ignoring [[Supernatural|supernatural]] influence. Used since at least [[Aristotle]] until the 19th century, the term referred to a branch of philosophy that included fields now considered science, such as physics, biology, chemistry and astronomy. === KEY: Natural_resource_253fbf
HEADWORD: Natural resource
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mining
ENTRY:
Material drawn from nature and used with few modifications, valued for commercial, industrial, aesthetic, scientific, or cultural purposes. On Earth, natural resources include sunlight, atmosphere, water, land, all minerals, and all vegetation and wildlife. A resource may exist as a separate entity, such as freshwater or a fish, or be transformed by extractive industries into a useful form, as with metal ores, rare-earth elements, petroleum, and timber.

Resources are classified by several criteria. By origin, biotic resources come from the biosphere and have life, such as flora, fauna, and fisheries; fossil fuels are included because they form from decayed organic matter. Abiotic resources come from non-living material, including land, water, air, and ores of metals such as gold, iron, copper, and silver. By stage of development, they are potential, actual, reserves, or stocks. By renewability, renewable resources such as solar energy, air, and water can be replenished naturally, though many can be depleted by overuse. Non-renewable resources, notably minerals and fossil fuels, form over long geological periods. Metals can be recycled, but coal and petroleum cannot. By ownership, resources are individual, community, national, or international.

Extraction withdraws resources from nature, from traditional preindustrial use to global industry. Extractive industries, such as hunting, trapping, [[Mining|mining]], oil and gas drilling, and forestry, along with agriculture, form the basis of the primary sector of the economy.

Allocation of resources can be at the center of economic and political confrontations within and between countries, particularly during scarcity. Extraction is also a major source of human rights violations and environmental damage. International agendas, including the Sustainable Development Goals, focus on more sustainable extraction. Others argue that predictions of long-term depletion have often proven overstated because of substitution and efficiency gains.

=== KEY: Natural_rubber_d4740e
HEADWORD: Natural rubber
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Congo Free State | Dutch East Indies | Euphorbiaceae | Joseph Priestley | Mesoamerica | Nazi Germany | Polymer
ENTRY:
Elastic polymer of isoprene harvested as latex from certain plants, chiefly the Pará rubber tree. Also called India rubber, caoutchouc, or Amazonian rubber. It consists of [[Polymer|polymers]] of isoprene with minor impurities and is classed as an elastomer. Latex, a sticky, milky colloid, is drawn off by cutting the bark in a process called tapping and refined for commercial processing. In most useful forms it has a large stretch ratio and high resilience, and it is buoyant and waterproof. Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, and Cambodia are leading producers.

The main commercial source is *Hevea brasiliensis*, a member of the spurge family ([[Euphorbiaceae]]), native to Brazil and now pan-tropical. Other sources include the rubber fig, guayule, which is prized for its hypoallergenic properties, and the Kazakh dandelion (*Taraxacum kok-saghyz*). Research in [[Nazi Germany]] on dandelion rubber failed, but in 2013 German scientists developed a cultivar that seems suitable for commercial production.

The first users were the indigenous cultures of [[Mesoamerica]]: the Olmec made rubber balls for the ballgame, and the Aztecs made containers and waterproofed textiles. In 1770 [[Joseph Priestley]] observed that rubber removed pencil marks, hence the name. Charles Goodyear redeveloped vulcanization in 1839, though Mesoamericans had used stabilized rubber as early as 1600 BC.

South America supplied most rubber for much of the 19th century. In 1876 Henry Wickham smuggled 70,000 seeds of the Amazonian rubber tree from Brazil to Kew Gardens, England; only 2,400 germinated. Seedlings went to India, Ceylon, the [[Dutch East Indies]], Singapore, and British Malaya, which became the biggest producer. In the early 1900s the [[Congo Free State]] was also a significant source, with latex mostly gathered by forced labor under King Leopold II. Synthetic rubber was first made chemically in 1909.

=== KEY: Natural_satellite_955164
HEADWORD: Natural satellite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dwarf planet | Johannes Kepler | Moon | Planet | Triton (moon)
ENTRY:
Astronomical body that orbits a [[Planet|planet]], [[Dwarf planet|dwarf planet]], or small Solar System body, or sometimes another natural satellite. Natural satellites are colloquially called moons, after the [[Moon]], Earth's natural satellite. In English, "Moon" capitalized denotes Earth's satellite, while lowercase "moon" is used generically.

In the Solar System there are six planetary satellite systems, comprising 456 natural satellites with confirmed orbits. Seven objects commonly considered dwarf planets also have satellites: Orcus, Pluto, Haumea, Quaoar, Makemake, Gonggong, and Eris. In all, 656 minor planets are known or suspected to have natural satellites.

The Moon was considered a planet until Copernicus published *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* in 1543. Galileo called the moons he discovered in 1610 *Planetæ* ("planets"). The German astronomer [[Johannes Kepler]] was the first to use the term satellite for orbiting bodies, in 1610, from the Latin *satelles*, meaning "guard," "attendant," or "companion." After the launch of Sputnik in 1957, "satellite" became linked with artificial objects, and "moon" regained respectability as an interchangeable term.

No lower size limit for a moon is established, and the upper limit is vague. The Earth–Moon system is sometimes described as a double planet, and some authors regard the Pluto–Charon system as a double dwarf planet because its barycenter lies above the surface of the larger body.

Regular satellites, which orbit close to the planet on prograde, uninclined circular orbits, are generally thought to have formed from the same collapsing region of the protoplanetary disk as their primary. Irregular satellites, on distant, inclined, eccentric, or retrograde orbits, are thought to be captured asteroids. The Moon and the moons of Pluto are thought to have originated from collisions of two large protoplanetary objects. [[Triton (moon)|Triton]], large and in a close, circular orbit but retrograde, is thought to be a captured dwarf planet.

=== KEY: Natural_science_733d8d
HEADWORD: Natural science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Biology | Chemistry | Earth science | Karl Popper | Natural philosophy | Peer review | Physics
ENTRY:
Branch of science concerned with the description, understanding and prediction of natural phenomena, based on empirical evidence from observation and experimentation. It is divided into two main branches: life science, also known as [[Biology|biology]], and physical science, which includes [[Physics|physics]], [[Astronomy|astronomy]], [[Earth science]] and [[Chemistry|chemistry]]. Natural science succeeded classical [[Natural philosophy|natural philosophy]]. It uses tools from mathematics and logic to convert information about nature into statements of natural laws. Systematic data collection succeeded natural history, which emerged in the 16th century by describing and classifying plants, animals and minerals. Mechanisms such as [[Peer review|peer review]] and reproducibility help ensure the validity of findings. [[Karl Popper]] proposed a controversial criterion of falsifiability to distinguish scientific endeavors from nonscientific ones.

=== KEY: Natural_selection_88afd4
HEADWORD: Natural selection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Aristotle | Charles Darwin | Evolution | Genetic drift | Lucretius | On the Origin of Species | Population (biology)
ENTRY:
Differential survival and reproduction of individuals owing to differences in their observable characteristics, a key mechanism of [[Evolution|evolution]] that changes the heritable traits of a [[Population (biology)|population]] over generations. Variation of traits, both genotypic and phenotypic, exists within all populations, and traits that facilitate survival and reproductive success are more likely to be passed on. Selection acts on existing variation, which arises mainly from mutation and recombination, and does not direct the production of adaptive variations. Some traits spread because they suit their environments; others because mating partners prefer them, a process called sexual selection.

[[Charles Darwin]] popularized the term, contrasting it with artificial selection, in which human breeders intentionally favor desirable traits. For Darwin it resulted from inheritance, variation, and the struggle for existence, which included both competition and cooperation. The Roman poet [[Lucretius]] had expressed the idea that only creatures able to provide for themselves and reproduce persist; [[Aristotle]] criticized such views. [[Alfred Russel Wallace]] independently formulated the theory, and their papers were presented jointly in 1858. Darwin elaborated it in his 1859 book *[[On the Origin of Species]]*, written before a valid theory of heredity existed.

The union of Darwinian evolution with classical genetics formed the modern synthesis of the mid-20th century. Twenty-first-century biologists have challenged its gene-centered view, producing several extended evolutionary syntheses. Genotypes can also change slowly by random [[Genetic drift|genetic drift]], but natural selection remains the primary explanation for adaptive evolution.

=== KEY: Natural_units_9560cc
HEADWORD: Natural units
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boltzmann constant | General relativity | Gravitational constant | Planck constant | Planck units | Quantum gravity | String theory
ENTRY:
Units of measurement based on universal physical constants. Stoney units, presented by George Johnstone Stoney in 1874, preceded Planck's by 30 years. Setting the speed of light c to 1 lets mass and energy be equated directly, so E = m rather than E = mc². A purely natural system has all of its dimensions collapsed, so the physical constants alone define the system. [[Planck units]] use the [[Gravitational constant|gravitational constant]], the [[Planck constant|reduced Planck constant]] and the [[Boltzmann constant]], and are common in theories of [[Quantum gravity|quantum gravity]], including [[String theory|string theory]]. Atomic units, first proposed by Douglas Hartree, are designed to simplify atomic and molecular physics, especially for the hydrogen atom. Geometrized units, used in [[General relativity]], set c and G to one.

=== KEY: Naturalism_philosophy_8a7d8b

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Philosophical belief that only natural laws and forces, not [[Supernatural|supernatural]] ones, operate in the universe. === KEY: Nature_d212ce
HEADWORD: Nature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Heraclitus | Industrial Revolution | Latin | Mind | Natural environment | Supernatural | Transcendentalism
ENTRY:
Physical world or universe in the broadest sense, comprising the laws, elements, and phenomena of the material world, life included. Humans are part of nature, although human activity, or humanity as a whole, is often described as at odds with it or even separate from it.

In narrower uses the word refers to geology and wildlife, to the realm of living beings, or to the processes of inanimate things, such as weather. It often means the [[Natural environment]] or wilderness: wild animals, rocks, forests, and generally whatever has not been substantially altered by human intervention. This sense implies a distinction between the natural and the artificial, which is brought into being by a human [[Mind|mind]]. Depending on context, "natural" may also be contrasted with the unnatural or the [[Supernatural|supernatural]].

The English word comes from Old French *nature*, derived from [[Latin]] *natura*, "essential qualities, innate disposition", which literally meant "birth". In ancient philosophy *natura* mostly translated the Greek *physis*, which originally referred to the way plants, animals, and other things develop of their own accord. Pre-Socratic philosophers, with a dynamic sense especially for [[Heraclitus]], extended it to the physical universe as a whole.

With the modern scientific method, nature came to be seen as a passive reality organized and moved by divine laws. After the [[Industrial Revolution]] it increasingly meant the part of reality not subject to intentional human intervention. Some traditions, such as Rousseau's and American [[Transcendentalism]], considered it sacred, while Hegel and Marx treated it as a mere backdrop to divine providence or human history. A vitalist vision closer to the pre-Socratic one was reborn at the same time, especially after [[Charles Darwin]].

=== KEY: Navier_Stokes_equations_612f6c
HEADWORD: Navier–Stokes equations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conservation of mass | Euclidean space | Maxwell's equations | Millennium Prize Problems | Momentum | Pollution | Siméon Denis Poisson
ENTRY:
System of partial differential equations describing the motion of viscous fluids. The equations are named after Claude-Louis Navier and George Gabriel Stokes, who developed them from 1822 (Navier) to 1842–1850 (Stokes); [[Siméon Denis Poisson]] independently reached the same results. They express [[Momentum|momentum]] balance for Newtonian fluids using the [[Conservation of mass|conservation of mass]], and generalize the Euler equations, which consider only inviscid flow. The equations model aircraft and car design, blood flow, power station design and [[Pollution|pollution]], and, coupled with [[Maxwell's equations]], form the basis of magnetohydrodynamics. The Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem asks whether solutions in three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]] remain smooth and bounded, or break down into unbounded ones. It is one of seven [[Millennium Prize Problems]], for which the Clay Mathematics Institute offered $1 million prizes in 2000. In September 2026 OpenAI announced a claimed counterexample; the claim, followed by a priority dispute, has yet to be independently verified.

=== KEY: Navigation_846495
HEADWORD: Navigation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Astrolabe | Christopher Columbus | Compass | Ferdinand Magellan | Global Positioning System | Longitude | Vasco da Gama
ENTRY:
Field of study and practice concerned with determining position and with monitoring and controlling the movement of a craft or vehicle from one place to another. It has four general categories: land, marine, aeronautic, and space navigation. All techniques involve locating the navigator's position relative to known locations or patterns.

Polynesian navigation, based on memory and observation of the stars, weather, wildlife, and wave size, is probably the earliest form of open-ocean navigation. Among the first instruments was the [[Compass|compass]], of Chinese origin from the Han dynasty; the Song dynasty adopted it for sea navigation in the 11th century, and its first recorded use in Western Europe and the Islamic world dates from around 1190. The oldest record of a sea [[Astrolabe|astrolabe]] is from 1295. The backstaff, invented by John Davis in 1595, overcame the errors of the cross-staff.

Widespread open-sea navigation began in the [[Age of Discovery]] of the 15th century. The Portuguese explored the Atlantic coast of Africa from 1418, and Bartolomeu Dias reached the Indian Ocean in 1488. In 1492 the Spanish monarchs funded [[Christopher Columbus]]'s westward expedition, and in 1498 [[Vasco da Gama]] reached India by sailing around Africa. The first circumnavigation of the earth was completed in 1522 by the expedition led by [[Ferdinand Magellan]] and finished by Juan Sebastián Elcano.

Mathematics, including logarithms and spherical trigonometry, allowed more accurate navigation from the 1700s. John Harrison's marine chronometer (1735) and the sextant (1757) helped solve the problem of [[Longitude|longitude]]. The 20th century brought radio ranges (1926), LORAN (1944), and inertial navigation (1950). The [[Global Positioning System]] reached full operational capability in 1995, and other satellite systems include Galileo, GLONASS, and BeiDou.

=== KEY: Nebular_hypothesis_1ad5b0
HEADWORD: Nebular hypothesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Giant planet | Immanuel Kant | Molecular cloud | Neptune | Pierre-Simon Laplace | Solar System | Uranus
ENTRY:
Model of how the [[Solar System]] formed from gas and dust orbiting the Sun. It is the most widely accepted model in cosmogony, and proposes that the planets accreted from this material. [[Immanuel Kant]] published the theory in 1755, and [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]] modified it in 1796. The modern solar nebular disk model explains features such as the nearly circular, coplanar orbits of the planets. Stars form in giant [[Molecular cloud|molecular clouds]], and a protoplanetary disk typically forms around each young star. A Sun-like star takes about 1 million years to form, and its disk evolves into a planetary system over 10–100 million years. Dust grains coagulate into kilometer-sized planetesimals, which merge into terrestrial planets. [[Giant planet|Giant planets]] form beyond the frost line and accrete hydrogen–helium gas once they reach 5–10 Earth masses. [[Uranus]] and [[Neptune]] are thought to be failed cores that formed too late.

=== KEY: Negative_number_48cf1a
HEADWORD: Negative number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brahmagupta | Celsius | Debt | Diophantus | Fahrenheit | Han dynasty
ENTRY:
Real number that is less than zero, usually written with a minus sign. Negative numbers are the opposite of positive real numbers and represent losses, deficiencies and [[Debt|debts]], as well as temperatures below zero on the [[Celsius]] and [[Fahrenheit]] scales. The laws of arithmetic ensure that the opposite of an opposite returns the original value: −(−3) = 3. Every real number other than zero is either positive or negative, while zero has no sign. Negative numbers appear in the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art, which in its present form dates from the [[Han dynasty]] (202 BC–AD 220). By the 7th century, Indian mathematicians such as [[Brahmagupta]] described their use, and Islamic mathematicians developed the rules for subtracting and multiplying them. [[Diophantus]] considered negative solutions "false," and Western mathematicians such as Leibniz held that they were invalid but still used them.

=== KEY: Negligence_9980e6
HEADWORD: Negligence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Contract | Damages | Tort
ENTRY:
Failure to exercise the care expected in similar circumstances. In [[Tort|tort]] law, negligence is harm caused by violating a duty of care through a negligent act or failure to act. A plaintiff must generally establish that the defendant owed a duty, breached it and caused harm, and that the plaintiff suffered [[Damages|damages]], though some jurisdictions recognize three elements and others five. Losses may include physical injury, property damage, psychiatric illness and economic loss. The modern law dates from *Donoghue v Stevenson* (1932), in which a woman who found a decomposed snail in a bottle of ginger beer sued the manufacturer, because privity barred a [[Contract|contractual]] claim. Lord Atkin defined a "neighbor" as a person so closely affected by one's acts that one should reasonably have them in contemplation. In England, *Caparo Industries Plc v Dickman* (1990) set a three-part test of foreseeability, proximity and what is fair, just and reasonable.

=== KEY: Neighbourhood_3fe04b
HEADWORD: Neighborhood
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Community | Democracy
ENTRY:
Geographically localized [[Community|community]] within a larger town, city, suburb or rural area, sometimes a single street and the buildings lining it. Neighborhoods are often social communities with considerable face-to-face interaction. Researchers lack an exact definition, but one describes a neighborhood as a specific geographic area and a set of social networks in which residents share values and maintain social control. Neighborhood sociology studies local life, and some scholars view the neighborhood as a small-scale [[Democracy|democracy]] regulated by reciprocity among neighbors.

=== KEY: Nelson_Mandela_f256b9
HEADWORD: Mandela, Nelson
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
South African anti-apartheid revolutionary and politician, president of South Africa (1994–99), the country's first black head of state and the first elected in a fully representative democratic election.

A Thembu royal family member, he worked as a lawyer in [[Johannesburg]]. He joined the [[African National Congress]] (ANC) in 1943 and co-founded its Youth League in 1944. After the National Party government established [[Apartheid|apartheid]], a system of racial segregation, he and the ANC committed themselves to its overthrow. Though initially committed to non-violent protest, in 1961 he co-founded the militant Umkhonto we Sizwe and led a sabotage campaign. Arrested in 1962, he was sentenced to [[Life imprisonment|life imprisonment]] in the Rivonia Trial.

Mandela served 27 years in prison, mainly on Robben Island. President F. W. de Klerk released him in 1990, and the two negotiated an end to apartheid and organized the 1994 multiracial election, which the ANC won. They shared the 1993 Nobel Peace Prize. Mandela's coalition government promulgated a new constitution, created the Truth and Reconciliation Commission to investigate past abuses, and pursued land reform, poverty reduction, and expanded healthcare. He was Secretary-General of the [[Non-Aligned Movement]] from 1998 to 1999, declined a second term, and was succeeded by Thabo Mbeki. He then campaigned against poverty and [[HIV/AIDS]].

Mandela was controversial for much of his life. Critics on the right denounced him as a communist terrorist; some on the radical left thought him too eager to reconcile with apartheid's supporters; others have criticized civilian casualties from Umkhonto we Sizwe bombings and his ties to [[Fidel Castro]] and [[Muammar Gaddafi]]. He received more than 250 honors.

=== KEY: Nelumbo_41ea5a

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Genus of aquatic plants with large, showy flowers, commonly called lotus. Members resemble water lilies (family Nymphaeaceae) but are only distantly related to them. The genus is ancient, with dozens of species known from fossils since the Early Cretaceous, but only two living species survive. *Nelumbo nucifera* is native to East Asia, South Asia and Southeast Asia, and *Nelumbo lutea* to North America and the Caribbean. Horticultural hybrids have been produced between the two species.

=== KEY: Nematode_476518
HEADWORD: Nematode
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 
ENTRY:
Worm of the phylum Nematoda, also called roundworm or eelworm, with a flexible cuticle. Unlike flatworms, nematodes have a tubular digestive system with openings at both ends. They are classified with arthropods and tardigrades in the clade Ecdysozoa. Most species are free-living, feeding on microorganisms, but many are parasitic, including pathogens of most plants and animals. They are found in marine, freshwater and terrestrial environments, from the polar regions to the tropics. They make up about 80% of all individual animals on Earth. Estimates of total species range widely, but are now placed closer to one million. About 35 species are human parasites.

Fourth and penultimate stage of ancient Assyrian history (911–609 BC). At its height, the empire ruled Mesopotamia, the Levant and Egypt, as well as parts of Anatolia, Arabia, Iran and Armenia. === KEY: Neoclassical_architecture_088645

Revivalist style of architecture, also called Classical Revival architecture. It was produced by the Neoclassical movement, which began in the mid-18th century in Italy, France and Germany. It became one of the most prominent architectural styles in the [[Western world]]. The movement aimed to strip away the excesses of Late [[Baroque]] and return to a purer, more authentic classical style adapted to modern purposes. === KEY: Neogene_3f5b09
HEADWORD: Neogene
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Cenozoic | Gulf Stream | Miocene | Pliocene | Quaternary
ENTRY:
Second geologic period of the [[Cenozoic]], spanning 20.46 million years, from 23.04 to 2.58 million years ago. It is divided into two epochs, the earlier [[Miocene]] and the later [[Pliocene]]. The term was coined in 1853 by the Austrian paleontologist Moritz Hörnes. During this period mammals and birds evolved into modern forms, while other groups remained relatively unchanged. The first humans, *Homo habilis*, appeared in [[Africa]] near the end of the period. The most significant continental movement was the connection of North and South America at the Isthmus of Panama, late in the Pliocene. This cut off warm Pacific currents from the Atlantic, leaving only the [[Gulf Stream]] to transfer heat to the Arctic Ocean. The global climate cooled considerably throughout the period, culminating in continental glaciations in the [[Quaternary]] Period that followed.

=== KEY: Neolithic_Revolution_6f5726
HEADWORD: Neolithic Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze Age | Fertile Crescent | Hunter-gatherer | Maize | Papua New Guinea | Sumer
ENTRY:
Wide-scale transition of many human cultures in Afro-Eurasia from hunting and gathering to agriculture and settlement, also called the First Agricultural Revolution. The term was coined by V. Gordon Childe in *Man Makes Himself* (1936).

The domestication of plants and animals occurred independently in separate parts of the world, beginning in the Holocene epoch about 11,700 years ago, after the last Ice Age. The process has been dated to 10,000–8,000 BCE in the [[Fertile Crescent]], and perhaps 8000 BCE at the Kuk Early Agricultural Site in [[Papua New Guinea]]. Everywhere it is associated with a shift from nomadic [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherer]] life to a more settled, agrarian one.

Surplus, plannable food supplies supported larger populations in permanent, densely populated villages and towns. Irrigation and deforestation transformed the environment, and widespread accompanying developments included pottery, polished stone tools, and rectangular houses. In many regions agriculture caused episodes of rapid population growth. Seasonal planning encouraged division of labor and specialization. These developments provided the basis for centralized administrations, hierarchical ideologies, writing, and greater property ownership. The earliest known civilization arose in [[Sumer]] in southern Mesopotamia (c. 6,500 BP), heralding the [[Bronze Age]].

Agriculture also had costs. It greatly narrowed the diversity of foods available and reduced nutritional quality compared with hunting and foraging. Reliance on a few staple crops can harm health: [[Maize|maize]], for example, is deficient in the amino acids lysine and tryptophan and is a poor source of iron. The sequence in which Neolithic characteristics emerged remains debated and is understood to vary from place to place.

=== KEY: Neolithic_b8ebde
HEADWORD: Neolithic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chalcolithic | Göbekli Tepe
ENTRY:
Archaeological period, the last division of the Stone Age, also called the New Stone Age. It lasted from c. 10,000 BC to c. 2,000 BC in Asia, Europe and Africa. The period saw the Neolithic Revolution, a set of developments including the introduction of farming, the domestication of animals and a shift from hunter-gatherer life to settlement. Farming appeared in West Asia about 12,000 years ago, and its founder crops included wheat, lentil, pea, chickpea, bitter vetch and flax. Independent domestication in Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia produced regional Neolithic cultures. In West Asia the period ended with the [[Chalcolithic]], or Copper Age, marked by the development of metallurgy. [[Göbekli Tepe]], a temple area in southeastern Turkey dated to around 9500 BC, may be regarded as the beginning of the period.

=== KEY: Neon_7a5ea0

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Chemical element of the noble gas group, with the symbol Ne and atomic number 10. Neon is a colorless, odorless, inert gas under standard conditions, with about two-thirds the density of air. British chemists Sir William Ramsay and Morris Travers discovered it in London in 1898, identifying it by its bright red emission spectrum. The name comes from the Greek word for "new." Neon is comparatively scarce on Earth but ranks fifth in cosmic abundance, after hydrogen, helium, oxygen and carbon. Its red-orange glow is used in neon signs and in the red light of helium–neon lasers. It is obtained mainly by fractional distillation of liquid air, making it more expensive than helium.

=== KEY: Neoplasm_adf553
HEADWORD: Neoplasm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Leukemia | Oncology
ENTRY:
Abnormal, excessive growth of cells, often forming a mass called a tumor. Neoplasia is the process that produces a neoplasm. Its growth is uncoordinated with that of the surrounding tissue and persists even if the original trigger is removed. Benign neoplasms, such as uterine fibroids and skin moles, are localized and do not transform into cancer. Malignant neoplasms, commonly called cancers, invade and destroy surrounding tissue, may form metastases, and are the focus of [[Oncology|oncology]]. The word derives from Ancient Greek *neo* (new) and *plasma* (formation). Some neoplasms, such as [[Leukemia|leukemia]], do not form a tumor. Tumor is not synonymous with cancer, since a tumor can be benign, precancerous or malignant.

Landlocked country in South Asia, mostly in the Himalayas. Kathmandu is the capital and largest city, and the country includes eight of the world's ten highest mountains. === KEY: Neptune_f8d3b6
HEADWORD: Neptune
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Great Red Spot | Hubble Space Telescope | Methane | Rayleigh scattering | Sun | Triton (moon) | Uranus | Voyager 2
ENTRY:
Eighth and farthest known planet from the [[Sun]], an ice giant named after the Roman god of the sea. It is the fourth-largest planet in the Solar System by diameter, the third most massive, and the densest giant planet, with 17 times the mass of Earth. It has no well-defined solid surface and orbits the Sun once every 164.8 years at a distance of 30.1 astronomical units (4.5 billion km).

Neptune is the only planet not first found by direct observation. Unexpected deviations in the orbit of [[Uranus]] led Alexis Bouvard to hypothesize an unknown perturbing body, and John Couch Adams and Urbain Le Verrier independently predicted its position mathematically. Johann Gottfried Galle observed it with a telescope on September 23, 1846, within a degree of Le Verrier's prediction. Its largest moon, [[Triton (moon)|Triton]], was discovered shortly afterward.

Detailed observations awaited the [[Hubble Space Telescope]] and large ground-based telescopes with adaptive optics. [[Voyager 2]], which flew past on August 25, 1989, remains the only spacecraft to visit. The atmosphere is mainly hydrogen and helium with traces of hydrocarbons, with a higher proportion of ices than Jupiter or Saturn. Traces of [[Methane|methane]] and [[Rayleigh scattering]] make the planet appear faintly blue.

Unlike Uranus, Neptune has active, consistently visible weather. Voyager 2 found a Great Dark Spot comparable to Jupiter's [[Great Red Spot]]; a newer main dark spot and a smaller one were identified in 2018. Winds, the strongest sustained of any planet, reach 2,100 km/h. Cloud-top temperatures approach 55 K (−218 °C), while the planet's center is about 5,400 K (5,100 °C). Faint rings were discovered in 1984.

ENTRY:
Radioactive [[Actinide|actinide]] metal with the symbol Np and atomic number 93. It is the first transuranic element and is named after the planet [[Neptune]], beyond [[Uranus]]. It was first synthesized by Edwin McMillan and Philip H. Abelson at the Berkeley Radiation Laboratory in 1940, although many false claims of its discovery had been made earlier. Most neptunium is now made as a by-product of neutron irradiation of uranium in nuclear reactors. It has no commercial uses at present, but it is a precursor of plutonium-238, which is used in radioisotope thermal generators for spacecraft. Like all actinides, it is radioactive, poisonous and pyrophoric, and it can accumulate in bones.

=== KEY: Nero_eb470d

ENTRY:
Roman emperor (r. AD 54–68) and final emperor of the [[Julio-Claudian dynasty]]. [[Claudius]] adopted him as heir after marrying his mother, Agrippina the Younger. Nero succeeded Claudius in AD 54. Early in his reign he was guided by his mother, his tutor [[Seneca the Younger]] and the praetorian prefect Burrus. Roman sources also implicate him in the deaths of his mother, his wife Claudia Octavia and his stepbrother Britannicus. He ordered the construction of amphitheaters and promoted athletic games, and he made public appearances as an actor, poet, musician and charioteer. His reign included a Roman–Parthian War (58–63), the suppression of a revolt in Britain led by [[Boudica]], and the start of the First Jewish–Roman War. After the senator Vindex rebelled with support from Galba, Nero was declared a public enemy, fled Rome and committed suicide. [[Tacitus]] claims Nero blamed [[Christians]] for the Great Fire of Rome.

=== KEY: Nerva_Antonine_dynasty_0c9f24
HEADWORD: Nerva–Antonine dynasty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hadrian | Marcus Aurelius | Niccolò Machiavelli | Roman emperor | Trajan
ENTRY:
Dynasty of seven [[Roman emperor|Roman emperors]] who ruled from AD 96 to 192. Its first five emperors, Nerva, [[Trajan]], [[Hadrian]], Antoninus Pius and [[Marcus Aurelius]], are known as the "Five Good Emperors." In the first five of six successions the reigning emperor had no male heir and adopted a successor. Under Roman law, adoption created a bond as strong as kinship. The label derives from [[Niccolò Machiavelli]]'s *The Discourses on Livy*, which judged the adopted emperors good. Marcus Aurelius named his son Commodus as heir, a choice regarded as the beginning of the empire's decline. The dynasty ended with Commodus's murder in 192, followed by the Year of the Five Emperors.

=== KEY: Nervous_system_3ec654
HEADWORD: Nervous system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Endocrine system | Neurology | Neuron | Neuroscience | Neurotransmitter | Peripheral nervous system | Sponge
ENTRY:
Highly complex part of an animal that coordinates its actions and sensory information by transmitting signals to and from different parts of its body. It detects environmental changes and works with the [[Endocrine system|endocrine system]] to respond to them. Nervous tissue first arose in wormlike organisms about 550 to 600 million years ago.

In vertebrates the system has two main parts: the [[Central nervous system]] (CNS), consisting of the brain and spinal cord, and the [[Peripheral nervous system]] (PNS), consisting mainly of nerves, bundles of long fibers called axons that connect the CNS to the rest of the body. Motor nerves carry signals from the brain, and sensory nerves carry information to the CNS. The PNS divides into somatic and autonomic systems; the autonomic system has sympathetic, parasympathetic, and enteric branches.

Nervous tissue is defined by the [[Neuron|neuron]], a cell that sends electrochemical impulses rapidly and precisely along axons. Signals pass to other cells through electrical synapses or by release of [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitters]] at chemical synapses. Connections among neurons form circuits that generate perception and determine behavior. Glial cells provide structural and metabolic support.

Nervous systems occur in most multicellular animals but vary greatly in complexity. Only [[Sponge|sponges]], placozoans, and mesozoans lack one. Comb jellies and cnidarians, which include anemones, hydras, corals, and jellyfish, have a diffuse nerve net. Other animals, apart from a few worms, have a brain, a central cord, and nerves radiating from them. Size ranges from a few hundred cells in the simplest worms to around 300 billion cells in African elephants.

Malfunction can result from genetic defects, trauma, toxicity, infection, or senescence. [[Neurology]] is the medical specialty treating its disorders, and [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]] is the field that studies the system.

=== KEY: Nervous_tissue_e315d1
HEADWORD: Nervous tissue
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Nervous system | Neuron | Peripheral nervous system
ENTRY:
Main tissue component of the [[Nervous system|nervous system]], also called neural tissue. It is composed of [[Neuron|neurons]], which receive and transmit impulses, and neuroglia, which support them and help propagate impulses. Each neuron has an axon, a long projection that sends action potentials to the next cell. Bundles of axons form nerves in the [[Peripheral nervous system|peripheral nervous system]] and tracts in the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]]. Functions of the nervous system are sensory input, integration, control of muscles and glands, homeostasis and mental activity. Neurons are classified by function as sensory, motor or interneurons, and by structure as multipolar, bipolar or pseudounipolar. Astrocytes are the most abundant cell type in the brain, and oligodendrocytes form myelin in the central nervous system.

Swiss multinational food and drink company, headquartered in Vevey. === KEY: Netball_9ae54b
HEADWORD: Netball
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basketball | Commonwealth of Nations | England
ENTRY:
Seven-a-side ball sport played mainly by women and girls. It is one of a few sports created exclusively for them and is most popular in the [[Commonwealth of Nations]]. Originating in [[England]] in 1895 as a modified form of [[Basketball|basketball]], it was standardized internationally in 1960. The objective is to shoot through the defender's goal ring while stopping the opposition from shooting through their own. Players are restricted to zones of the court by their positions and may hold the ball for only three seconds. More than 20 million people play the sport in more than 80 countries, according to World Netball. The World Netball Championships and Commonwealth Games are held every four years. In 1995 the sport's governing body became an International Olympic Committee recognized federation, but netball has not yet been played at the Olympics.

=== KEY: Netherlands_a67d4c

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amsterdam | Belgium | Dutch Golden Age | European Union | Germany | Holland | Polder | The Hague
ENTRY:
Country in northwestern Europe and the Caribbean. The Netherlands, also informally known as [[Holland]], is the largest of the four constituent countries of the Kingdom of the Netherlands. European Netherlands borders [[Germany]] to the east and [[Belgium]] to the south, with a coastline on the North Sea. About 26% of the country lies below sea level, largely on [[Polder|polders]] created by land reclamation that began in the 14th century. During the [[Dutch Golden Age]], its trading companies established colonies and trading posts. The country was invaded and occupied by Nazi Germany in 1940, and the occupation lasted until the end of the war in 1945. A parliamentary constitutional monarchy since 1848, it is a founding member of the [[European Union]]. [[Amsterdam]] is the nominal capital, while [[The Hague]] houses the main national political institutions. It was the first country to legalize same-sex marriage, in 2001.
