American country singer, guitarist and songwriter, a main figure of the outlaw country subgenre that developed in the early 1970s. After moving to [[Austin, Texas|Austin]] in 1972, he recorded albums such as *Shotgun Willie* (1973), *Red Headed Stranger* (1975) and *Stardust* (1978), whose success made him one of the most recognized artists in [[Country music|country music]]. === KEY: Willis_Tower_852adc
HEADWORD: Willis Tower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chicago | Illinois | United Airlines
ENTRY:
Skyscraper in the Loop of [[Chicago]], [[Illinois]], formerly known as the Sears Tower. Designed by Bruce Graham and Fazlur Rahman Khan of Skidmore, Owings & Merrill, the 110-story, 1,451-foot (442.3 m) building opened in 1973 as the world's tallest building, a title it held for nearly 25 years. Its nine square tubes are clustered in a 3×3 matrix. It was renamed in 2009 under a naming-rights lease with the Willis Group. Its Skydeck, the highest observation deck in the United States, draws more than 1.7 million visitors a year, and [[United Airlines]] is its largest tenant.

=== KEY: Willow_9498cf
HEADWORD: Willow
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Linnaeus | Latin | Old English | Shrub | Soil | Tree
ENTRY:
Genus of mostly deciduous [[Tree|trees]] and [[Shrub|shrubs]] (*Salix*), also called sallows or osiers, that grows primarily on moist [[Soil|soils]] in cold and temperate regions. Narrow-leaved shrub species are called osiers and broader-leaved species sallows, from [[Old English]] *sealh*, related to the [[Latin]] word *salix*. Some arctic and alpine species are low-growing, creeping shrubs; the dwarf willow rarely exceeds 6 cm in height. Willows have soft, usually pliant, tough wood and large, fibrous roots that readily sprout from aerial parts. Most willows are dioecious, with male and female catkins on separate plants, and they are among the earliest woody plants to leaf out in spring. Its scientific name, *Salix*, was first used by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1753.

=== KEY: Willy_Brandt_cc8178
HEADWORD: Brandt, Willy
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Eastern Europe | Social Democratic Party of Germany | Vietnam War | West Germany
ENTRY:
German politician and statesman, chancellor of [[West Germany]] from 1969 to 1974. Born Herbert Frahm in Lübeck, he fled to Norway and then Sweden during the Nazi regime and adopted the pseudonym Willy Brandt, formally taking the name in 1948. He led the [[Social Democratic Party of Germany]] from 1964 to 1987 and was the first Social Democratic chancellor since 1930. His Ostpolitik sought better relations with [[Eastern Europe]], and in 1971 he received the Nobel Peace Prize for strengthening cooperation in Western Europe. In 1970 he knelt in silence at a memorial to the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (the *Kniefall von Warschau*). He resigned in 1974 after an aide, Günter Guillaume, was exposed as an agent of the East German secret service. He was criticized from the right for his Ostpolitik and from the left for his support of American policies, including his silence on the [[Vietnam War]] until 1973.

ENTRY:
American sprinter who overcame childhood [[Polio|polio]] to become a world-record-holding Olympic champion in [[Track and field]]. Rudolph won a bronze medal in the 4 × 100-meter relay at the 1956 Summer Olympics in [[Melbourne]], Australia. At the 1960 Summer Olympics in [[Rome]], she won three gold medals, in the 100- and 200-meter events and the 4 × 100-meter relay, becoming the first American woman to win three gold medals at a single [[Olympic Games]]. She was acclaimed as the fastest woman in the world. Her success made her an international star and a role model for Black and female athletes. She retired in 1962 as world record-holder in the 100- and 200-meter events, then became an educator and coach. She is also regarded as a civil rights and women's rights pioneer.

=== KEY: Wind_52258b
HEADWORD: Wind
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric circulation | Atmospheric pressure | Coriolis force | Monsoon | Solar wind | Wind power
ENTRY:
Natural movement of air or other gases relative to a planet's surface. Winds range from thunderstorm flows lasting tens of minutes, through local breezes lasting a few hours, to global winds resulting from the difference in absorption of solar energy between climate zones. The study of wind is called anemology.

Wind is caused by differences in [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]], primarily due to temperature differences; air moves from higher to lower pressure. On a rotating planet it is also deflected by the [[Coriolis force|Coriolis effect]], except exactly on the equator. The two major drivers of large-scale [[Atmospheric circulation|atmospheric circulation]] are differential heating between the equator and the poles and the planet's rotation. Thermal lows over terrain can drive [[Monsoon|monsoon]] circulations, and sea and land breezes define local winds on coasts.

Wind direction is the direction from which it originates, so a westerly wind blows from the west. Weather vanes and windsocks indicate direction, and speed is measured by anemometers, most commonly with rotating cups or propellers. Short bursts are gusts, and strong winds of around one minute are squalls; long-duration winds are named by average strength, such as breeze, gale, storm, and hurricane.

In outer space, the [[Solar wind|solar wind]] is the movement of gases or charged particles from the Sun, and planetary wind is the outgassing of light elements from an atmosphere. The strongest observed winds in the Solar System are on Neptune and Saturn.

Wind shapes landforms through aeolian processes such as erosion and the formation of loess, spreads wildfires, and disperses seeds. Humans have used it for sailing ships, hot air balloons, and as a source of [[Wind power|power]].

=== KEY: Wind_instrument_551a36
HEADWORD: Wind instrument
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brass instrument | Clarinet | Flute | Trumpet | Tuba | Valve | Woodwind instrument
ENTRY:
Class of musical instruments in which a column of air inside a resonator, usually a tube, is set into vibration by the player blowing into or over a mouthpiece. Pitch depends on the length of the tube and on manual changes to its effective length, such as keys or [[Valve|valves]]. Wind instruments are grouped into [[Brass instrument|brass]] and [[Woodwind instrument|woodwind]] families, classified by how sound is produced rather than by material: saxophones are typically made of brass but are woodwinds. In brass instruments, such as the [[Trumpet]] and [[Tuba]], the player's lips vibrate. Woodwinds produce sound through a reed, as in the [[Clarinet]] and oboe, or by blowing across an edge, as in the [[Flute]].

=== KEY: Wind_power_f858b0
HEADWORD: Wind power
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Electrical grid | Fossil fuel | Solar power | Transformer | Wind turbine
ENTRY:
Use of wind energy to generate useful work, today mostly electricity. Historically used by sails, windmills, and windpumps, it is now generated almost completely by [[Wind turbine|wind turbines]], generally grouped into wind farms and connected to the [[Electrical grid|electrical grid]].

Wind supplied about 2,700 TWh of electricity in 2025, over 8% of world electricity. About 100 GW was added during 2021, mostly in [[China]] and the United States, and global installed capacity exceeded 800 GW. In 2024, 30 countries generated more than a tenth of their electricity from wind, and generation has nearly tripled since 2015.

The power in moving air is proportional to the cube of wind speed, so available power increases eightfold when speed doubles. A turbine's theoretical efficiency is capped at 59.3% by the Betz limit. Almost all large turbines share one design: a horizontal-axis machine with an upwind rotor of 3 blades on a nacelle atop a tall tubular tower. Turbines in a farm are linked by a medium-voltage system, often 34.5 kV, and a substation [[Transformer|transformer]] raises the voltage for transmission.

Supporters describe wind as renewable, with a much smaller environmental impact than burning [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] and among the lowest costs per unit of energy; in many locations new onshore wind farms are cheaper than new coal or gas plants. Because output is variable, a reliable supply needs energy storage or dispatchable generation. Onshore farms have a greater visual impact than most other power stations per energy produced; offshore farms have less but generally cost more. Critics also cite wildlife effects, including bird and bat collisions. Wind output is often higher at night and in winter, when [[Solar power|solar]] output is low.

=== KEY: Wind_turbine_24fae5
HEADWORD: Wind turbine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electrical grid | Kinetic energy | Renewable energy
ENTRY:
Device for converting the [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] of wind into electrical energy. Turbines are built in a wide range of sizes, with horizontal or vertical axes, although horizontal is most common; commercial horizontal-axis turbines usually have three blades, upwind of their towers. Vertical-axis designs include the Darrieus, the Savonius, and floating and airborne types. Blades are most commonly made of glass-fiber composites, with carbon fiber also used. Larger turbines can contribute to a domestic power supply while selling unused power back to the [[Electrical grid|electrical grid]]. Wind turbines produce among the cheapest [[Renewable energy|renewable]] energy and are clean, emitting no greenhouse gases, but their output varies with the wind rather than with demand, so they are unreliable unless energy storage is available. As of 2024, hundreds of thousands of large turbines, in wind farms, were generating over 1,136 gigawatts, with 117 GW added each year.
