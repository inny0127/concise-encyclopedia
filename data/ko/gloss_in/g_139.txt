=== KEY: Trigonometric_functions_f07b75
HEADWORD: Trigonometric functions
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angle | Fourier analysis | Geodesy | Hyperbolic functions | Navigation | Unit circle
ENTRY:
Real-valued functions of an [[Angle|angle]], defined as ratios of two side lengths of a right triangle. The most common are sine, cosine and tangent, with reciprocals cosecant, secant and cotangent. Each has an inverse function and an analog among the [[Hyperbolic functions|hyperbolic functions]]. They are used in all sciences related to geometry, such as [[Navigation|navigation]], solid mechanics, celestial mechanics and [[Geodesy|geodesy]], and they are among the simplest periodic functions, widely used for studying periodic phenomena through [[Fourier analysis|Fourier analysis]]. Early definitions applied only to acute angles; the standard [[Unit circle|unit circle]] extends sine and cosine to the whole real line, and modern definitions use infinite series or differential equations to extend them to the complex plane. A superscript −1 denotes the inverse function, so sin⁻¹x means arcsin x, not a reciprocal.

=== KEY: Trigonometry_be8958
HEADWORD: Trigonometry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aryabhata | Geodesy | Hipparchus | Navigation | Ptolemy | Trigonometric functions | Unit circle
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerned with relationships between angles and side lengths of triangles. The name comes from Ancient Greek *trígōnon*, "triangle," and *métron*, "measure." The field emerged in the Hellenistic world during the 3rd century BC from applications of geometry to astronomical studies. The Greeks focused on calculating chords, while mathematicians in India created the earliest known tables of values for trigonometric ratios such as sine.

[[Hipparchus|Hipparchus]] created the first known trigonometric tables, based on chord lengths in a circle. In the 2nd century, [[Ptolemy|Ptolemy]] compiled the *Almagest*, with a table of chords at half-degree intervals and proofs of identities such as the sum and difference formulas. [[Aryabhata|Aryabhata]], in the 5th century, introduced sine tables that influenced later development.

The [[Trigonometric functions|trigonometric functions]] relate the angles of a right triangle to ratios of its sides. Sine is the ratio of the side opposite an angle to the hypotenuse, cosine the ratio of the adjacent side to the hypotenuse, and tangent the ratio of the opposite side to the adjacent side. Their reciprocals are cosecant, secant, and cotangent. The mnemonic SOH-CAH-TOA is a common memory aid. The law of sines and the law of cosines allow all unknown angles and sides of a triangle to be computed once two sides and their included angle, two angles and a side, or three sides are known. Defined on the [[Unit circle|unit circle]], the functions extend to all positive and negative arguments and model periodic phenomena.

Applications include [[Geodesy|geodesy]], surveying, celestial mechanics, and [[Navigation|navigation]], and spherical trigonometry calculates the positions of stars and planets. Trigonometric functions describe waves and oscillations in physics, and they are used in engineering, computer graphics, and electronics.

=== KEY: Trilobite_09f55d
HEADWORD: Trilobite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Calcite | Cambrian | Devonian | Ordovician | Permian | Plankton | Plate tectonics
ENTRY:
Class of extinct marine [[Arthropod|arthropods]], named for their three-lobed body. Trilobites were among the most successful early animals, existing in oceans for almost 270 million years, with more than 22,000 species described. Their mineralized exoskeletons, made of [[Calcite|calcite]], left an extensive fossil record that contributed to biostratigraphy, paleontology and [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. They first appear about 521 million years ago, in the [[Cambrian|Early Cambrian]], and diversity peaked in the Late Cambrian and [[Ordovician]]. Several [[Devonian]] extinctions reduced them sharply, and the last trilobites disappeared in the end-[[Permian]] mass extinction about 251.9 million years ago. They filled many niches as predators, scavengers, filter feeders and swimmers that fed on [[Plankton|plankton]]; some crawled onto land. The largest exceeded 70 centimeters (28 in) in length.

=== KEY: Trinidad_and_Tobago_63965a

Archipelagic republic and southernmost country in the [[Caribbean]], off the northeast coast of [[Venezuela]]. Its capital is [[Port of Spain]]. [[Christopher Columbus]] first sighted Trinidad in 1498, and Spain ruled it until a British fleet took it in 1797. === KEY: Triple_point_1b23bd
HEADWORD: Triple point
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: International System of Units | Kelvin | Lord Kelvin | Mercury (element) | Phase diagram
ENTRY:
Temperature and pressure at which a substance's gas, liquid and solid phases coexist in thermodynamic equilibrium. It is where the sublimation, fusion and vaporization curves meet on a [[Phase diagram|phase diagram]]. The triple point of [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] is at −38.8 °C (−37.8 °F) and 0.165 mPa. Helium-4 has no sublimation curve and so no triple point where its solid meets its gas. The term was first used on August 3, 1871, by James Thomson, brother of [[Lord Kelvin]]. Triple points of several substances define points on the ITS-90 temperature scale, from hydrogen (13.8033 K) to water (273.16 K, 0.01 °C). Before 2019 the triple point of water defined the [[Kelvin|kelvin]]; under the [[International System of Units]] it is now an experimentally measured constant of about 273.16 K and a vapor pressure of 611.657 pascals.

=== KEY: Tristan_Tzara_5afd48

ENTRY:
Romanian-French avant-garde poet, essayist and performance artist, and a founder of [[Dada]]. === KEY: Tristan_und_Isolde_8cb763
HEADWORD: Tristan und Isolde
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthur Schopenhauer | Benjamin Britten | Gustav Mahler | Richard Strauss | Richard Wagner
ENTRY:
Music drama in three acts by [[Richard Wagner]], premiered in Munich on June 10, 1865, with Hans von Bülow conducting. Loosely based on Gottfried von Strassburg's medieval 12th-century romance *Tristan and Iseult*, it was composed from 1857 to 1859. Wagner preferred the term *Handlung* ("action") for its continuous flow, or "endless melody," in contrast to conventional opera of recitatives and showpiece arias. Its concern with striving and love beyond death was inspired in part by the philosophy of [[Arthur Schopenhauer]]. The prelude opens with the Tristan chord, whose unprecedented chromaticism and tonal ambiguity marked a turning point in the evolution of Western music. The harmony initially divided audiences, but the opera became enormously influential, directly inspiring [[Gustav Mahler]], [[Richard Strauss]] and [[Benjamin Britten]].

=== KEY: Tritium_bdbfb2
HEADWORD: Tritium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beta decay | Deuterium | Ernest Rutherford | Helium-3 | Hydrogen | Lithium | Nuclear fusion
ENTRY:
Radioactive [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] isotope with one proton and two neutrons, half-life 12.32 years. Also called hydrogen-3, it is rare on Earth, formed in the atmosphere by cosmic rays and produced in nuclear reactors by irradiating [[Lithium|lithium]]. It decays by [[Beta decay|beta]] emission into [[Helium-3]], releasing 18.6 keV. Tritium powers self-illuminating lights in watches and firearm night sights, serves as a tracer in medical and scientific work, and fuels [[Nuclear fusion|fusion]] reactors with [[Deuterium]]. It is a component of hydrogen bombs. [[Ernest Rutherford]] and colleagues first detected it in 1934.

=== KEY: Triton_moon_fb8f9e
HEADWORD: Triton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dwarf planet | Kuiper belt | Neptune | Solar System | Voyager 2
ENTRY:
Largest moon of [[Neptune]]. Triton is the only moon of Neptune massive enough to be rounded under its own gravity. It has a thin, hazy atmosphere of mostly nitrogen, with minor amounts of methane and carbon monoxide. Triton orbits in a retrograde direction, opposite to Neptune's rotation, the only large moon in the [[Solar System]] to do so. It is thought to have once been a [[Dwarf planet|dwarf planet]] from the [[Kuiper belt]], captured by Neptune's gravity. At 2,706.8 kilometers (1,681.9 mi) in diameter, it is the seventh-largest moon in the Solar System. Its surface is covered by frozen nitrogen and has very few impact craters. William Lassell discovered Triton on October 10, 1846, just 17 days after Neptune itself was found. The 1989 flyby by [[Voyager 2]] remains the only up-close visit, and the probe studied only about 40% of the surface.

=== KEY: Trogon_f367d3
HEADWORD: Trogon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eocene | Fruit | Insect | Owl
ENTRY:
Family of colorful birds that includes the quetzals. Trogons, residents of tropical forests worldwide, form the single family Trogonidae in the order Trogoniformes, with 49 species in seven genera. The word *trogon* is Greek for "nibbling," because the birds gnaw holes in trees to make their nests. They feed on [[Insect|insects]] and [[Fruit|fruit]], and their broad bills and weak legs reflect their diet and tree-dwelling habits. Trogons are generally not migratory, and males and females have distinctive plumage. They are the only type of animal with a heterodactyl toe arrangement. The fossil record dates back 49 million years to the [[Eocene|Early Eocene]]. Their relationships have long been a mystery, with suggested relatives including parrots, cuckoos and [[Owl|owls]].

Wooden horse in Greek mythology. The Greeks built it during the [[Trojan War]] and hid warriors inside, then pretended to sail away. === KEY: Trojan_War_b48f8f
HEADWORD: Trojan War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze Age | Eratosthenes | Heinrich Schliemann | Homer | Iliad | Odyssey | Troy
ENTRY:
Legendary war in Greek mythology, set around the thirteenth or early twelfth century BC. The Achaeans (Greeks) waged it against the city of [[Troy]] after Paris, prince of Troy, took Helen from her husband Menelaus, king of Sparta. [[Homer]]'s *[[Iliad]]* covers four days and two nights in the tenth year of the siege, and the *[[Odyssey]]* follows the homeward journey of Odysseus. In 1868, Frank Calvert convinced [[Heinrich Schliemann]] that Troy lay at what is now Hisarlık in Turkey, a claim most scholars now accept. Many scholars think the Homeric stories fuse sieges and expeditions by Mycenaean Greeks during the [[Bronze Age]]. Those who see a specific historical conflict usually date it to the twelfth or eleventh century BC, often preferring the dates of [[Eratosthenes]], 1194–1184 BC.

=== KEY: Trombone_06ee32
HEADWORD: Trombone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baroque | Claudio Monteverdi | George Frideric Handel | Johann Sebastian Bach
ENTRY:
Brass instrument whose pitch is altered by a telescoping slide rather than valves. As with all brass instruments, sound is produced when the player's lips vibrate in a mouthpiece. The name comes from the Italian *tromba* (trumpet) and *-one* (large), meaning "large trumpet." The tenor, pitched in B♭, is the most common type, with a bass trombone also widely used. The alto, pitched in E♭, became less common as tenor technique improved but is regaining popularity for its lighter sound. Its Renaissance predecessor, the sackbut, appeared in the 15th century and was used across Europe, declining in most places by the mid to late 17th century. [[Claudio Monteverdi]] wrote for it, and [[Johann Sebastian Bach]] and [[George Frideric Handel]] used trombones on a few occasions in the [[Baroque]] period. A player is called a trombonist.

=== KEY: Tropaeolum_d136d2
HEADWORD: Tropaeolum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Linnaeus | Chile | Flowering plant | Hybrid (biology)
ENTRY:
Genus of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]], commonly known as nasturtium. It comprises roughly 80 species of annual and perennial herbaceous plants native to South and Central America. [[Carl Linnaeus]] named it in *Species Plantarum*, recalling the Roman custom of erecting a trophy pole: the round, shield-shaped leaves reminded him of shields and the flowers of blood-stained helmets. Common garden species include *T. majus*, *T. peregrinum* and *T. speciosum*, while *T. polyphyllum* from [[Chile]] survives winter underground at elevations of 3,300 meters (11,000 ft). The bisexual flowers have five petals and a funnel-shaped nectar spur. The most common cultivated nasturtium is a [[Hybrid (biology)|hybrid]] of *T. majus*, *T. minus* and *T. peltophorum*, usually grown from seed as a half-hardy annual that thrives in poor soil and dry conditions.

=== KEY: Tropic_of_Cancer_6ecd6f
HEADWORD: Tropic of Cancer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Equator | NATO | Sahara
ENTRY:
Northernmost circle of latitude at which the Sun can be directly overhead. It lies at 23°26′08.9″ north of the [[Equator]], a position set by Earth's [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]] that currently drifts south by about 15 meters per year. The Sun reaches overhead here at the June solstice, when the Northern Hemisphere is tilted toward it to its maximum extent. At this latitude, at solar midnight on the December solstice, the Sun is 90 degrees below the horizon in all directions. The name, from the Latin for crab, dates to the last centuries BC, when the Sun was in the zodiacal constellation Cancer at the June solstice; precession has since moved that alignment. The line marks the southern limit of the mutual defense obligation of [[NATO]], since member states are not obligated to defend territory south of it. Much land on or near the line is part of the [[Sahara]], where summer temperatures often reach 45 °C (113 °F).

=== KEY: Tropic_of_Capricorn_dcf9fd
HEADWORD: Tropic of Capricorn
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Brazil | Chile | Equator | Madagascar | South Africa | Tropics
ENTRY:
Southernmost circle of latitude at which the Sun can be directly overhead. It is the southern of the two [[Tropics|tropics]], lying at 23°26′08.9″ south of the [[Equator]] and marking the dividing line between the Southern Temperate Zone and the tropics. The Sun is directly overhead here at the December solstice. The line is drifting northward by about 15 meters per year as [[Axial tilt|Earth's axial tilt]] changes. It passes through [[Chile]], Argentina, Paraguay, [[Brazil]], Namibia, Botswana, [[South Africa]], Mozambique, [[Madagascar]] and Australia. Daylight lasts about 13 hours and 35 minutes at the December solstice and about 10 hours and 41 minutes at the June solstice. The name recalls the zodiacal constellation Capricorn, where the Sun stood at the northern solstice when the line was named; precession has since moved that alignment.

=== KEY: Tropical_climate_070a40
HEADWORD: Tropical climate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equator | Humidity | Köppen climate classification | Monsoon | Precipitation
ENTRY:
Major climate group in the [[Köppen climate classification|Köppen]] system, marked by the letter A. Tropical climates have a monthly average temperature of 18 °C (64 °F) or higher in the coolest month, with hot temperatures and high [[Humidity|humidity]] all year. [[Precipitation|Annual precipitation]] is often abundant, and there are normally only two seasons, a wet (rainy or [[Monsoon|monsoon]]) season and a dry season. The annual temperature range is very small, and sunlight is intense. The three subtypes are tropical rainforest (Af), tropical monsoon (Am) and tropical savanna (Aw or As), distinguished by the precipitation of the driest month. Tropical rainforest climates usually extend only 5–10 degrees north and south of the [[Equator]], with yearly average temperatures between 21 and 30 °C (70 and 86 °F).

=== KEY: Tropical_cyclone_241ecd
HEADWORD: Tropical cyclone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Climate change | Equator | Flood | Storm | Typhoon
ENTRY:
Rapidly rotating storm system with a low-pressure center, strong winds and a spiral arrangement of thunderstorms. Depending on location, it is called a hurricane in the [[Atlantic Ocean]] or northeastern Pacific, a [[Typhoon|typhoon]] in the northwestern Pacific, or a tropical cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. About 80 to 90 named tropical cyclones form each year, and over half develop winds of 65 kn (120 km/h; 75 mph) or more. They form over large bodies of warm water and draw energy from evaporation, unlike mid-latitude storms powered by temperature contrasts. They rarely form within 5° of the [[Equator]]. Coastal damage comes from strong winds, high waves, [[Storm|storm surges]] and tornadoes. Extremely heavy rain can reach 40 km (25 mi) from the coastline, causing [[Flood|flooding]]. [[Climate change]] can increase the duration, occurrence and intensity of these storms.

=== KEY: Tropical_rainforest_c5427d
HEADWORD: Tropical rainforest
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deforestation | Equator
ENTRY:
Dense, warm forest with high rainfall, typically found within 10° north and south of the [[Equator]]. It is a type of tropical moist broadleaf forest, and true rainforests have no dry season, with at least 60 mm (2.4 in) of average precipitation in every month. Temperatures exceed 18 °C (64 °F) every month. Rainforests are home to 40–75% of all species globally, including half of the world's animal and plant species and two-thirds of flowering plant species. Described as the "world's largest pharmacy," they hold over a quarter of natural medicines discovered to date. Soils are typically nutrient-poor, and the forest is stratified into emergent, canopy, understory and forest-floor layers. Logging and agricultural expansion threaten rainforests through [[Deforestation|deforestation]] and habitat fragmentation, and REDD and REDD+ programs aim to curb forest loss.

=== KEY: Tropical_savanna_climate_fd31a4
HEADWORD: Tropical savanna climate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australia | India | Köppen climate classification | Sahel | Savanna
ENTRY:
Tropical climate subtype with a pronounced dry season, corresponding to the [[Köppen climate classification|Köppen]] categories Aw (dry winter) and As (dry summer). Its driest month receives less than 60 mm (2.4 in) of precipitation, and it tends to have less overall rainfall than a tropical monsoon climate or a more pronounced dry season. Total annual rainfall cannot exceed 2,500 mm (98 in). Droughts often prevail, and the vegetation is typically tree-studded grassland, or [[Savanna|savanna]], rather than thick jungle. Such regions include parts of [[India]], northern [[Australia]], Brazil and the [[Sahel]] of Africa.

=== KEY: Tropics_a46426
HEADWORD: Tropics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Equator | Rainforest | Savanna | Tropic of Cancer | Tropic of Capricorn
ENTRY:
Region of Earth surrounding the [[Equator]], where the Sun may shine directly overhead. The tropics receive the most solar energy over the year and have the highest temperatures on the planet, but the lowest seasonal temperature variation, so seasons are more often divided by precipitation than by temperature. Their width in latitude is twice Earth's [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]], bounded by the [[Tropic of Cancer]] and the [[Tropic of Capricorn]]. The tropics cover 39.8% of Earth's surface and contain 36% of its landmass. As of 2014 they were home to 40% of the world's population, a share projected to reach 50% by 2050. Climates within them include [[Rainforest|rain forests]], monsoons, [[Savanna|savannahs]], deserts and high-altitude mountains. Global warming is expanding tropical weather conditions into subtropical areas, with more heatwaves and intense storms.

=== KEY: Trousers_10daae

[[Clothing|Garment]] covering each leg separately from the waist to the knees or ankles. === KEY: Trout_3c85f1
HEADWORD: Trout
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brown bear | Osprey | Otter | Salmon
ENTRY:
Freshwater fish of the subfamily Salmoninae, closely related to [[Salmon|salmon]]. The name covers many species in the genera *Salmo*, *Oncorhynchus* and *Salvelinus*, though brook trout and three other North American "trout" are actually char. Most trout spend their lives in freshwater, spawning in the shallow gravel beds of headwater creeks, while steelhead and sea trout are anadromous and can spend up to three years at sea. As mid-level predators, trout feed on crustaceans, insects, worms and tadpoles, and they are prey for [[Brown bear|brown bears]], [[Otter|otters]], [[Osprey|ospreys]], gulls and kingfishers. Their discarded remains nourish scavengers and riparian plants, making trout keystone species. Trout are oily fish and have long been important food for humans.

Ancient city in northwest Asia Minor, now the site of Hisarlık in Turkey. Known as Ilion or Ilium in Greek and Latin, it is the setting of the [[Trojan War]] in [[Homer]]'s *[[Iliad]]*. === KEY: Trust_sentiment_379eaf

The trustor becomes vulnerable to the trustee on the presumption that the trustee will act for the trustor's benefit, but cannot control the trustee's actions. === KEY: Truth_2a51e2
HEADWORD: Truth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Belief | Deductive reasoning | Ethics | Journalism | Paradox | Proposition
ENTRY:
Conformity to reality or fact. Truth contrasts with falsity or misrepresentation and is typically treated as a property of sentences, [[Proposition|propositions]], or [[Belief|beliefs]] that describe things as they are. The word comes from Old English *trēowth*, "fidelity," and entered Modern English through Middle English *trewthe*.

Its precise definition is contested. The correspondence theory holds that a statement is true if it corresponds to facts. According to the coherence theory, a belief is true if it is consistent with, and supported by, other beliefs. Deflationary theories hold that truth has no significant intrinsic nature and that understanding it means understanding the linguistic role of the word "true." Pluralists assert that the definition varies with the domain of analysis, while relativists maintain that a statement can be true from one perspective and false from another. Logical paradoxes, such as the liar [[Paradox|paradox]], challenge these theories.

Entities that can be true or false are called truthbearers; sentences are public but depend on context and language, propositions are abstract meanings, and beliefs link truth to cognition but are private.

Truth is a goal of inquiry in the sciences. The empirical sciences seek it through observation and experiment, the formal sciences through [[Deductive reasoning|deductive reasoning]] from fundamental principles. Many religious traditions regard truth as a virtue grounded in a divine source, and it acts as a guiding norm in [[Ethics|ethics]] and [[Journalism|journalism]]. Discussion arose in antiquity in Hindu, Jain, Buddhist, and Greek thought; [[Aristotle|Aristotle]] wrote in his *Metaphysics* that "to say of what is that it is, or of what is not that it is not, is true." Christian and Islamic philosophers in the medieval period explored the relation between truth and God.

=== KEY: Tuberculosis_432448
HEADWORD: Tuberculosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antibiotic | HIV | Immune system | Lung | Robert Koch | Vaccine
ENTRY:
Contagious bacterial disease, historically called consumption, usually caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*. It often infects the [[Lung|lungs]] first but can spread to the kidneys, spine, brain, and other organs. Most infections cause no symptoms and are termed latent, in which case they are not contagious. A small proportion progress to active disease, which can be fatal if untreated. Typical symptoms are chronic cough with blood-containing mucus, fever, night sweats, and weight loss.

The disease spreads through the air when people with active TB in their lungs cough, spit, speak, or sneeze. Latent infection is more likely to become active in people with weakened [[Immune system|immune systems]]. An estimated 5% to 15% of untreated latent cases progress to active TB. Active disease involves the lungs in about 90% of cases; in 15–20% the infection spreads outside them, and in more than 50% of active cases in people with [[HIV]].

Prevention involves screening those at high risk, early detection and treatment, and vaccination with the bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) [[Vaccine|vaccine]]. Treatment requires multiple [[Antibiotic|antibiotics]] over a long period; drug-susceptible TB is typically treated with a six-month regimen including isoniazid and rifampicin. Since the 1980s, antibiotic resistance has produced more multidrug-resistant TB.

Latent TB is estimated to affect one-quarter of the world's population, approximately 2 billion people. In 2024, an estimated 10.7 million new cases and 1.23 million deaths made it the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. In the 1800s it caused an estimated quarter of all deaths in Europe. [[Robert Koch]] identified the bacterium in 1882.

=== KEY: Turbine_8fa876
HEADWORD: Turbine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Energy | Hero of Alexandria | Impulse (physics) | Steam turbine | Torque | Windmill | Work (physics)
ENTRY:
Rotary mechanical device that extracts [[Energy|energy]] from a fluid flow and converts it into useful [[Work (physics)|work]]. The work may drive a generator to produce electricity. A turbine is a turbomachine with at least one moving part. Its moving part, the rotor assembly, is a shaft or drum with attached blades; the fluid acts on the blades to impart rotational energy. Gas, steam and water turbines have a casing that contains and controls the working fluid. [[Impulse (physics)|Impulse]] turbines change the direction of a high-velocity fluid jet, while reaction turbines develop [[Torque|torque]] by reacting to pressure changes in the fluid. The word *turbine* was first applied to the device in 1822 by French mining engineer Claude Burdin, from Latin *turbo*, meaning vortex. [[Hero of Alexandria]] demonstrated the principle with an aeolipile in the first century AD, and early examples include [[Windmill|windmills]] and waterwheels. Modern [[Steam turbine|steam turbines]] often combine reaction and impulse designs.

=== KEY: Turbulence_2e65f9
HEADWORD: Turbulence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric circulation | Drag (physics) | Kinetic energy | Pressure | Reynolds number | Richard Feynman | Viscosity
ENTRY:
Fluid motion characterized by chaotic changes in [[Pressure|pressure]] and flow velocity. It contrasts with laminar flow, in which fluid moves in parallel layers with no disruption between them. Most fluid flows in nature and engineering are turbulent, as with surf, fast-flowing rivers and chimney smoke. Turbulence arises when excess [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] overcomes the damping effect of [[Viscosity|viscosity]], producing unsteady vortices that increase [[Drag (physics)|drag]] due to friction. The onset of turbulence can be predicted by the dimensionless [[Reynolds number]], the ratio of kinetic energy to viscous damping, yet turbulence has long resisted detailed physical analysis. Physicist [[Richard Feynman]] described turbulence as the most important unsolved problem in classical physics. Most of the terrestrial [[Atmospheric circulation|atmospheric circulation]] is turbulent. Its intensity affects fish ecology, air pollution, precipitation and climate change.

Mathematical model of computation describing an [[Abstract machine|abstract machine]] that manipulates symbols on a strip of tape. ENTRY:
Reptile of the order *Testudines*, characterized by a shell developed mainly from its ribs. Turtles fall into two major groups, *Pleurodira* (side-necked turtles) and *Cryptodira* (hidden-necked turtles), which differ in how the head retracts. About 360 living or extinct species are known, including land-dwelling [[Tortoise|tortoises]] and freshwater terrapins. The domed carapace and flatter plastron form a shell made mostly of [[Bone|bone]] and covered in keratin scales. Turtles are ectothermic and generally opportunistic omnivores. [[Sea turtle|Sea turtles]] are the only reptiles that migrate long distances to lay eggs on a favored beach. The leatherback, the largest living species, can exceed 2.7 m (8 ft 10 in) in length. Many species are extinct or threatened because of hunting, bycatch in fishing nets and habitat destruction.

=== KEY: Tuscany_b6111b

ENTRY:
Region of central [[Italy]]. Its capital and largest city is [[Florence]]. Tuscany is regarded as the birthplace of the [[Renaissance|Italian Renaissance]] and of the foundations of the Italian language, which were shaped by the literature of [[Dante Alighieri]], [[Petrarch]], Giovanni Boccaccio, [[Niccolò Machiavelli]] and Francesco Guicciardini. Its museums include the [[Uffizi]] and the Palazzo Pitti, and its wines include Chianti and Brunello di Montalcino. The [[Etruscan civilization|Etruscans]] created the first major civilization in the region. Eight Tuscan localities are World Heritage Sites, including the historic centers of Florence, San Gimignano and Siena. The region has over 120 protected nature reserves. In 2018 Florence alone had over 5 million tourist arrivals.

Egyptian [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]] of the Eighteenth Dynasty (r. c. 1332–1323 BC). Born Tutankhaten, he restored the traditional polytheistic [[Ancient Egyptian religion|religion]] of ancient Egypt, reversing the Atenism of [[Akhenaten]]'s reign, and changed his name to Tutankhamun. He moved the royal court back to Memphis and reestablished diplomatic relations with the [[Mitanni]]. His military campaigns reached [[Nubia]] and the Near East. He died at about 19 and was buried in a small tomb, because his royal tomb in the [[Valley of the Kings]] was unfinished. Excavators led by Howard Carter discovered the tomb in 1922, which had been robbed in antiquity but retained much of its contents, including over 5,000 artifacts and the king's undisturbed mummy. His mask, preserved at the [[Egyptian Museum]], remains a popular symbol.

ENTRY:
Republic of [[Russia]] at the geographical center of [[Asia]], in southern [[Siberia]]. Its 336,651 people (2021 census) are 88.7% ethnic Tuvan and 10.1% ethnic Russian. Chinese dynasties and nomadic khanates controlled the area before the [[Qing dynasty]] incorporated it in 1758 as part of Outer Mongolia. A Russian protectorate followed in 1914, under [[Nicholas II]]. The nominally independent Tuvan People's Republic, recognized only by the [[Soviet Union]] and Mongolia, was annexed into the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic in 1944. As of 2012, 61.8% adhered to [[Buddhism]] and 8% to [[Shamanism|Tuvan shamanism]].

ENTRY:
Archipelagic island country in the [[Polynesia|Polynesian]] subregion of [[Oceania]], midway between Hawaii and Australia. It is composed of three reef islands and six atolls, lying west of the [[International Date Line]]. The 2022 census recorded 10,643 people, making it the second least populated member of the [[United Nations]]. Its first inhabitants, Polynesians, arrived as part of a migration that began about three thousand years ago. A British protectorate was proclaimed in 1892, and Tuvalu became a separate colony in 1976. It became fully independent on 1 October 1978, as a constitutional monarchy within the [[Commonwealth of Nations|Commonwealth]] with [[Charles III]] as king. With little soil, the country relies on fishing licenses, remittances and aid. Being low-lying, it is extremely vulnerable to [[Climate change|sea level rise]].
