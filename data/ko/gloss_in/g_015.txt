ENTRY:
Arctic and sub-Arctic [[Cetacean|cetacean]], the only member of the genus *Delphinapterus*. Its all-white adult coloration and lack of a dorsal fin allow it to swim under ice. A large, deformable melon at the front of the head houses an echolocation organ. Adults reach 5.5 m (18 ft) and weigh up to 1,600 kg (3,530 lb). === KEY: Bengali_language_cf49f5
HEADWORD: Bengali language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bangladesh | Bengal | Government of India | Indo-Aryan languages | Pakistan | Rabindranath Tagore | UNESCO | West Bengal
ENTRY:
Eastern [[Indo-Aryan languages|Indo-Aryan]] language of the Indo-European family, also called Bangla, spoken chiefly in the [[Bengal]] region of South Asia. It has over 242 million native speakers and another 43 million second-language speakers as of 2025, making it the sixth most spoken native language and the seventh most spoken language overall.

Bengali is the official, national, and most widely spoken language of [[Bangladesh]], where 98% of the population uses it as a first language. It is the second most widely spoken language in India and the official language of [[West Bengal]], Tripura, and the Barak Valley region of Assam. It has been a second official language of Jharkhand since September 2011. The [[Government of India]] accorded Bengali classical-language status on October 3, 2024.

The language has developed over more than 1,400 years. A Sanskrit-Chinese dictionary compiled in 782 shows the presence of Bengali, and its ancestor was the language of the Pala Empire and the Sena dynasty. Middle Bengali absorbed Arabic and Persian vocabulary after Arab and Turco-Persian settlement in the 13th century, and Bengali gained prominence over Persian at the court of the Sultans of Bengal.

Bengali literature, with a millennium-old history, flourished during the Bengali Renaissance and produced figures such as [[Rabindranath Tagore]], who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1913. The Bengali language movement of 1948–56, demanding official status for Bengali in [[Pakistan]], fostered Bengali nationalism in East Bengal and contributed to the emergence of Bangladesh in 1971. In 1999 [[UNESCO]] recognized February 21 as International Mother Language Day in honor of the movement.

Colorless, flammable aromatic [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbon]] with the formula C₆H₆. Its molecule is a planar hexagonal ring of six carbon atoms, each bonded to one hydrogen atom. Benzene is a natural constituent of petroleum and one of the elementary [[Petrochemical|petrochemicals]]. Benzene is classified as a [[Carcinogen|carcinogen]]; chronic exposure is linked to bone marrow suppression and [[Leukemia|leukemia]].

=== KEY: Bernoulli_s_principle_59f7bd
HEADWORD: Bernoulli's principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conservation of energy | Daniel Bernoulli | Fluid dynamics | Isaac Newton | Leonhard Euler | Mach number | Turbulence
ENTRY:
Principle of [[Fluid dynamics|fluid dynamics]] relating pressure, speed and height. For a fluid flowing horizontally, an increase in speed occurs with a decrease in pressure, so the highest speed occurs where the pressure is lowest. The principle is named after Swiss mathematician and physicist [[Daniel Bernoulli]], who published it in *Hydrodynamica* in 1738. [[Leonhard Euler]] derived Bernoulli's equation in its usual form in 1752. The principle can be derived from [[Isaac Newton]]'s second law of motion or from [[Conservation of energy|conservation of energy]], in which a gain in kinetic energy is balanced by a loss in potential and internal energy. It applies to isentropic flows, in which the effects of [[Turbulence|turbulence]] and other irreversible processes are small. The simple form applies to incompressible flows, such as most liquid flows and gases at low [[Mach number|Mach number]]. It underlies devices such as the Venturi meter and pitot-static tubes.

=== KEY: Bicycle_78efca
HEADWORD: Bicycle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Car | Cycle sport | Transport
ENTRY:
Human-powered or motor-assisted, pedal-driven, single-track vehicle with two wheels attached to a frame, one behind the other. Its rider is called a cyclist or bicyclist.

The bicycle was invented in Europe in the 19th century. It developed from the velocipede, itself an advance on Karl Drais's steerable, pedal-less two-wheeler of 1817. The first chain-driven model appeared around 1885, with John Kemp Starley's Rover safety bicycle, which had two similar-sized wheels and a diamond frame; John Boyd Dunlop's pneumatic tire followed in 1888. The basic form of the upright "safety" bicycle has changed little since, though modern materials and computer-aided design have produced many specialized types.

By the early 21st century there were more than 1 billion bicycles. They are the principal means of [[Transport|transport]] in many regions and are used for commuting, shopping, fitness, military and police work, courier services, recreation, and [[Cycle sport|racing]]. Types include utility, mountain, racing, touring, BMX, and electric bicycles.

A bicycle stays upright by being steered to keep its center of mass over the wheels. Turns are initiated by countersteering, either by turning the handlebars directly or by leaning the bicycle. Mechanically the bicycle is highly efficient: up to 99% of the energy a rider delivers to the pedals reaches the wheels. At 16–24 km/h a rider uses only the power required to walk. A seated upright rider creates about 75% of total drag; a 2016 hour record with a highly streamlined bicycle exceeded 92 km/h.

Ball bearings, pneumatic tires, chain-driven sprockets, and tension-spoked wheels were first developed for bicycles and later played key roles in the development of the [[Car|car]].

=== KEY: Big_Bang_01dc58
HEADWORD: Big Bang
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dark energy | Dark matter | Edwin Hubble | Georges Lemaître | Hydrogen | Redshift
ENTRY:
Physical theory describing how the universe expanded from an early state of high density and temperature. Cosmological models based on it explain the abundance of light elements, the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, the [[Redshift|redshift]] of galaxies, and large-scale structure. The uniformity of the universe is explained by cosmic inflation, a phase of accelerated expansion in the earliest stages. Measurements of the expansion rate place the age of the universe at an estimated 13.787±0.02 billion years.

Extrapolating the expansion backward, the models describe an extraordinarily hot, dense primordial universe. The modern view incorporates inflation, followed by a reheating event that produced a hot, dense plasma. As the universe expanded and cooled, subatomic particles and then atoms formed. Primordial elements, mostly [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] with some helium and lithium, coalesced under gravity, aided by [[Dark matter|dark matter]], into the first stars and galaxies. The theory describes the expansion of space itself, not an explosion in preexisting space.

Alexander Friedmann introduced the concept of an expanding universe in 1922 through the Friedmann equations. [[Edwin Hubble]] published the earliest empirical observation of expansion, now called Hubble's law, in 1929, showing that galaxies recede from Earth at rates proportional to their distance. In 1931 [[Georges Lemaître]] proposed that the universe emerged from a "primeval atom." The CMB was discovered accidentally in 1964, and its uniformity and spectrum fit the Big Bang models; by the late 1960s most cosmologists had rejected the competing steady-state model.

In the current universe, luminous matter makes up less than 5% of the density, dark matter 27%, and [[Dark energy|dark energy]], to which accelerating expansion is attributed, 68%. Unexplained aspects include the matter-antimatter imbalance and the nature of dark matter.

=== KEY: Big_Crunch_76cba5
HEADWORD: Big Crunch
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang
ENTRY:
Hypothetical scenario for the ultimate fate of the universe, in which expansion reverses and the universe recollapses. The idea dates to 1922, when Russian physicist Alexander Friedmann showed in his equations that the universe could either expand or contract, depending on its density. Gravity would reverse the expansion, and the universe would end in a fireball of near-infinite temperature, possibly followed by a new [[Big Bang]]. Most available evidence, however, indicates that the hypothesis is incorrect. Observations show that the expansion of the universe is accelerating, which makes a Big Chill more likely.
