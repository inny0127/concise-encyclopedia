Renunciation or cessation of resentment, indignation or [[Anger|anger]] toward someone who has caused harm. Psychologically, forgiveness is distinct from condoning, excusing or forgetting, and theorists differ on whether it requires reconciliation with the offender. Eating utensil with a long handle ending in a head that branches into several narrow tines for spearing food. === KEY: Formal_language_47c5df
HEADWORD: Formal language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boolean algebra | George Boole | Gottlob Frege | Grammar | Noam Chomsky | Programming language
ENTRY:
Set of strings whose symbols are drawn from a fixed alphabet, studied in logic, mathematics, computer science and linguistics. The symbols concatenate into strings called words, and words that belong to a language are its well-formed words. Languages are often defined by a [[Grammar|formal grammar]], such as a regular or context-free grammar. In computer science, they underpin the grammars of [[Programming language|programming languages]] and controlled natural languages. In computational complexity theory, decision problems are typically defined as formal languages. In logic and the foundations of mathematics, they represent the syntax of axiomatic systems. Formal language theory grew out of linguistics. In the mid-19th century, [[George Boole]] established [[Boolean algebra]], and [[Gottlob Frege]] later attempted to realize Leibniz's ideas through a notation first outlined in *Begriffsschrift* (1879). [[Noam Chomsky]] devised the Chomsky hierarchy, an abstract classification of formal and natural languages.

=== KEY: Formation_and_evolution_of_the_Solar_System_a78384
HEADWORD: Formation and evolution of the Solar System
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Immanuel Kant | Molecular cloud | Nebular hypothesis | Pierre-Simon Laplace | Planetary nebula | Red giant | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Process by which the Solar System formed from a collapsing [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]] about 4.6 billion years ago and evolved into its present form. Most of the collapsing mass gathered at the center to form the Sun, while the rest flattened into a protoplanetary disk from which the planets, moons, asteroids and other small bodies formed. The standard model, the [[Nebular hypothesis|nebular hypothesis]], was developed in the 18th century by Emanuel Swedenborg, [[Immanuel Kant]] and [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]]. Its chief objection, that it could not explain the Sun's relative lack of angular momentum, was set aside after studies from the early 1980s showed young stars surrounded by discs of dust and gas. Later evolution included giant collisions, possibly producing Earth's Moon, and planetary migration, which shaped the present structure. In roughly five billion years the Sun will become a [[Red giant|red giant]], shed its outer layers as a [[Planetary nebula|planetary nebula]] and leave a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]].

=== KEY: Fortification_6d902d
HEADWORD: Fortification
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cannon | Carolingian Empire | Castle | Celts | Latin | Mycenaean Greece | Uruk
ENTRY:
Military construction designed for the defense of territory in warfare, also used to establish rule in a region during peacetime. The word derives from [[Latin]] *fortis* ("strong") and *facere* ("to make"). Fortification is usually divided into permanent and field fortification, with an intermediate semipermanent branch. Castles differ from the generic fort in being the residence of a monarch or noble and commanding a specific defensive territory.

Walls have been necessary for cities from early history. The walled town of Sesklo in Greece dates from 6800 BC, and Solnitsata in Bulgaria, from 4700 BC, held 350 people in two-story houses behind a stone wall. [[Uruk]] in Sumer is one of the world's oldest known walled cities, and the Egyptians built frontier fortresses and mud brick city walls. A prehistoric stone wall of 3200 BC, 4 m thick and 4 m tall, surrounded the Ness of Brodgar temple in Scotland. In [[Mycenaean Greece]], large "cyclopean" stone walls were fitted without mortar, and Athens built the Long Walls to its seaport at Piraeus. The [[Celts]] of Central Europe built large fortified settlements called oppida.

The [[Castle|castle]] emerged in the 9th century in the [[Carolingian Empire]], with Roman forts and hill forts as its main antecedents. [[Cannon|Cannons]] of the 14th century made medieval-style fortifications largely obsolete. Fortifications in the age of black powder became much lower, with ditches and earth ramparts to absorb the energy of cannon fire. Explosive shells of the 19th century disrupted the intricate arrangements of star forts, and steel-and-concrete fortifications were common in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The advances in modern warfare since World War I have made large-scale fortifications obsolete in most situations.

=== KEY: Fossil_22fc4f
HEADWORD: Fossil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amber | Feces
ENTRY:
Preserved remains, impression or trace of a once-living thing from a past geological age. Examples include bones, shells, exoskeletons, objects preserved in [[Amber|amber]], petrified wood and DNA remnants. The totality of fossils is the fossil record, which is incomplete but sufficient to show the broad pattern of life's diversification on Earth. Body fossils preserve parts of an organism, usually mineralized tissues such as bones and teeth, while trace fossils record activity such as tracks or [[Feces|feces]]. The oldest fossils are around 3.48 to 4.1 billion years old. Radiometric dating, developed in the early 20th century, allowed the absolute ages of fossils to be measured. The largest known is a *Sequoia* sp. measuring 88 m (289 ft) at Coaldale, Nevada. Ancient peoples also collected fossils: Paleolithic stone knives had fossil echinoderms set at the grip, and Egyptian temples kept fossil bones of hippo-like species.

=== KEY: Fossil_fuel_70a0da
HEADWORD: Fossil fuel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Coal | Hydrocarbon | Mikhail Lomonosov | Natural gas | Petroleum
ENTRY:
Flammable carbon compound or [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbon]] material formed naturally in the Earth's crust from the buried remains of prehistoric organisms. The main fossil fuels are [[Coal|coal]], [[Petroleum|petroleum]], and [[Natural gas|natural gas]]. They originate in the anaerobic decomposition of buried dead organisms over millions of years and so are considered nonrenewable. Terrestrial plants tend to form coal and methane, and many coal fields date to the Carboniferous period.

The theory that fossil fuels formed from dead plants was first introduced by Andreas Libavius in 1597 and later by [[Mikhail Lomonosov]]. Peat has been a domestic fuel since before recorded history, coal was burned in early furnaces for smelting, and commercial exploitation of petroleum began in the 19th century. Fossil fuels are burned for heat, to power engines, and to generate electricity, and some are refined into kerosene, gasoline, and diesel or converted into petrochemicals such as plastics.

In 2023, 77% of world primary energy consumption and over 60% of its electricity supply came from fossil fuels. Supporters credit them with high energy density, dispatchable reliability, and rising prosperity since the Industrial Revolution. Critics cite environmental costs: over 70% of the greenhouse gas emissions due to human activity in 2022 was carbon dioxide released from burning fossil fuels, the main source of emissions causing global warming and ocean acidification. Most air pollution deaths are due to fossil fuel particulates and noxious gases.

Recognition of these effects has led to a policy transition toward renewable energy, supported by the Paris Climate Agreement. In 2021, the International Energy Agency concluded that no new extraction projects could be opened if the world is to avoid the worst effects of [[Climate change|climate change]].

ENTRY:
Pendulum named after French physicist [[Léon Foucault|Léon Foucault]], conceived to demonstrate Earth's rotation. === KEY: Foundation_engineering_0bad41
HEADWORD: Foundation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Concrete | Earthquake | Flood | Soil
ENTRY:
Element of a structure that connects it to the ground, or more rarely to water, transferring its loads to the soil or rock. Foundations are generally classed as shallow or deep, and foundation engineering applies soil and rock mechanics to their design. Foundations spread weight over a large area to avoid overloading the [[Soil|soil]] and causing unequal settlement, anchor structures against [[Earthquake|earthquakes]], [[Flood|floods]], droughts, frost heaves and wind, and provide a level surface for construction. Shallow foundations, often called footings, are typically about a meter deep; the spread footing is a common type, a strip or pad of [[Concrete|concrete]] that transfers weight from walls and columns to the soil or bedrock. Rubble-trench foundations are shallow trenches filled with stone that extend below the frost line. Historically, buildings rested on padstones or dry stone, and timber pilings were used on soft or wet ground.

=== KEY: Fourier_analysis_66aca6
HEADWORD: Fourier analysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acoustics | Fourier series | Harmonic analysis | Joseph Fourier | Signal processing
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics that studies how general functions can be represented or approximated by sums of trigonometric functions or complex exponentials. It is named after [[Joseph Fourier|Joseph Fourier]], who showed that such representations greatly simplify the study of heat transfer, and it grew out of the study of [[Fourier series|Fourier series]]. Decomposing a function into oscillatory components is called Fourier analysis, and rebuilding it is Fourier synthesis. Applied to a sampled signal, it gives the amplitudes of sines and cosines at frequencies that are multiples of 1/T, a basis of [[Signal processing|signal processing]]. The field has been extended to [[Harmonic analysis|harmonic analysis]] on general groups and applies in [[Acoustics|acoustics]], optics, probability and number theory.

=== KEY: Fourier_series_0e939a
HEADWORD: Fourier series
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electrical engineering | Fourier transform | Heat equation | Joseph Fourier | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Expansion of a periodic function as an infinite sum of sine and cosine terms. [[Joseph Fourier|Joseph Fourier]] used such series to solve the [[Heat equation|heat equation]], arguing in an 1807 memoir on heat propagation that arbitrary functions could be expressed this way. The method makes many problems easier because the derivatives of trigonometric functions follow simple patterns. Fourier series cannot approximate arbitrary functions, since many have series that do not converge, but smooth, well-behaved functions converge to the original. Their coefficients are integrals of the function multiplied by sines and cosines over one period. The series are closely related to the [[Fourier transform|Fourier transform]], which handles functions that are not periodic, and they are used in [[Electrical engineering|electrical engineering]] and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].

=== KEY: Fourier_transform_889b94
HEADWORD: Fourier transform
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fast Fourier transform | Gaussian function | Heat equation | Joseph Fourier | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Integral transform that converts a function of time or space into a function describing the frequencies present in it. The output is a complex-valued function of frequency, analogous to decomposing the sound of a musical chord into the intensities of its pitches. Functions localized in time have transforms spread across frequency, and vice versa, a relationship known as the uncertainty principle. The [[Gaussian function|Gaussian function]] is the critical case, and its Fourier transform is another Gaussian. [[Joseph Fourier|Joseph Fourier]] introduced sine and cosine transforms in his study of heat transfer, where Gaussians appear as solutions of the [[Heat equation|heat equation]]. The transform generalizes to functions of several variables and is central to the study of waves and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. The [[Fast Fourier transform|fast Fourier transform]] is an algorithm for computing the discrete Fourier transform.

=== KEY: Fractal_96ec52

ENTRY:
Geometric shape or set with detailed structure at arbitrarily small scales. Fractals are typically self-similar, with parts resembling the whole, and their fractal dimension strictly exceeds their topological dimension. === KEY: Fraction_ef9935
HEADWORD: Fraction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Integer | Latin | Rational number
ENTRY:
Number representing a part of a whole or, more generally, any number of equal parts. The word comes from [[Latin]] *fractus*, "broken." A simple fraction, such as 1/2 or 17/3, consists of an [[Integer|integer]] numerator written above a line (or before a slash) and a non-zero integer denominator written below (or after) it. For positive integers, the numerator counts equal parts and the denominator states how many such parts make up a whole. In 3/4, the numerator 3 means three equal parts, and the denominator 4 means that four parts make up a whole.

Fractions represent ratios and division: 3/4 can stand for the ratio 3:4 or for the division 3 ÷ 4. In mathematics, a [[Rational number|rational number]] is a number representable as a/b, where a and b are integers and b is not zero. The set of rational numbers is commonly written ℚ or Q, for "quotient."

English denominators are generally expressed as ordinal numbers, pluralized when the numerator is not 1, as in one-fifth and two-fifths; exceptions include half for denominator 2, quarter for 4, and percent for 100. Spelled-out fractions are hyphenated, so two-fifths is a single value, whereas two fifths means two separate instances of 1/5.

Babylonian mathematicians used a base-60 system around 1800 BC to express fractional parts. The Egyptians, in the Rhind Papyrus from approximately 1650 BC, represented fractions mainly as sums of distinct unit fractions, such as 2/3 = 1/2 + 1/6. Arab mathematicians introduced the horizontal fraction bar. Fractions are classed as proper (numerator less than denominator), improper (numerator greater than or equal to denominator), and mixed numbers.

=== KEY: Frame_of_reference_c0c73f
HEADWORD: Frame of reference
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Centrifugal force | Coordinate system | Coriolis force | General relativity | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Abstract [[Coordinate system|coordinate system]], with an origin, orientation and scale specified in physical space, used to describe position and motion. A frame is defined by reference points identified both mathematically, by coordinate values, and physically, by markers. An inertial frame is one in which Newton's first law holds: an object at rest stays at rest, and one moving at constant velocity continues so, unless a force acts on it. Non-inertial frames, such as one fixed to Earth's surface, require fictitious forces like the [[Coriolis force]] and [[Centrifugal force|centrifugal force]] to explain observations. According to [[Special relativity]], the laws of physics and the speed of light are the same in all inertial frames, so time belongs to a frame rather than being absolute. [[General relativity]] extended the framework to non-inertial frames by describing gravity as the curvature of spacetime.

Country primarily in Western Europe (pop., 2026: over 69.1 million), officially the French Republic. Capacity of agents to choose between possible courses of action, to exercise the control over their actions that moral responsibility requires, or to be the ultimate source of those actions. The central debate concerns whether and how such freedom can coexist with physical determinism, divine foreknowledge, and other constraints. Free will is closely linked to moral responsibility, praise, blame, and deliberation, since traditionally only freely willed actions are seen as deserving credit or blame.

The problem was identified in ancient Greek literature. A compatibilist notion of free will has been attributed to [[Aristotle]] and to Epictetus; according to Susanne Bobzien, an incompatibilist notion appears first in the works of Alexander of Aphrodisias in the 3rd century AD. The term *liberum arbitrium* was introduced by Christian philosophy in the 4th century AD and meant a lack of necessity in the human will.

[[Determinism]] holds that the natural world is governed by cause and effect, so that only one course of events is possible. Incompatibilists regard free will as inconsistent with it. Metaphysical libertarians argue that determinism is false and free will therefore possible; hard determinists argue that determinism is true and free will therefore impossible. Hard incompatibilists, or illusionists, hold that indeterminism is equally incompatible with free will.

Compatibilists hold that free will can coexist with determinism, though they define it differently. Classical compatibilists treated it as freedom of action: a person is free if, had they wanted to do otherwise, they could have done so without physical impediment. Many contemporary compatibilists treat it as a psychological capacity to direct one's behavior in a way that is responsive to reason.

Human right to practice, or not practice, a religion free from interference by government. Considered by most nations a fundamental human right, freedom of religion is protected by major conventions, including the [[International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights]]. It includes freedom of belief, covering [[Atheism|atheism]], [[Humanism|humanism]] and other forms of nonbelief, and some hold that it must also include freedom of practice. Courts often decide whether religious practices that break secular law should be permitted. In a country with a state religion, it generally means the government allows the practices of other faiths. The [[Statue of Liberty]] in New York, created to commemorate the abolition of slavery, later came to represent hope for religious refugees, and the [[Golden Temple]] in [[Amritsar]], India, symbolizes freedom of worship. In some countries, [[Apostasy|apostasy laws]] still punish religious conversion.
