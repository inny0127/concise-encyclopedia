=== KEY: Gothic_War_535_554_ca821f

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Byzantine–Gothic war in Italy and nearby islands, 535–554. It was the last of the Gothic wars against the [[Byzantine Empire]], and it aimed to recover former Western Roman provinces lost to invading tribes during the [[Migration Period]], under [[Justinian I]]. === KEY: Gothic_architecture_1a5879
HEADWORD: Gothic architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Castle | Giorgio Vasari | Goths | Paris | Renaissance architecture | Romanesque architecture | Île-de-France
ENTRY:
Architectural style prevalent in Europe from the late 12th to the 16th century. It evolved from [[Romanesque architecture]] and is characterized by pointed arches, rib vaults, flying buttresses and large, traceried stained-glass windows. It originated in the [[Île-de-France]] and Picardy regions of northern France, and the Abbey of Saint-Denis near [[Paris]] is considered the first Gothic building. [[Renaissance architecture|Renaissance architecture]] supplanted the style in Italy in the mid-15th century, but it flourished longer in England and what is now Belgium. Though especially associated with churches, it was also used for [[Castle|castles]], palaces, town halls, universities and houses. The term "Gothic" was first applied contemptuously during the later Renaissance, and [[Giorgio Vasari]] attributed its features to the [[Goths]].

=== KEY: Gothic_art_a69f64
HEADWORD: Gothic art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gothic architecture | Guild | New Testament | Old Testament | Romanesque art | Western Europe
ENTRY:
Medieval style of art, emerging in northern France in the early 12th century. It developed out of [[Romanesque art]] alongside [[Gothic architecture]] and spread across [[Western Europe]] and much of Central Europe, never quite effacing more classical styles in Italy. Primary media were sculpture, panel painting, stained glass, fresco and illuminated manuscripts. The earliest works were monumental sculptures on cathedral and abbey walls. Christian art was often typological, showing scenes from the [[Old Testament]] beside those of the [[New Testament]]. Images of the Virgin Mary became more human and affectionate. As cities, universities and trade grew, secular art flourished, and artists were often required to join [[Guild|guilds]], so more of them are known by name than in any earlier period.

=== KEY: Gout_660abd

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcohol (drug) | Ancient Egypt | Arthritis | Seafood
ENTRY:
Form of [[Arthritis|arthritis]] caused by excess uric acid in the blood. Gout causes recurrent attacks of a painful, red, hot, swollen joint, reaching maximum intensity in less than 12 hours. The base of the big toe is affected in about half of cases. Uric acid crystals deposit in joints, tendons and surrounding tissues, and risk rises with diets rich in meat and [[Seafood|seafood]], [[Alcohol (drug)|alcohol]] and sugar-sweetened drinks, and with obesity. Treatment uses nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids or colchicine, while allopurinol or probenecid provides long-term prevention for those with frequent attacks. Gout affects about 1–5% of adults in the developed world at some point, and older men are most commonly affected. Historically called "the disease of kings", it has been recognized since at least the time of [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egyptians]].

=== KEY: Government_18450d
HEADWORD: Government
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Autocracy | Democracy | Oligarchy | State (polity) | Sumer | Welfare state
ENTRY:
System or group of people that leads a [[State (polity)|state]]. A government is responsible for directing the only entity whose use of force is socially acceptable, and it is the major organizing power in modern societies. Cambridge Dictionary defines it as an organization that officially manages and controls a country or region, creating laws, collecting taxes, and providing public services. The word is often used of the approximately 200 independent national governments on Earth and of their subsidiary state, provincial, and local governments. It derives from the Greek verb *kubernáō*, meaning to steer with a rudder, a metaphor attested in classical antiquity, including Plato's Ship of State.

Governments have been dominated by one person ([[Autocracy|autocracy]]), a small ruling class ([[Oligarchy|oligarchy]]), or the masses ([[Democracy|democracy]]). The main types of modern political system are democracies, totalitarian regimes, and authoritarian regimes, with hybrid regimes and exceptions such as monarchies, which preserve pre-modern forms of traditional legitimacy. Other classifications distinguish unitary systems, which concentrate power centrally, from federal systems, which divide it between levels, and presidential, parliamentary, and semi-presidential systems by the relations between executive and legislature. Core functions include providing security, delivering justice, regulating economic activity, and supplying public goods such as infrastructure. In many systems, separation of powers into legislative, executive, and judicial branches serves to constrain arbitrary rule.

About 5,000 years ago the first small city-states appeared, and by the third to second millennia BC some had developed into larger governed areas: [[Sumer]], ancient Egypt, the Indus Valley civilization, and the Yellow River civilization. One explanation of their emergence is agriculture: since the Neolithic Revolution, food surpluses allowed people to specialize, including in ruling others. Another is the need to manage infrastructure such as water systems, though archaeological evidence also shows complex societies that were more egalitarian and decentralized.

From the end of the 17th century republican forms grew more prevalent. The English Civil War, the Glorious Revolution, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution contributed to the growth of representative government. The Soviet Union was the first large country with a Communist government, and since the fall of the Berlin Wall liberal democracy has become more prevalent. In the 19th and 20th centuries government grew greatly in size and scale at the national level, including the regulation of corporations and the development of the [[Welfare state|welfare state]].

Classifying governments is difficult because self-identification is not objective and official form can differ from practice. The Soviet Union was officially a federal socialist republic but in practice a centralized autocratic one-party state under Joseph Stalin. Voltaire argued that the Holy Roman Empire was "neither Holy, nor Roman, nor an Empire." Terms such as "conservatism" also mean different things in different countries. Government quality can be measured by the Government effectiveness index, which relates to political efficacy and state capacity.

Ancient Roman tribunes of the plebs, brothers known for social reform. His brother Gaius Gracchus, tribune in 122–121 BC, extended these agrarian reforms and started colonization outside Italy; his reforms were far broader, also touching on provincial commands and jury composition. === KEY: Grace_in_Christianity_cd3cb5
HEADWORD: Grace in Christianity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arminianism | Augustine of Hippo | Christianity | Eucharist | John Calvin | Reformation
ENTRY:
Concept in [[Christianity]] of God's unearned favor and gift. In Western belief, grace is God's favor and "a share in the divine life of God", which cannot be earned and manifests most in the salvation of sinners. In the Eastern Orthodox Church, grace is the uncreated energies of God. Catholics, Eastern Orthodox and Protestants agree that grace is a gift from God. The Catholic Church holds that the sacraments confer the grace they signify, and John Wesley called the [[Eucharist]] the "grand channel" through which grace reaches believers. Calvinists teach irresistible grace, which regenerates only those predestined to salvation, while [[Arminianism|Arminians]] see grace cooperating with free will. [[Augustine of Hippo]] defended the necessity of grace against Pelagianism in the fifth century, an emphasis that influenced [[John Calvin]] and the [[Reformation]].

=== KEY: Gradient_descent_f694b9

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
First-order iterative algorithm for minimizing a differentiable multivariate function. It takes repeated steps opposite to the [[Gradient|gradient]] at the current point, the direction of steepest descent; stepping along the gradient instead is gradient ascent. Generally attributed to [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]], who first suggested it in 1847, the method is widely used in [[Machine learning]] and [[Artificial intelligence]] to minimize cost or loss functions. Its stochastic extension is the most basic algorithm for training most [[Deep learning|deep networks]] today.

=== KEY: Grammar_d305bb
HEADWORD: Grammar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comparative linguistics | Linguistics | Morphology (linguistics) | Phonology | Pāṇini | Sanskrit | Semantics | Syntax
ENTRY:
System of rules governing how a natural language is structured and used. In [[Linguistics|linguistics]], the term also covers the study of such rules, which includes [[Phonology|phonology]], [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphology]], and [[Syntax|syntax]], together with phonetics, [[Semantics|semantics]], and pragmatics. Speakers internalize most rules by observing others, largely in early childhood. A descriptive grammar records constructions in detail, in contrast to prescription, which codifies some constructions while marginalizing others. Outside linguistics, the word may also include spelling and punctuation, or mean prescriptive norms only.

The word derives from the Greek *grammatikḕ téchnē*, "art of letters." The first systematic grammar of [[Sanskrit]] originated in Iron Age India with Yaska (6th century BC), [[Pāṇini]] (6th–5th century BC), and later commentators including Patanjali (2nd century BC). The oldest known grammar handbook is the *Art of Grammar* by Dionysius Thrax (c. 170 – c. 90 BC), which remained the primary Greek grammar textbook until the twelfth century AD and whose format underlies grammar guides in many languages. Latin grammar followed Greek models from the 1st century BC. Irish grammar originated in the 7th century, and Arabic grammar emerged with Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali in the same century.

In the Middle Ages grammar belonged to the trivium of the seven liberal arts. Antonio de Nebrija published the first Spanish grammar in 1492, and the first grammar of German appeared in 1578. From the latter part of the 18th century grammar became a subfield of modern linguistics. Franz Bopp's *Comparative Grammar* of 1833 was the starting point of modern [[Comparative linguistics|comparative linguistics]]. Theoretical approaches include generative grammar, which posits universal principles underlying diverse grammars, and functional approaches, which emphasize usage-based patterns; controversy persists over the relative contributions of innate capacities and emergent learning.

ENTRY:
[[Grammar|Grammatical]] category expressing time reference, usually marked on verbs. Tense places an action or state relative to the moment of speaking, typically as past, present or future. === KEY: Grassland_d4e3b7
HEADWORD: Grassland
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Biome | Cretaceous | Pleistocene | Poaceae | Prairie | Savanna
ENTRY:
Ecosystem or area where the vegetation is dominated by grasses of the family [[Poaceae]]. Sedges and rushes also occur, along with legumes such as clover and other herbs. Grasslands occur naturally on all continents except [[Antarctica]] and are among the largest [[Biome|biomes]] on Earth, covering 31–69% of its land area. They include natural, semi-natural, and agricultural grasslands, with subtypes such as tropical [[Savanna|savannas]] and temperate [[Prairie|prairies]].

Grasses became widespread toward the end of the [[Cretaceous]] period. The first true grasslands occurred around 5 million years ago, in the Late Miocene in the New World and the Pliocene in the Old World, as forest biomes declined. Grasslands have existed in Europe throughout the [[Pleistocene]], the last 1.8 million years.

Semi-natural grasslands first appeared when humans began farming and clearing forests in Europe. Their plant communities are natural, but they depend on grazing and mowing. They are among the most species-rich ecosystems in the world: one meadow in Estonia held 76 plant species in one square meter, and chalk downlands in England can support over 40 species per square meter. They also support a rich invertebrate fauna, pollinators, and bird specialists such as the snipe and the little bustard. Agriculturally improved grasslands are usually poor in wild plant species.

Most grassland has been converted to arable fields, and original North American prairie is now rare. Almost 90% of European semi-natural grasslands disappeared during the 20th century, for political and economic reasons. Overgrazing by livestock can lead to desertification in semi-arid zones, whereas rotational grazing and controlled burns can restore productivity and ecological function.

=== KEY: Gravitational_acceleration_666dd2

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Acceleration]] of an object in free fall within a vacuum, caused exclusively by gravitational attraction. === KEY: Gravitational_singularity_dbef66
HEADWORD: Gravitational singularity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Black hole | Event horizon | General relativity | Schwarzschild radius | Spacetime
ENTRY:
Theoretical condition in which gravity is predicted to be so intense that [[Spacetime|spacetime]] itself would break down. In [[General relativity|general relativity]], a singularity is defined by curvature becoming infinite or by an incomplete geodesic. The theory predicts that any object collapsing beyond its [[Schwarzschild radius]] forms a [[Black hole|black hole]], inside which a singularity forms; an [[Event horizon|event horizon]] covers it. === KEY: Gravitational_wave_ef77af
HEADWORD: Gravitational wave
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Binary star | Black hole | General relativity | Pulsar | Spacetime | Supernova
ENTRY:
Wave of [[Spacetime|spacetime]] curvature produced by the relative motion of gravitating masses, propagating at the speed of light. [[Albert Einstein]] first predicted them as a consequence of his [[General relativity|general theory of relativity]]. Hundreds have since been observed, first indirectly through [[Pulsar|binary-pulsar]] observations and, since 2015, directly by dedicated observatories. Newtonian gravity, by contrast, has no such waves, because it treats gravity as acting instantaneously everywhere. Unlike electromagnetic radiation, gravitational waves are not affected by intervening matter, so they can reveal [[Binary star|binary star]] systems, [[Supernova|supernovae]] and the early universe. The first direct detection came in September 2015, when detectors received a signal from the merger of two [[Black hole|black holes]]. Gravitational waves carry energy as gravitational radiation.

ENTRY:

=== KEY: Gravity_8a88c3
HEADWORD: Gravity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Aristotle | Black hole | General relativity | Mass | Newton's law of universal gravitation | Spacetime
ENTRY:
Fundamental interaction by which objects with [[Mass|mass]] or energy attract one another. The word comes from Latin *gravitas*, "weight." Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces but has infinite range, and it is the primary driver of large-scale structure in the universe: attraction between primordial hydrogen and dark matter caused gas to coalesce into stars, galaxies, and clusters. The ratio of the gravitational attraction of two electrons to their electrical repulsion is 1 to 4.17×10⁴², so gravity can generally be neglected at subatomic scales. It determines the motion of satellites, planets, stars, galaxies, and even light.

[[Newton's law of universal gravitation|Newton's law]], formulated in 1687, describes gravity as an attractive force between any two bodies, proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. It remains a sufficient approximation for most applications. [[Albert Einstein]] proposed [[General relativity|general relativity]] in 1915, which describes gravity as the curvature of [[Spacetime|spacetime]] caused by the uneven distribution of mass. The most extreme example is a [[Black hole|black hole]], from which nothing, not even light, can escape. Gravitational waves, predicted by the theory, were directly detected in 2015. Scientists seek a theory of quantum gravity that would unify gravity with the other fundamental interactions.

On Earth, gravity gives weight to objects and contributes to lunar tides and weather patterns. In ancient Greece, [[Aristotle]] held that each classical element had a natural place in the universe and that a falling object's speed increases with its weight, a conclusion later shown to be false. In 628 CE the Indian astronomer Brahmagupta proposed that gravity is an attractive force that draws objects to the Earth.

=== KEY: Gravity_assist_c82f29
HEADWORD: Gravity assist
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomical object | Jupiter | Momentum | Saturn
ENTRY:
Spaceflight flyby that uses the gravity of a planet or other [[Astronomical object|astronomical object]] to alter a spacecraft's path and speed. Typically used to save propellant and reduce expense, the maneuver can accelerate or decelerate a spacecraft or redirect it. The first use came in 1959, when the Soviet probe Luna 3 photographed the far side of Earth's Moon. Interplanetary probes have used it from Mariner 10 onward, and the two Voyager probes made notable flybys of [[Jupiter]] and [[Saturn]]. [[Momentum]] gained by the spacecraft is matched by a loss to the body, in line with Newton's Third Law, but for a planet the change in speed is negligibly small. By minimizing reliance on chemical rockets, gravity assists have made ambitious deep-space missions feasible.

=== KEY: Great_Depression_7ffbce
HEADWORD: Great Depression
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolf Hitler | Dust Bowl | Federal Reserve | Franklin D. Roosevelt | Herbert Hoover | New Deal | Wall Street crash of 1929 | World War II
ENTRY:
Severe global economic downturn lasting from 1929 to 1939. It was marked by high unemployment and poverty, drastic reductions in industrial production and international trade, and widespread bank and business failures. It began in the United States with the [[Wall Street crash of 1929]], often considered its beginning. The U.S., the U.K., and Germany were among the countries with the most unemployed.

The Depression was preceded by the "Roaring Twenties," a boom in which much profit went into speculation and banks faced minimal regulation. After the October 1929 crash, share values slid until July 1932. By 1933 U.S. unemployment had risen to 25%, about one-third of farmers had lost their land, and 9,000 of its 25,000 banks had failed. President [[Herbert Hoover]] was unwilling to intervene heavily in the economy, and in 1930 he signed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act, which inadvertently worsened the Depression. In 1932 he was defeated by [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]], who from 1933 pursued expansive [[New Deal]] programs of relief and job creation. A severe drought also struck the agricultural heartland, as in the [[Dust Bowl]].

Between 1929 and 1932, worldwide gross domestic product fell by an estimated 15%, and U.S. GDP contracted by 30%. International trade fell by more than 50%, and unemployment in some countries rose as high as 33%. In Germany, which depended heavily on U.S. loans, unemployment rose to nearly 30% and political extremism grew, paving the way for [[Adolf Hitler]]'s Nazi Party to take power in 1933. The outbreak of [[World War II]] in 1939 ended the Depression by stimulating factory production.

The causes remain disputed. Some historians emphasize non-monetary economic causes, regarding the crash either as the main cause or as a symptom of broader trends. Others hold that failures of monetary policy, including the [[Federal Reserve]]'s inadequate response to liquidity crises and bank failures, explain the downturn's severity and length.

Its borders cannot be exactly determined, but it reached its largest extent under Prince Svatopluk I, who ruled from 870 to 894. The realm saw the rise of the first Slavic literary culture, in Old Church Slavonic. Missionaries [[Cyril and Methodius]] arrived in 863, introducing the Glagolitic alphabet, the first alphabet devoted to a Slavic language, and Slavonic liturgy, which Pope Adrian II later approved. Separatism and internal conflicts after Svatopluk's death contributed to the state's fall. The [[Hungary|Hungarians]] overran it, and its collapse occurred between 902 and 907.

=== KEY: Great_Northern_War_8f4f56
HEADWORD: Great Northern War
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Conflict between Sweden and a coalition led by Russia, 1700–1721. The allies were Peter I of Russia ([[Peter the Great]]), Frederick IV of Denmark–Norway and Augustus II the Strong of Saxony, who opposed Sweden under [[Charles XII of Sweden|Charles XII]]. Sweden parried the Danes at Travendal (August 1700) and the Russians at Narva (November 1700). Russia recovered and founded [[Saint Petersburg]] in 1703 to secure access to the Baltic Sea. Charles XII's main army was destroyed at the Battle of Poltava in 1709, after which the coalition revived and Hanover and Prussia joined it. Charles XII was killed in the trenches before Fredriksten during a campaign against Norway. The Treaty of Nystad (1721) ended the war, with Sweden losing its Baltic provinces and Russia becoming the dominant power in the Baltic region. Sweden's absolute monarchy had ended with Charles XII's death, and its Age of Liberty began.

=== KEY: Great_Oxidation_Event_46e723
HEADWORD: Great Oxidation Event
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Cyanobacteria | Mitochondria | Oxygen
ENTRY:
Paleoproterozoic rise in free [[Oxygen|oxygen]] in Earth's atmosphere and shallow seas. The event began about 2.46–2.426 billion years ago and ended around 2.06 billion years ago. It is attributed to [[Cyanobacteria]], which evolved chlorophyll-based photosynthesis that released oxygen as a byproduct. The atmosphere changed from a weakly reducing state, practically devoid of oxygen, to an oxidizing one containing persistent free oxygen. Oxygen levels stayed below 1% to as high as about 10% of the present atmospheric level (PAL) for most of the Proterozoic. Free oxygen can oxidize organic compounds, and the event may have caused the extinction of many early, mostly anaerobic organisms. Symbiosis between surviving [[Archaea|archaea]] and aerobic proteobacteria, which became [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]], may have led to the rise of eukaryotic life.
