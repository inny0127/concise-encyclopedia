LINK TARGETS: Antimatter | Dirac equation | Erwin Schrödinger | Quantum electrodynamics | Quantum mechanics | Special relativity | University of Cambridge
ENTRY:
English theoretical physicist and a founder of [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. In 1928 he formulated the [[Dirac equation]], which combined [[Special relativity|special relativity]] with quantum mechanics and predicted the existence of [[Antimatter|antimatter]]. He shared the 1933 Nobel Prize in Physics with [[Erwin Schrödinger]] for new productive forms of atomic theory. He laid foundations for [[Quantum electrodynamics|quantum electrodynamics]] and quantum field theory, and contributed to Fermi–Dirac statistics, which describes fermions. He was Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at the [[University of Cambridge|University of Cambridge]] from 1932 to 1969, and a professor at Florida State University from 1970 to 1984. His 1930 monograph *The Principles of Quantum Mechanics* is among the most influential texts on the subject.

=== KEY: Pauli_exclusion_principle_788ee0
HEADWORD: Pauli exclusion principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Periodic table | Photon | Wolfgang Pauli
ENTRY:
Quantum mechanics principle barring identical fermions from sharing a quantum state. Formulated by Austrian physicist [[Wolfgang Pauli]] in 1925 for electrons, and extended to all fermions through his spin–statistics theorem of 1940, it holds that identical particles with half-integer spin cannot occupy the same quantum state. For electrons in an atom, no two can have the same values of all four quantum numbers: principal, azimuthal, magnetic and spin. This restriction explains how electron shells fill and underlies the structure of the [[Periodic table]], which limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins. It also underpins the stability of everyday matter. Bosons, particles with integer spin such as [[Photon|photons]], are not subject to the principle.

=== KEY: Pauli_matrices_115b36

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Set of three 2×2 complex matrices used in quantum mechanics. Named after physicist [[Wolfgang Pauli]], they are traceless, Hermitian, involutory and unitary, and are usually denoted by σ (sigma). === KEY: Peace_cc2073
HEADWORD: Peace
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Christianity | Immanuel Kant | Islam | Mahatma Gandhi | Martin Luther King Jr. | Tacitus | United Nations
ENTRY:
State of harmony in the absence of hostility and violence. In a societal sense, the term commonly means a lack of conflict and freedom from fear of violence between individuals or groups. Promotion of peace is a core tenet of many philosophies, religions, and ideologies, including [[Buddhism]], [[Christianity]], Jainism, and [[Islam]]; of figures such as [[Mahatma Gandhi]] and [[Martin Luther King Jr.]]; of writings such as [[Immanuel Kant]]'s *Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch*; and of pacifism. No universal definition exists; the meaning of peace and its symbols varies with culture, context, and history.

The English word comes from Anglo-French *pes* and Old French *pais*, from Latin *pax*. From c. 1300 it was used in greetings as a translation of the Hebrew *shalom*.

Peace has been forged through diplomacy, as in royal marriages between Greek princesses and the kings of Phrygia and Lydia around 800 BC and 600 BC. It has also been enforced by victors through violence and coercion. In *Agricola*, [[Tacitus]] attributes to the Caledonian chieftain Calgacus the charge that the Romans, "where they make a desert, they call it peace." Societal peace can be understood as the simple absence of war, or as absence of war accompanied by justice, mutual respect, respect for law, and good will. Scholars likewise distinguish negative peace, the mere cessation of direct violence, from positive peace, which includes equitable institutions and social justice. Debate continues over whether pacifist ideals suffice or whether military readiness and balances of power better secure stability, and the democratic peace theory faces scrutiny over its causal claims. Since 1945 the [[United Nations]] and its Security Council have operated under the aim of resolving conflicts without war, though nations have entered numerous military conflicts since.

Hard, shiny object produced within the mantle of a living shelled mollusk. Pearls are composed of [[Calcium carbonate|calcium carbonate]], mainly aragonite, deposited in concentric layers. === KEY: Peat_44f4fa

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Accumulation of partially decayed vegetation or organic matter, formed in waterlogged wetlands. Peat forms when plant material does not fully decay in acidic, oxygen-poor conditions, and [[Sphagnum]] moss is among its most common components. It accumulates at about a millimeter a year, and its deposits preserve pollen and plant remains that record past vegetation and climate. Peatlands cover 3.7 million square kilometers and store up to 550 gigatonnes of carbon, about 42% of soil carbon, although they cover just 3% of the land's surface. Peat is often the first step in the formation of [[Coal|coal]]. Burning and draining peat have released large amounts of [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] into the atmosphere.

=== KEY: Pedosphere_f1a334
HEADWORD: Pedosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biosphere | Hydrosphere | Lithosphere | Soil | Weathering
ENTRY:
Outermost layer of the Earth, made up of [[Soil|soil]]. The name comes from Ancient Greek words for "ground" and "sphere." The pedosphere lies at the interface of the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]], atmosphere, [[Hydrosphere|hydrosphere]] and [[Biosphere|biosphere]], and it is the foundation of terrestrial ecosystems. It mediates the chemical and biogeochemical flows between these systems. Soil formation begins when [[Weathering|weathering]] breaks down rock; biology speeds the process, with lichens, mosses and seed-bearing plants among the pioneers, and their secreted acids help break rock apart. The soil layer is typically 1–2 meters thick and holds approximately 2,500 gigatons of organic carbon, over three times the atmospheric amount. The term was coined in 1938 by Swedish soil chemist Sante Mattson.

=== KEY: Perception_da1691
HEADWORD: Perception
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Attention | Brain | Learning | Memory | Nervous system | Psychology
ENTRY:
Identification, interpretation, and organization of sensory information in order to represent and understand the environment. The term comes from Latin *perceptio* ('gathering, receiving'). All perception involves signals passing through the [[Nervous system|nervous system]], resulting from physical or chemical stimulation of the sensory system: vision involves light striking the retina, smell is mediated by odor molecules, and hearing involves pressure waves. It is not passive receipt of these signals but is shaped by the perceiver's [[Learning|learning]], [[Memory|memory]], expectation, and [[Attention|attention]].

The process begins with a distal stimulus, an object in the real world, which stimulates the sensory organs. These transform the input energy into neural activity, called transduction, and the raw pattern is the proximal stimulus. Once transmitted to the [[Brain|brain]] and processed, it yields the percept, the mental re-creation of the distal stimulus.

Perception depends on complex nervous system functions but seems effortless because the processing happens outside conscious awareness. Since the rise of experimental [[Psychology|psychology]] in the 19th century, understanding has advanced through psychophysics, sensory neuroscience, and computational study. Illusions and ambiguous images show that perceptual systems actively and pre-consciously try to make sense of their input. Debate continues over whether perception is an active process of hypothesis testing, analogous to science, or whether sensory information is rich enough to make this unnecessary. Brains are modular, with different areas processing different kinds of sensory information, often in sensory maps, and the modules influence each other; taste, for instance, is strongly influenced by smell. Philosophers ask to what extent qualities such as sound, smell, or color exist in objective reality rather than in the mind of the perceiver.

=== KEY: Performing_arts_0e20e3
HEADWORD: Performing arts
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Dance | Isadora Duncan | Kabuki | Konstantin Stanislavski | Music | Noh | Opera
ENTRY:
Arts such as [[Music|music]], [[Dance|dance]], and drama performed for an audience, distinguished from the visual arts, which produce physical or static objects. They are performed in purpose-built buildings such as theaters and opera houses, in open-air festivals and street settings, and in recorded formats such as film and television. Traditions exist in every society. Music and dance date to prehistoric times, while theatrical forms appear in [[Ancient Greece]], India, and China, serving combinations of religious, ceremonial, and entertainment functions. Japanese [[Noh]] and [[Kabuki]] and Indian classical dance have been recognized by UNESCO as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Theater acts out stories before an audience through speech, gesture, music, dance, and spectacle, and is linked to ritual in ancient cultures. Its most familiar form is the scripted play; it has also diversified into musicals, which integrate song and dance, and [[Opera|opera]], in which all the words are set to music. In improvisational theater actors invent material instead of following a script. Dance is human movement, typically rhythmic and to music, ranging from functional forms such as folk dance to codified techniques such as ballet; choreography is the art of composing dances. Modern dance emerged in the 20th century as a response to the strictures of ballet. Music organizes sound using timbre, pitch, rhythm, and dynamics and is divided into genres such as folk, jazz, hip-hop, pop, and rock. Opera originated in Italy at the end of the 16th century and became the prestige entertainment of aristocratic and later bourgeois society. The field also includes magic, mime, spoken word, puppetry, performance art, and stand-up comedy.

As early as the 19th century BC, a large religious ceremony in Egypt may have had theater-like elements; the Dramatic Ramesseum Papyrus has been interpreted as an early example of dramatic literature, although its classification remains debated. Greek playwrights including Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides developed tragedy in the 5th century BC, while Aristophanes, Cratinus, and Menander developed comedy. In India the *Natya Shastra*, a Sanskrit treatise on dramaturgy attributed to Bharata and possibly compiled 200 BC–AD 200, formulated theatrical theory. In China the dramatic tradition goes back to the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BC). Shadow puppetry emerged under the Han (202 BC–AD 220), Emperor Xuanzong established the Pear Garden to train performers under the Tang (618–907), and the Zaju variety play peaked under the Yuan (1271–1368), with Beijing opera as its descendant.

Western performing arts history spans Greek tragedy and comedy, medieval mystery plays, the emergence of opera and ballet in the Renaissance, and the Romantic expansion of grand opera. In the modernist revolutions of the early 20th century, [[Isadora Duncan]], [[Konstantin Stanislavski]], and Sergei Diaghilev reworked the principles of dance and theater. Audio and video recording has allowed private consumption of the performing arts.

=== KEY: Periodic_table_a093c7
HEADWORD: Periodic table
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic number | Chemical element | Dmitri Mendeleev | Electron configuration | Isotope | Quantum mechanics | Synthetic element
ENTRY:
Ordered arrangement of the [[Chemical element|chemical elements]] into rows ("periods") and columns ("groups"). Elements in the same group tend to show similar chemical characteristics, and the table is also divided into four roughly rectangular blocks. It depicts the periodic law, which states that when elements are arranged by their [[Atomic number|atomic numbers]], an approximate recurrence of their properties is evident.

The first generally accepted table was that of the Russian chemist [[Dmitri Mendeleev]] in 1869, who formulated the periodic law in terms of atomic mass. He left gaps for elements not yet known and successfully used the law to predict properties of some of them. The law was explained early in the 20th century by the discovery of atomic numbers and by pioneering work in [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. A recognizably modern form was reached in 1945 with Glenn T. Seaborg's discovery that the actinides are f-block rather than d-block elements.

Each element has a unique atomic number, the number of protons in its nucleus. Atoms with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons are [[Isotope|isotopes]] of the same element and are grouped together in the table. A new row begins when a new electron shell receives its first electron, and columns are determined by [[Electron configuration|electron configuration]]; oxygen, sulfur, and selenium share a column because each has four electrons in the outermost p-subshell. Of the 118 known elements, the first 94 occur naturally, 83 of them primordial and 11 only in decay chains; the remaining 24, americium to oganesson (95–118), are [[Synthetic element|synthetic]]. By 2010 all 118 were known, completing the first seven rows.

ENTRY:
Part of the nervous system outside the brain and spinal cord, made of nerves and ganglia. The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is one of two components of the nervous system of bilateral animals, the other being the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]] (CNS). Its main function is to connect the CNS to the limbs and organs, relaying signals between the brain and spinal cord and the rest of the body. === KEY: Permian_Triassic_extinction_event_5912ca
HEADWORD: Permian–Triassic extinction event
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Methane | Permian | Siberian Traps | Triassic
ENTRY:
Mass extinction at the boundary of the [[Permian]] and [[Triassic]] periods, about 251.902 million years ago. It is Earth's most severe known extinction event, with 57% of biological families, 62% of genera, 81% of marine species and 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species lost. It is also the greatest known mass extinction of insects, and is called the Great Dying. The scientific consensus is that the main cause was the flood basalt eruptions that formed the [[Siberian Traps]], which released sulfur dioxide and [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]]. The result was euxinia (oxygen-starved, sulfurous oceans), elevated temperatures and acidified oceans. Atmospheric carbon dioxide rose from around 400 ppm to 2,500 ppm. Other proposed factors include [[Methane|methane]] released from methane clathrates and an extraterrestrial impact, though the chronology and mechanisms remain debated.

Geologic period and last division of the Paleozoic Era, spanning 47 million years. Sir Roderick Murchison introduced the name in 1841, after the Russian region of Perm. The period saw the diversification of synapsids and sauropsids (reptiles). On land, ecosystems took about 30 million years to recover.
