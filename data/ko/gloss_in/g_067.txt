International police organization that facilitates worldwide police cooperation and crime control. It is headquartered in [[Lyon]], [[France]], with a National Central Bureau in each of its 196 member states. Founded in [[Vienna]] on September 7, 1923, as the International Criminal Police Commission, it came under Nazi control in 1938, and its headquarters was moved to the Berlin building housing the [[Gestapo]]. === KEY: Intersection_set_theory_327593
HEADWORD: Intersection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: De Morgan's laws | Empty set | Geometry | Point (geometry) | Set (mathematics) | Set theory | Union (set theory)
ENTRY:
Set containing the elements common to all of some [[Set (mathematics)|sets]]. In [[Set theory|set theory]], the intersection of two sets A and B, written A∩B, is the set of all objects that are members of both. It generalizes to any nonempty collection of sets. For example, {1,2,3}∩{2,3,4} = {2,3}, and {1,2,3}∩{4,5,6} is the [[Empty set|empty set]]. Two sets are disjoint when their intersection is empty. The operation is associative, commutative and idempotent, and it distributes over [[Union (set theory)|union]]. By [[De Morgan's laws]], A∩B equals the complement of the union of the complements of A and B. The concept was generalized from [[Geometry|geometry]], where two distinct non-parallel lines in a plane intersect in a single [[Point (geometry)|point]].

=== KEY: Intersex_a4d3f9
HEADWORD: Intersex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chromosome | Discrimination | Human rights
ENTRY:
Congenital variation in sex characteristics, such as [[Chromosome|chromosomes]], gonads or genitals, that does not fit typical binary notions of male or female bodies. Sex assignment at birth usually aligns with external genitalia, and births with ambiguous genitals fall in the range of 1:4,500–1:2,000 (0.02%–0.05%). Estimates of the intersex population range from 0.018% (one in 5,500 births) to 1.7%, depending on which conditions are included. Historically, intersex people were called "hermaphrodites" or "congenital eunuchs," terms now considered misleading and stigmatizing. Intersex people face [[Discrimination|discrimination]] from birth or after traits are discovered at puberty. Some infants have been surgically or hormonally altered to create more socially acceptable sex characteristics, a practice considered controversial, with no firm evidence of favorable outcomes. Increasingly, such issues are considered [[Human rights|human rights]] abuses. In April 2015, Malta became the first country to outlaw non-consensual medical interventions to modify sex anatomy.

=== KEY: Interstellar_medium_0abf5b

ENTRY:
Matter and radiation filling the space between the [[Star system|star systems]] in a [[Galaxy|galaxy]]. It includes gas in ionic, atomic and molecular forms, as well as dust and cosmic rays. Within the Milky Way, by mass, about 99% of it is gas and 1% dust, and the gas is about 70% hydrogen, 28% helium and 1.5% heavier elements. It exists in several phases distinguished by temperature and density, and magnetic fields and turbulence provide pressure that is typically more important than thermal pressure. Stars form in its densest regions and return matter through [[Planetary nebula|planetary nebulae]], stellar winds and [[Supernova|supernovae]]. [[Voyager 1]] reached it on August 25, 2012, the first artificial object from Earth to do so, and [[Voyager 2]] entered it on November 5, 2018.

=== KEY: Intertropical_Convergence_Zone_00ffab
HEADWORD: Intertropical Convergence Zone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equator | Hadley cell | Monsoon | Trade winds
ENTRY:
Band of thunderstorms where the northeast and southeast [[Trade winds|trade winds]] converge near the [[Equator]]. Sailors have called it the doldrums for its monotonous, windless weather. It encircles Earth near the thermal equator and shifts seasonally. Its north–south migration largely follows the Sun over oceans, but over continents its position can be decoupled from the region of maximum rainfall. Its convective thunderstorms, driven by solar heating, form the ascending branch of the [[Hadley cell]] and produce a significant portion of global tropical rainfall. Shifts in its position influence the onset and intensity of [[Monsoon|monsoons]] and drought.

=== KEY: Introspection_9eca6e
HEADWORD: Introspection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Behaviorism | Epistemology | John B. Watson | Plato | Psychology | Wilhelm Wundt
ENTRY:
Examination of one's own conscious thoughts and feelings. In [[Psychology|psychology]], it relies on observing one's own mental states and is generally considered to give privileged access to those states, not mediated by other sources of knowledge. Philosophers have discussed it since antiquity; [[Plato]] urged calmly reviewing one's own thoughts. In [[Epistemology|epistemology]], it is compared with perception, reason, memory and testimony as a source of knowledge. [[Wilhelm Wundt]], the father of experimental psychology, used controlled introspection in his laboratory at the University of Leipzig and set strict procedures so that experiments could be replicated. Its use declined with the rise of [[Behaviorism]], championed by [[John B. Watson]], who dismissed it as unverifiable. Modern forms such as think-aloud protocols persist, though researchers recognize its limits, including retrospective bias.

=== KEY: Invasion_bd1a66
HEADWORD: Invasion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aggression | Great Wall of China | Hadrian's Wall | Napoleon | Russia
ENTRY:
Military offensive by which one polity forcibly enters the territory of another, usually with large numbers of combatants. Its foremost tactical objective is normally to occupy part or all of the invaded territory. Motives have included conquest and annexation, self-defense if the invader was attacked first, liberation, and toppling or altering a government. Today an invasion without prior attack is widely considered an international crime and condemned as [[Aggression|aggression]]. Invasions have been frequent since prehistory, and states with hostile neighbors have built defenses such as rivers, marshes, fortifications and barriers, including the [[Great Wall of China]] and [[Hadrian's Wall]]. Large static defenses became largely obsolete in modern times, as the failure of the Maginot Line at the start of World War II showed. [[Napoleon]]'s 1812 campaign into [[Russia]] illustrates the perils of overextension.

=== KEY: Inverse_function_6de390
HEADWORD: Inverse function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Function composition | Logarithm | Mathematics
ENTRY:
Function reversing the effect of another function. In [[Mathematics|mathematics]], the inverse of a function f, written f⁻¹, exists if and only if f is injective (one-to-one). For a function f: X→Y, its inverse sends each element y in Y to the unique element x in X such that f(x) = y. For example, f(x) = 5x − 7 multiplies its input by 5 and then subtracts 7, so its inverse adds 7 to the output and divides the result by 5. The notation f⁻¹ was introduced by John Frederick William Herschel in 1813. Using [[Function composition|composition of functions]], f⁻¹(f(x)) = x for every x in the domain. The function f(x) = x² on all real numbers is not injective and has no inverse, but restricted to nonnegative reals it is invertible. The exponential function and the [[Logarithm|natural logarithm]] are inverses of each other.

=== KEY: Inverse_square_law_b92c16
HEADWORD: Inverse-square law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Light | Newton's law of universal gravitation | Radar | Sound | Waveguide
ENTRY:
Physical law of intensity inversely proportional to the square of distance from a source. The law generally applies when a force, energy or other conserved quantity spreads evenly outward from a point source in three-dimensional space, because the surface area of a sphere grows with the square of its radius. [[Newton's law of universal gravitation]] follows the law, as do electricity, [[Light|light]], [[Sound|sound]] and radiation. For [[Radar|radar]], energy returns as the inverse fourth power of range, because it spreads on both the transmitted and reflected paths. A [[Waveguide|waveguide]] limits the spreading of a signal, much as a canal confines water.

Inverses of the trigonometric functions, defined on restricted domains. === KEY: Invertebrate_d21e39
HEADWORD: Invertebrate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Annelid | Arthropod | Cnidaria | Mollusca | Sponge | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Animal lacking a vertebral column, or backbone. The term is a paraphyletic grouping of all animals other than the chordate subphylum [[Vertebrate|Vertebrata]]. Most invertebrates belong to the clade Protostomia, including [[Arthropod|arthropods]], [[Mollusca|molluscs]], [[Annelid|annelids]] and flatworms. Echinoderms and tunicates are deuterostomes, while [[Cnidaria|cnidarians]] and [[Sponge|sponges]] lie outside the protostome–deuterostome split. Invertebrates make up about 97% of animal species by one estimate, and over 95% of described species according to IUCN figures. They range in size from 10 μm myxozoans to the 9–10 m colossal squid. === KEY: Investiture_Controversy_63a4c9
HEADWORD: Investiture Controversy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Henry I of England | Pope | Pope Gregory VII
ENTRY:
Medieval conflict between church and state over the appointment of bishops, abbots and [[Pope|popes]] (1076–1122). It began in 1076 as a power struggle between [[Pope Gregory VII]] and Henry IV, then king and later Holy Roman Emperor, and lasted nearly 50 years. Under the Concordat of Worms, agreed in 1122 by Pope Callixtus II and Emperor Henry V, bishops swore an oath of fealty to the secular monarch, who held authority "by the lance" but left selection to the church. A shorter struggle between Pope Paschal II and [[Henry I of England|Henry I]] of England, from 1103 to 1107, ended with a similar Concordat of London.

=== KEY: Io_moon_6260a8
HEADWORD: Io
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Europa (moon) | Galilean moons | Galileo Galilei | Ganymede (moon) | Jupiter | Mount Everest
ENTRY:
Innermost and second smallest of the four [[Galilean moons]] of [[Jupiter]]. Slightly larger than Earth's Moon, Io is the fourth-largest natural satellite in the Solar System. With over 400 active volcanoes, it is the most geologically active object in the Solar System. Tidal heating from friction, generated as Jupiter pulls Io between [[Europa (moon)|Europa]] and [[Ganymede (moon)|Ganymede]], drives this activity. Volcanic plumes of sulfur and sulfur dioxide rise as high as 500 km above the surface. More than 100 mountains rise on its surface, some taller than [[Mount Everest]]. Unlike most outer moons, which are mostly water ice, Io is mainly silicate rock around a molten iron or iron sulfide core. It was discovered in 1610 by [[Galileo Galilei]] and named after a priestess of Hera who became one of Zeus's lovers. The Voyager spacecraft revealed it as geologically active in 1979.

=== KEY: Iodine_478ee9
HEADWORD: Iodine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acetic acid | Halogen | Thyroid
ENTRY:
Chemical element with symbol I and atomic number 53. The heaviest of the stable [[Halogen|halogens]], iodine is a semi-lustrous, nonmetallic solid under standard conditions. It melts to a deep violet liquid at 114 °C (237 °F) and boils to a violet gas at 184 °C (363 °F). French chemist Bernard Courtois discovered it in 1811 while investigating corrosion of copper vessels used to process seaweed ash for saltpeter. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac named it two years later, after the Ancient Greek word for violet. Iodine is required for the synthesis of [[Thyroid|thyroid]] hormones, and its deficiency affects about two billion people, making it the leading preventable cause of intellectual disabilities. Chile and Japan are the dominant producers today. Radioactive isotopes of iodine treat thyroid cancer, and iodine is used as a catalyst in producing [[Acetic acid|acetic acid]]. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

=== KEY: Ion_e0befa
HEADWORD: Ion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical bond | Chloride | Electron | Michael Faraday | Potassium | Proton | Salt (chemistry) | Svante Arrhenius
ENTRY:
Atom or molecule with a net electrical charge. The net charge is nonzero because the atom or molecule has a different number of [[Electron|electrons]] than [[Proton|protons]]. A cation is positively charged, with fewer electrons than protons, as in the [[Potassium|potassium]] ion K⁺. An anion is negatively charged, with more electrons, as in the [[Chloride|chloride]] ion Cl⁻ and the hydroxide ion OH⁻. Opposite charges attract, so cations and anions readily form [[Chemical bond|ionic compounds]]. An ion of a single atom is monatomic; one of two or more atoms is polyatomic. The term was coined by [[Michael Faraday]] in 1834, from the Greek *ienai*, meaning to go. [[Svante Arrhenius|Svante Arrhenius]] proposed in his 1884 dissertation that [[Salt (chemistry)|salts]] split into charged particles when dissolved, and he won the 1903 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

=== KEY: Ionic_bonding_ab305b
HEADWORD: Ionic bonding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | Electronegativity | Ion | Metal | Nonmetal | Sodium chloride | Water
ENTRY:
Type of chemical bonding based on electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. It results from the transfer of [[Electron|electrons]] from a [[Metal|metal]] to a [[Nonmetal|nonmetal]], which gives both atoms full valence shells. The metal becomes a positively charged [[Ion|cation]] and the nonmetal a negatively charged anion. Clean transfer never occurs, since all ionic compounds have some covalent character. The term applies when ionic character exceeds covalent character, which requires a large difference in [[Electronegativity|electronegativity]] between the atoms. Ionic compounds conduct electricity when molten or dissolved but usually not when solid. They generally have high melting points, which rise with the charge of the ions, and they tend to dissolve in [[Water|water]]. The classic example is [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]], in which a sodium atom gives its electron to a chlorine atom.

=== KEY: Ionization_63dd02
HEADWORD: Ionization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aurora | Electromagnetic radiation | Electron | Ion | Mass spectrometry
ENTRY:
Process by which an atom or molecule acquires a negative or positive charge by gaining or losing [[Electron|electrons]]. The resulting charged particle is an [[Ion|ion]]. Ionization can follow the loss of an electron after collisions with subatomic particles, with other atoms or molecules, or through interaction with [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]]. It can also occur through heterolytic bond cleavage, which produces ion pairs, and in radioactive decay, when an excited nucleus transfers its energy to an inner-shell electron that is ejected. Thermal, photo, impact and field ionization are among its mechanisms. The energy needed, called the ionization energy, varies by element and electron shell. Ionization is central to plasma physics, [[Aurora|auroral]] phenomena and [[Mass spectrometry|mass spectrometry]], a technique in analytical chemistry for identifying molecules.

=== KEY: Ionizing_radiation_f41fb7
HEADWORD: Ionizing radiation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acute radiation syndrome | Cosmic ray | Electronvolt | Gamma ray | Ultraviolet | X-ray
ENTRY:
Subatomic particles or electromagnetic waves with enough energy to ionize atoms or molecules. Ionizing radiation starts around 10 [[Electronvolt|electronvolts]] (eV). [[Gamma ray|Gamma rays]], [[X-ray|X-rays]] and the higher-energy part of the [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] spectrum are ionizing, while lower-energy ultraviolet, visible light, infrared, microwaves and radio waves are not. Ionizing particles include alpha particles, beta particles and neutrons, which radioactive decay produces. [[Cosmic ray|Cosmic rays]] and radioactive decay are the main natural sources, and artificial sources include X-ray tubes, particle accelerators and nuclear fission. Because human senses cannot detect it, instruments such as Geiger counters measure it. High acute doses cause radiation burns and [[Acute radiation syndrome|radiation sickness]], while lower doses over long periods can cause cancer. Used in medicine, nuclear power and industry, it is a health hazard without proper measures against excessive exposure. The International Commission on Radiological Protection issues guidance on protection.

Iran is home to one of the world's oldest continuous major civilizations. The territory was first unified under the Medes in the 7th century BC and reached its height in the 6th century BC, when [[Cyrus the Great]] founded the [[Achaemenid Empire]]. Alexander the Great conquered the empire in the 4th century BC; the Parthian and Sasanian empires followed. Islamization came after the 7th-century Muslim conquest. In the 16th century the [[Safavid dynasty]] re-established a unified state with Twelver Shia Islam as the official religion. The Pahlavi dynasty was founded in 1925 by Reza Shah, and attempts by Mohammad Mosaddegh to nationalize the oil industry led to the Anglo-American coup in 1953.

The [[Iranian Revolution]] of 1979 overthrew the monarchy, and [[Ruhollah Khomeini]] became the first supreme leader of the Islamic Republic. Iraq invaded in 1980, beginning the eight-year [[Iran–Iraq War]].

Iran is a theocratic Islamic republic in which ultimate authority rests with the supreme leader; key offices, including head of state and the military, are not subject to public vote. The government has been widely criticized internationally for restrictions on assembly, expression, and the press and for its treatment of women, ethnic minorities, and dissidents. The economy is centrally planned with significant state ownership, and large fossil fuel reserves make Iran a regional power. Its nuclear program is among the most scrutinized in the world.

Israeli strikes in 2025 escalated into the Twelve-Day War. In 2026 the United States and Israel launched a war with Iran in which supreme leader Ali Khamenei was killed; his son Mojtaba Khamenei succeeded him.

=== KEY: Iron_Age_a0eae7
HEADWORD: Iron Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Anatolia | Bronze Age | Late Bronze Age collapse
ENTRY:
Final epoch of the three-age system of technology, following the Copper Age and the [[Bronze Age]], defined by smelted iron, especially steel tools and weapons, replacing bronze equivalents in common use. Its start is fixed locally by archaeological convention, a notion now being challenged.

In [[Anatolia]] and the Caucasus, or Southeast Europe, the Iron Age began c. 1300 BC. In the ancient Near East it coincided with the [[Late Bronze Age collapse]] in the 12th century BC. The technology spread through the Mediterranean basin and to South Asia between the 12th and 11th centuries BC, and reached Northern Europe only c. the 5th century BC. In India the period runs from the ironworking Painted Grey Ware culture, c. 1200 BC, to the reign of Ashoka in the 3rd century BC. Many parts of Africa passed directly from stone to iron, and some archaeologists believe iron metallurgy developed independently in sub-Saharan Africa as early as 2000 BC.

Meteoric iron, a natural iron–nickel alloy, was used thousands of years earlier; the oldest known artifacts are nine small beads dated to 3200 BC from burials at Gerzeh in Lower Egypt. An Iron Age culture is marked by mass production of tools and weapons from smelted steel alloys with added carbon, which are harder and lighter than bronze. Smelting iron requires temperatures above 1,250 °C, whereas copper melts at 1,085 °C and tin at 231.9 °C. The earliest tentative evidence of iron-making is a few steel-like fragments from Kaman-Kalehöyük in Turkey, dated to 2200–2000 BC.

End dates vary: c. 550 BC with the Persian [[Achaemenid Empire]] in the Near East, the 1st century BC with Roman conquest in Central and Western Europe, and c. AD 800 with the Viking Age in Scandinavia.

=== KEY: Iron_cefa8a
HEADWORD: Iron
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's crust | Hematite | Hemoglobin | Iron Age | Magnetite | Periodic table | Rust | Steel
ENTRY:
Chemical element, symbol Fe (from Latin *ferrum*), atomic number 26, a transition metal in group 8 of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. By mass it is the most common element on Earth, forming much of the core, and the fourth most abundant in the [[Earth's crust]]. Pure iron is a lustrous, ductile, silvery metal that melts at 1538 °C and boils at 2861 °C.

Extracting usable metal from ores such as [[Hematite|hematite]] and [[Magnetite|magnetite]] requires furnaces reaching 1,500 °C, about 500 °C hotter than for copper. Humans mastered this in Eurasia during the 2nd millennium BC, and the displacement of copper alloys by iron tools and weapons marks the start of the [[Iron Age]]. Iron alloys, above all [[Steel|steel]], are by far the most common industrial metals because of their mechanical properties and low cost; nearly 98% of mined iron ore goes into steel production.

Solid iron has several crystal forms. Molten iron freezes at 1538 °C into body-centered cubic δ-iron, changes to face-centered cubic γ-iron at 1394 °C, and returns to body-centered cubic α-iron at 912 °C and below. Below its Curie point of 770 °C, α-iron is ferromagnetic.

Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form brown-to-black hydrated oxides known as [[Rust|rust]]. Because rust occupies more volume than the metal and flakes off, it exposes fresh surface to corrosion. The most common oxidation states are +2 and +3, but compounds range from −2 to +7.

Iron is essential to nearly all living organisms. An adult human body contains about 4 grams, mostly in [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]] and myoglobin, which carry and store oxygen, and in enzymes of cellular respiration. Iron deficiency is a common cause of anemia, while excess iron can be toxic.

[[Real number]] that cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers. === KEY: Irrigation_8ec3b2
HEADWORD: Irrigation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aquifer | Crop | Drainage | Groundwater | India | Mesopotamia
ENTRY:
Agricultural practice of applying controlled amounts of water to land to grow [[Crop|crops]], landscape plants, and lawns. It has been a key aspect of agriculture for over 5,000 years. It sustains crops in dry areas and during below-average rainfall, and is also used to protect plants from frost, suppress weeds, and cool livestock. [[Drainage]] is often studied alongside it, and both may be needed to sustain high production.

Methods differ in how water reaches plants. Surface (gravity) irrigation is the oldest. Sprinkler irrigation distributes piped water through overhead high-pressure devices. Micro-irrigation delivers water at low pressure through a piped network, and drip irrigation delivers it directly to the root zone. Subirrigation raises the water table to moisten soil below the roots. Water comes from [[Groundwater|groundwater]], from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs, or from sources such as treated wastewater, desalinated water, and fog collection.

Early systems emerged in [[Mesopotamia]], Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China. Irrigated area worldwide grew from 8 million ha in 1800 to 94 million in 1950 and 235 million in 1990. The land equipped for irrigation reached 355 million ha in 2023, up 23% from 289 million ha in 2000, and made up 23% of all croplands. Asia holds 71% of the total. [[India]] (76 million ha) and China (75 million ha) have the largest equipped areas, far ahead of the United States (25 million ha). Irrigated croplands produce 48% of crops by value, and their yield averages 76% higher than that of rainfed land.

Irrigation has environmental costs. Overdrafting depletes underground [[Aquifer|aquifers]], and over-irrigation wastes water and chemicals, may cause water pollution, and can raise water tables, leading to salinity problems that require subsurface drainage.

=== KEY: Is_ought_problem_34ce1c
HEADWORD: Is–ought problem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aesthetics | David Hume | Epistemology | Morality
ENTRY:
Philosophical problem articulated by [[David Hume]] concerning whether [[Morality|moral]] statements about what ought to be can be inferred from objective statements about what is. Hume distinguished descriptive from prescriptive statements and argued that the transition between them is not obvious. In *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), he insisted the step needs explanation. Hume's law, or Hume's guillotine, is the thesis that an ethical conclusion cannot be inferred from purely descriptive factual premises. G. E. Moore's open-question argument reaches a similar view and was aimed at ethical naturalists, who identify moral properties with natural ones. The problem is closely related to the fact–value distinction in [[Epistemology]], although that discussion may also encompass [[Aesthetics|aesthetics]]. Responses include non-cognitivist views such as emotivism, which treat "ought" statements as expressions of attitude, and John Searle's 1964 argument that institutional facts such as promises carry normative force. Consensus on a resolution remains elusive.

English polymath: mathematician, physicist, astronomer, alchemist, theologian, historian, and inventor, a key figure in the Scientific Revolution.

His book [[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica|*Principia*]] (1687) formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation and established [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]], the dominant scientific viewpoint for centuries until it was superseded by the theory of relativity. He served as Member of Parliament for the University of Cambridge in 1689–1690 and 1701–1702 and was knighted by Queen Anne in 1705. === KEY: Islam_2ee698

Abrahamic monotheistic religion based on the [[Quran]] and the teachings of [[Muhammad]], with an estimated 2 billion adherents, called [[Muslims]]. It is the world's second-largest religious population after Christianity.

Muslims believe in a primordial faith revealed many times through earlier prophets, including Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, of which Islam is the universal and complete version. They regard the Quran as the verbatim word of God and the final revelation, and Muhammad as the final prophet. His teachings and examples, called the Sunnah, are documented in accounts called hadith. Islam originated in [[Mecca]] c. 610, when Muslims believe Muhammad received his first revelation. Muslims form a majority in 53 countries. Pew Research has found Muslims to be the world's fastest-growing major religious group, mainly because of a higher fertility rate and younger age structure.

=== KEY: Islamic_Golden_Age_32589c
HEADWORD: Islamic Golden Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abbasid Caliphate | Al-Andalus | Al-Khwarizmi | Averroes | Baghdad | Harun al-Rashid | House of Wisdom | Ibn al-Haytham
ENTRY:
Period of cultural and scientific flourishing in the history of Islam, dated from the 8th century to the 14th century. It is understood to have begun under the [[Abbasid Caliphate]] during the reign of [[Harun al-Rashid]] (786–809), with the establishment of the [[House of Wisdom]] in [[Baghdad]], then one of the world's largest cities. The institution attracted scholars from across the Muslim world to translate the classical knowledge of the known world into Arabic.

Activity flourished in other centers too, including Cairo and Umayyad Córdoba, capital of [[Al-Andalus]]. Works were translated from Greek, Syriac, Middle Persian, and Sanskrit, with Christians contributing as translators and physicians. Figures associated with the period include [[Al-Khwarizmi]], whose work laid foundations for algebra; al-Razi, in clinical observation and pharmacology; [[Ibn al-Haytham]], in experimental optics; and [[Averroes]], in philosophy. Suggested causes include Quranic injunctions valuing knowledge, heavy state patronage of scholars, and openness to the learning of conquered civilizations.

The dating is contested. The period is said to have ended with the collapse of the Abbasid caliphate after the Mongol siege of Baghdad in 1258. Some scholars extend it to c. 1350, to include the Timurid Renaissance, or to the 15th or even 16th century. Others place the true golden age between 750 and 950, arguing that decline began earlier.

The golden-age metaphor was applied in 19th-century writing on Islamic history and became frequent only in the second half of the 20th century. A dominant approach among scholars, the "decline theory," treats the period after the 11th century as an age of decline, a framing that has been challenged. Explanations for the end range from external shocks such as the Mongol sack to internal shifts toward theological orthodoxy, political fragmentation, and changing trade routes.

Philosophy that emerges from the Islamic tradition, closely tied to the translation of Greek texts into Arabic. Early Islamic philosophy began with [[Al-Kindi]] in the 2nd century of the [[Islamic calendar|Islamic calendar]] (early 9th century CE) and declined with [[Averroes|Ibn Rushd]] in the 6th century of that calendar (late 12th century CE), broadly coinciding with the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. It drew on [[Aristotle]] and Neoplatonism, and its major figures included [[Al-Farabi]] and [[Avicenna|Ibn Sina]]. Philosophy persisted longer in the east of the Islamic world, in Safavid Iran, the Ottoman Empire, and the Mughal Empire, where schools such as Avicennism and Illuminationism flourished. [[Ibn Khaldun]] made important contributions to the philosophy of history in his *Muqaddimah*. Interest revived during the Nahda ("Awakening") movement in the late 19th and early 20th centuries.

=== KEY: Islamic_schools_and_branches_7e3ee5
HEADWORD: Islamic schools and branches
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ahmadiyya | Islam | Muhammad | Shia Islam | Sufism | Sunni Islam
ENTRY:
Divisions within [[Islam]] comprising sects, schools of jurisprudence, and schools of theology. The original schism among Kharijites, Sunnis, and Shias concerned succession after the death of the Prophet [[Muhammad]], and the largest division today is between [[Sunni Islam|Sunni]] and [[Shia Islam|Shia]] Muslims, of whom Sunnis make up 87–90%. Sunni Islam has four main schools of jurisprudence, Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali, and theological schools including Ash'ari, Maturidi, and Athari. Shia Islam is divided into Twelvers, who make up about 85% of Shias by a 2012 estimate, Ismailis, and Zaydis. The Ibadis are the only surviving branch of the Kharijites. [[Sufism]], a mystical orientation, cuts across both Sunni and Shia contexts, and Sunnis frequently regard groups such as the [[Ahmadiyya]] as deviant. Muslims who do not identify with any identifiable school are known as non-denominational Muslims.

=== KEY: Islamism_5e6a80
HEADWORD: Islamism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arab Spring | Communism | Hamas | Islam | Muslim Brotherhood | Ruhollah Khomeini | Sayyid Qutb | Sharia
ENTRY:
Range of religious and political movements holding that [[Islam]] should influence political systems; their proponents hold that an Islamic political system is superior to [[Communism]], liberal democracy, and capitalism. Advocates emphasize the implementation of [[Sharia]], pan-Islamic political unity, and the creation of Islamic states. Prominent 20th-century figures include Rashid Rida, Hassan al-Banna, founder of the [[Muslim Brotherhood]], [[Sayyid Qutb]], Abul A'la Maududi, and [[Ruhollah Khomeini]], founder of the Islamic Republic of Iran. Groups include the Muslim Brotherhood, Turkey's Justice and Development Party, and [[Hamas]]. After the [[Arab Spring]], some post-Islamist currents became involved in democratic politics, while others formed militias such as the Islamic State of Iraq and the Levant. Islamists themselves prefer terms such as "Islamic movement," and critics say the term may encourage Islamophobia and stereotyping.

=== KEY: Island_ac0ce9
HEADWORD: Island
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archipelago | Climate change | Continent | Hawaiian Islands | Plate tectonics | Zealandia
ENTRY:
Piece of land, distinct from a [[Continent|continent]], completely surrounded by water. Islands occur in seas, lakes, and rivers, and no standard of size separates them from continents, which are defined geologically as the largest landmass of a tectonic plate.

Continental islands were split from a continent by [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]; oceanic islands were never part of one. Oceanic islands form through volcanic activity, grow into atolls from coral reefs, or build up from shoreline sediment as barrier islands. Many islands occur in [[Archipelago|archipelagos]]. Chains such as the [[Hawaiian Islands]] are thought to form as plates move over stationary volcanic hotspots, so that islands farther from the hotspot are older and more eroded; the oldest Hawaiian island is 25 million years old, and the youngest, Hawaii, is still active. New Zealand is what remains of a continent that shrank and sank; [[Zealandia]] has had 93% of its original surface area submerged.

Isolation shapes island life. Species reaching oceanic islands evolve in isolation, while continental islands share life with their parent continent but may diverge through natural selection. Islands cover about 6.7% of Earth's land surface yet support roughly 20% of terrestrial biodiversity and around 50% of threatened species. Habitat alteration and invasive species have caused many extinctions, such as the dodo's.

Up to 10% of the world's population lives on islands. European colonization put the majority of Pacific islands under European control, and decolonization left lasting effects related to industrialization, invasive species, nuclear weapons testing, and tourism. [[Climate change]] threatens islands: sea level rise could submerge the Maldives, the Marshall Islands, and Tuvalu completely, and more intense tropical cyclones damage infrastructure and habitats.

In [[Nuclear physics|nuclear physics]], a predicted set of superheavy isotopes that may have much longer [[Half-life|half-lives]] than known isotopes of those elements. Its existence is attributed to predicted magic numbers of [[Proton|protons]] and [[Neutron|neutrons]]. It is generally thought to center near copernicium and flerovium isotopes at N = 184. Estimates of half-lives range from minutes or days to, in optimistic proposals, millions of years. Such nuclides have never been found in nature, and no reaction has yet been found to produce them near the center. Synthesis of elements up to [[Oganesson|oganesson]] (Z = 118) shows a slight stabilizing effect around elements 110 to 114.

=== KEY: Isle_of_Man_9a3075
HEADWORD: Isle of Man
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles III | Christianity | Great Britain | Ireland | Irish Sea | Manx cat | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Self-governing British Crown Dependency in the [[Irish Sea]], between [[Great Britain]] and [[Ireland]]. [[Charles III]] holds the title Lord of Mann as head of state and is represented by a Lieutenant Governor, while the United Kingdom handles defense and represents the island abroad. Irish missionaries following Saint Patrick spread [[Christianity]] there. The island passed through Norwegian and Scottish rule before coming under the feudal lordship of the English Crown in 1399. Its parliament, Tynwald, became in 1881 the first national legislative body to give women the right to vote in a general election, though this excluded married women. The economy relies on low taxes and offshore banking, and insurance and online gambling each generate 17% of GNI. The island is known for the TT (Tourist Trophy) motorcycle races and the tailless [[Manx cat]], and [[UNESCO]] awarded it biosphere reserve status in 2016.

=== KEY: Isle_of_Wight_1362b9
HEADWORD: Isle of Wight
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: English Channel | Hampshire | Hovercraft | Queen Victoria
ENTRY:
Island and unitary authority off the south coast of England (est. pop., 2024: 141,660). Separated from the mainland by the Solent strait and otherwise surrounded by the [[English Channel]], it is the largest island in England, with 380 sq km (150 sq mi) of land. It was historically part of [[Hampshire]]. The largely rural island is said to be the sunniest place in Great Britain and has been designated a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve. Victorian-era tourism developed there, and [[Queen Victoria]] built Osborne House at East Cowes. Its maritime and industrial tradition includes boat-building, sail-making, flying boats and [[Hovercraft|hovercraft]].

Small archipelago in Cornwall, England, about 45 km (28 mi) southwest of Land's End, with a population of 2,100 at the 2021 [[United Kingdom]] census. People have lived there since at least the [[Iron Age]]. The islands were a Royalist base during the [[English Civil War]] until Parliament captured them in 1651. In 1707, four Royal Navy ships were wrecked off the islands, and at least 1,450 people died. The islands are known for pilot gig racing, and rock climbing on the [[Granite|granite]] coast has been practiced since 1977. [[Tourism]] is a major part of the economy.

=== KEY: Isma_il_Pasha_of_Egypt_8221eb
HEADWORD: Isma'il Pasha
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Egypt | Ottoman Empire | Suez Canal | Urbanization
ENTRY:
Khedive of [[Egypt]] and Sudan, 1863–1879, also known as Ismail the Magnificent. Sharing the outlook of his grandfather, Muhammad Ali Pasha, he invested heavily in industry, [[Urbanization|urbanization]] and the expansion of the country's boundaries in Africa. He built over 900 miles (1,400 km) of railroads and 4,500 schools. In 1867 he paid the [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] sultan for recognition of his title of khedive, with succession passing only to his direct descendants. His spending, including much of the money for the [[Suez Canal]], left the khedivate deeply in debt, and Egypt's shares in the Suez Canal Company were sold to the British government. He was removed in 1879 under British and French pressure. The city of Ismailia is named in his honor.

=== KEY: Ismail_I_35b53b
HEADWORD: Ismail I
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Anatolia | Middle East | Ottoman Empire | Safavid Iran | Shia Islam | Tabriz
ENTRY:
Founder and first shah of [[Safavid Iran]], ruling 1501–1524. Ismail inherited leadership of the Safavid Sufi order as a child and was crowned shah in 1501, with [[Tabriz]] as the capital. His expansion into eastern [[Anatolia]] brought conflict with the [[Ottoman Empire]], which decisively defeated the Safavids at the Battle of Chaldiran in 1514. He made [[Shia Islam|Twelver Shia Islam]] the official religion of the Safavid state, a turning point in the history of Islam. In 1508 he destroyed the tombs of the Abbasid caliphs, Abu Hanifa and Abdul Qadir Gilani, causing sectarian tensions in the [[Middle East]]. He was also a prolific poet, writing in a southern Turkic dialect under the pen name Khaṭāʾī.

=== KEY: Ismail_Kadare_ecd5f6
HEADWORD: Kadare, Ismail
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Fable | Franz Kafka | Kosovo | Totalitarianism
ENTRY:
Albanian novelist, poet, essayist and playwright. Living under strict censorship in Albania, he used parable, myth, [[Fable|fable]], folk-tale and allegory to outwit Communist censors, who had banned three of his books. His first novel, *The General of the Dead Army*, made him famous internationally. In 1990 he defected to Paris to escape the Communist regime and its Sigurimi secret police. A Neustadt Prize juror called him the successor of [[Franz Kafka]], and some regard him as a universal voice against [[Totalitarianism|totalitarianism]]. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature 15 times and won the 2005 inaugural Man Booker International Prize. In 2023 he was granted citizenship of [[Kosovo]].
