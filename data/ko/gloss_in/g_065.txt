=== KEY: Igloo_cb95d8
HEADWORD: Igloo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Greenland | Inuit | Snow
ENTRY:
Dome-shaped shelter built of blocks of [[Snow|snow]]. Traditionally, igloos were built by the [[Inuit]] of Canada's Central Arctic and in the Qaanaaq area of [[Greenland]]. They served as temporary shelters on hunting trips or, in larger forms, as semi-permanent family dwellings. Snow insulates because of the air trapped in it, so inside temperatures may range from −7 to 16 °C (19 to 61 °F) when warmed by body heat alone, even when outside temperatures fall to −45 °C (−49 °F). Snow igloos are built in a catenary curve rather than a sphere, and the largest could have up to five rooms and house 20 people. Igloo building has declined in modern times, but it is still taught for heritage preservation in some Inuit communities.

Spanish Catholic priest and theologian, co-founder of the [[Jesuits|Society of Jesus]]. A former soldier, Ignatius envisioned the society's purpose as missionary work and teaching. === KEY: Ignaz_Semmelweis_2ba1a2
HEADWORD: Semmelweis, Ignaz
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Joseph Lister | Louis Pasteur
ENTRY:
Hungarian physician of German descent and early pioneer of antiseptic procedures. In 1847 Semmelweis proposed hand washing with chlorinated lime solutions at the First Obstetrical Clinic of Vienna General Hospital, where doctors' wards had thrice the mortality of midwives' wards. The maternal mortality rate dropped from 18% to less than 2%. He published his findings in *Etiology, Concept and Prophylaxis of Childbed Fever* in 1861. Many in the medical community rejected his ideas, in part because he could not offer a theoretical explanation for them. He was committed to an asylum in 1865 and died 14 days later from a gangrenous wound. His findings gained acceptance after [[Louis Pasteur]] confirmed the germ theory of disease and [[Joseph Lister]] pioneered antiseptic surgery.

=== KEY: Igneous_rock_aeccec
HEADWORD: Igneous rock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basalt | Granite | Lava | Magma | Metamorphic rock | Sedimentary rock
ENTRY:
Rock formed by the cooling and solidification of [[Magma|magma]] or [[Lava|lava]]. It is one of the three main rock types, alongside [[Sedimentary rock|sedimentary]] and [[Metamorphic rock|metamorphic]] rock. Intrusive igneous rocks cool slowly underground and are coarse-grained, as in [[Granite|granite]] and gabbro. Extrusive, or volcanic, rocks form rapidly at or near the surface and are fine-grained or glassy; [[Basalt|basalt]] is the most common. Igneous and metamorphic rocks make up 90–95% of the top 16 km (9.9 mi) of Earth's crust by volume. Igneous rocks form about 15% of Earth's current land surface and most of the oceanic crust. Tungsten, tin and uranium are commonly associated with granites, while chromium and platinum are associated with gabbros.

ENTRY:
Russian-born composer and conductor, a pivotal figure in modernist music. Genus of large, bulky herbivorous iguanodontian [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]] of the Early [[Cretaceous]]. Named in 1825 by Gideon Mantell from fossils found in England, it was the second dinosaur formally named, after *Megalosaurus*. Modern revision defines the genus by *I. bernissartensis*, which lived in [[Belgium]], Germany, Spain and possibly England, between about 126 and 122 million years ago. === KEY: Imaginary_number_a102ba
HEADWORD: Imaginary number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustin-Louis Cauchy | Carl Friedrich Gauss | Complex number | Imaginary unit | Leonhard Euler | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Product of a real number and the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]] *i*, which is defined by *i*² = −1. For example, 5*i* is imaginary and its square is −25; zero is considered both real and imaginary. [[René Descartes]] coined the term in the 17th century as a derogatory word for what he regarded as fictitious or useless. The concept gained wide acceptance through the work of [[Leonhard Euler]] in the 18th century and of [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] and [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] in the early 19th century. An imaginary number added to a real number forms a [[Complex number|complex number]], *a* + *bi*. Geometrically, imaginary numbers lie on the vertical axis of the complex plane, and multiplying by *i* rotates a point 90 degrees counterclockwise about the origin.

=== KEY: Imaginary_unit_217941

ENTRY:
Mathematical constant that solves the equation *x*² = −1, usually denoted *i*. Its two square roots, *i* and −*i*, are additive inverses of each other. The powers of *i* cycle through 1, *i*, −1 and −*i*. Historically denoted by the square root of −1, the symbol is now rare. === KEY: Imagination_97a731
HEADWORD: Imagination
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Fairy tale | Memory | Plato | Science fiction
ENTRY:
Capacity to represent sensations or physical objects in the mind without any immediate input of the senses.

It is often described as forming pictures in the mind and is commonly equated with mental imagery, though imaginary experiences can also include other sensory experiences, thoughts, and emotions. Imaginings may re-create past experiences, with or without changes, or be completely invented. Imagination helps apply knowledge to solve problems and is fundamental to integrating experience and to learning. Drawing on actual perceptions, it engages both semantic and episodic [[Memory|memory]] to generate new or refined ideas. Children exercise it through pretend play, and it can be trained through storytelling in forms such as [[Fairy tale|fairy tales]] and [[Science fiction|science fiction]].

The English word derives from the Latin *imaginatio*, the standard translation of the Greek *phantasia*. Ancient Greek philosophers conceived it as working with mental pictures. [[Aristotle]], in *De Anima*, identified it as a faculty that enables an image to occur within a person and distinguished it from perception and rational thinking. [[Plato]] compared imagination about the future to a painter within the soul, an illustrator rather than a creator. In the Middle Ages the concept covered religion, literature, art, and above all poetry, and Chaucer wrote of the "mind's eye" in *The Man of Law's Tale* (c. 1390). Medieval faculty psychology treated it as an internal sense, alongside memory and common sense, that receives images, organizes them, and passes them to the reasoning faculties.

Psychologists distinguish reproductive from constructive imagination. Constructive imagination divides into voluntary forms driven by the lateral prefrontal cortex, such as mental rotation, and involuntary forms such as REM sleep dreaming, daydreaming, hallucinations, and spontaneous insight. Clinicians use visual imagery in treating anxiety disorders, depression, schizophrenia, and Parkinson's disease.

=== KEY: Immanuel_Kant_27791f
HEADWORD: Kant, Immanuel
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Critique of Pure Reason | Isaac Newton | Kingdom of Prussia | Königsberg | Solar System | Tidal locking
ENTRY:
German philosopher, a central thinker of the Enlightenment.

Born Emanuel Kant in [[Königsberg]] in the [[Kingdom of Prussia]], into a Lutheran family that stressed Pietist values, he changed the spelling of his name after learning Hebrew. He enrolled at the University of Königsberg at age 16 and studied under Martin Knutzen, who introduced him to the physics of [[Isaac Newton]]. After his father's death in 1746 he worked as a private tutor, publishing his first philosophical work in 1749. In 1754 he argued that the Moon's gravity would slow the Earth's spin and eventually produce [[Tidal locking|tidal locking]]; the next year he extended this reasoning to the formation of the [[Solar System]]. He later lived so orderly a life that neighbors were said to set their watches by his daily walks.

Kant's philosophy centers on the human subject and aims to secure the possibility of both knowledge and morality against skepticism and determinism. In the [[Critique of Pure Reason]] (1781/1787) he argued for transcendental idealism: space and time are "forms of intuition" that structure all experience, and knowledge extends only to appearances, not to things in themselves. He likened this reversal, in which objects conform to the mind's forms and categories, to the Copernican Revolution. In ethics, set out in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), he held that reason is the source of morality and that the categorical imperative binds all rational agents. The *Critique of Judgment* (1790) treated aesthetics as arising from a faculty of disinterested judgment. He hoped perpetual peace could be secured through an international federation of republican states, and held that true religion is grounded on morality, though the exact nature of his religious views is disputed.

=== KEY: Immune_system_56f8e8
HEADWORD: Immune system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Immunology
ENTRY:
Network of biological systems that protects an organism from disease.

It detects and responds to a wide variety of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and parasites, as well as cancer cells and foreign objects, distinguishing them from the organism's own healthy tissue. All responses depend on telling self from non-self molecules; antigens are non-self substances that bind to specific immune receptors and elicit a response. Nearly all organisms have some form of immune system. Bacteria have a rudimentary one, in the form of enzymes that protect against viral infection. Ancient mechanisms including phagocytosis, antimicrobial peptides called defensins, RNA interference, and the complement system remain in the modern descendants of early plants and animals.

Defense is layered, with increasing specificity. Physical barriers such as skin, the insect exoskeleton, and the waxy leaf cuticle come first, supported by coughing, sneezing, tears, and mucus. Chemical barriers include antimicrobial peptides, enzymes such as lysozyme in saliva and tears, and gastric acid in the stomach. Commensal flora act as biological barriers by competing with pathogenic bacteria for food and space. A pathogen that gets past these meets the innate immune system, found in all animals, which gives an immediate but non-specific response and confers no long-lasting immunity. Vertebrates also have an adaptive immune system, activated by the innate response, which tailors its response to each stimulus and retains it as immunological memory, allowing faster and stronger attacks on repeat encounters. This mechanism is the basis of vaccination.

Dysfunction causes disease. Immunodeficiency, an underactive system, leads to recurring and life-threatening infections and can result from genetic disease, acquired conditions such as HIV/AIDS, or immunosuppressive medication. Autoimmunity, in which a hyperactive system attacks normal tissue, underlies rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, and systemic lupus erythematosus. [[Immunology]] is the study of the system.

=== KEY: Immunology_ceb4fd
HEADWORD: Immunology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antibody | Cancer | Nobel Prize | Thucydides
ENTRY:
Branch of biology and medicine that studies immune systems in all organisms. It charts the functioning of the immune system in health and disease, including autoimmune diseases, hypersensitivities, immune deficiency and transplant rejection. The term was coined by the Russian biologist Ilya Ilyich Mechnikov, who first observed phagocytosis and shared the 1908 [[Nobel Prize|Nobel Prize]] with Paul Ehrlich. The earliest written mention of immunity dates to the plague of Athens in 430 BC, recorded by [[Thucydides]]. The immune system is divided into innate immunity and, in vertebrates, adaptive immunity, which has humoral (antibody) and cell-mediated components. [[Antibody|Antibodies]] are proteins released by B lymphocytes that identify targets for destruction rather than killing pathogens directly. The field has applications in organ transplantation, [[Cancer|oncology]], rheumatology, virology and dermatology.

HEADWORD: Impact crater
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Erosion | Mercury (planet) | Moon
ENTRY:
Depression in the surface of a solid astronomical body, formed by the hypervelocity impact of a smaller object. Unlike volcanic craters, impact craters typically have raised rims and floors lower than the surrounding terrain. They range from microscopic pits on lunar rocks to vast, multi-ringed impact basins. === KEY: Impartiality_12545c

ENTRY:
Principle of [[Justice|justice]] holding that decisions should rest on objective criteria, not [[Bias|bias]] or [[Prejudice|prejudice]]. In philosophy, impartiality is a formal property of choices: a choice is impartial when considerations such as who benefits or is harmed have no influence on it. Many moral theories, including [[Utilitarianism]], Kantian ethics and contractualism, treat the impartial point of view as the standpoint from which moral questions are judged. === KEY: Imperialism_917e27
HEADWORD: Imperialism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Empire | Colonialism | Edward Said | Great Game | Scramble for Africa | Vladimir Lenin | World War I
ENTRY:
Maintaining and extending of power over foreign nations, particularly through expansionism, using both hard power (military and economic) and soft power (diplomatic and cultural).

It aims at establishing or maintaining hegemony and a more formal empire. The word derives from the Latin *imperium*, "to command," and was coined in the 19th century to describe Napoleon III's attempts to gain political support by invasion. It was applied to the [[British Empire]] during the 1870s and by the 1880s had acquired a positive connotation in the West. Definitions have varied. Hannah Arendt and Joseph Schumpeter defined it as expansion for the sake of expansion. Hobson and, in 1917, [[Vladimir Lenin|Lenin]] called it the "highest stage of capitalism," as firms exported capital to dominate economically rather than territorially. In 1978 [[Edward Said]] used the term for domination and subordination between an imperial core and a periphery, including the cultural attitudes, such as a "civilizing" mission, that accompany it. Kenneth Waltz suggests its root cause is great power, since strength tempts its holders to exercise control.

Scholars often distinguish it from [[Colonialism|colonialism]]. Said held that colonialism refers to settlements on a distant territory, while imperialism is the practice, theory, and attitudes of a dominating metropolitan center. Contiguous land empires such as the Russian, Chinese, and Ottoman have traditionally been called imperialist, although they also sent populations into remote territories.

The "Age of Imperialism" refers to the period before [[World War I]], conventionally ending in 1914 and beginning anywhere between 1760 and 1870. In it, industrializing European nations, joined late in the 19th century by the United States and Japan, intensified colonizing and annexing other regions, as in the [[Scramble for Africa]] and the [[Great Game]]. In the 1970s John Gallagher and Ronald Robinson argued that informal control of independent areas mattered more than formal rule, summing it up as empire "informally if possible and formally if necessary."

=== KEY: Impulse_physics_e8eb30
HEADWORD: Impulse
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Momentum | Rocket
ENTRY:
Change in [[Momentum|momentum]] of an object, usually produced by a comparatively large force acting over a short time, as in an impact or collision. Impulse is a vector quantity with the same units as momentum. For a force acting over time, impulse is the integral of force with respect to time; for constant mass it equals the change in velocity multiplied by mass, J = Δp = mv₂ − mv₁. === KEY: Inca_Empire_8df2ba
HEADWORD: Inca Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cusco | Lake Titicaca | Peru
ENTRY:
Largest empire of pre-Columbian America, centered on the Andes in western South America (1438–1533).

Officially the Realm of the Four Parts (Quechua: *Tawantinsuyu*), it had its administrative, political, and military center in [[Cusco]]. The Inca civilization rose from the Peruvian highlands in the early 13th century. From 1438 to 1533 the Incas incorporated a large part of western South America through conquest and peaceful assimilation. At its largest the empire joined modern [[Peru]] with western Ecuador, western and south-central Bolivia, northwest Argentina, the southwesternmost tip of Colombia, and much of Chile. Its official language was Quechua. Inca nobles, probably numbering only 15,000 to 40,000, ruled a population of around 10 million. The empire was preceded by the Tiwanaku, around [[Lake Titicaca]], and the Wari, and drew many of its characteristics from these earlier Andean cultures.

The Incas built "one of the greatest imperial states in human history," in the anthropologist Gordon McEwan's words, without the wheel, draft animals, iron or steel, or a system of writing. Notable features were monumental stonework, an extensive road network (Qhapaq Ñan), fine textiles, agricultural innovation in a difficult environment, and knotted strings (*quipu*) for record keeping. The empire functioned largely without money and markets: exchange rested on reciprocity, and "taxes" took the form of a labor obligation to the state, which in return granted access to land and goods and provided feasts. Scholars have debated how to characterize this economy. Local forms of worship persisted, but the leadership promoted the sun god Inti, and the ruler, the Sapa Inca, was regarded as the "son of the Sun."

The Portuguese explorer Aleixo Garcia was the first European to reach the empire, in 1524. The Spanish began its conquest in 1532, and by 1572 the last Inca state was fully conquered.

Country in South Asia (pop., 2025: approx. 1,464,000,000), capital [[New Delhi]].

Officially the Republic of India, it is the world's seventh-largest country by area and its most populous. It is bounded by the [[Indian Ocean]], the Arabian Sea, and the Bay of Bengal, and borders Pakistan, China, Nepal, Bhutan, Bangladesh, and Myanmar.

Modern humans reached the subcontinent by 55,000 years ago. Settled life emerged in the Indus basin 9,000 years ago and developed into the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] of the third millennium BCE. By 1200 BCE an archaic form of Sanskrit had spread from the northwest, its earliest hymns containing elements of what became [[Hinduism]]. After 500 BCE a hereditary, caste-based social stratification solidified, while Buddhism and Jainism arose proclaiming social orders unlinked to heredity. Early in the 2nd millennium Muslim armies from Central Asia established the [[Delhi Sultanate]]. The [[Mughal Empire]] brought two centuries of economic expansion and relative peace. The East India Company consolidated control over the subcontinent, and Crown rule, the [[British Raj]], began in 1858. A nationalist movement, noted for nonviolent resistance after 1920, was a major factor in ending British rule. In 1947 the British Indian Empire was partitioned into a Hindu-majority India and a Muslim-majority Pakistan, with large-scale loss of life and unprecedented migration.

The [[Constitution of India]], adopted in 1950, declared a federal parliamentary republic and guarantees freedom of speech, freedom of press, and religious pluralism. The Green Revolution of the 1970s brought national food security. Economic reforms in the 1990s integrated the economy with the world's, and growth was stably high by the 2010s. Challenges include widening income gaps, gender inequality, and environmental degradation, and some observers describe a political shift toward majoritarianism. India is a nuclear-weapon state, a founding member of the United Nations, and among the world's largest economies. It has 45 UNESCO World Heritage Sites and four biodiversity hotspots.

=== KEY: Indian_Ocean_fe6fc8
HEADWORD: Indian Ocean
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andaman Sea | Atlantic Ocean | Bay of Bengal | Monsoon | Pacific Ocean | Southern Ocean | Zheng He
ENTRY:
Third-largest of the world's five oceanic divisions, bounded by Asia to the north, Africa to the west, and Australia to the east.

It covers 70,560,000 sq km, about 20% of Earth's water surface, including the Red Sea and Persian Gulf but excluding the [[Southern Ocean]], or Antarctica, which bounds it to the south depending on the definition in use. Its volume is 264,000,000 cu km, with an average depth of 3,741 m and a maximum depth of 7,290 m. The [[Atlantic Ocean]] boundary is the 20° east meridian, running south from Cape Agulhas, South Africa, and the [[Pacific Ocean]] boundary is the meridian of 146°49′E, running south from South East Cape, Tasmania. Its large marginal seas include the [[Andaman Sea]], the Arabian Sea, the [[Bay of Bengal]], and the Laccadive Sea. It is enclosed by major landmasses on three sides and does not stretch from pole to pole. More than two billion people live in countries bordering it.

Geologically it is the youngest ocean, preceded by the Tethys Ocean, which fragmented into the Mediterranean Sea and the Indian Ocean only 20 million years ago. Its continental shelves are narrow, making up 15% of the ocean. It is the warmest ocean and strongly affects global climate; its waters are shaped by the Indian Ocean Walker circulation, producing distinctive currents and upwelling. The surrounding climate is characterized by [[Monsoon|monsoons]]. Coral reefs, mangroves, and seagrass beds support diverse ecosystems, and the ocean hosts a significant portion of the world's tuna catch and a number of endangered marine species.

Its name comes from India, which projects into it, and has been attested since at least 1515, in the Latin *Oceanus Orientalis Indicus*. Chinese explorers such as [[Zheng He]] in the 15th century called it the Western Oceans, and the ancient Greeks the Erythraean Sea. It has long linked diverse regions through trade, innovation, and religion.

Uprising in India in 1857–58 against the rule of the British East India Company. It began on May 10, 1857, as a mutiny of sepoys in Meerut and spread to civilian revolts, chiefly in the upper Gangetic plain and central India. Rebels reached Delhi, where the 81-year-old Mughal ruler Bahadur Shah Zafar was declared Emperor of Hindustan. Art forms of the Indian subcontinent, including painting, sculpture, pottery and textile arts such as woven silk. === KEY: Indian_classical_music_11a0e8
HEADWORD: Indian classical music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carnatic music | Counterpoint | Harmony | Hinduism
ENTRY:
Classical music of the Indian subcontinent, with two major traditions. North Indian Hindustani music emphasizes improvisation and exploration of all aspects of a raga, while South Indian [[Carnatic music|Carnatic]] performances tend to be short and composition-based, though the two systems share more features than differences. Both rest on the raga, a framework of notes including microtones, and the tala, a time cycle. It traditionally eschews Western classical concepts such as [[Harmony|harmony]] and [[Counterpoint|counterpoint]]. Its roots lie in the Vedic literature of [[Hinduism]] and the Natyashastra, a Sanskrit text on the performing arts by Bharata Muni. The 13th-century text Sangeeta-Ratnakara by Sarangadeva is regarded as definitive by both traditions.

=== KEY: Indian_cuisine_854658

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cauliflower | Indus Valley Civilisation | Lentil | Muslims

Regional cooking traditions of the Indian subcontinent, shaped by religion, culture and history. Religious faiths influence the cuisines: Hindus often do not eat beef, [[Muslims]] avoid pork, and Jains are strict lacto-vegetarians. Agriculture reached the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] around 7000 BCE, and turmeric, cardamom, black pepper and mustard were harvested by 3,000 BCE. Mughlai cuisine combined Persian and Hindu cooking, producing dishes such as pilau, biryani and kebabs. The Portuguese and British introduced baking and New World foods including tomato, potato, sweet potato, peanut and chilli, and the British introduced [[Cauliflower|cauliflower]] in 1822. Staple foods include rice and many [[Lentil|lentils]], which are often split into dal.

=== KEY: Indian_independence_movement_037c40
HEADWORD: Indian independence movement
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constitution of India | Indian National Congress | Jallianwala Bagh massacre | Mahatma Gandhi | Punjab
ENTRY:
Series of political efforts to end British colonial rule in India, from the mid-1880s to 1947. The first nationalist movement took root with the formation of the [[Indian National Congress]] in 1885. Protests against the Partition of Bengal (1905) marked a more radical turn, which evolved into the Non-cooperation Movement (1919–1922), the Civil Disobedience Movement (1929–1931) and the Quit India Movement (1942), which demanded an end to British presence. From the 1920s [[Mahatma Gandhi]] led the movement’s adoption of non-violence and civil disobedience, though some leaders adopted violent approaches. The Rowlatt Act, which permitted indefinite detention without trial, sparked protests in [[Punjab]] that were violently suppressed in the [[Jallianwala Bagh massacre]]. The Indian Independence Act 1947 partitioned British India into the Dominion of India and the Dominion of Pakistan. The [[Constitution of India]] took effect on January 26, 1950, establishing the Republic of India.

Literature produced on the Indian subcontinent until 1947 and in the Republic of India thereafter. The Eighth Schedule to the Constitution of India recognizes 22 languages. === KEY: Indian_philosophy_aba025

Philosophical traditions of the Indian subcontinent, often called *darśana*, meaning “to see” or “looking at.” Traditional classification divides the schools into āstika (orthodox) schools, which accept the Vedas as an essential source, and nāstika (heterodox) schools. The six major āstika schools are Nyāya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mīmāṃsā and [[Vedanta]]. The five major non-Vedic schools are Jain, Buddhist, Ajivika, Ajñana and Charvaka. === KEY: Indigenous_peoples_d7ab9d
HEADWORD: Indigenous peoples
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Self-determination | United Nations
ENTRY:
Groups characterized by self-identification, cultural difference from other groups in a state, a special relationship with their traditional territory, and an experience of subjugation and discrimination under a dominant cultural model.

There is no generally accepted definition in the [[United Nations]] or international law, and various bodies have either proposed definitions or declined to offer one. The UN Permanent Forum on Indigenous Issues holds that no formal universal definition is necessary, since any single definition would be either over- or under-inclusive. Estimates of the population range from 250 million to 600 million, in some 5,000 distinct peoples spread across every inhabited climate zone and continent. Many have experienced colonization by settlers from European nations, but Indigenous identity is not determined by Western colonization.

The English word derives from the Latin *indigena*, "sprung from the land, native." Europeans first used it to distinguish the Indigenous peoples of the Americas from enslaved Africans; the first known use is by Sir Thomas Browne in 1646. In the 1970s it came to link the struggles of colonized peoples across borders and to describe a legal category in international and national law.

The 1989 International Labour Organization Convention on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples covers peoples regarded as indigenous because of descent from populations that inhabited a country or region at the time of conquest, colonization, or the establishment of present state boundaries, and makes self-identification a fundamental criterion. In 2007 the UN adopted a Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, affirming rights to [[Self-determination|self-determination]] and to protect cultures, languages, and ceremonies. Indigenous peoples continue to face threats to their sovereignty, economic well-being, languages, and access to resources. Critics of the open-ended definition argue that reliance on self-identification can enable expansive claims, while supporters regard it as respecting the right of peoples to determine their own identity.

=== KEY: Indo_European_languages_e8a295
HEADWORD: Indo-European languages
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze Age | Historical linguistics | Neolithic | Yamnaya culture
ENTRY:
Language family native to the northern Indian subcontinent, most of Europe, and Greater Iran. Over 3.4 billion people (42% of the global population) speak an Indo-European language as a first language, by far the most of any language family. According to an Ethnologue estimate, about 446 Indo-European languages are living, of which 313 belong to the Indo-Iranian branch. All are descended from Proto-Indo-European, a prehistoric language reconstructed by linguists and spoken sometime during the [[Neolithic]] or early [[Bronze Age]] (c. 3300 – c. 1200 BC). The academic consensus supports the Kurgan hypothesis, which places the homeland on the Pontic–Caspian steppe, associated with the [[Yamnaya culture]]. Written evidence appeared during the Bronze Age in Mycenaean Greek and the Anatolian languages Hittite and Luwian. Eight branches are still spoken, while Anatolian and Tocharian are extinct. The family was central to the development of [[Historical linguistics|historical linguistics]] as an academic discipline in the 19th century.

=== KEY: Indo_Gangetic_Plain_14f893

Fertile plain of northern South Asia, named after the Indus and Ganges river systems. It spans 700,000 sq km (270,000 sq mi) across northern, western and eastern India, eastern Pakistan, southern Nepal, Bhutan and almost all of Bangladesh, stretching from the [[Himalayas]] to the northern edge of the [[Deccan Plateau]]. Deposits from the Indus, Ganges and Brahmaputra formed the world's largest expanse of uninterrupted alluvium, and its rich water resources make the plain one of the world's most densely populated and intensely farmed areas. The [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] arose here, and the Mauryas and [[Kushan Empire|Kushans]] had their political centers in the region. [[Babur]] established the [[Mughal Empire]] in 1526 CE, and it ruled for almost the next three centuries. After the defeat of the Marathas, the [[British Raj]] controlled the region until Indian independence in 1947.

=== KEY: Indo_Greek_Kingdom_3e5308
HEADWORD: Indo-Greek Kingdom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bactria | Menander I | Punjab
ENTRY:
Hellenistic-era Greek kingdoms in the northwestern Indian subcontinent, covering most of modern-day Pakistan, parts of northwestern India and some eastern parts of Afghanistan. The kingdom was founded when the Graeco-Bactrian king Demetrius I invaded India from [[Bactria]] around 200 BC. Its rulers combined Greek and Indian languages and symbols on their coins and blended Greek and Indian ideas, a hybrid culture visible in archaeological remains. [[Menander I]], the best known of the kings, had his capital at Sakala in the [[Punjab]] (present-day Sialkot). After his death most of the empire splintered. The Indo-Greeks disappeared as a political entity around 10 AD after invasions by the Indo-Scythians, though Greek populations probably remained for centuries under later rulers, and the Western Satraps lingered on, encompassing local Greeks, until 415 AD.

=== KEY: Indo_Scythian_Kingdom_1bd2f5
HEADWORD: Indo-Scythian Kingdom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central Asia | Chandragupta II | Kanishka | Kushan Empire | Ptolemy
ENTRY:
Nomadic Iranian peoples of Saka and Scythian origin, also known as Indo-Sakas. They migrated from [[Central Asia]] southward into present-day Afghanistan, eastern Iran and northwestern India between the middle of the 2nd century BCE and the 4th century CE. The first Saka king in the subcontinent was Maues (1st century BCE), who established Saka power in Gandhara and the Indus Valley. The Indo-Scythians extended their supremacy over the northwest, conquering the Indo-Greeks, but were apparently subjugated by the [[Kushan Empire]] under Kujula Kadphises or [[Kanishka]]. The Saka continued to govern as satraps, forming the Northern and Western Satraps. Their power declined in the 2nd century CE after defeat by the Satavahana emperor Gautamiputra Satakarni. Rule in the northwest ended when the last Western Satrap, Rudrasimha III, was defeated by the Gupta emperor [[Chandragupta II]] in 395 CE. Ancient historians including Arrian and [[Ptolemy|Claudius Ptolemy]] described the Sakas as nomadic.

Archipelagic country, Southeast Asia and Oceania, between the Indian and Pacific oceans, with over 290 million people.

Indonesia comprises between 13,000 and 17,000 islands, including Sumatra, [[Java]], Sulawesi, and parts of [[Borneo]] and New Guinea. It is the world's largest archipelagic state and the 14th-largest country by area,. The capital is [[Jakarta]]. Indonesia ranks fourth in the world by population and has the largest Muslim population of any country. It has hundreds of ethnic groups and more than 700 languages; Indonesian, a standardized variety of Malay, is the official language and lingua franca.

Fossils of *Homo erectus* have been found on Java, and stone artefacts on Flores show that hominins had reached that island by at least 1.02 million years ago. Austronesian-speaking communities moved into the archipelago from the third millennium BC. Hindu and Buddhist kingdoms developed by the early centuries AD. [[Srivijaya]], a maritime trading polity, was founded in southeastern Sumatra in the late 7th century. Central Javanese rulers built monuments including [[Borobudur]] in the 8th–10th centuries, and [[Majapahit]] emerged in eastern Java in the late 13th century. Dutch colonial control expanded from the 17th century and covered most of the archipelago by the early 20th century. Indonesia proclaimed independence in the aftermath of World War II.

Since independence the country has experienced separatist conflicts, corruption, political upheaval, and natural disasters, alongside democratization and rapid economic growth; the authoritarian rule of [[Suharto]] ended in 1998. Indonesia is a presidential republic with 38 provinces. It has the largest economy in Southeast Asia and belongs to the United Nations, the G20, the Non-Aligned Movement, and [[ASEAN]].

Armed conflict and diplomatic struggle between the [[Indonesia|Republic of Indonesia]] and the Dutch Empire, also known as the Indonesian War of Independence. Sukarno and Mohammad Hatta proclaimed independence on August 17, 1945, and the struggle ended when the [[Netherlands]] transferred sovereignty over the [[Dutch East Indies]] to the Republic of the United States of Indonesia at the end of 1949. The four-year struggle combined sporadic but bloody armed conflict, internal political and communal upheavals, and two major international diplomatic interventions. Dutch forces controlled the major towns and industrial assets of [[Java]] and [[Sumatra]] but not the countryside. By 1949, international pressure, including threats by the [[United States]] to cut off economic aid for postwar reconstruction in the Netherlands, and a partial military stalemate forced the transfer. The revolution ended colonial administration except in Dutch New Guinea and reduced the power of many local rulers, but did not significantly improve the economic or political fortunes of most Indonesians.

Official and national language of Indonesia, a standardized variety of [[Malay language|Malay]]. It has been used as a [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]] across the Indonesian [[Archipelago|archipelago]] for centuries. According to Indonesia's 2025 Intercensal Population Survey (SUPAS 2025), 98.12% of Indonesians are fluent in it, making it the largest language by number of speakers in [[Southeast Asia]]. Its vocabulary draws on regional languages such as Javanese, Sundanese, Minangkabau and Balinese, and on foreign languages including Arabic, Dutch, Portuguese, [[Sanskrit]] and English. Most Indonesians also speak one of more than 700 indigenous languages at home, but formal education, nearly all national [[Mass media|mass media]], governance and the judiciary use Indonesian. It was the official language of East Timor under Indonesian rule from 1976 to 1999, and in November 2023 it was recognized as one of the official languages of the [[UNESCO]] General Conference.

Largest city of the Indian state of [[Madhya Pradesh]] and administrative headquarters of its district and division. Established on the banks of the Kanh and Saraswati rivers, Indore traces its roots to a 16th-century founding as a trading hub between the [[Deccan Plateau]] and [[Delhi]]. It came under the [[Maratha Empire]] after the conquest of Malwa by Peshwa Baji Rao I, and was later annexed by the [[East India Company|British East India Company]]. Before Indian independence it was the Kingdom of Indore, a nineteen-gun salute princely state of the [[British Raj]] ruled by the Holkars. The metropolitan region, which includes Ujjain and Pithampur, had an estimated population of 5.6 million in 2025 and is a major center for pharmaceutical and automotive manufacturing. The city is home to an Indian Institute of Technology and an Indian Institute of Management, and it is consistently ranked India's cleanest city.

Vedic god of weather, considered the king of the devas in Hinduism. He is associated with the sky, [[Lightning|lightning]], [[Thunder|thunder]], storms, rains and war. Indra is the most frequently mentioned deity in the *Rigveda*. He killed the asura Vritra, who obstructed human prosperity, and thereby brought rain and sunshine as the savior of mankind. His significance diminishes in post-Vedic literature, and in [[Buddhism]] he is reduced to a figurehead who suffers rebirth. He wields the vajra and rides the elephant Airavata. He resembles [[Zeus]], [[Jupiter (god)|Jupiter]] and [[Thor]] as a thunder god of Proto-Indo-European mythology.

=== KEY: Inductance_028c23
HEADWORD: Inductance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ampere | Electromagnetic induction | Inductor | Michael Faraday | Volt
ENTRY:
Property of an electrical conductor that opposes changes in the electric current flowing through it. The current generates a magnetic field, and any change in that field through a circuit induces a voltage, a process known as [[Electromagnetic induction]]. This induced voltage opposes the change, as stated by Lenz's law, and is called back EMF. Inductance is defined as the ratio of induced voltage to the rate of change of current, and depends on the geometry of the conductor and the magnetic permeability of nearby materials. A component designed to add inductance to a circuit is an [[Inductor]]. The term was coined by Oliver Heaviside in May 1884. The SI unit is the henry (H), which produces one [[Volt|volt]] of induced voltage when the current changes at one [[Ampere|ampere]] per second. It is named after Joseph Henry, who discovered inductance independently of [[Michael Faraday|Faraday]].
