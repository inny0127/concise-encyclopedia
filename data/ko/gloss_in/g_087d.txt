=== KEY: Mesozoic_86c89c
HEADWORD: Mesozoic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cenozoic | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Flowering plant | Pangaea | Permian–Triassic extinction event
ENTRY:
Era of Earth's geological history lasting from about 252 to 66 million years ago, comprising the Triassic, Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. It began after the [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]], the largest mass extinction in Earth's history, and ended with the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]], which killed the non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs, mosasaurs and plesiosaurs. Known as the Age of the Dinosaurs, it saw dinosaurs become the dominant terrestrial vertebrates by the Late Triassic or Early Jurassic. The supercontinent [[Pangaea]] began to break apart, and the climate was generally hotter than today. The first mammals appeared but remained smaller than 15 kg until the [[Cenozoic]]. [[Flowering plant|Flowering plants]] appeared in the Early Cretaceous and rapidly diversified, replacing conifers and other gymnosperms as the dominant plants.

English-language oratorio by German-born Baroque composer [[George Frideric Handel]], composed in 1741. Charles Jennens compiled its text from the King James Bible and the Coverdale Psalter. It was first performed in [[Dublin]] on 13 April 1742 and received its London premiere a year later, becoming one of the best-known choral works in Western music. === KEY: Meta_analysis_fb98d7
HEADWORD: Meta-analysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bias
ENTRY:
Statistical method for combining quantitative results from multiple independent studies of a common research question. Analysts compute a combined effect size across the studies, which increases statistical power and can resolve discrepancies among individual studies. Meta-analyses are often part of a systematic review and help shape treatment guidelines and health policies. The technique emerged in the social sciences in the 1970s, and the term was coined in 1976 by psychologist Gene V. Glass. It is used in medicine, psychology, education and ecology. Common models assume either a single true effect or variation between studies, and results are often displayed in forest plots. Challenges include [[Bias|publication bias]], which favors statistically significant findings, and the combination of heterogeneous studies.

=== KEY: Metabolism_671281
HEADWORD: Metabolism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Cancer | Cellular respiration | Enzyme | Glucose | Glycolysis | Nucleic acid
ENTRY:
Set of life-sustaining chemical reactions occurring within living organisms. Its three main functions are converting the energy in food into a form usable for cellular processes, converting food into building blocks of macromolecules such as proteins, lipids, [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]], and some carbohydrates, and excreting metabolic wastes. These enzyme-catalyzed reactions allow organisms to grow, reproduce, maintain their structures, and respond to their environments.

Reactions are catabolic, the breaking down of compounds (for example, of [[Glucose|glucose]] to pyruvate by [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]]), or anabolic, the building up of compounds by biosynthesis. Usually catabolism releases energy and anabolism consumes it. Catabolism supplies [[Adenosine triphosphate|adenosine triphosphate]] (ATP), the primary energy currency, produced through pathways such as [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] and, under aerobic conditions, mainly in the mitochondria.

Reactions are organized into metabolic pathways, in which one chemical is transformed through a series of steps, each facilitated by a specific [[Enzyme|enzyme]]. Enzymes allow organisms to drive reactions that require energy by coupling them to spontaneous reactions that release energy, and they allow regulation of reaction rates.

An organism's metabolic system determines which substances it finds nutritious and which poisonous: some prokaryotes use hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) as a nutrient, yet the gas is poisonous to some animals. The basal metabolic rate measures the energy consumed by all these reactions. Basic pathways are similar across vastly different species; the carboxylic acids of the citric acid cycle occur in all known organisms, from *Escherichia coli* to elephants, probably reflecting their early appearance in evolution. Normal metabolism is disrupted in diseases such as type II diabetes and [[Cancer|cancer]], whose cells metabolize differently from normal cells, a difference that can help identify therapeutic targets.

=== KEY: Metal_eaa57a
HEADWORD: Metal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Bronze | Chemical element | Copper | Hooke's law | Materials science | Metallurgy | Pauli exclusion principle
ENTRY:
Material that, when polished or fractured, shows a lustrous appearance and conducts electricity and heat relatively well. The English word derives from Ancient Greek *métallon*, "mine, quarry, metal." These properties are associated with having electrons available at the Fermi level, as opposed to nonmetallic materials, which do not. Metals are typically ductile, able to be drawn into a wire, and malleable, able to be shaped by hammering or pressing.

A metal may be a [[Chemical element|chemical element]], such as iron, an [[Alloy|alloy]], such as stainless steel, or a molecular compound, such as polymeric sulfur nitride. The general science of metals is [[Metallurgy|metallurgy]], a subtopic of [[Materials science|materials science]]; their electronic and thermal properties also fall within condensed matter physics and solid-state chemistry. In everyday use, steel alloys are called metals, while polymers, wood, and ceramics are nonmetallic. Many elements and compounds become metallic under high pressure; iodine gradually becomes a metal at a pressure between 40 and 170 thousand times atmospheric.

Most metals are shiny, at least when polished or fractured. Densities vary widely: lithium is the least dense of the elemental metals (0.534 g/cm³) and osmium the most dense (22.59 g/cm³). Magnesium, aluminum, and titanium are light metals of commercial importance, with densities of 1.7, 2.7, and 4.5 g/cm³, compared with 7.9 for iron and 8.9 for copper. Metals typically deform under stress without cleaving, and their reversible elastic deformation is described well by [[Hooke's law]]. The atoms of simple metals are often in one of three crystal structures: body-centered cubic, face-centered cubic, or hexagonal close-packed.

Metals conduct electricity because, when a voltage is applied, some electrons shift to slightly higher-momentum states, which are available at energies near the highest occupied levels; by the [[Pauli exclusion principle]], no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state. Semiconductors such as silicon instead have an energy gap between filled and empty states. The elemental metals have electrical conductivities from 6.9 × 10³ S/cm for manganese to 6.3 × 10⁵ S/cm for silver, and conductivity usually decreases with heating. All metallic alloys, as well as conducting ceramics and polymers, are metals by the same definition, as are liquids such as mercury.

The strength and resilience of some metals have led to frequent use in high-rise building and bridge construction, most vehicles, many home appliances, tools, pipes, and railroad tracks. Precious metals were historically used as coinage, and coinage metals have now extended to at least 23 of the chemical elements. The history of refined metals is thought to begin with the use of [[Copper|copper]] about 11,000 years ago. Gold, silver, iron (as meteoric iron), lead, and brass were likewise in use before the first known appearance of [[Bronze|bronze]] in the fifth millennium BCE. Sodium, the first light metal, was discovered in 1809, and since the end of World War II more sophisticated alloys have been developed.

=== KEY: Metallic_bonding_fb8118
HEADWORD: Metallic bonding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical bond | Covalent bond | Gallium | Ion
ENTRY:
Type of [[Chemical bond|chemical bond]] in metals, arising from electrostatic attraction between delocalized conduction electrons and positively charged metal ions. Metal atoms lose their valence electrons to a large, delocalized electron cloud, leaving [[Ion|cations]] that form a crystal lattice held together by the negative charge of that cloud. It can be described as a more delocalized version of [[Covalent bond|covalent bonding]]. Metallic bonding accounts for many properties of metals, including strength, ductility, thermal and electrical conductivity, opacity, and luster. A metal can also show other bonds: elemental [[Gallium|gallium]] consists of covalently bound atom pairs that form a crystal structure with metallic bonding between them. Quantum mechanics later gave the picture a formal basis in the free electron model, which treats electrons as a gas moving through the solid.

=== KEY: Metallic_hydrogen_a31c87
HEADWORD: Metallic hydrogen
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Exoplanet | Jupiter | Saturn | Superconductivity
ENTRY:
Electrically conducting phase of hydrogen. Eugene Wigner and Hillard Bell Huntington predicted it in 1935 on theoretical grounds. === KEY: Metallicity_7214bd
HEADWORD: Metallicity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Planet | Star | Sun | Supernova
ENTRY:
Relative abundance of elements heavier than hydrogen and helium in a [[Star|star]] or other astronomical object. Astronomers use the word metals for all elements except hydrogen and helium, a usage distinct from the chemical definition of a metal. Metallicity is most often expressed as [Fe/H], the logarithmic ratio of iron to hydrogen relative to the [[Sun]]'s value. Iron became the default reference element in the mid-20th century because it could be measured reliably. Heavy elements form in the cores of stars and are spread by stellar winds and [[Supernova|supernovae]]. Older stars formed in the metal-poor early universe and generally have lower metallicities than younger stars. In exoplanet systems, stars with elevated metallicity are more likely to host giant [[Planet|planets]].

=== KEY: Metalloid_606b51
HEADWORD: Metalloid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antimony | Arsenic | Boron | Germanium | Semiconductor | Silicon | Tellurium
ENTRY:
Element, or category of elements, with properties intermediate between metals and nonmetals. The word comes from the Latin *metallum*, meaning metal, but there is no standard definition of a metalloid and no complete agreement on which elements qualify. The six commonly recognized metalloids are [[Boron]], [[Silicon]], [[Germanium]], [[Arsenic]], [[Antimony]], and [[Tellurium]]. Carbon, aluminum, selenium, polonium, and astatine are less frequently classified as metalloids. Typical metalloids look metallic, may be brittle, and are only fair conductors of electricity. They and their compounds are used in alloys, catalysts, flame retardants, glasses, [[Semiconductor|semiconductors]], and electronics. Metalloids are sometimes called semimetals, a practice that has been discouraged because semimetal more commonly denotes a specific kind of electronic band structure.

=== KEY: Metallurgy_4a5cb6
HEADWORD: Metallurgy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Egypt | Materials science | Metalworking | Nanotechnology | Serbia | Smelting
ENTRY:
Domain of [[Materials science|materials science]] and engineering that studies the physical and chemical behavior of metallic elements, their intermetallic compounds, and their mixtures, known as [[Alloy|alloys]]. It covers both the science and the technology of metals, including their production. It is distinct from the craft of [[Metalworking|metalworking]] in providing a scientific foundation for it, much as medical science supports the practice of medicine.

The science divides into chemical metallurgy, concerned chiefly with the reduction and oxidation of metals and their chemical performance (mineral processing, extraction, thermodynamics, electrochemistry, corrosion), and physical metallurgy, which focuses on mechanical and physical properties (crystallography, material characterization, phase transformations, failure mechanisms). Metal production is subdivided into ferrous metallurgy, based on iron, and non-ferrous metallurgy, based on other metals; ferrous production accounts for 95% of world metal production. Emerging areas include [[Nanotechnology|nanotechnology]], superconductors, composites, and biomedical materials.

The earliest metal employed by humans appears to be gold, which occurs native; natural gold dating to the late Paleolithic, 40,000 BCE, has been found in Spanish caves. Early cold metallurgy using native copper is documented in Anatolia and at Tell Maghzaliyah in Iraq, from the 7th to 6th millennia BCE. The earliest evidence of [[Smelting|smelting]] in Eurasia comes from the Balkans and Carpathian Mountains, with objects dated to around 6,200 to 5,000 BCE; a smelted copper axe from Pločnik, in present-day [[Serbia]], dates to 5,500 BCE. The earliest documented use of lead in the Near East dates from the 6th millennium BCE. Copper smelting is documented in southeastern Iran from c. 5,000 BCE and in northern [[Egypt]] from c. 4,000 BCE.

=== KEY: Metalworking_a4f579
HEADWORD: Metalworking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gold | Iraq | Iron | Smelting | Trade | Welding
ENTRY:
Process of shaping and reshaping metals to create useful objects, parts, and large structures. Modern metalworking falls into three broad areas: forming, cutting, and joining. Its roots predate recorded history, when people worked soft native metals such as [[Gold|gold]] with simple hand tools. The oldest archaeological evidence of copper working is a pendant from [[Iraq|northern Iraq]] dated to 8,700 BCE. Later came [[Smelting|smelting]] of ores and hot forging of [[Iron|iron]], and modern techniques include machining and [[Welding|welding]]. Metalworking has been an industry, a driver of [[Trade|trade]], a hobby, and an art. Machine shops hold a wide range of machine tools for precise products, while blacksmithing is no longer economically competitive on a large scale in developed countries.

=== KEY: Metamaterial_444a0d

ENTRY:
Engineered material with properties arising from its designed structure rather than its chemical makeup. Metamaterials are typically fashioned from metals and plastics arranged in repeating patterns smaller than the [[Wavelength|wavelengths]] of the waves they affect. === KEY: Metamorphic_rock_a7b4bd
HEADWORD: Metamorphic rock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's crust | Gneiss | James Hutton | Marble | Schist | Slate
ENTRY:
Rock altered by heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids without fully melting. Metamorphic rock forms from a parent rock called the protolith, which may be igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic. Temperatures greater than 150 to 200 °C (300 to 400 °F) and elevated pressure of 100 megapascals (1,000 bar) or more cause the rock to recrystallize while it remains mostly solid. Metamorphic rocks make up a large part of the [[Earth's crust]] and form 12% of Earth's land surface. Common examples include [[Gneiss]], [[Slate]], [[Marble]], [[Schist]], and quartzite. Studying them reveals the temperatures and pressures that occur at great depths within the crust. The importance of heat was first noted by [[James Hutton]], who is often described as the father of modern geology.

=== KEY: Metamorphoses_3abe47
HEADWORD: Metamorphoses
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dante Alighieri | Geoffrey Chaucer | Giovanni Boccaccio | Julius Caesar | Ovid | Renaissance | William Shakespeare
ENTRY:
Latin narrative poem by [[Ovid]] in dactylic hexameter. Composed around 8 CE, *Metamorphoses* contains 15 books and over 250 myths, tracing the history of the world from its creation to the deification of [[Julius Caesar]]. The poem defies simple genre classification, though it is generally considered to meet the criteria for an epic. === KEY: Metamorphosis_e231f7
HEADWORD: Metamorphosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Frog | Hormone | Insect | Pupa | Tadpole
ENTRY:
Conspicuous, relatively abrupt change in an animal's body structure during its development. Some [[Insect|insects]], fish, amphibians, mollusks, crustaceans, cnidarians, echinoderms, and tunicates undergo metamorphosis, often with a change in nutrition or behavior. Animals with complete metamorphosis pass through radically different stages, while those with incomplete metamorphosis change between somewhat similar stages. In insects, [[Hormone|hormones]] control the process: ecdysone induces molting, and juvenile hormone prevents adult features during molts. Holometabolous insects enter an inactive [[Pupa|pupal]] stage, called a chrysalis in butterflies, before emerging as adults. Amphibians such as [[Frog|frogs]] transform from [[Tadpole|tadpoles]] into terrestrial adults. The evolution of metamorphosis in insects is thought to have fueled their dramatic radiation.

=== KEY: Metaphysics_96628a
HEADWORD: Metaphysics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Free will | Immanuel Kant | Philosophy
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Philosophy|philosophy]] that examines the basic nature or most fundamental structure of reality. It is traditionally seen as the study of mind-independent features of the world, though some theorists view it as an inquiry into the conceptual framework of human understanding. Some philosophers, including [[Aristotle]], call it "first philosophy," suggesting that it is more fundamental than other forms of philosophical inquiry.

Its topics include existence, the features all entities share and their division into categories of being, possibility and necessity, space, time, and change, causation, the relation of mind and matter, and whether there is [[Free will|free will]]. An influential division is between particulars, unique individual entities such as a specific apple, and universals, general features such as the color red. Metaphysics differs from the individual sciences, which examine more specific features of particular classes of entities.

Starting with [[Immanuel Kant]], an alternative conception focused on conceptual schemes rather than external reality. P. F. Strawson contrasted descriptive metaphysics, which articulates the schemes commonly used to understand the world, with revisionary metaphysics, which aims to produce better ones. Metaphysicians typically rely on rational intuitions and abstract reasoning but also use empirical approaches linked to scientific theories; critics question the reliability of its methods and the meaningfulness of its theories.

The name comes from the Greek *ta meta ta phusika*, "what comes after the physics," possibly because an editor, likely Andronicus of Rhodes, placed Aristotle's treatise after his works on physics. Its roots lie in the Upanishads of ancient India, Taoism in ancient China, and pre-Socratic philosophy in ancient Greece. The modern period produced comprehensive systems, many of them idealist; in the 20th century, traditional metaphysics and idealism in particular faced criticism that prompted new approaches.

=== KEY: Metaphysics_Aristotle_17f113
HEADWORD: Metaphysics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Plato
ENTRY:
Collection of texts by [[Aristotle]] on substance, causation, and first philosophy. Many of Aristotle's works are highly compressed, and the treatises are often thought to be lecture notes. The title, from the Greek for those after the physics, reflects the work's place after the *Physics* in an arrangement by Andronicus of Rhodes. The text covers substance theory, different kinds of causation, form and matter, and the existence of mathematical objects. Book Alpha surveys earlier philosophers' views on first principles and causes, from the Ionian monists up to [[Plato]]. Book Lambda culminates in an eternal, unmoved mover that serves as the final cause of cosmic motion. The order in which the books were written is unknown, since later editors arranged them.

=== KEY: Metastasis_a5e887
HEADWORD: Metastasis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Breast cancer
ENTRY:
Spread of a pathogenic agent from a primary site to a secondary site in the body. The term usually refers to a cancerous tumor, and the new sites are called metastases. Cancer cells can penetrate blood or lymphatic vessels and travel to other tissues, where they form a secondary tumor resembling the original. [[Breast cancer]] that spreads to the lungs forms a tumor called metastatic breast cancer, not lung cancer. In the TNM staging system, metastasis is represented by the letter M and places a cancer in Stage IV. The chance of curative treatment is greatly reduced once a cancer has metastasized.
