=== KEY: Papua_New_Guinea_f670d3

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Island country in [[Melanesia]], in Oceania, comprising the eastern half of [[New Guinea]] and offshore islands. Its capital is [[Port Moresby]]. Germany and Britain divided the eastern half of New Guinea in 1884. [[Australia]] took over the British portion in 1902 and occupied the German portion during World War I. The two territories were united in 1949, and Papua New Guinea became independent in 1975 as a [[Commonwealth of Nations|Commonwealth realm]]. Only 14% of the population lived in urban centers in 2023, and nearly 40% are subsistence farmers. Official estimates put the population at about 11.8 million, while satellite data suggest closer to 17 million. In March 2025 its constitution was amended to adopt Christianity as the official state religion.

=== KEY: Papyrus_f31721
HEADWORD: Papyrus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Codex | Great Pyramid of Giza | Paper | Parchment | Scroll
ENTRY:
Writing material made from the pith of the wetland sedge *Cyperus papyrus*. It was first manufactured in [[Ancient Egypt|Egypt]] in the 3rd millennium BCE, and Egyptians also used the reeds for boats, mats, rope, sandals, and baskets. Papyrus rolls excavated in 2012 and 2013 date from c. 2560–2550 BCE and describe the final years of building the [[Great Pyramid of Giza]]. Papyrus was rolled into [[Scroll|scrolls]], and later sheets were cut to form [[Codex|codices]]. [[Parchment]], made from animal skins, gradually replaced papyrus in West Asia and Europe because it was more durable in moist climates and could be written on both sides. Papyrus use in Egypt continued until less expensive [[Paper|paper]], introduced by the Islamic world, replaced it.

=== KEY: Parabola_df5a05
HEADWORD: Parabola
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollonius of Perga | Archimedes | Conic section | Isaac Newton
ENTRY:
Mirror-symmetrical, approximately U-shaped plane curve and a [[Conic section|conic section]]. It is the set of points equidistant from a fixed point, the focus, and a fixed line, the directrix. A parabola is also the graph of a quadratic function, y = ax² + bx + c. Light traveling parallel to its axis of symmetry is reflected to the focus, a property used in parabolic antennas, microphones, telescopes, and headlight reflectors. [[Apollonius of Perga]] gave the curve its name, and [[Archimedes]] calculated the area of a parabolic segment in the 3rd century BC. When [[Isaac Newton]] built the first reflecting telescope in 1668, he used a spherical mirror because a parabolic one was difficult to make.

Swiss physician, alchemist, and philosopher of the German [[Renaissance]]. === KEY: Paradigm_3e9adb
HEADWORD: Paradigm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Ferdinand de Saussure | Philosophy of science
ENTRY:
Distinct set of concepts or thought patterns, including theories, research methods, postulates, and standards for legitimate contributions to a field. The word comes from [[Ancient Greek]] *parádeigma*, meaning "pattern" or "example." In linguistics, [[Ferdinand de Saussure]] used the term for a class of elements with similarities. The historian of science Thomas Kuhn gave the word its contemporary meaning in *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962). For Kuhn, a paradigm is a set of accepted achievements that provide model problems and solutions to a community of practitioners. Normal science proceeds within a paradigm until anomalies accumulate and a paradigm shift occurs. The term is closely related to the discussion of theory-ladenness in the [[Philosophy of science|philosophy of science]]. In grammar, a paradigm is a systematic table of the inflected forms of a word.

=== KEY: Paraguayan_War_48d5bf

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Paraguay | South America
ENTRY:
Conflict in [[South America]], fought from 1864 to 1870 between [[Paraguay]] and the Triple Alliance of Argentina, Brazil and Uruguay. Also called the War of the Triple Alliance, it was the deadliest interstate war in Latin American history. It began in late 1864 from a conflict between Paraguay and Brazil arising from the Uruguayan War, and Argentina and Uruguay joined in 1865. === KEY: Parallax_c62628
HEADWORD: Parallax
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binoculars | Cosmic distance ladder | Microscope | Sun
ENTRY:
Apparent displacement of an object viewed from two different lines of sight. The displacement is measured by the angle between those sight lines, and nearby objects show a larger parallax than distant ones, so parallax can be used to find distances. In astronomy, stellar parallax, observed when Earth is on opposite sides of the [[Sun]] in its orbit, forms the lowest rung of the [[Cosmic distance ladder|cosmic distance ladder]]. German astronomer Friedrich Bessel measured the parallax of 61 Cygni as 0.314 arcseconds in 1838. Parallax also affects optical instruments such as [[Binoculars]] and [[Microscope|microscopes]]. Many animals, including humans, use it for depth perception through stereopsis, in which the brain combines the slightly different views from the two eyes. Parallax scrolling creates an illusion of depth in some 2D video games.

=== KEY: Parallel_computing_b61980
HEADWORD: Parallel computing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer architecture | Computer science | Graphics processing unit | Supercomputer
ENTRY:
Computation in which many calculations or processes are carried out simultaneously. Large problems are divided into smaller ones that are solved at the same time, using bit-level, instruction-level, data or task parallelism. Parallelism has long been used in high-performance computing. Physical limits on processor frequency scaling, along with concern over power consumption and heat, have made parallelism the dominant paradigm in [[Computer architecture]], chiefly as multi-core processors. In [[Computer science]], parallelism differs from concurrency: parallel programs use several CPU cores at once, while concurrency lets a single core switch between tasks. Clusters and [[Supercomputer|supercomputers]] use multiple computers on one task, and [[Graphics processing unit|GPUs]] accelerate specialized work. Explicitly parallel programs are harder to write, because concurrency introduces bugs such as race conditions. Amdahl's law sets an upper bound on speed-up, limited by the fraction of a program that can be parallelized.

=== KEY: Paramecium_c35814
HEADWORD: Paramecium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ciliate | Model organism
ENTRY:
Genus of single-celled [[Ciliate|ciliates]] found in freshwater, brackish and marine habitats. Paramecia are covered with thousands of short, hair-like cilia that beat in rows to propel the cell and sweep food into an oral groove. They typically measure 50 to 350 μm long, and they often abound in stagnant ponds. A large macronucleus controls metabolism and gene expression, while smaller micronuclei are involved in reproduction. Most reproduction is asexual, by transverse binary fission. Sexual reproduction occurs through conjugation, in which compatible mating types exchange genetic material. A contractile vacuole expels excess water in freshwater habitats. Because they are easy to culture, *P. aurelia* and *P. caudatum* are widely used as [[Model organism|model organisms]], and they have been called the "lab rats" of the phylum Ciliophora. *P. bursaria* hosts symbiotic green algae.

=== KEY: Paranormal_b1c7e9

ENTRY:
Purported phenomena outside normal scientific understanding. === KEY: Paranthropus_204541
HEADWORD: Paranthropus
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Contested genus of extinct hominins, sometimes treated as synonymous with [[Australopithecus]]. Three species are widely accepted: *P. aethiopicus*, *P. robustus* and *P. boisei*. They lived from about 2.9 to at least 1.2 million years ago, from the end of the [[Pliocene]] into the Middle Pleistocene. Their robust skulls had prominent sagittal crests, which suggest strong chewing muscles, and broad teeth for grinding. Their bodies were small: males of *P. robustus* were estimated at 40 kg and 132 cm, and males of *P. boisei* at 50 kg and 137 cm. *P. robustus* was probably an omnivore, while *P. boisei* was likely herbivorous. Large carnivores of the time, including crocodiles, leopards and hyenas, preyed on them.

=== KEY: Parasitism_e8bceb
HEADWORD: Parasitism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Malaria | Predation
ENTRY:
Close interaction between species in which a parasite, living on or in a host, harms it. Parasites range from single-celled protozoans, such as the agents of [[Malaria]] and sleeping sickness, to animals such as hookworms, lice and vampire bats, and plants such as mistletoe. Unlike [[Predation|predators]], parasites are usually much smaller than their hosts and usually do not kill them, and they often live in or on a host for an extended period. An endoparasite lives inside its host, and an ectoparasite lives on its surface. Parasitism shades into predation through parasitoidism, and through evolution into mutualism. Human knowledge of parasites dates to ancient Egypt, Greece and Rome, and [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] observed *Giardia lamblia* in 1681. In fiction, Bram Stoker's *Dracula* (1897) features a blood-drinking parasite.

=== KEY: Parchment_90a04e
HEADWORD: Parchment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Middle Ages | Paper | Papyrus | Pumice
ENTRY:
Writing material made from the prepared, untanned skins of animals, primarily sheep, calves and goats. It was used as a writing medium in West Asia and Europe for more than two millennia. By 400 AD, many works intended for preservation had been transferred from [[Papyrus]] to parchment. According to ancient sources, it was developed at Pergamum in the second century BC as an alternative after an export embargo from Egypt. Vellum is a finer parchment made from calfskin. Hides are soaked in water and lime, scraped, stretched on a frame and smoothed with [[Pumice]]. One hide yields only a few sheets; a 229-folio book required about 115 skins. Parchment dominated early [[Middle Ages|medieval]] Northwestern Europe, especially for illuminated manuscripts and legal documents, until cheaper [[Paper|paper]] spread from the 12th century. It remains sensitive to humidity and water.

=== KEY: Parenting_ac22e7
HEADWORD: Parenting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Culture | Government | Grandparent | Orphanage | Parent | Social class
ENTRY:
Process of promoting and supporting a child's physical, cognitive, social, emotional, and educational development from infancy to adulthood. Parenting concerns the raising of a child and is not exclusively a biological relationship. The most common caregivers are biological [[Parent|parents]], but a caregiver may be an older sibling, step-parent, [[Grandparent|grandparent]], legal guardian, aunt, uncle, other relative, or family friend. [[Government|Governments]] and society may also have a role. Orphaned or abandoned children may be cared for by non-relatives, adopted, raised in foster care, or placed in an [[Orphanage|orphanage]].

Styles vary by historical period, [[Culture|culture]], [[Social class|social class]], and personal preferences, and no single style is necessarily correct, since styles affect children differently depending on circumstances and temperament. Developmental psychologist Diana Baumrind proposed three main styles, authoritative, authoritarian, and permissive, later expanded to four with an uninvolved style. Authoritative parenting, which Baumrind called the "just right" style, combines medium demands with medium responsiveness and relies on positive reinforcement and infrequent punishment; children raised this way score higher in competence, mental health, and social development. Authoritarian parenting is rigid and strict, with high demands, little responsiveness, and non-negotiable rules; it is strongly associated with corporal punishment, and in 1983 Baumrind found that such children were less cheerful, moodier, and more vulnerable to stress. Permissive parenting (undemanding, with little punishment) has been more popular among middle-class than working-class families roughly since the end of World War II. Critics regard Baumrind's typology as overly broad and as idealizing the authoritative style.

Research also indicates that parents' own attachment history and psychopathology can influence parental sensitivity and child outcomes, and that warm adoptive parenting is associated with reduced behavioral problems in adopted children over time.

=== KEY: Pareto_principle_949c28
HEADWORD: Pareto principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Kingdom of Italy | Vilfredo Pareto
ENTRY:
Empirical rule of thumb: roughly 80% of effects from 20% of causes. Also called the 80:20 rule or the law of the vital few, it is named after [[Vilfredo Pareto]], an Italian economist who observed that about 80% of land in the [[Kingdom of Italy]] was owned by 20% of the population. In 1941 management consultant Joseph M. Juran developed the concept for quality control. The rule is associated with the power-law distribution called the Pareto distribution. Ratios vary, and critics note that the principle is a heuristic that risks oversimplification.

=== KEY: Paris_Commune_fd70f0
HEADWORD: Paris Commune
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolphe Thiers | Franco-Prussian War | Friedrich Engels | Karl Marx | New Caledonia | Paris
ENTRY:
Revolutionary government in [[Paris]] that held power from March 18 to May 28, 1871. It arose after the French defeat in the [[Franco-Prussian War]], when National Guard soldiers seized the city and refused to accept the authority of the Third Republic. The Commune's policies included the separation of church and state, the remission of rents, the abolition of child labor and the closing of Catholic churches and schools. Socialist, feminist and anarchist currents all played roles. === KEY: Parity_mathematics_638ed4

Property of an [[Integer|integer]] as even or odd. An integer is even if it is divisible by 2 and odd if it is not; for example, −4, 0 and 82 are even, while −7, 5 and 23 are odd. === KEY: Particle_detector_868953

=== KEY: Particle_physics_f17da3
HEADWORD: Particle physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antimatter | CERN | Hans Bethe | Higgs boson | Large Hadron Collider | Lise Meitner | Quark | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Branch of physics, also called high-energy physics, that studies the fundamental particles and forces that constitute matter and radiation. The [[Standard Model]], the dominant theory, classifies fundamental particles as fermions (matter particles) and bosons (force-carrying particles). Ordinary matter is made only from the first of three fermion generations: up and down [[Quark|quarks]], which form protons and neutrons, and electrons and electron neutrinos. Quarks cannot exist alone but form hadrons; baryons contain an odd number of quarks and mesons an even number. Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and opposite electric charge, such as the positron for the electron, and such particles could form [[Antimatter|antimatter]].

The idea that matter is composed of elementary particles dates from at least the 6th century BC. Nuclear fission was proved in 1939 by [[Lise Meitner]], based on experiments by Otto Hahn, and nuclear fusion by [[Hans Bethe]] the same year. In the 1950s and 1960s collisions at ever higher energies revealed a bewildering "particle zoo," which the Standard Model, formulated in the 1970s, explained as combinations of a small number of more fundamental particles. The model contains 24 fundamental fermions, eight gluons, the W⁻, W⁺, and Z bosons, and the photon. It also predicted the [[Higgs boson]]; on July 4, 2012, physicists at the [[Large Hadron Collider]] at [[CERN]] announced a new particle that behaves as expected for it.

The Standard Model agrees with almost all experimental tests, but most particle physicists consider it incomplete. It does not incorporate gravity, and measurements of neutrino mass provide the first experimental deviations from it.

ENTRY:
Infinite triangular array of binomial coefficients, used in [[Combinatorics|combinatorics]], [[Probability theory|probability theory]], and algebra. Each entry is the sum of the two numbers above it, starting from a single 1 at the apex. The nth row gives the coefficients in the expansion of (a + b)ⁿ, linking the array to the [[Binomial theorem|binomial theorem]]. === KEY: Pascal_s_wager_f7a959

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blaise Pascal | God | Heaven | Hell
ENTRY:
Philosophical argument for believing in [[God]], advanced by [[Blaise Pascal]]. A rational person should wager on God's existence. If God does not exist, the believer loses only certain pleasures. If God exists, the believer gains an eternity in [[Heaven]] and avoids an eternity in [[Hell]]. Reason cannot settle the question, so a wager is unavoidable. === KEY: Pascal_unit_9e5632
HEADWORD: Pascal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blaise Pascal | International System of Units | Pressure | Standard atmosphere (unit) | Stress (mechanics)
ENTRY:
Unit of [[Pressure|pressure]] in the [[International System of Units]] (SI). The pascal (Pa) is defined as the pressure exerted by a force of one newton on an area of one square meter, or one newton per square meter (N/m²). In SI base units it equals one kilogram per meter per second squared (kg/(m·s²)). The unit is named after [[Blaise Pascal]], noted for his work on hydrodynamics, hydrostatics, and experiments with a barometer, and it was adopted by the 14th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1971. Because the pascal is small, larger multiples are common: the kilopascal (kPa) and the hectopascal (hPa), which equals one millibar. The [[Standard atmosphere (unit)|standard atmosphere]] is defined as 101,325 Pa, and meteorologists report sea-level pressure in hectopascals, typically about 1,013 hPa. Engineers prefer the megapascal (MPa) for the [[Stress (mechanics)|stress]], tensile strength, and stiffness of materials.

=== KEY: Pasteurization_cb5f38

ENTRY:
Process of preserving foods, such as [[Milk|milk]] and fruit juices, with mild heat. Organism or agent that can produce disease, also called an infectious agent or germ. The term came into use in the 1880s. In 1976 she was convicted of bank robbery and sentenced to 35 years in prison, later reduced to seven years. [[Jimmy Carter]] commuted her sentence, and [[Bill Clinton]] later pardoned her. In the 1990s she began acting after [[John Waters]] approached her.

American businessman and philanthropist who co-founded [[Microsoft]] with [[Bill Gates]] in 1975. He owned the Seattle Seahawks of the [[National Football League]] and the Portland Trail Blazers of the [[National Basketball Association]]. Through Vulcan Inc., founded in 1986 to manage his business and philanthropy, he gave more than $2 billion to causes including education, conservation and the arts. He funded SpaceShipOne, the first crewed private spaceplane. He also discovered the wrecks of the Japanese battleship *Musashi* and USS *Indianapolis*.

Canadian-born singer, songwriter and actor. His song "Diana" went to number one on the Canadian and U.S. charts. He wrote the English lyrics to "My Way," [[Frank Sinatra]]'s signature song, and the theme for *The Tonight Show Starring Johnny Carson*. He wrote "She's a Lady" for [[Tom Jones (singer)|Tom Jones]] and toured Britain and Australia with [[Buddy Holly]]. He co-wrote three songs with [[Michael Jackson]], which became posthumous hits for Jackson.
