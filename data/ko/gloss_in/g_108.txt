Maritime facility of one or more wharves or loading areas where ships load and discharge cargo and passengers. Ports are usually situated on a sea coast or estuary, but some lie far inland, such as [[Hamburg]], and reach the sea by rivers or canals. Ports carry 70% of global merchandise trade by value, and port cities often supply the labor for processing and handling goods. The greatest growth in port development is in [[Asia]], home to some of the world's largest and busiest ports, such as Singapore and Shanghai. One of the world's oldest known artificial harbors is at Wadi al-Jarf on the [[Red Sea]]. Ostia Antica was the port of [[Ancient Rome|ancient Rome]], with Portus established by [[Claudius]] and enlarged by [[Trajan]]. Dredging and spills affect water quality, and most port infrastructure is extremely vulnerable to sea level rise and coastal flooding caused by [[Climate change|climate change]].

=== KEY: Portuguese_Empire_ca24ba
HEADWORD: Portuguese Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | India | Lisbon | Macau | Nagasaki | Pedro Álvares Cabral | Philip II of Spain | Vasco da Gama
ENTRY:
The first and longest-lasting of the European colonial empires, initiated during the [[Age of Discovery]]. Portuguese sailors explored Africa's coast from 1418–1419 in search of a sea route to the spice trade. [[Vasco da Gama]] reached [[India]] in 1498, and [[Pedro Álvares Cabral]] reached present-day Brazil in 1500. By 1571, a string of outposts linked [[Lisbon]] to [[Nagasaki]]. === KEY: Portuguese_language_f74348
HEADWORD: Portuguese language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angola | Brazil | Equatorial Guinea | Latin | Macau | Mozambique | Phonology | Portugal
ENTRY:
Western Romance language of the Indo-European language family. With approximately 267 million native speakers, it is the fifth-most spoken native language in the world and the most widely spoken in the Southern Hemisphere. It is the official language of eight countries: [[Portugal]], [[Brazil]], [[Angola]], [[Mozambique]], Cape Verde, Guinea-Bissau, São Tomé and Príncipe, and Timor-Leste. It is co-official with other languages in [[Macau]] and [[Equatorial Guinea]].

Portuguese belongs to the Ibero-Romance group, which evolved from dialects of Vulgar [[Latin]] in the medieval Kingdom of Galicia and the County of Portugal, and it retains some Celtic phonology. The Roman presence in the Iberian Peninsula began in 218 BC. In Gallaecia, the northwest, Vulgar Latin lost intervocalic *l* and *n* (*luna* to *lua*) and simplified double consonants (*annu* to *ano*). Between the 9th and 12th centuries the language emerged as Galician-Portuguese; the earliest known Portuguese document, the *Notícia de Fiadores*, dates to 1175. King Denis (1279–1325) promoted its use in literature and administration. Maritime expansion in the 15th and 16th centuries carried it to Africa, Asia, and the Americas.

Its [[Phonology|phonology]] has a rich system of nasal vowels and varied guttural *r* sounds. Spelling uses the Latin alphabet and is largely phonemic. Grammar retains many Latin verb forms and has unusual features such as the future subjunctive and the personal infinitive. Vocabulary is largely Latin, with loanwords from Celtic, Germanic, Arabic, African, Amerindian, and Asian languages.

The two main standardized varieties are European Portuguese and Brazilian Portuguese, each with regional accents. African and Asian varieties generally follow the European written standard. The varieties are broadly mutually intelligible, and a 1990 orthographic agreement sought a unified spelling.

=== KEY: Positional_notation_5c5389
HEADWORD: Positional notation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Degree (angle) | Hindu–Arabic numeral system | Real number | Roman numerals
ENTRY:
Numeral system property in which each symbol's value depends on its position, as a power of a fixed base. The [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]] is the most common today, a base-ten positional system in which each digit is multiplied by a power of ten. Earlier additive systems, such as [[Roman numerals]], give each symbol a fixed value. The Babylonians developed the first positional system, in base 60, whose legacy persists in 60 minutes in an hour and 360 [[Degree (angle)|degrees]] in a circle. Early Babylonian numerals lacked a true zero, so 2 and 120 could look the same. Binary, in base two, is used in almost all computers because it is easier to implement in electronic circuits. A radix point extends positional notation to fractions and to any [[Real number|real number]] with arbitrary accuracy. The notation made arithmetic far simpler, which led to its rapid spread in western Europe.

=== KEY: Positivism_c82e6c
HEADWORD: Positivism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Auguste Comte | Economics | Mathematics | Psychology | Reductionism | Scientific method
ENTRY:
Philosophical school holding that genuine knowledge is either true by definition or derived from sensory experience through reason and logic. It rejects intuition, introspection and religious faith as ways of knowing. Modern positivism was first articulated in the early 19th century by [[Auguste Comte]], whose sociological positivism held that society, like the physical world, operates according to scientific laws. Comte ranked the sciences by exactness, from [[Mathematics]] to sociology, which he called the "queen science". Positivist schools later arose in [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Economics|economics]], logic and historiography, attempting to apply the [[Scientific method|scientific method]] to their fields. Since the turn of the 20th century, positivism has declined under criticism for its alleged scientism, [[Reductionism|reductionism]] and overgeneralizations. It also influenced Kardecism.

=== KEY: Positron_3df650
HEADWORD: Positron
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cloud chamber | Cosmic ray | Dirac equation | Electron | Nobel Prize | Paul Dirac | Photon | Weak interaction
ENTRY:
Antiparticle of the [[Electron|electron]], with the same mass and an electric charge of +1e. When a positron collides with an electron at low energies, annihilation produces two or more [[Photon|photons]]. Positrons arise from positron-emission radioactive decay, which proceeds through the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]], or from pair production when a sufficiently energetic photon interacts with matter. In 1928, [[Paul Dirac]] published the [[Dirac equation]], and in 1931 he predicted an "anti-electron" with the electron's mass and opposite charge. Carl David Anderson discovered the positron in 1932 at the California Institute of Technology, using [[Cosmic ray|cosmic ray]] tracks in a [[Cloud chamber|cloud chamber]]. The discovery earned him the 1936 [[Nobel Prize]] in Physics.

=== KEY: Post_Impressionism_acc217
HEADWORD: Post-Impressionism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Georges Seurat | Impressionism | Paul Cézanne | Paul Gauguin | Vincent van Gogh
ENTRY:
Predominantly French art movement that developed roughly between 1886 and 1905, from the last [[Impressionism|Impressionist]] exhibition to the birth of Fauvism. It emerged as a reaction against Impressionist concern with the naturalistic depiction of light and color. Its principal artists were [[Paul Cézanne]], [[Paul Gauguin]], [[Vincent van Gogh]] and [[Georges Seurat]]. Cézanne reduced objects to basic shapes while keeping Impressionist color, and Seurat developed pointillism, the systematic use of tiny dots of color. Post-Impressionists extended Impressionism while rejecting its limitations, emphasizing geometric form, expressive distortion and unnatural color, but they did not agree on a cohesive movement. Critic Roger Fry popularized the term, using it for his 1910 exhibition *Manet and the Post-Impressionists*.

=== KEY: Post_World_War_II_economic_expansion_819eb9
HEADWORD: Post–World War II economic expansion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | France | Italy | Japan | Richard Nixon | Sweden | West Germany | World War II
ENTRY:
Long period of worldwide economic growth after [[World War II]], also called the Golden Age of Capitalism. It began in the aftermath of the war and ended with the 1973–1975 recession, which followed the 1971 collapse of the Bretton Woods monetary system and the closing of the gold window by [[Richard Nixon]]. The United States, the Soviet Union, Australia and Western European and East Asian countries experienced unusually high growth, together with full employment. Countries devastated by war grew strongly as well, including [[Japan]], [[West Germany]], [[Italy]] and [[France]], while relatively unaffected [[Sweden]] also grew considerably. === KEY: Post_classical_history_393b7d
HEADWORD: Post-classical history
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black Death | Feudalism | Gunpowder | Islamic Golden Age | Middle Ages | Mongol Empire | Silk Road
ENTRY:
Period of world history from about 500 to about 1450 or 1500, roughly corresponding to the European [[Middle Ages]] after the decline of the Western Roman Empire. It is also called the medieval era, post-antiquity era, post-ancient era, or pre-modern era. The post-classical period is marked by the geographic expansion of certain civilizations, ongoing wars over land, resources, and religion, and the development of trade networks between distant civilizations.

The term is a periodization used by world historians, a school developed in the late 20th and early 21st centuries, and is one of five or six major periods: early civilization, classical societies, post-classical, early modern, the long nineteenth century, and the contemporary era. Its start varies by region, following the end of Han China (220), the Western Roman Empire (476), the Gupta Empire (543), and the Sasanian Empire (651). Some historians use it to avoid preconceptions attached to the terms Middle Ages and Dark Ages, though applying it globally is problematic and may be Eurocentric.

In Asia and the Middle East, the spread of Islam produced a series of caliphates that fostered the [[Islamic Golden Age]], with advances in science and greater trade among Asia, Africa, and Europe. East Asia saw the entrenchment of a unitary imperial China, whose dynastic governance and culture influenced Japan, Korea, and Vietnam. Buddhism and neo-Confucianism spread there, while Christianity became entrenched in Europe. [[Gunpowder]] was developed in China. The [[Mongol Empire]], which one account dates 1206–1368, conquered much of Europe and Asia and permitted more trade and cultural exchange along the [[Silk Road]]. In West Africa the Mali and Songhai Empires formed, and in the Americas Mesoamerican and Andean civilizations developed complex urban centers. In Western Europe, the fall of Rome gave way to [[Feudalism|feudalism]] and manorialism, while the Eastern Roman Empire (Byzantium) retained many features of old Rome.

World population broadly doubled, from approximately 210 million in 500 to some 461 million in 1500. Growth was slow, with high mortality and rapid declines caused by events including the Plague of Justinian, the Mongol invasions, and the [[Black Death]] (1347–1351). Some commentators view the history of the Earth's climate as a useful approach, noting that certain climate events affected all human populations.

=== KEY: Post_traumatic_stress_disorder_a65b18
HEADWORD: Post-traumatic stress disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benzodiazepine | Mental disorder | Rape | Sexual assault | United States | Vietnam War
ENTRY:
[[Mental disorder]] that develops after a traumatic event, such as [[Sexual assault]], domestic violence, war or natural disaster. Symptoms include intrusive thoughts or dreams about the event, distress at reminders, and avoidance, lasting more than a month. Most people exposed to trauma do not develop it, but interpersonal violence such as [[Rape]] carries higher risk than accidents. In the [[United States]], about 3.5% of adults have it in a given year, and 9% develop it at some point in their lives. Psychotherapy and medication are the main treatments, and SSRI or SNRI antidepressants are first-line, moderately beneficial for about half of people. [[Benzodiazepine|Benzodiazepines]] may worsen outcomes. The term came into use in the 1970s, largely through diagnoses of [[Vietnam War]] veterans.

=== KEY: Postage_stamp_898b81

Small adhesive paper issued by a postal authority to show that postage has been paid. It is affixed to mail and canceled with a postmark so that it cannot be reused. Stamps feature the issuing nation's name and a denomination, except in the [[United Kingdom]], where the monarch's image signifies origin. === KEY: Postmodern_architecture_38ebd3
HEADWORD: Postmodern architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Modern architecture | Museum of Modern Art | Philip Johnson
ENTRY:
Architectural style of the 1960s, a reaction against the austerity and formality of [[Modern architecture|modern architecture]]. Robert Venturi's 1966 book *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture*, published by the [[Museum of Modern Art]] in New York, called for complexity, historical references and ornament over modernist purity. In *Learning from Las Vegas* (1972), Venturi and Denise Scott Brown urged architects to celebrate existing architecture rather than impose visionary utopias. Charles Jencks declared the "death" of modern architecture in *The Language of Post-Modern Architecture* (1977). The style flourished in the 1980s and 1990s through the work of [[Philip Johnson]], Charles Moore and Michael Graves. In the late 1990s it divided into high-tech architecture, neo-futurism, new classical architecture and deconstructivism.

=== KEY: Postmodernism_75abbb
HEADWORD: Postmodernism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Feminism | Jacques Derrida | Michel Foucault | Modernism | Relativism
ENTRY:
Set of artistic, cultural and philosophical movements that arose in the mid-20th century as a skeptical response to [[Modernism|modernism]]. It emphasizes the instability of meaning, the rejection of universal truths and the critique of grand narratives. Its practice includes eclectic styles, performative irony and pluralism. In the 1990s the term came to denote a generally celebratory response to cultural pluralism, aligned with [[Feminism|feminism]] and multiculturalism. Its philosophical core drew on [[Jacques Derrida]] and [[Michel Foucault]], and Jean-François Lyotard defined it as "incredulity toward metanarratives." Critics argue that it leads to a nihilistic form of [[Relativism|relativism]] and supplants moral and political ideals with style and spectacle. Its influence persists in arts, architecture, literature, music and fashion.

=== KEY: Potassium_3a4edc
HEADWORD: Potassium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alkali metal | Electrolysis | Fertilizer | Humphry Davy | Igneous rock | Potash | Sodium
ENTRY:
Chemical element, atomic number 19, symbol K. A silvery white [[Alkali metal|alkali metal]] soft enough to cut with a knife, potassium reacts rapidly with atmospheric oxygen and vigorously with water. It was first isolated in 1807 by [[Humphry Davy]] through [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]], and its name derives from [[Potash|potash]], the ashes of plants. Potassium is chemically similar to [[Sodium|sodium]], and most of its industrial uses exploit the high water solubility of its compounds. It occurs in many minerals, such as orthoclase, a common constituent of granites and other [[Igneous rock|igneous rocks]], and it is dissolved in seawater. Potassium ions are vital to all living cells, and both deficiency and excess can cause an abnormal heart rhythm. Agricultural [[Fertilizer|fertilizers]] containing potassium account for 95% of global potassium chemical production. Naturally occurring potassium includes radioactive 40K, the most common radioisotope in the human body.

Metal halide salt of potassium and chlorine (KCl). It is odorless, forms white or colorless crystals, and dissolves readily in water, with a salt-like taste. === KEY: Potassium_hydroxide_6ad7ff

Inorganic compound (KOH), commonly called caustic potash. === KEY: Potato_bb8e09
HEADWORD: Potato
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Starchy tuberous vegetable native to the Americas, eaten as a staple food in many parts of the world. Potatoes are underground stem tubers of *Solanum tuberosum*, a perennial in the nightshade family [[Solanaceae]]. Wild species occur from the southern United States to southern Chile. The cultivated potato has a single origin in present-day southern Peru and extreme northwestern Bolivia, around [[Lake Titicaca]], where it was domesticated about 7,000–10,000 years ago from a species in the *S. brevicaule* complex.

=== KEY: Potential_energy_4b883e

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Energy an object or system has because of its position relative to other objects, or the configuration of its particles. The term was introduced by the Scottish engineer and physicist William Rankine, who coined it in 1853, though it has links to [[Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s concept of potentiality. === KEY: Pottery_4299ed
HEADWORD: Pottery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ceramic art | China | Clay | Mesopotamia | Neolithic | Porcelain | Potter's wheel | Tang dynasty
ENTRY:
Craft and products of forming vessels and other objects from [[Clay|clay]] and other raw materials, fired at high temperatures to give them a hard and durable form. Firing, at 600–1,400 °C in a bonfire, pit, or kiln, causes permanent changes that increase strength and rigidity. End uses include tableware, decorative ware, sanitary ware, and electrical insulators. Some pottery is regarded as [[Ceramic art|ceramic art]]. In art history and archaeology, pottery often means only vessels, and sculpted figurines of the same material are called terracottas.

It is one of the oldest human inventions, originating before the [[Neolithic]] period. The earliest known vessels, from Jiangxi, [[China]], date to 18,000 BC. Other early finds come from the Russian Far East (14,000 BC), Jōmon Japan (10,500 BC), and Sub-Saharan Africa (9,400 BC). Early methods were hand-building, by pinching or coiling clay; the [[Potter's wheel|potter's wheel]] was invented in [[Mesopotamia]] around 3500 BC.

Pottery is traditionally divided into earthenware, stoneware, and porcelain, each of which may be glazed or unglazed. Earthenware, the earliest form, can be fired as low as 600 °C and is normally fired below 1,200 °C; unglazed, it is porous, and reddish varieties are called terracotta. Stoneware, fired at about 1,100–1,200 °C, is non-porous and strong. The Chinese developed it very early, whereas in Europe it could be produced only from the late Middle Ages and remained a speciality of Germany until the Renaissance. [[Porcelain]], made from materials generally including kaolin and fired at 1,200–1,400 °C is tougher, stronger, and translucent. First made in China, it reached considerable whiteness and translucency by the [[Tang dynasty]] (AD 618–906), and the modern level of whiteness came only in the 14th century. Korea and Japan made it from the end of the 16th century.

=== KEY: Poverty_c31ca1
HEADWORD: Poverty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deforestation | Ethnic conflict | European Union | Non-governmental organization | Purchasing power parity | United Nations
ENTRY:
State in which an individual or community lacks the financial resources and essentials for a basic standard of living. Statisticians and economists use two main measures. Absolute poverty compares income against the amount needed to meet basic needs such as food, clothing, and shelter; relative poverty applies when a person cannot meet a minimum level of living standards compared with others in the same time and place, and its definition varies between societies. The "dollar a day" line, introduced in 1990, is set by [[Purchasing power parity]] rather than market exchange rates.

Definitions differ by institution. The [[United Nations|UN]] describes poverty as "a denial of choices and opportunities, a violation of human dignity." The [[European Union]] measures it relative to each member country's income distribution. As of 2019, 85% of people lived on less than $30 per day in purchasing power parity dollars, two-thirds on less than $10, and 10% on less than $1.90.

Gender, disability, race, and ethnicity can exacerbate poverty, with women, children, and minorities frequently bearing unequal burdens. Poor people are more vulnerable to climate change and natural disasters, and economic pressure on poor communities frequently contributes to [[Deforestation|deforestation]], biodiversity loss, and [[Ethnic conflict|ethnic conflict]]. Governments and [[Non-governmental organization|non-governmental organizations]] have tried policies such as rural electrification. The UN's 2015 framework for poverty alleviation is Sustainable Development Goal 1, "No Poverty."

Extreme poverty is estimated to have afflicted over 90% of humanity in 1820 and under 10% today, a decline some analysts credit to economic growth in regions such as East Asia. Analysts disagree on causes, and some argue that relative definitions tied to national medians conflate inequality with destitution.

=== KEY: Power_political_science_8e7e0f
HEADWORD: Power
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Government | Hannah Arendt | Institution | Max Weber | Military | State (polity)
ENTRY:
Ability to direct the actions and conduct of others, a central concept of political science.

Political power is usually exercised through [[Government|government]] offices with authority, over political societies often termed [[State (polity)|states]]. It does not refer only to the threat or use of force (coercion) by one actor against another but may also be exerted through diffuse means such as [[Institution|institutions]]. Authority is the term often used for power perceived as legitimate or socially approved by the social structure; political authority grants members of a government the right to rule over citizens; citizens have an obligation to obey.

Thinkers have defined power differently. Robert Dahl views it as a form of "relations among people" in which one party can command another. [[Hannah Arendt]] defined it as an instrument of political rule and dissociated it from the ability to use violence. [[Max Weber]] defined it as "any chance (regardless of the basis of this chance) to carry through one's own will", even against outright opposition.

Scholars distinguish soft power, the ability to influence or persuade by persuasive means, from hard power, the use of [[Military|military]] and economic means to influence the behavior or interests of other political bodies.

Approach/inhibition theory, developed by D. Keltner and colleagues, holds that power promotes approach tendencies such as action, self-promotion, and seeking rewards, while reduced power promotes inhibition, such as self-protection and avoiding threats. In 1959 the social psychologists John R. P. French and Bertram Raven proposed five bases of power.

=== KEY: Power_supply_7bdc6b

=== KEY: Pragmatics_cbfd23

Branch of [[Linguistics|linguistics]] and [[Semiotics|semiotics]] that studies how context contributes to meaning. === KEY: Prayer_10a51e
HEADWORD: Prayer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deity | Fasting | Incense | Liturgy | Mantra | Mecca | Meditation
ENTRY:
Invocation or act that seeks to activate a rapport with an object of worship through deliberate communication.

In the narrow sense, prayer is supplication or intercession directed to a [[Deity|deity]] or a deified ancestor; it may also give thanks or praise, and in comparative religion it is closely associated with more abstract forms of [[Meditation|meditation]] and with charms or spells. It may be part of a set [[Liturgy|liturgy]] or ritual, and may be performed alone or in groups, as a hymn, incantation, formal creedal statement, or spontaneous utterance. Prayer is attested in written sources as early as five thousand years ago. Most major religions involve it; some ritualize it, requiring a strict sequence of actions or restricting who may pray, while others teach that anyone may pray spontaneously at any time.

The English word derives from Medieval Latin *precaria*, "petition, prayer". Practices vary widely. Some Christians bow their heads and fold their hands, Hindus chant [[Mantra|mantras]], Jewish prayer may involve swaying and bowing, Muslim prayer involves bowing, kneeling, and prostration, and some Sufis whirl. Accompanying acts include burning [[Incense|incense]], lighting candles, facing [[Mecca]], and [[Fasting|fasting]].

The early Christian posture was standing with outspread arms; kneeling with clasped hands appears to have been introduced only in the high medieval period, presumably adopted from a gesture of feudal homage. In animist traditions, communication with the spirit world is usually accomplished through a shaman in a trance.

Scientific studies have concentrated mostly on prayer's effect on the healing of the sick or injured, with contradictory results; many well-controlled trials found no effect. Other studies report psychological benefits, such as reduced anxiety, for the person praying.

HEADWORD: Pre-Columbian era
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaeology | Bering Strait | Cahokia | Christopher Columbus | Clovis culture | Inca Empire | Maize | Maya civilization
ENTRY:
Period of the history of the Americas from the initial peopling of the continents to the onset of European colonization.

The era, also called the pre-contact, pre-Hispanic, or (in Brazil) pre-Cabraline era, runs from the Upper Paleolithic to the voyage of [[Christopher Columbus|Columbus]] in 1492, though in some regions significant European influence came only decades or centuries later. Many Indigenous civilizations developed cities, agriculture, monumental architecture, and complex social hierarchies. Some had declined before the first permanent European colonies and are known mainly through [[Archaeology|archaeological]] research and oral histories; others, such as the [[Maya civilization|Maya]], who kept written records, were documented in European accounts.

Asian Paleo-Indians are thought to have entered the Americas by way of the Bering Land Bridge ([[Bering Strait]]) and possibly along the coast. The date is much debated. The [[Clovis culture]] has sites some 13,000 years old, but sites of 20,000 years have been claimed. A short chronology puts the first movement beyond Alaska no earlier than 14,000–17,000 years ago; a long chronology proposes 30,000–40,000 years ago or earlier.

By the time of contact, populations had diversified into thousands of cultures, estimated at 45–80 million people. Domesticated crops included [[Maize|maize]], potatoes, and quinoa. Major societies included the mound-building Mississippian culture, which built [[Cahokia]]; the Maya, with hieroglyphic writing and calendars; and the [[Inca Empire]] of the Andes, with stonework and roads spanning over 40,000 km. Many of these societies also practiced warfare, slavery, and human sacrifice. North American populations peaked around 1150 before regional declines.

HEADWORD: Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mannerism | Raphael
ENTRY:
Loose association of English painters, poets, and art critics, founded in London in 1848. Its seven founding members, including William Holman Hunt, John Everett Millais, and Dante Gabriel Rossetti, sought a return to the detail, intense colors, and complex compositions of Quattrocento Italian art. They rejected the Classical poses of [[Raphael]] and the mechanistic approach of the later [[Mannerism|Mannerist]] artists. Later followers included Edward Burne-Jones and William Morris.

=== KEY: Precambrian_9aec51
HEADWORD: Precambrian
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archean | Cambrian | Hadean | Phanerozoic | Proterozoic | Wales
ENTRY:
Informal unit of geologic time, the earliest part of Earth's history, before the [[Phanerozoic]] Eon. It spans from the formation of Earth about 4.6 billion years ago to the start of the [[Cambrian]] Period about 538.8 million years ago, when hard-shelled creatures appeared in abundance. The name refers to its preceding the Cambrian, named after Cambria, the Latinized name for [[Wales]]. The Precambrian accounts for 88% of Earth's geologic time and is divided into the [[Hadean]], [[Archean]], and [[Proterozoic]] eons. Its fossil record is poor, because many rocks have been heavily metamorphosed, eroded, or buried beneath younger strata. Zircon crystals from Western Australia have been dated to 4,404 ± 8 million years old, and a stable crust was apparently in place by 4,433 million years ago. Bacterial fossils older than 3.46 billion years have been found in Western Australia.

=== KEY: Precedent_c5f74f
HEADWORD: Precedent
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Civil law (legal system) | Common law | Statute
ENTRY:
Judicial decision that serves as an authority in deciding later identical or similar cases. Fundamental to [[Common law]] legal systems, precedent operates under the principle of *stare decisis* ("to stand by things decided"), which promotes consistency and predictability. A precedent may be binding, which courts must follow, or persuasive, which courts may consider without being obliged to follow it. [[Civil law (legal system)|Civil law]] systems, by contrast, rely mainly on comprehensive codes and detailed [[Statute|statutes]], with little emphasis on precedent. A precedent may be applied or adopted, distinguished on materially different facts, modified by a higher court, or overruled if found erroneous in law or overtaken by legislation. === KEY: Precipitation_3b30ab
HEADWORD: Precipitation
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Product of the condensation of atmospheric [[Water vapor|water vapor]] that falls from clouds under the pull of gravity. The main forms are drizzle, rain, sleet, snow, ice pellets, graupel, and hail. Fog and mist are not precipitation, because their water vapor does not condense enough to fall. Global average annual precipitation is 990 millimeters (39 in), but over land it is only 715 millimeters (28.1 in). The [[Köppen climate classification]] uses average annual rainfall to distinguish climate regimes.

Biological interaction in which one organism, the predator, kills and eats another, its prey. === KEY: Pregnancy_0b1e9b
HEADWORD: Pregnancy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abortion | Childbirth | Embryo | Fetus | Niger | South Korea | Uterus
ENTRY:
Time during which one or more offspring gestates inside a woman's [[Uterus|uterus]].

A multiple pregnancy involves more than one offspring, as with twins. Conception usually follows intercourse but may occur through assisted reproductive technology. A pregnancy may end in live birth, miscarriage, induced [[Abortion|abortion]], or stillbirth. [[Childbirth]] typically occurs around 40 weeks from the start of the last menstrual period, known as gestational age; counted from fertilization the length is about 38 weeks. Implantation occurs on average 8–9 days after fertilization. The developing offspring is an [[Embryo|embryo]] during the first seven weeks following implantation (ten weeks' gestational age) and a [[Fetus|fetus]] thereafter.

Pregnancy is divided into three trimesters of about three months each. Miscarriage risk is highest in the first trimester; fetal movement may be felt around the middle of the second. At 28 weeks more than 90% of babies can survive outside the uterus with high-quality medical care, though serious complications are likely. Prenatal care improves outcomes. Complications include high blood pressure disorders, gestational diabetes, and iron-deficiency anemia.

Birth before 37 weeks is preterm, 37–39 weeks early term, 39–41 weeks full term, 41–42 weeks late term, and after 42 weeks post-term.

About 213 million pregnancies occurred in 2012, 190 million (89%) in the developing world and 23 million (11%) in the developed world. In 2024 the total fertility rate was estimated highest in [[Niger]] (6.64 children per woman) and lowest in [[South Korea]] (1.12). About 10% to 15% of recognized pregnancies end in miscarriage. Complications of pregnancy caused 230,600 maternal deaths in 2016, down from 377,000 in 1990.

=== KEY: Prehistoric_art_5424e7
HEADWORD: Prehistoric art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cave painting | Homo erectus | Metalworking | Neolithic | Upper Paleolithic
ENTRY:
Art produced in preliterate cultures, from the earliest human workmanship with an artistic purpose until the spread of writing or other record-keeping. The earliest clear examples date to the [[Upper Paleolithic|Upper Paleolithic]] about 40,000 years ago, although evidence suggests the preference for the aesthetic emerged in the Middle Paleolithic. === KEY: Prehistoric_religion_029cc9
HEADWORD: Prehistoric religion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Neolithic | Paleolithic | Stonehenge
ENTRY:
Religious practice of prehistoric cultures, which left no written records of their beliefs. Prehistory accounts for the bulk of human experience, and over 99% of it occurred during the [[Paleolithic]] period. Religion emerged with certainty in the Upper Paleolithic around 50,000 years ago, possibly in a shamanic form centered on spiritual leaders who entered trance states, as suggested by cave art and Venus figurines. The [[Neolithic]] Revolution, around 12,000 BC, established agriculture and made societies more hierarchical, and religion may have become more centralized, including ancestor worship. Stone circles in the British Isles and Brittany, the most prominent of which is [[Stonehenge]], are a notable feature. Late Neolithic and Chalcolithic religion includes Proto-Indo-European mythology. === KEY: Prehistory_80b048
HEADWORD: Prehistory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaeology | Bronze Age | Chalcolithic | Indus Valley Civilisation | Iron Age | Pre-Columbian era | Radiocarbon dating | Stone Age
ENTRY:
Period of human history between the first known use of stone tools by hominins and the beginning of recorded history with the invention of writing.

The first stone tools date to c. 3.3 million years ago; the earliest known writing systems appeared c. 5,200 years ago. Because writing was adopted gradually across the globe, prehistory ended at different times in different places, and the term is less often used for societies where it ended recently. The period when a culture is written about by others but has no writing of its own is often called its protohistory. The beginning varies regionally too: African prehistory begins c. 2.5 million years ago, Asian 1.8 million, and European c. 1.3 million. Prehistory is generally held to have ended around 3100 BC in Egypt, in the 1870s in New Guinea, and in 1788 in Australia.

By definition there are no written records, so prehistory is known only from material archaeological and anthropological evidence: remains and artifacts, and the human remains themselves. It deals with archaeological cultures rather than named nations or individuals, and labels such as "Neanderthal" or "Iron Age" are modern terms whose definitions are sometimes debated. The word first appeared in English in 1836, and although some historians have been uncomfortable with it, archaeologists regard it as a meaningful and necessary distinction.

The principal researchers are anthropologists, including [[Archaeology|archaeologists]] and physical anthropologists, who use excavation, geological and geographic surveys, and other scientific analysis. Population geneticists and historical linguists also contribute, as do biology, geology, paleontology, and many other fields. Dating is the key step; the most common technique is [[Radiocarbon dating|radiocarbon dating]], and newer methods include forensic chemical analysis and genetic analysis of bones.

For much of Eurasia and North Africa, prehistory is divided by the three-age system, named for predominant tool-making technologies: the [[Stone Age]], [[Bronze Age]], and [[Iron Age]], with a transitional [[Chalcolithic]], or Copper Age in some areas. The Stone Age lasted roughly 3.4 million years, ended between 4000 BC and 2000 BC with the advent of metalworking, and accounts for 99% of human history. The earliest civilizations to develop scripts and keep records, including Sumer, the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]], and Egypt, did so in the early Bronze Age; most other societies reached the end of prehistory in the Iron Age. The three-age system is not generally used where hard metals arrived abruptly through contact with Eurasian cultures, such as Oceania, Australasia, much of sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of the Americas. For the Americas, see [[Pre-Columbian era]].

=== KEY: Price_360114

=== KEY: Primate_254505
HEADWORD: Primate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baboon | Carl Linnaeus | Color vision | Deforestation | Gorilla | Mammal | Sense of smell
ENTRY:
Order of mammals comprising lemurs, lorises, galagos, tarsiers, monkeys, and apes.

Primates are divided into the strepsirrhines, which include lemurs, galagos, and lorisids, and the haplorhines, which include tarsiers and simians (monkeys and apes). They arose 74–63 million years ago from small terrestrial [[Mammal|mammals]] adapted to life in tropical forests. Many characteristics reflect adaptation to the treetops: large brains relative to body size, binocular and [[Color vision|color vision]], shoulder girdles permitting wide movement of the upper limbs, and, in most species, opposable thumbs. Reliance on visual acuity is greater, and on the [[Sense of smell|sense of smell]], dominant in most mammals, lesser. Madame Berthe's mouse lemur weighs 30 g, and the eastern gorilla over 200 kg. There are 376–524 living species, depending on the classification used; over 25 were described in the 2000s, 36 in the 2010s, and six in the 2020s.

All species have adaptations for climbing trees, though some, including [[Gorilla|gorillas]], humans, and [[Baboon|baboons]], are mainly ground-dwelling. Primates are among the most social animals, forming pairs, family groups, one-male harems, and multi-male, multi-female groups. They develop more slowly than similarly sized mammals, mature later, and live longer. Non-human primates have been recorded using tools, and communicate by facial and hand gestures, smells, and vocalizations.

The name derives from Latin *primas*, "one of the first", and was given by [[Carl Linnaeus]], who considered the order the "highest" of animals.

Close contact between humans and non-human primates can transmit zoonotic diseases, including herpes, measles, ebola, rabies, and hepatitis. About 60% of species are threatened with extinction, chiefly by [[Deforestation|deforestation]], hunting, and clearing of tropical forest for agriculture.

=== KEY: Prime_meridian_05c71b
HEADWORD: Prime meridian
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Equator | Longitude | Ptolemy | Washington, D.C.
ENTRY:
Line of [[Longitude|longitude]] at which longitude is defined to be 0°. Together with its anti-meridian at 180°, it divides [[Earth|Earth]] into the Eastern and Western Hemispheres. Unlike the [[Equator|equator]], the prime meridian is astronomically arbitrary, and historically different regions used different conventions. [[Ptolemy]] was the first geographer to use a consistent meridian for a world map, basing it on islands in the Atlantic. The Greenwich meridian was adopted as the international standard at a conference in [[Washington, D.C.|Washington, D.C.]] in 1884, by 22 votes to 1 with 2 abstentions, since about two-thirds of the world's shipping already used Greenwich-based charts. Earth's current standard is the IERS Reference Meridian, which is derived from Greenwich but differs slightly.

=== KEY: Prime_number_cbe1d8
HEADWORD: Prime number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclid's Elements | Fibonacci | Fundamental theorem of arithmetic | Number theory | Pierre de Fermat | Prime number theorem | Public-key cryptography | Sieve of Eratosthenes
ENTRY:
Natural number greater than 1 that is not a product of two smaller natural numbers.

A natural number greater than 1 that is not prime is called composite. The number 5 is prime because its only factorizations, 1 × 5 and 5 × 1, involve 5 itself, while 4 is composite because 4 = 2 × 2. Equivalently, a prime has exactly two positive divisors, 1 and itself, so 1 is not prime. Primes are central to [[Number theory|number theory]] because of the [[Fundamental theorem of arithmetic]]: every natural number greater than 1 is either prime or a product of primes that is unique up to order.

The earliest surviving records of the study of primes come from ancient Greek mathematicians. [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]] (c. 300 BC) proves that there are infinitely many primes. The [[Sieve of Eratosthenes]] is still used to list primes. [[Fibonacci]] brought Islamic innovations to Europe, and his *Liber Abaci* (1202) was the first work to describe trial division for testing primality. In 1640 [[Pierre de Fermat]] stated Fermat's little theorem without proof.

No known simple formula separates primes from composites, but their distribution can be modeled statistically. The [[Prime number theorem]], proven at the end of the 19th century, says roughly that the probability of a randomly chosen large number being prime is inversely proportional to its number of digits.

Trial division is simple but slow. The Miller–Rabin test is fast but has a small chance of error. Unsolved questions include Goldbach's conjecture, that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes, and the twin prime conjecture. Primes underlie [[Public-key cryptography|public-key cryptography]], which relies on the difficulty of factoring large numbers into prime factors.

=== KEY: Prime_number_theorem_2114a2
HEADWORD: Prime number theorem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrien-Marie Legendre | Bernhard Riemann | Carl Friedrich Gauss | Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet | Prime number | Riemann zeta function
ENTRY:
Theorem describing the asymptotic distribution of [[Prime number|prime numbers]] among the positive integers. It states that the prime-counting function π(x), the number of primes less than or equal to x, is asymptotically equivalent to x / log x, in the sense that their ratio approaches 1 as x grows without bound. Consequently the average gap between consecutive primes among the first N integers is roughly log N. [[Adrien-Marie Legendre|Legendre]] and [[Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet|Dirichlet]] proposed approximating formulas that imply this equivalence, and [[Carl Friedrich Gauss|Gauss]] also studied the question. Jacques Hadamard and Charles Jean de la Vallée Poussin proved the theorem independently in 1896, using ideas introduced by [[Bernhard Riemann]], particularly the [[Riemann zeta function]]. Among positive integers of at most 1000 digits, about one in 2300 is prime.

=== KEY: Primulaceae_d43c73
HEADWORD: Primulaceae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Flowering plant | Leaf
ENTRY:
Family of herbaceous and woody [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]], commonly called the primrose family. Most species are perennial, although the scarlet pimpernel is an annual. The family was once one of three in the order Primulales. Molecular phylogenetic studies led the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group to submerge that order in an enlarged order Ericales, and the original family became subfamily Primuloideae within an enlarged Primulaceae. Flowers are bisexual and radially symmetrical, with a stamen opposite each petal, and the ovules are attached to a central axis without partitions. [[Leaf|Leaves]] are simple and usually lack stipules, often forming a rosette at the base of the stem. Seeds develop in capsules that split apart to release them, and the family includes garden favorites and wildflowers.

=== KEY: Prince_Albert_of_Saxe_Coburg_and_Gotha_f07ba5

ENTRY:
German-born prince and husband of [[Queen Victoria]]. He married his first cousin Victoria in 1840 and was her consort until his death. Victoria granted him the title Prince Consort in 1857. Initially constrained by a role without power, he gained a reputation for supporting educational reform and the worldwide abolition of slavery. He organized the Great Exhibition of 1851, ran the Queen's household and estates, and persuaded Victoria to be less partisan in dealings with Parliament, aiding the development of [[Constitutional monarchy|constitutional monarchy]]. Their eldest son later succeeded as [[Edward VII]].

=== KEY: Prince_Edward_Island_222794
HEADWORD: Prince Edward Island
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Potato | Seven Years' War
ENTRY:
Island province of Canada, in the Gulf of St. Lawrence. It is the smallest Canadian province by land area and population, yet has the highest population density, and it is the only province consisting entirely of islands. Its capital is Charlottetown. Ceded to Britain at the end of the [[Seven Years' War|Seven Years' War]], the island was part of Nova Scotia before becoming a separate colony in 1769. It joined Canadian Confederation as the seventh province in 1873. Farming is central to the economy: the island produces 25% of Canada's [[Potato|potatoes]], and fisheries and tourism are also important.

=== KEY: Prince_Eugene_of_Savoy_9e0d41
HEADWORD: Eugene of Savoy
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Belgrade | Holy Roman Empire | Vienna | War of the Spanish Succession
ENTRY:
Paris-born military commander in the service of Austria and the [[Holy Roman Empire]]. Eugene was raised at the court of Louis XIV. Denied a commission in the French army, he entered the service of Emperor Leopold I, cousin and rival of Louis XIV, and distinguished himself during the Ottoman siege of [[Vienna]] in 1683. His victory at Zenta in 1697 ended the Ottoman threat for nearly 20 years. In the [[War of the Spanish Succession|War of the Spanish Succession]] he partnered with the Duke of Marlborough, winning at Blenheim, Oudenaarde and Malplaquet, and he later won at Petrovaradin in 1716 and at [[Belgrade]] in 1717. He also held senior office at the imperial court in Vienna, spending six decades in the service of three emperors.

=== KEY: Prince_Henry_the_Navigator_d4549d
HEADWORD: Henry the Navigator
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Ceuta | Iberian Peninsula | Portuguese Empire
ENTRY:
Portuguese prince and patron of maritime exploration. Henry was the third child of King John I, who founded the House of Aviz, and he is often credited as one of the main initiators of the [[Age of Discovery|Age of Discovery]]. Using the new caravel ship, he directed the early development of Portuguese exploration of western Africa, the Atlantic islands and the search for new routes. He supported his father's 1415 conquest of [[Ceuta]], a Muslim port across the Strait of Gibraltar from the [[Iberian Peninsula]]. Under his sponsorship, Portuguese explorers passed Cape Bojador in 1434 and reached the Senegal River by 1445, and ventures secured trade in gold, pepper and enslaved Africans. His expeditions yielded no sea route to India during his lifetime, but they advanced navigation and laid foundations for the [[Portuguese Empire|Portuguese empire]].

=== KEY: Prince_musician_34583e
HEADWORD: Prince
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Funk | Minneapolis | Rock and Roll Hall of Fame
ENTRY:
American singer, songwriter, musician and filmmaker. Often credited as one of the greatest musicians of his generation, Prince pioneered the [[Minneapolis|Minneapolis]] sound and influenced genres including [[Funk|funk]], rock and pop. Born and raised in Minneapolis, he signed a record deal with Warner Bros. Records at 18 and released his first album, *For You*, two years later. In 1984 he became the first singer to have a number-one film, album and single in the United States at the same time, with *Purple Rain*, its soundtrack and "When Doves Cry". The album spent 24 consecutive weeks at number one on the U.S. Billboard 200. Prince's estimated sales exceed 100 million records. In 1993, during a contract dispute with Warner Bros., he changed his name to an unpronounceable symbol, and he reverted to Prince in 2000. He was inducted into the [[Rock and Roll Hall of Fame]] in 2004.
