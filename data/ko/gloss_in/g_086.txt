=== KEY: Materialism_3c6158
HEADWORD: Materialism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consciousness | Democritus | Epicurus | Idealism | Monism | Naturalism (philosophy) | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Philosophical doctrine that matter is the fundamental substance of nature. Materialism is a form of [[Monism|monism]] holding that all things, including mind and [[Consciousness|consciousness]], arise from material interactions and depend on physical processes. It contrasts with [[Idealism|idealism]], which treats consciousness as fundamental, and it is closely related to [[Naturalism (philosophy)|naturalism]] and to physicalism, which extends matter to include energy, forces and spacetime. Ancient atomists such as [[Democritus|Democritus]] and [[Epicurus|Epicurus]] explained all change through the motion of atoms, and [[Thomas Hobbes|Thomas Hobbes]] advanced materialism in the modern era. A central controversy is the hard problem of consciousness: critics argue that material accounts fail to capture subjective experience, while proponents hold that it emerges from complex neural configurations.

=== KEY: Materials_science_8fbb97
HEADWORD: Materials science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Josiah Willard Gibbs | Metallurgy | Nanotechnology | Semiconductor | Space Race
ENTRY:
Interdisciplinary field concerned with the structure of materials, their properties, and how to design them for specific applications. The internal structure of a material, from atomic arrangements to microscopic features, strongly influences its mechanical, electrical, thermal and optical behavior. Practitioners use the processing–structure–properties–performance paradigm, in which processing determines structure, structure determines properties, and properties control performance in service. The field grew out of [[Metallurgy|metallurgy]] and ceramics, among the oldest forms of engineering, and drew on chemistry, physics and engineering from the [[Age of Enlightenment|Enlightenment]] onward. It was widely recognized as a distinct field only from the 1940s. In the late 19th century, [[Josiah Willard Gibbs|Josiah Willard Gibbs]] showed that the thermodynamic properties of atomic phases relate to the physical properties of materials. Work for the [[Space Race|Space Race]] advanced metal alloys and silica and carbon materials. The field has contributed to biomaterials, [[Nanotechnology|nanotechnology]] and [[Semiconductor|semiconductors]].

=== KEY: Mathematical_analysis_5b0aaa
HEADWORD: Mathematical analysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Calculus | Complex analysis | Functional analysis | Galileo Galilei | Harmonic analysis | Johannes Kepler | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics that studies functions, spaces, and operators through methods of approximation and convergence. It grew out of [[Calculus|calculus]], especially the use of derivatives and integrals to study variable quantities, and in the 19th century its foundations were reformulated with greater rigor. Its basic objects include the real numbers, functions, sequences, series, and limits. Analysis connects closely with the sciences, providing methods for formulating mathematical models, constructing approximations, and estimating their errors.

Typical questions concern how functions, sequences, and operators behave under limiting processes: convergence and continuity, regularity and stability, quantitative bounds, and the accuracy of approximations. Modern analysis studies these in many settings, including Euclidean, metric, topological, and measure spaces and function spaces. Major areas include [[Complex analysis|complex analysis]], [[Functional analysis|functional analysis]], measure theory, [[Harmonic analysis|harmonic analysis]], and the theory of ordinary and partial differential equations.

Analysis formally developed in the 17th century during the [[Scientific Revolution]], but many of its ideas are older. An infinite geometric sum is implicit in Zeno's paradox of the dichotomy. Eudoxus and [[Archimedes]] used the method of exhaustion to compute areas and volumes, and Liu Hui used it in the 3rd century CE to find the area of a circle. In the medieval period, Ibn al-Haytham worked on sums of powers and area problems; the Oxford Calculators studied uniformly accelerated motion; Nicole Oresme proved the divergence of the harmonic series; Bhāskara II used infinitesimal reasoning; and the Kerala school, especially Madhava, developed infinite series for trigonometric functions. In the early 17th century, [[Johannes Kepler]] used infinitesimal arguments for area and volume, [[Galileo Galilei]] connected mathematics with motion, and Bonaventura Cavalieri developed the method of indivisibles. These were precursors rather than analysis in the modern sense. The differential and integral calculus of Newton and Leibniz became the starting point for much of later analysis.

=== KEY: Mathematical_induction_975fda
HEADWORD: Mathematical induction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Inductive reasoning | Pierre de Fermat | Recursion
ENTRY:
Method of proof showing that a statement holds for every natural number. The proof has two parts. The base case establishes the statement for the first case, usually n = 0 or n = 1, and the induction step shows that if it holds for n = k, it also holds for n = k + 1. The idea is often illustrated by falling dominoes or climbing a ladder. Mathematical induction is an inference rule in formal proofs and the foundation of most correctness proofs for computer programs. It extends to well-founded structures such as trees, a generalization known as structural induction that is closely related to [[Recursion|recursion]]. Despite its name, it differs from [[Inductive reasoning|inductive reasoning]] in philosophy, because it gives a rigorous proof by a finite chain of deductive reasoning rather than a probable conclusion. Al-Karaji used an implicit form of the argument around 1000, and [[Pierre de Fermat|Pierre de Fermat]] employed it in number theory in the 17th century.

=== KEY: Mathematical_logic_70faae
HEADWORD: Mathematical logic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computability theory | David Hilbert | Kurt Gödel | Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory
ENTRY:
Subfield of mathematics that studies logic through formal systems defining rules of reasoning and their axioms. Its major subareas are model theory, proof theory, set theory and [[Computability theory|recursion theory]]. Research addresses the expressive and deductive power of formal systems and the foundations of mathematics. The field emerged in the mid-19th century, and in the early 20th century [[David Hilbert|David Hilbert]] proposed a program to prove the consistency of mathematics. Results of [[Kurt Gödel|Kurt Gödel]] and Gerhard Gentzen provided only partial resolution, but clarified the issues involved. Work in [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory|set theory]] showed that all mathematics can be formalized in terms of sets, and this led to proof assistants such as Lean, which can completely verify difficult theorems.

=== KEY: Mathematical_optimization_3f9b51
HEADWORD: Mathematical optimization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economics | Gradient descent | Linear programming | Machine learning | Nonlinear programming | Operations research
ENTRY:
Study of algorithms for selecting the best element from a set of alternatives, usually by maximizing or minimizing a function. It is divided into discrete optimization, where the sought object is an integer, permutation or graph, and continuous optimization, where it is drawn from a continuous set. The function is called an objective, cost, loss or utility function, and optimal solutions are sought among feasible solutions that satisfy constraints. === KEY: Mathematical_proof_8ccaa1
HEADWORD: Mathematical proof
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axiom | Binomial theorem | Conjecture | Euclid | Formal language | Inductive reasoning | Non-Euclidean geometry | Pythagorean theorem
ENTRY:
Deductive argument for a mathematical statement, showing that the stated assumptions logically guarantee the conclusion. A proof may use previously established theorems, but every proof can in principle be built from basic assumptions called [[Axiom|axioms]] and accepted rules of inference. Proofs establish logical certainty, unlike empirical arguments or non-exhaustive [[Inductive reasoning|inductive reasoning]]; presenting many cases in which a statement holds is not enough, since a proof must show it true in all possible cases. A proposition believed true but not yet proved is a [[Conjecture|conjecture]].

Proofs combine mathematical symbols with natural language, and most mathematical literature uses rigorous informal logic. Purely formal proofs, written entirely in a [[Formal language|formal language]], are the subject of proof theory: a formal proof is a sequence of formulas, each a logical consequence of the preceding ones. The standard of rigor is not absolute and has varied through history, and a proof must meet communal standards to be accepted.

The word derives from the Latin *probare*, "to test". Plausibility arguments using pictures and analogies preceded strict proof, which is primarily the product of ancient Greek mathematics. Thales and Hippocrates of Chios gave some of the first known proofs of geometric theorems. [[Euclid]] revolutionized the field by introducing the axiomatic method still in use, which starts from undefined terms and axioms and proves theorems by deductive logic. His *Elements* includes the [[Pythagorean theorem|Pythagorean theorem]], and a proof that the square root of two is irrational. In medieval Islamic mathematics, Al-Hashimi proved algebraic propositions about numbers, including the existence of irrational numbers, and Al-Karaji used an inductive proof in the *Al-Fakhri* (1000) to prove the [[Binomial theorem|binomial theorem]] and properties of Pascal's triangle. Modern proof theory treats proofs as inductively defined data structures and does not require axioms to be true in any sense, allowing parallel theories such as axiomatic set theory and [[Non-Euclidean geometry|non-Euclidean geometry]].

=== KEY: Mathematics_540b21
HEADWORD: Mathematics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axiom | Economics | Engineering | Euclid's Elements | Leonhard Euler | Number theory | Pierre de Fermat | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Field of knowledge concerned with abstract concepts such as numbers, geometric shapes, sets, functions, and probabilities. It uses logical reasoning and proof to establish their properties, often expressed as theorems, formulas, and equations. Mathematics is widely used to model and solve problems in science, [[Engineering|engineering]], technology, [[Economics|economics]], and everyday life. Its objects are either abstractions from nature or purely abstract entities stipulated to have certain properties, called [[Axiom|axioms]]. Although it is used to model empirical phenomena, its results are established by deductive proof rather than by experiment, and the relationship between mathematical truth, logic, and reality is a subject of philosophical debate.

Major areas include [[Number theory|number theory]] (the study of integers and their properties), algebra (the study of operations and the structures they form), geometry (the study of shapes and the spaces that contain them), analysis (the quantitative study of approximation and convergence), and set theory, presently used as a foundation for all mathematics. Some areas, such as game theory, develop in close correlation with applications and are grouped under applied mathematics; others develop independently but often find practical applications later. The 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification lists 63 first-level areas.

Mathematical written records first appeared in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, but the concept of proof and its associated rigor began in ancient Greek mathematics, exemplified in [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]]. Until the 16th and 17th centuries mathematics was primarily divided into geometry and arithmetic; during the Renaissance, modern algebra and calculus became new areas, and combinatorics became a separate branch in the 17th century. Before then, pseudosciences such as numerology and astrology were not clearly distinguished from it. At the end of the 19th century the foundational crisis of mathematics led to the systematic use of the axiomatic method, which greatly increased the number of areas and their applications.

Number theory evolved from the manipulation of natural numbers, later extended to integers and rational numbers. Two prominent early number theorists were Euclid and Diophantus of Alexandria, and the modern abstract study is largely attributed to [[Pierre de Fermat]] and [[Leonhard Euler]]. Fermat's Last Theorem, stated in 1637, was proved only in 1994 by Andrew Wiles, using tools from algebraic geometry, category theory, and homological algebra. Goldbach's conjecture, stated in 1742, asserts that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two prime numbers and remains unproven.

Geometry is one of the oldest branches, beginning with empirical recipes for surveying and architecture. The ancient Greeks introduced the requirement that every assertion be proved, systematized by Euclid around 300 BC. Euclidean geometry developed without change of methods until the 17th century, when [[René Descartes]] introduced Cartesian coordinates, allowing algebra and later calculus to be used to solve geometric problems.

=== KEY: Matrix_mathematics_72c0f0
HEADWORD: Matrix
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthur Cayley | Determinant | Graph theory | Linear algebra | Matrix multiplication
ENTRY:
Rectangular array of numbers or other mathematical objects arranged in rows and columns. A matrix with m rows and n columns is an m × n matrix, and a matrix with the same number of rows and columns is called square. Matrices are used in [[Linear algebra|linear algebra]] as linear maps, in geometry for transformations such as rotations, and in numerical analysis, where many computational problems are reduced to matrix computations. A square matrix is invertible if and only if its [[Determinant|determinant]] is nonzero. Matrix theory began as a sub-branch of linear algebra but grew to include [[Graph theory|graph theory]], algebra, combinatorics and statistics. The term was coined by James Joseph Sylvester in 1850, and [[Arthur Cayley|Arthur Cayley]] formalized [[Matrix multiplication|matrix multiplication]] in the 1850s.

=== KEY: Matter_125795
HEADWORD: Matter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Democritus | Kaṇāda
ENTRY:
In physical science, anything that has mass and occupies space by having volume. All everyday objects that can be touched are ultimately composed of [[Atom|atoms]], which are made up of interacting subatomic particles. Matter generally includes atoms and anything made up of them, and any particles that act as if they have both rest mass and volume. It does not include massless particles such as photons, or other energy phenomena or waves such as light or heat.

Matter exists in various states, or phases. These include the everyday solid, liquid, and gas, as with water as ice, liquid, and steam, and also plasma, Bose–Einstein condensates, fermionic condensates, and quark–gluon plasma. An atom can be imagined as a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by a cloud of orbiting electrons, but this is only somewhat correct, because subatomic particles are governed by quantum mechanics: they can act like waves as well as particles and lack well-defined sizes or positions. In the Standard Model of particle physics, matter is not a fundamental concept, because the elementary constituents of atoms have no inherent size or volume in the everyday sense. Because of the exclusion principle and other fundamental interactions, fermions (quarks and leptons) and many composites and atoms are effectively forced to keep a distance from other particles, which produces the property that matter takes up space.

The idea that matter is built of discrete building blocks, the particulate theory of matter, appeared in both ancient Greece and ancient India. Early proponents include the Indian philosopher [[Kaṇāda]] (c. 6th century BCE) and the Greek philosophers Leucippus (c. 490 BCE) and [[Democritus]] (c. 470–380 BCE).

Definitions vary by scale. One definition holds that matter is made of atoms, which also includes charged atoms and molecules and so covers plasmas and electrolytes. A finer definition counts anything made of protons, neutrons, and electrons, which includes electron beams in an old cathode ray tube television and white dwarf matter. At the level of elementary particles, ordinary matter is everything composed of quarks and leptons, excluding antiquarks and antileptons, which are antimatter. Ordinary matter is composed entirely of first-generation particles, the up and down quarks plus the electron and its neutrino. The W and Z bosons, which mediate the weak force, are not ordinary matter even though they have mass.

Matter is distinct from mass. Mass is a well-defined, extensive property of matter and other substances or systems, not a substance itself. Most of the mass of everyday objects comes from the interaction energy of its elementary components: the three quarks in a nucleon sum to approximately 12.5 MeV/c², low compared to the nucleon's approximately 938 MeV/c², with most of the rest coming from the binding energy of quarks within protons and neutrons.

[[Probability distribution]] of particle speeds in an idealized gas at thermal equilibrium. === KEY: Maxwell_s_demon_c5abc2

ENTRY:
Thought experiment of 1867 devised by [[James Clerk Maxwell]]. A demon controls a door between two gas-filled chambers, letting only fast-moving molecules pass one way and slow-moving ones the other. One chamber warms and the other cools, decreasing entropy without any work and seemingly violating the [[Second law of thermodynamics|second law of thermodynamics]]. === KEY: Maxwell_s_equations_a9640e

ENTRY:
Set of coupled [[Partial differential equation|partial differential equations]] describing [[Electromagnetism|classical electromagnetism]]. Together with the [[Lorentz force|Lorentz force law]], they describe how [[Electric field|electric]] and [[Magnetic field|magnetic fields]] are generated by electric charges and currents, and they form the foundation of classical optics and of electric and magnetic circuits. === KEY: Maya_civilization_e73ec9
HEADWORD: Maya civilization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belize | Chichen Itza | Guatemala | Mesoamerica | Mexico | Teotihuacan | Tikal
ENTRY:
[[Mesoamerica|Mesoamerican]] civilization (c. 2000 BC–1697 AD), known for its temples, art, architecture, mathematics, calendar, and astronomical system. The Maya script is the most sophisticated and highly developed writing system in the pre-Columbian Americas. The civilization developed in an area comprising southeastern [[Mexico|Mexico]], all of [[Guatemala|Guatemala]] and [[Belize|Belize]], and the western portions of Honduras and El Salvador. Its descendants number well over 6 million and speak more than twenty-eight surviving Mayan languages. "Maya" is a modern collective term; Maya peoples had no common ethnic identity or political unity for most of their history.

The Preclassic period (c. 2000 BC to 250 AD) saw the first complex societies and the cultivation of staples such as maize, beans, squashes, and chili peppers. The first cities developed around 750 BC, and hieroglyphic writing was in use by the 3rd century BC. The Classic period, from around 250 AD, is largely defined by sculpted monuments bearing Long Count dates. Many city-states were linked by trade, and the rival cities of [[Tikal]] and Calakmul became powerful; the central Mexican city of [[Teotihuacan]] intervened in Maya dynastic politics. In the 9th century a widespread political collapse in the central region brought civil wars, abandoned cities, and a northward shift of population. The Postclassic period saw the rise of [[Chichen Itza]] in the north and of the Kʼicheʼ kingdom in the Guatemalan Highlands. In the 16th century the Spanish Empire colonized the region, and Nojpetén, the last Maya city, fell in 1697.

During the Classic period rule centered on the "divine king", thought to mediate between mortals and the supernatural realm. By the Late Classic the aristocracy had grown, reducing the king's exclusive power. The literate elite recorded history and ritual knowledge in screenfold books, of which only three uncontested examples remain, and on stelae and ceramics. They developed interlocking ritual calendars and mathematics that included one of the earliest known instances of the explicit zero. Their religion included human sacrifice.

=== KEY: Measurement_911842
HEADWORD: Measurement
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Sanders Peirce | Michelson–Morley experiment
ENTRY:
Quantification of attributes of an object or event, determining how large or small a physical quantity is compared with a basic reference quantity of the same kind. Its scope depends on discipline. In natural science and engineering, measurement does not apply to nominal properties, but in statistics and the social and behavioral sciences it can occur at nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio levels. Measurement is a cornerstone of trade, science, technology, and quantitative research, and the science of measurement is metrology.

A measurement is characterized by its type, magnitude, unit, and uncertainty. The magnitude is the numerical value, usually obtained with a measuring instrument; the unit is a standard quantity assigned a weighting factor; the uncertainty represents the random and systematic errors of the procedure.

Historically, many measurement systems existed, often set by local agreement between trading partners. Since the 18th century, developments moved toward unified standards, resulting in the modern International System of Units (SI), which reduces all physical measurements to a combination of seven base units: kilogram, meter, candela, second, ampere, kelvin, and mole. These are defined without reference to a physical object, instead fixing values to physical constants or invariable natural phenomena. [[Charles Sanders Peirce]] first proposed tying a base unit to an experimental standard, defining the meter by the wavelength of a spectral line, which directly influenced the [[Michelson–Morley experiment]].

The General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), established in 1875 by the Metre Convention, oversees the SI. The meter was redefined in 1983 in terms of the speed of light, and the kilogram in 2019 in terms of the Planck constant. The British systems of English units and later imperial units, known in the United States as U.S. customary units, remain in use there and in a few Caribbean countries.

=== KEY: Meat_e4b662

=== KEY: Mechanical_engineering_8c7ebd
HEADWORD: Mechanical engineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Christiaan Huygens | Industrial Revolution | Isaac Newton | Materials science | Mechanics | Su Song | Thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Engineering discipline concerned with the design, analysis, manufacture, and operation of mechanical systems. It combines engineering physics and mathematics with [[Materials science|materials science]] to develop and improve machines, structures, manufacturing processes, and fluid, thermal, and energy systems. Core areas include [[Mechanics|mechanics]], dynamics, [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]], design, and structural analysis. Engineers use computer-aided design, manufacturing, and engineering (CAD, CAM, CAE) and product lifecycle management for industrial equipment, heating and cooling systems, motor vehicles, aircraft, robotics, and medical devices.

The field emerged during the [[Industrial Revolution]] in Europe in the 18th century, but its development can be traced back several thousand years. The wedge and inclined plane were known since prehistoric times, the lever appeared around 5,000 years ago in the Near East, and the shadoof, the first crane, in Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC. Water wheels and watermills appeared in the Persian Empire by the early 4th century BC. [[Archimedes]] (287–212 BC) influenced mechanics in the Western tradition. Al-Jazari wrote his book on ingenious mechanical devices in 1206.

In the 17th century, [[Christiaan Huygens]] invented the pendulum clock in 1657, the first reliable timekeeper for almost 300 years, and [[Isaac Newton]] formulated his laws of motion and developed calculus. In the early 19th century, machine tools developed in England, Germany, and Scotland allowed mechanical engineering to become a separate field. It overlaps with aerospace, civil, electrical, chemical, and industrial engineering, and today's engineers pursue composites, mechatronics, and nanotechnology.

Substance used to diagnose, cure, treat, or prevent disease; also called medicine, medicament, pharmaceutical drug, or simply [[Drug|drug]]. Drug therapy (pharmacotherapy) relies on [[Pharmacology|pharmacology]] for continual advancement and on [[Pharmacy|pharmacy]] for management. [[Encyclopædia Britannica|Britannica]] defines it as "a substance used in treating a disease or relieving pain." Dosage forms include tablets, capsules, liquids, creams, and patches, and routes of administration include by mouth, by infusion into a vein, and by drops in the ear or eye. A medication with no active ingredient, used in research studies, is a [[Placebo|placebo]].

Definitions vary by jurisdiction. In Europe the term is "medicinal product," defined by EU law as any substance presented as having properties for treating or preventing disease, or usable to restore, correct, or modify physiological functions or to make a medical diagnosis. In the U.S., a "drug" is a substance, other than food, intended to affect the structure or any function of the body or to diagnose, cure, mitigate, treat, or prevent disease.

Drugs are classified in many ways. One key division is by level of control, separating [[Prescription drug|prescription drugs]], dispensed only on prescription, from over-the-counter drugs. Another is between small-molecule drugs, usually derived from chemical synthesis, and biological products such as recombinant proteins, vaccines, gene therapy, and cell therapy. A widely used scheme is the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) system. The [[World Health Organization]] keeps a list of essential medicines.

Historically, drugs were found by isolating active ingredients from traditional remedies or by serendipity; since the sequencing of the human genome, high-throughput screening of large compound libraries has become common. Discovery and development are complex and expensive. Governments generally regulate which drugs can be marketed and how. Controversies have arisen over drug pricing and disposal of used medications.

Discipline at the intersection of [[Chemistry|chemistry]] and [[Pharmacy|pharmacy]] concerned with designing and developing drugs. It identifies, synthesizes and develops new chemical entities for therapeutic use, and studies the biological properties of existing drugs and their quantitative structure-activity relationships. Most medicines are [[Organic compound|organic compounds]], including small molecules such as [[Atorvastatin|atorvastatin]] and protein-based biologics such as insulin glargine. Metal-based agents include [[Platinum|platinum]], [[Lithium|lithium]] and gallium compounds, such as cisplatin, lithium carbonate and gallium nitrate. The field combines [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]] with biochemistry, computational chemistry, [[Pharmacology|pharmacology]], molecular biology, statistics and physical chemistry. Pharmaceutical chemistry focuses on the quality of medicines and their fitness for purpose.

Science and practice of caring for patients, managing the diagnosis, prognosis, prevention, treatment, and palliation of injury or disease while promoting health. It encompasses health care practices that evolved to maintain and restore health through the prevention and treatment of illness and infection. Contemporary medicine applies biomedical sciences, biomedical research, genetics, and medical technology to diagnose, treat, and prevent injury and disease, typically through pharmaceuticals or [[Surgery|surgery]], but also through therapies such as psychotherapy, external splints and traction, medical devices, biologics, and ionizing radiation. The English word derives from Latin *medicus*, "a physician."

Medicine has been practiced since prehistoric times, and for most of that time it was an art, frequently connected to the religious and philosophical beliefs of local culture. A medicine man would apply herbs and say prayers for healing; an ancient philosopher and physician would apply bloodletting according to the theory of humorism, or the four humors. Since the advent of modern science, most medicine has become a combination of art and science. [[Hippocrates]], around 460 BC, emphasized natural over supernatural causes and is associated with the Hippocratic Oath. In the 19th century, [[Louis Pasteur]]'s germ theory and [[Robert Koch]]'s postulates linking microbes to specific diseases shifted the paradigm from miasma to microbial causation. [[Alexander Fleming]] discovered penicillin in 1928, and vaccination, built on [[Edward Jenner]]'s smallpox inoculation, led to the disease's eradication by 1980.

Prescientific forms, now known as [[Traditional medicine|traditional medicine]] or folk medicine, remain commonly used in the absence of scientific medicine and are thus called alternative medicine. Alternative treatments outside scientific medicine with ethical, safety, and efficacy concerns are termed [[Quackery|quackery]] or are described as based on fringe science. Modern scientific medicine is highly developed in the Western world, whereas populations in parts of Africa, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and Latin America often rely more heavily on traditional medicine. Even in the developed world, evidence-based medicine is not universally applied: a 2007 survey of literature reviews found that about 49% of medical interventions lacked sufficient evidence to support either benefit or harm.

In clinical practice, physicians and mid-level practitioners such as physician assistants assess patients personally. An encounter typically begins with a review of the medical history and record, followed by a medical interview and a physical examination. The examination looks for objective signs of disease, as distinct from symptoms volunteered by the patient, and rests on four actions: inspection, palpation, percussion, and auscultation, generally in that order. The doctor may order medical tests, take a biopsy, prescribe drugs or other therapies, or refer the patient to a specialist. The encounter is documented in the medical record, a legal document in many jurisdictions. Medical practice is bound by the bioethical principle of non-maleficence, historically associated with the Hippocratic Oath and its duty to "first, do no harm."

Sacred and secular music of Western Europe during the [[Middle Ages]], roughly the 6th to 15th centuries. It is the first and longest major era of [[Classical music|Western classical music]], followed by [[Renaissance music]]. Musicologists group these eras as early music, which precedes the common practice period. Medieval music is traditionally divided into Early (500–1150), High (1150–1300) and Late (1300–1400) periods. It includes liturgical music for the church, other sacred music and secular music. Much of it is purely vocal, such as [[Gregorian chant]], while other works used only instruments or combined voices and instruments. The period saw the creation of systems of [[Musical notation|notation]] that let composers document and transmit music, although notation coexisted with oral tradition.

Philosophy of the [[Middle Ages]], extending from the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century. Medieval philosophy as independent inquiry began in Baghdad in the mid-8th century, and in the court of [[Charlemagne]] at [[Aachen]] in the last quarter of that century. It was shaped by the rediscovery of ancient Greek and Roman thought and by the need to integrate sacred doctrine with secular learning. God was the central concern of Jewish, Christian and Muslim thinkers alike. Major problems included the relation of [[Faith|faith]] to [[Reason|reason]], the existence and simplicity of God, and universals. The 12th to 14th centuries in the Latin West were a "golden age", when the recovery of ancient philosophy culminated, aided by Arabic commentators. Renaissance humanists later dismissed the Scholastic period as a barbaric "middle period".

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
City in western [[Saudi Arabia]], capital of Medina Province (pop., 2022: 1,477,023). Medina is the second holiest city in Islam after [[Mecca]] and was the base of [[Muhammad]]'s leadership after his migration from Mecca (the *Hijrah*) in 622. Originally called Yathrib, the city is home to *al-Masjid an-Nabawi* (the Prophet's Mosque), which houses Muhammad's tomb, and to the Quba Mosque, regarded as the oldest mosque in Islam. Successive powers ruled the region, among them the Mamluks, Ottomans and Hashemites, and it has been part of Saudi Arabia since 1925. Pilgrims and tourists visit its mosques, while the Saudi government has destroyed several historical structures in Medina and Mecca.
