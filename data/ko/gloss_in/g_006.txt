Ancient Northwest Semitic-speaking [[Bronze Age]] people from western [[Mesopotamia]]. First mentioned in Sumerian records c. 2500 BC, they ruled most of the [[Levant]] and Mesopotamia, and parts of Egypt, from the 21st century BC to the start of the 16th century BC. Shu-Sin of the Third Dynasty of Ur built a 270 km wall between the Tigris and Euphrates to hold them off.

=== KEY: Amorphous_solid_a5049d
HEADWORD: Amorphous solid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crystal | Glass | Plastic | Polymer
ENTRY:
Solid that lacks the long-range order characteristic of a [[Crystal|crystal]]. Also called a non-crystalline solid, it includes [[Glass|glasses]], metallic glasses, and certain [[Plastic|plastics]] and [[Polymer|polymers]]. The terms 'glass' and 'glassy solid' are sometimes used as synonyms, but refer specifically to amorphous materials that undergo a glass transition. Amorphous materials show short-range order over typically 1–2 atomic spacings, but no repeating unit cell, so statistical measures such as the radial distribution function describe their structure. The freezing of a liquid into an amorphous solid is one of the important unsolved problems of physics. Below about 1–10 K, amorphous solids share anomalous properties, including nearly linear specific heat and nearly quadratic thermal conductivity, that current theories explain only partly.

=== KEY: Amount_of_substance_6a915b
HEADWORD: Amount of substance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Avogadro constant | Dalton (unit) | International System of Units | Mole (unit) | Molecule
ENTRY:
Base quantity of the [[International System of Units|International System of Units (SI)]], defined as the ratio of the number of elementary entities in a sample to the [[Avogadro constant]]. Its SI unit is the [[Mole (unit)|mole]] (mol). Since 2019 the Avogadro constant has been defined as exactly 6.02214076×10²³ mol⁻¹, which makes the mole convenient for laboratory-scale chemistry. The entities are usually [[Molecule|molecules]], atoms, ions or ion pairs. The amount of a substance can be calculated from measured mass or volume, given its molar mass or the molar volume of an ideal gas. Because of how the mole and the [[Dalton (unit)|dalton]] are defined, a mole of water has a mass of about 18.0153 grams, close to the mass of one water molecule in daltons. The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry deprecates the term 'number of moles'.

=== KEY: Ampere_2ee3b9
HEADWORD: Ampere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: André-Marie Ampère | Coulomb | Electromagnetism | Hans Christian Ørsted | International System of Units
ENTRY:
[[International System of Units|SI]] base unit of electric current, equal to one [[Coulomb|coulomb]] of charge passing a point per second. It is named after French mathematician and physicist [[André-Marie Ampère]], who with [[Hans Christian Ørsted]] is considered the father of [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]]. Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the ampere is defined by fixing the elementary charge at exactly 1.602176634×10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, so one ampere corresponds to about 6.241509074×10¹⁸ elementary charges per second. Previously it was defined by the force between two parallel wires one meter apart in a vacuum, equal to 2×10⁻⁷ newtons per meter of length. An international convention signed at the 1881 International Exposition of Electricity established the ampere as a standard unit.

=== KEY: Amphibian_436ae7
HEADWORD: Amphibian
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caecilian | Devonian | Frog | Lizard | Metamorphosis | Reptile | Salamander | Tetrapod
ENTRY:
Class of ectothermic, anamniotic, four-limbed vertebrates.

In its broadest sense the group is paraphyletic, encompassing all [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]] except the amniotes (modern reptiles, birds, and mammals). All living amphibians belong to the subclass Lissamphibia, with three orders: Anura ([[Frog|frogs]] and toads), Urodela ([[Salamander|salamanders]]), and Gymnophiona ([[Caecilian|caecilians]]). About 8,000 species are known, nearly 90% of them frogs.

The typical life cycle begins with aquatic, gilled larvae (tadpoles) that undergo [[Metamorphosis|metamorphosis]], driven largely by thyroid hormones, into air-breathing, lunged adults. The skin serves as a secondary respiratory surface, and some small terrestrial salamanders and frogs lack lungs entirely. Though superficially similar to [[Lizard|lizards]], amphibians require access to water to breed, and adults generally cannot breathe underwater. Their permeable skins and complex reproductive needs make them ecological indicators of habitat conditions. The smallest amphibian, and smallest vertebrate, is the New Guinea frog *Paedophryne amauensis*, at 7.7 mm. The largest living amphibian is the South China giant salamander (*Andrias sligoi*), at 1.8 m; the prehistoric temnospondyl *Mastodonsaurus* could reach up to 6 m.

The earliest amphibians evolved in the [[Devonian]] from lobe-finned fishes that had developed primitive lungs. They diversified and became ecologically dominant in the Carboniferous and Permian periods, then were displaced on land by early [[Reptile|reptiles]] and basal synapsids. Modern lissamphibians first appeared in the Early Triassic, around 250 million years ago; their origin is contentious, the most popular hypothesis tracing them to temnospondyls and another to lepospondyls.

Amphibian populations have declined dramatically in recent decades. Of assessed species, 41% are threatened with extinction, owing to habitat destruction, pathogens such as *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, and climate-driven disruption. The study of amphibians is batrachology; that of reptiles and amphibians together is herpetology.

=== KEY: Amplitude_f4d00f
HEADWORD: Amplitude
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Electrical engineering | Exoplanet | Oscilloscope
ENTRY:
Measure of change in a periodic variable over one period, or of the magnitude of a non-periodic signal relative to a reference value. Peak-to-peak amplitude is the change between peak and trough, written Vpp when applied to voltages, and is often read on an [[Oscilloscope|oscilloscope]]. Semi-amplitude is half of that. Most scientific literature uses 'amplitude' or 'peak amplitude' to mean semi-amplitude. In astronomy, semi-amplitude is the most widely used measure of orbital wobble, and small radial velocity semi-amplitudes help in the search for [[Exoplanet|exoplanets]]. Root mean square (RMS) amplitude is used especially in [[Electrical engineering|electrical engineering]], because the average power transmitted by a wave is proportional to the square of the RMS amplitude. For [[Alternating current|alternating current]] power, the universal practice is to specify RMS values of a sinusoidal waveform.

Regional military governors gained autonomy, and their secessionism continued into the [[Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period]]. The later [[Song dynasty]] distrusted prominent commanders to avoid a repeat, and the rebellion began the long decline of the Guanzhong region.

Family of statistical methods for comparing the means of two or more groups by analyzing [[Variance|variance]]. It is often said to have begun with [[Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet]]'s 1837 introduction of Dirichlet L-functions, which he used to prove Dirichlet's theorem on arithmetic progressions. Multiplicative number theory studies the distribution of [[Prime number|primes]], including the [[Prime number theorem]], which states that the number of primes up to x is approximately x / ln(x). === KEY: Analytical_chemistry_885bbc
HEADWORD: Analytical chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amount of substance | Chromatography | Gustav Kirchhoff | Justus von Liebig | Machine learning | Mass spectrometry | Spectroscopy
ENTRY:
Branch of chemistry concerned with identifying the composition of materials and quantifying the components of mixtures.

Analytical chemists develop and apply methods to identify unknown compounds and to express a compound's presence as [[Amount of substance|amount of substance]], concentration, percentage by mass, number of moles, or, for gases, partial pressure. Qualitative analysis establishes which substances are present; quantitative analysis determines how much.

Classical techniques, such as titration and gravimetric analysis, remain the basis of undergraduate laboratory teaching, and qualitative tests include the acid test for gold. Modern work is dominated by instrumental methods: [[Spectroscopy|spectroscopy]], [[Chromatography|chromatography]], [[Mass spectrometry|mass spectrometry]], and electrochemical methods.

The field has been important since the early days of chemistry. [[Justus von Liebig]] developed systematic elemental analysis. The first instrumental analysis was flame emissive spectrometry, developed by Robert Bunsen and [[Gustav Kirchhoff]], who discovered rubidium and caesium in 1860. Most major developments came after 1900, as basic spectroscopic and spectrometric techniques were discovered in the early 20th century and refined in the late 20th century. From the 1970s, techniques were combined as hybrid methods to characterize samples completely, and the field grew to include biological questions (bioanalytical chemistry). Applications widened from academic questions to forensic, environmental, industrial, and medical ones, including pharmaceutical quality assurance and drug discovery.

In the 21st century the field has been shaped by digitalization. Large datasets from instruments such as Orbitrap mass spectrometers have made data analysis, including [[Machine learning|machine learning]], an essential skill. Real-time, point-of-care diagnostic sensors, automation, and miniaturization are strong trends, and Green Analytical Chemistry aims to minimize the environmental impact of analyses.

=== KEY: Anarchism_d20091
HEADWORD: Anarchism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Laozi | Libertarianism | Paris Commune | Pierre-Joseph Proudhon | Russian Civil War | Socrates | Spanish Civil War
ENTRY:
Political philosophy and movement seeking to abolish institutions that perpetuate authority, coercion, or hierarchy, chiefly the state and capitalism.

Anarchists advocate replacing hierarchical structures with stateless societies of horizontal networks of voluntary associations, based on free association and reciprocity. Anarchism is described as part of the libertarian wing of the socialist movement, known broadly as libertarian socialism.

The word comes from the Ancient Greek *anarkhia*, "without a ruler," and appears in English from 1642. [[Pierre-Joseph Proudhon]] was the first political philosopher to call himself an anarchist and give the word a positive meaning. Since the 1890s, beginning in France, [[Libertarianism|libertarianism]] has often been a synonym; in the United States some usages refer only to individualistic free-market philosophy. Many scholars reject anarcho-capitalism as a misunderstanding of anarchist principles.

Precursors are traced to Taoist philosophers Zhuang Zhou and [[Laozi]] and to the Stoics, to the Greek tragedians Aeschylus and Sophocles, and to [[Socrates]], who insisted on freedom of conscience. In the Sasanian Empire, Mazdak called for an egalitarian society and the abolition of monarchy. Modern anarchism emerged from the Enlightenment. It flourished worldwide from the late 19th century into the early 20th, with a significant role in workers' struggles. Anarchists took part in several revolutions, most notably the [[Paris Commune]], the [[Russian Civil War]], and the [[Spanish Civil War]], whose conclusion marked the end of the classical era of anarchism. It has been resurgent since the late 20th century.

Approaches are often categorized as evolutionary, favoring gradual and often nonviolent change, or revolutionary, seeking to destroy oppressive states and institutions.

=== KEY: Anatomy_e080e3
HEADWORD: Anatomy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andreas Vesalius | Dissection | Endoscopy | Galen | Liver | Medical imaging | Plant anatomy
ENTRY:
Branch of morphology concerned with the internal and external structure of organisms and their parts.

The word derives from the Greek *anatomē*, "dissection." Anatomy is a natural science with beginnings in prehistoric times, and it is distinct from physiology and biochemistry, which deal with function and chemical processes. An anatomist studies the shape, size, position, structure, blood supply, and innervation of an organ such as the [[Liver|liver]]; a physiologist studies, for example, the production of bile. The two are often studied together, and human anatomy is a basic science of medicine.

The discipline divides into macroscopic (gross) anatomy, the study of structures visible to the naked eye, which includes surface anatomy, and microscopic anatomy, which includes histology (the study of tissues), cytology (cells), and embryology. Regional anatomy treats all structures in one body region, such as the abdomen; systemic anatomy treats the structures of a body system, such as the digestive system. Methods include [[Dissection|dissection]], [[Endoscopy|endoscopy]], and angiography, which visualizes blood vessels. The term usually means human anatomy but also covers other animals, sometimes called zootomy; plant structures are studied in [[Plant anatomy]].

Study of anatomy dates to the Hippocratic school in the 5th century BC, which documented body structures through observation and animal dissections. In the Renaissance, [[Andreas Vesalius]] published *De Humani Corporis Fabrica* in 1543, correcting longstanding errors from [[Galen]] with illustrated dissections based on direct human observation. Methods advanced from dissection of animal carcasses and cadavers to 20th-century [[Medical imaging|medical imaging]], including X-ray, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging.

=== KEY: Ancient_Celtic_religion_d98ebb

Polytheistic belief system of the Celtic peoples of Europe, also called Celtic polytheism or Celtic paganism. === KEY: Ancient_Egypt_b7e128
HEADWORD: Ancient Egypt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Levant | Narmer | Nile | Nubia | Pharaoh | Ptolemaic Kingdom
ENTRY:
Bronze Age civilization along the lower [[Nile]] in northeastern Africa, and a cradle of civilization.

It emerged around 3150 BC (by conventional chronology), when Upper and Lower Egypt were united by Menes, whom most Egyptologists believe to be the same person as [[Narmer]]. Its history comprised stable kingdoms, the Old, Middle, and New, separated by Intermediate Periods of instability. The New Kingdom was the pinnacle of Egyptian power, extending its rule over much of [[Nubia]] and a considerable portion of the [[Levant]]. Egypt then slowly declined and was invaded or conquered by the Hyksos, Kushites, Assyrians, Persians, Greeks, and Romans. The end of ancient Egypt is variously placed at 332 BC, during the wars of [[Alexander the Great]], or at 30 BC, when the Greek-ruled [[Ptolemaic Kingdom]] fell to Rome.

Success rested on the Nile's predictable flooding and controlled irrigation, which produced surplus crops and supported dense population. A bureaucracy of scribes, religious leaders, and administrators served the [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]], who was believed to embody the god Horus on earth. Predynastic cultures with firm control of agriculture and animal husbandry had developed by about 5500 BC; the Naqada culture grew over about 1,000 years from small farming communities into a powerful civilization.

Achievements include quarrying, surveying, and construction techniques behind monumental pyramids, temples, and obelisks; mathematics, as in the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus; medicine; irrigation; the first known planked boats; faience and glass technology; and the earliest known peace treaty, with the Hittite Empire. Religion centered on preparation for the afterlife through mummification. Egyptian art and architecture were widely copied, and its ruins inspired travelers and writers for millennia.

=== KEY: Ancient_Egyptian_architecture_02f3ef
HEADWORD: Ancient Egyptian architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arch | Column | Papyrus | Pyramid | Ramesses II | Sphinx
ENTRY:
Set of changing styles of building in ancient Egypt, most of whose surviving examples are religious and funerary structures. Because wood was scarce, the main materials were sun-dried mud brick and stone, chiefly limestone, with sandstone and granite used in considerable quantities. From the Old Kingdom onward stone was generally reserved for tombs and temples, while brick was used for palaces, fortresses and town walls. Monumental buildings used post-and-lintel construction, with thick sloping walls and closely spaced [[Column|columns]], and many were aligned astronomically. Although the [[Arch]] was developed in the fourth dynasty, monumental buildings did not adopt it. Column capitals were carved to resemble plants such as the [[Papyrus|papyrus]], and walls bore painted carvings. The best-known examples are the [[Pyramid|pyramids]] and the [[Sphinx]]. The Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, built by [[Ramesses II]], has columns up to 20 meters high. Egyptian motifs later influenced architecture elsewhere, first in the Greek Orientalizing period and again in 19th-century Egyptomania.

=== KEY: Ancient_Egyptian_literature_9db6d9
HEADWORD: Ancient Egyptian literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Papyrus
ENTRY:
Body of writing in the Egyptian language from the pharaonic period to the end of Roman domination. Writing appeared in the late 4th millennium BC, and the literature is the oldest corpus of Egyptian writing; with Sumerian literature, it is considered the world’s earliest. Old Kingdom works included funerary texts, letters, hymns, poems and autobiographies of administrative officials. A narrative literature emerged only in the early Middle Kingdom, reflecting a rising class of scribes and unprecedented literacy. Writing was an elite exercise, monopolized by scribes attached to government offices and the royal court. Popular tales included *The Story of Sinuhe* and *The Eloquent Peasant*, and teaching texts such as *The Instructions of Amenemhat* were often pseudonymous, attributed to famous figures. Texts survive on [[Papyrus|papyrus]], ostraca, wooden boards, monuments and coffins. The moist Nile floodplain destroyed most papyri, so much of the literature is lost.

=== KEY: Ancient_Egyptian_religion_3dee8c

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Polytheistic belief system of ancient Egypt, centered on interactions with about 1,500 known deities. Rituals such as prayer and offerings sought the gods’ favor. Formal religion centered on the [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]], regarded as divine, who maintained *Ma’at*, the cosmic order, and repelled *Isfet*, chaos, through rituals and offerings. The state devoted enormous resources to temples. Individuals sought help through prayer or magic, and this popular tradition grew more prominent as the pharaoh’s status declined. Great effort went into securing the soul’s survival after death through tombs, grave goods and offerings. The religion lasted for 3,500 years. === KEY: Ancient_Greece_d6d280
HEADWORD: Ancient Greece
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Ancient Rome | City-state | Herodotus | Mycenaean Greece | Parthenon | Peloponnesian War | Philip II of Macedon
ENTRY:
Civilization of culturally and linguistically related but independent [[City-state|city-states]] in the northeastern Mediterranean, from the Greek Dark Ages (12th–9th centuries BC) to the end of classical antiquity (c. 600 AD).

Before Roman rule the area was unified only once, from 338 to 323 BC under Macedon. Ancient Greece begins with [[Mycenaean Greece]], which declined in the Bronze Age collapse c. 1100 BC. Urban city-states (poleis) formed in the 8th century BC, ushering in the Archaic period (c. 800–500 BC) and Greek colonization of the Mediterranean; Near Eastern contact inspired coinage and the Greek alphabet.

The Classical period runs from the Persian invasion of 480 BC to the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC. It included the Golden Age of Athens, the [[Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BC) between Athens and Sparta, and the creation of the Hellenic league under [[Philip II of Macedon]], whose son [[Alexander the Great]] conquered Persia's Achaemenid Empire. Athenian direct democracy was restricted to free adult males. Alexander's conquests spread Greek culture into North Africa and Western Asia in the Hellenistic period (323–146 BC), which ended c. 30 BC when Egypt's Ptolemaic Kingdom was annexed by Rome. Late antiquity closed with Justinian I's shutting of the Academy of Athens in 529.

Ancient Greece is the first period attested in comprehensive narrative history. [[Herodotus]], called the "father of history," wrote the *Histories* between the 450s and 420s BC, and his accuracy is debated. Successors such as Thucydides were mostly Athenian or pro-Athenian, so more is known of Athens than of other cities. Classical Greek culture, as exemplified by the [[Parthenon]], especially its philosophy, strongly influenced [[Ancient Rome]], and Classical Greece is generally considered the cradle of Western civilization.

=== KEY: Ancient_Greek_philosophy_bb1b51
HEADWORD: Ancient Greek philosophy
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Rational inquiry into the cosmos, knowledge and ethics, from the 6th century BC into the Hellenistic period. It dealt with astronomy, epistemology, mathematics, political philosophy, ethics, metaphysics, logic, biology, rhetoric and aesthetics. Philosophers before Socrates, called pre-Socratics, were concerned mainly with cosmology, ontology and mathematics, although the term is now often replaced by “Early Greek Philosophy.” [[Socrates]], [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] formed the Athenian school. === KEY: Ancient_Greek_religion_720097

Polytheistic beliefs, rituals and myths of the ancient Greeks, in both public religion and cult practice. The Greeks had no word for religion in the modern sense, and no writer classified gods or cults into separate religions; [[Herodotus]] described the Hellenes as sharing common shrines and sacrifices. Most recognized twelve major Olympian gods, including [[Zeus]], Hera, [[Poseidon]], [[Athena]] and [[Aphrodite]], although [[Stoicism]] and some forms of Platonism assumed a single transcendent deity. Religious beliefs and practices of the civilizations of Sumer, Akkad, Assyria and Babylonia, from about 6000 BC to 500 AD. It was a consistent tradition that adapted to the needs of its adherents over millennia. Worship of natural forces as providers of sustenance is evident from the mid-4th millennium BC, when writing was invented. The [[Sumer|Sumerians]] created the first city-states, such as [[Uruk]] and [[Ur]], and developed writing, early mathematics, wheeled chariots, astronomy, a written code of law and the calendar. In the 3rd millennium BC, objects of worship became an expansive cast of deities. Later, the gods were structured into a hierarchy with the national god of each state at its head. [[Enlil]] and [[Inanna]] were among the most important figures. Worship centered on temples, daily offerings and festivals, and [[Divination|divination]] sought divine intentions. Under [[Sargon of Akkad]], the Akkadian Empire united Mesopotamia around 2335 BC. The religion declined with Christianization between the 1st and 5th centuries AD.

Athletic competitions among [[City-state|city-states]], held every four years at Olympia in honor of [[Zeus]]. Traditionally dated to 776 BC, they were one of the Panhellenic Games of ancient Greece. === KEY: Ancient_Roman_architecture_2a5922
HEADWORD: Ancient Roman architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek architecture | Arch | Colosseum | Dome | Pompeii | Romanesque architecture
ENTRY:
Architecture of ancient Rome, from the founding of the Republic in 509 BC to about the 4th century AD. It adopted the external language of classical [[Ancient Greek architecture|Greek architecture]] but developed a new style, and the two are often considered one body of classical architecture. Most surviving buildings date from the Empire, and the greatest achievements came from about 40 BC to 230 AD. Romans used [[Dome|domes]], [[Arch|arches]] and Roman concrete, moving from column-and-lintel construction to massive walls punctuated by arches. Western European building from about 1000 is called [[Romanesque architecture|Romanesque]], reflecting its dependence on Roman forms.

=== KEY: Ancient_Rome_c921a0
HEADWORD: Ancient Rome
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeneid | Aqueduct (water supply) | Augustus | Roman Empire | Roman Kingdom | Roman Republic | Romulus and Remus | Trajan
ENTRY:
Roman civilization from the founding of the city of Rome in the 8th century BC to the collapse of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century AD.

It comprises the [[Roman Kingdom]] (753–509 BC), the [[Roman Republic]] (509–27 BC), and the [[Roman Empire]] (27 BC–476 AD) until the fall of the western empire. Rome began as an Italic settlement beside the Tiber, traditionally dated to 753 BC. Myth credited [[Romulus and Remus]], offspring of Mars; a rival legend, told by Virgil in the [[Aeneid]], had Aeneas lead Trojans to found a new Troy. By the end of the 6th century BC the city controlled some 780 sq km, with a population perhaps as high as 35,000.

The Republic was established c. 509 BC, according to tradition, when the last king, Tarquin the Proud, was deposed. It was governed by annually elected magistrates, notably two consuls, with a Senate and representative assemblies, under a constitution of checks and balances and separation of powers. Rome came to control its neighbors through treaties and military strength and became the dominant power in the Mediterranean. The state evolved from an elective monarchy to a republic and then to an increasingly autocratic military dictatorship, with [[Augustus]] as emperor from 27 BC.

At its height, under [[Trajan]], the empire covered around 5 million sq km in AD 117, with an estimated 50 to 90 million inhabitants, roughly 20% of the world's population. A professional army, roads, and [[Aqueduct (water supply)|aqueducts]] sustained administration. The last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, was deposed in 476 AD; the Eastern Empire lasted until 1453. The res publica inspired modern republics such as the United States and France.

=== KEY: Ancient_history_a42622
HEADWORD: Ancient history
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cuneiform | Fertile Crescent | Göbekli Tepe | Hammurabi | Indus Valley Civilisation | Mesopotamia | Neolithic Revolution | Sargon of Akkad
ENTRY:
Period from the beginning of writing and recorded human history through late antiquity, covering all inhabited continents c. 3000 BC–AD 500.

Recorded history spans roughly 5,000 years, beginning with the development of Sumerian [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] script. The three-age system divides the period into the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age, with recorded history usually counted from the Bronze Age; the start and end of each age vary by region. The world population grew exponentially as the [[Neolithic Revolution]] progressed: an estimated 2 million in 10,000 BC, 45 million by 3000 BC, 72 million by 1000 BC, and 209 million by AD 500, a hundredfold increase in 10,500 years.

Knowledge of the preceding prehistory comes largely from archaeology. *Homo erectus* spread across Eurasia 1.8 million years ago. Dates for the emergence of *Homo sapiens* range from 250,000 to 160,000 years ago; some 50,000 years ago they migrated out of Africa, reaching Australia about 45,000 years ago and the Americas about 15,000 years ago. Evidence for agriculture appears about 9000 BC in eastern Turkey and spread through the [[Fertile Crescent]]; settlement at [[Göbekli Tepe]] began around 9500 BC and may have the world's oldest temple. Farming arose independently in the Nile Valley, West Africa, China, New Guinea, and Mesoamerica. Dogs were domesticated at least 15,000 years ago, sheep and goats around 9000 BC, cattle and water buffalo around 7000 BC, and horses, donkeys, and camels by about 4000 BC. Copper smelting began around 6000 BC in western Asia, and bronze alloys about 3500 BC in Mesopotamia and independently in China by 2000 BC. The potter's wheel was invented between 5000 and 4000 BC and adapted into wheeled vehicles by 3000 BC.

Writing developed separately in Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, China, and Mesoamerica. Proto-literate cuneiform spread in the Middle East by 3400 BC, Egyptian hieroglyphs appeared about 3200 BC, and the undeciphered Indus script had developed by 2800 BC in the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]]. Chinese characters arose in oracle bone script dated 1600 to 1100 BC, and writing in Mesoamerica dates to 600 BC.

The ancient Near East is considered the cradle of civilization: it produced the first centralized governments, law codes, and empires, along with social stratification, slavery, and organized warfare, and began astronomy and mathematics. In [[Mesopotamia]], agricultural communities emerged with the Halaf culture around 8000 BC, and cities began in the Uruk period (4000–3100 BC). [[Hammurabi]] created a Babylonian empire from the former kingdoms of Sumer and Akkad. Akkad reached its height between about 2330 and 2150 BC under [[Sargon of Akkad]], and by 1450 BC its language had become the main language of diplomacy in the Near East. Assyria, which at its height controlled the Fertile Crescent, Egypt, and much of Anatolia, ruled in three periods: Old, Middle, and Neo-Assyrian.

=== KEY: Andean_civilizations_646c92
HEADWORD: Andean civilizations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andes | Chavín culture | Chimor | Cusco | Inca Empire | Llama | Potato | Tiwanaku
ENTRY:
Complex indigenous societies of South America, stretching 4,000 km along the [[Andes]] from southern Colombia through Ecuador and Peru to northern Chile and northwest Argentina.

The Caral or Norte Chico civilization of coastal Peru, dating back to 3500 BC, is the oldest known civilization in the Americas, and scholars deem Andean civilization "pristine," meaning it developed independently of other civilizations. Earliest settlement of the region dates to about 15,000 BC. Later complex societies included [[Chavín culture]] (900–200 BC), Paracas (800–200 BC), its successor Nazca (200 BC–800 AD), Moche (100–700), Wari and [[Tiwanaku]] (both 600–1000), and [[Chimor]] (900–1470). In 1438 the Incas, from [[Cusco]], founded Tahuantinsuyu, the [[Inca Empire]], the largest empire the Americas had seen; it rose from the Kingdom of Cuzco, founded around 1230. In the 16th century Spanish colonizers subjugated the indigenous kingdoms and brought the region into the Spanish Empire.

Despite high mountains and hyper-arid desert, Andean peoples domesticated crops of worldwide importance, including [[Potato|potatoes]], peppers, peanuts, manioc, and coca. Notable achievements were monumental architecture, an extensive road system, and textile weaving.

Andean civilizations lacked a written language, using instead the quipu, a system of knotted and colored strings that survives in few examples and has not been fully deciphered; scholars differ on whether it recorded only numerical data or also narrative. They also lacked wheeled vehicles and draft animals: goods moved by human or by [[Llama|llama]], which could carry loads up to one-fourth of its weight, a maximum of 45 kg. Most economies were organized on reciprocity and redistribution rather than money.

=== KEY: Andes_d7a688
HEADWORD: Andes
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Longest continental mountain range in the world, forming a continuous highland along the western edge of [[South America]].

The range is 200 to 700 km (120 to 430 mi) wide, widest between 18°S and 20°S latitude, and has an average height of about 4,000 m (13,000 ft). It runs south to north through seven countries: Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela.

The Andes are the highest mountain range outside Asia. The highest peak, [[Aconcagua]] in Argentina, rises to about 6,961 m (22,838 ft). Chimborazo in Ecuador is farther from the Earth's center than any other point on the Earth's surface, because of the equatorial bulge. The Andes are an orogenic belt along the Pacific [[Ring of Fire]], raised during the Mesozoic and Tertiary eras as the Nazca Plate converges with the South American Plate and oceanic crust is subducted beneath it. The setting produces frequent [[Earthquake|earthquakes]] and volcanic activity. To the east, the range is bounded by sedimentary basins, including the Orinoco, Amazon, and Madre de Dios basins and the [[Gran Chaco]]. Blood disorder in which the blood has a reduced ability to carry oxygen. === KEY: Anesthesia_c1f84e
HEADWORD: Anesthesia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amnesia | Benzodiazepine | Boston | Central nervous system | Chloroform | Cocaine
ENTRY:
Medically induced, controlled, temporary loss of sensation or awareness.

Anesthesia is induced for medical, veterinary, or criminal purposes and may include some or all of analgesia (relief from or prevention of pain), paralysis (muscle relaxation), amnesia (loss of memory), and unconsciousness. Its three basic goals are hypnosis, analgesia, and muscle relaxation; different techniques affect these endpoints differently, and the aim is to reach those the procedure requires with the least risk to the patient.

Three broad categories exist. General anesthesia suppresses [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]] activity and produces unconsciousness and total lack of sensation, using injected or inhaled drugs. Sedation suppresses the system to a lesser degree, inhibiting anxiety and the creation of long-term memories without causing unconsciousness; [[Benzodiazepine]]-type sedatives favor [[Amnesia|amnesia]]. Regional and local anesthesia block nerve impulses from a specific part of the body and include local infiltration, peripheral nerve blocks, and neuraxial blockade (mainly epidural and spinal anesthesia).

Preparation begins with a pre-operative risk assessment of medical history, physical examination, and lab tests. Risks are related to the individual's health, which has the greatest impact, and to the anesthetic technique. Major perioperative risks include death, heart attack, and pulmonary embolism; minor ones include postoperative nausea and vomiting. An estimated 22% of people dream under general anesthesia, and one or two cases in a thousand have some consciousness, termed "anesthesia awareness."

Modern anesthesia dates from the mid-19th century. On October 16, 1846, William T.G. Morton used ether to anesthetize a patient during a tumor removal at Massachusetts General Hospital in [[Boston]]. [[Chloroform]] followed in 1847 and [[Cocaine|cocaine]] as a local anesthetic in 1884.

=== KEY: Aneurysm_62fd84
HEADWORD: Aneurysm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atherosclerosis | Blood pressure | Brain | Myocardial infarction
ENTRY:
Bulge or balloon-like swelling in the wall of a blood vessel, caused by a localized weakness. Aneurysms may be hereditary or acquired, and they often develop without symptoms. As an aneurysm grows, the risk of rupture rises, which can cause uncontrolled bleeding. Most affect arteries. The most dangerous include cerebral aneurysms in the [[Brain|brain]] and aortic aneurysms in the chest and abdomen. Risk factors include [[Blood pressure|high blood pressure]], smoking and [[Atherosclerosis]]. Aneurysms occur in about 1–2 percent of the population. Aneurysms can also serve as the starting point for blood clots, and they can arise in the heart after a [[Myocardial infarction|heart attack]].
