=== KEY: Time_a76d4e
HEADWORD: Time
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Calendar | General relativity | Gregorian calendar | Julius Caesar | Second | Spacetime | Sundial
ENTRY:
Continuous progression of existence in an apparently irreversible succession from the past, through the present, and into the future. Time sequences events, compares their durations or the intervals between them, and quantifies rates of change in material reality or conscious experience. It is often called the fourth dimension, in addition to the three spatial dimensions. Time has been a central subject of study in religion, philosophy, and science, and temporal measurement has been a prime motivation in navigation and astronomy.

Defining time without circularity has eluded scholars. Traditional definitions relied on periodic motion such as the sun's apparent path, the phases of the moon, and the swing of a pendulum. In physics, time is a fundamental concept used to define quantities such as velocity and is operationally defined as "what a clock reads," a count of repeating events such as the SI second. Physicists developed the concept of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], in which events have four coordinates, three for space and one for time. Under [[General relativity|general relativity]] time can be dilated by velocity and matter, though the effect is negligible outside extreme conditions such as relativistic speeds or the gravity of black holes. Measurable time is believed to have begun with the [[Big Bang]] 13.8 billion years ago. Reconciling general relativity with quantum mechanics, in which time is a universal and absolute parameter, is called the problem of time, and no generally accepted theory of quantum general relativity exists.

Measurement takes two forms: the [[Calendar|calendar]], used for periods longer than a day, and the clock, used for shorter periods. The [[Second|second]], the SI base unit, is defined by the electronic transition frequency of caesium atoms. Artifacts from the Paleolithic suggest the moon was used to reckon time at least 6,000 years ago. Lunar calendars had years of 12 or 13 lunar months (354 or 384 days), and lunisolar calendars add a thirteenth month to some years. The Maya developed interrelated calendars, including a 260-day sacred calendar, the Tzolk'in. The reforms of [[Julius Caesar]] in 45 BC put the Roman world on a solar calendar, which Pope Gregory XIII corrected in 1582; the [[Gregorian calendar]] is now the most commonly used worldwide. The French Republican Calendar, with ten hours of a hundred minutes of a hundred seconds in each day, was abolished in 1806. Egyptians are credited with dividing the day into smaller parts using the [[Sundial|sundial]]; obelisks used as gnomons were built as early as c. 3500 BC, and a device dating to c. 1500 BC also measured time by shadow.

=== KEY: Time_dilation_8c3941
HEADWORD: Time dilation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Global Positioning System | Satellite navigation | Special relativity | Speed of light | Velocity
ENTRY:
Difference in elapsed time between two clocks, caused by [[Velocity|relative velocity]] or by a difference in gravitational potential. [[Special relativity]] predicts that a clock moving relative to an observer ticks more slowly, and [[Albert Einstein]] showed in 1905 that the effect concerns the nature of time itself. The faster the relative velocity, the greater the dilation, with time slowing to a stop as a clock approaches the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. Predictions have been repeatedly confirmed by experiment, and the effect is of practical concern in [[Satellite navigation]] systems such as the [[Global Positioning System]].

=== KEY: Time_travel_bfca0e
HEADWORD: Time travel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: General relativity | H. G. Wells | Qin dynasty | Quantum mechanics | Science fiction | Song dynasty | Sun Wukong | Time dilation
ENTRY:
Hypothetical travel into the past or future. The idea of a time machine was popularized by [[H. G. Wells]]'s 1895 novel *The Time Machine*, and the concept appears in philosophy, physics and fiction, particularly [[Science fiction]]. Forward time travel is an observed phenomenon through [[Time dilation|time dilation]] and is well understood within special and [[General relativity]]. Making one body advance or delay more than a few milliseconds relative to another is not feasible with current technology. Whether travel to the past is physically possible is uncertain, and it may raise questions of causality. Some solutions of general relativity allow it, such as a rotating black hole, but travel to an arbitrary point in spacetime has limited support and is usually linked to [[Quantum mechanics]] or wormholes. One early example of backward time travel is the Chinese novel *A Supplement to the Journey to the West* (c. 1640), in which [[Sun Wukong]] travels back to the [[Qin dynasty]] and forward to the [[Song dynasty]].

=== KEY: Time_zone_1c9648
HEADWORD: Time zone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coordinated Universal Time | Daylight saving time | Greenwich Mean Time | India | Longitude
ENTRY:
Area that observes a uniform standard time for legal, commercial and social purposes. Time zones tend to follow national borders rather than strict [[Longitude]]. Each is defined by an offset from [[Coordinated Universal Time]] (UTC), ranging from UTC−12:00 to UTC+14:00. Most offsets are whole hours, but some, such as those in [[India]] and Nepal, differ by 30 or 45 minutes. Many areas use [[Daylight saving time]] in spring and summer, one hour ahead. Each place once kept its own solar time, which varies by about four minutes for each degree of longitude. The Royal Observatory at Greenwich, founded in 1675, established [[Greenwich Mean Time]] (GMT) as a standard reference for mariners. The Great Western Railway began using GMT in 1840, and North American railroads implemented four continental zones on November 18, 1883.

=== KEY: Timeline_of_the_far_future_ae8619

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Scientific projections of far-future events, from 3001 CE onward. Predictions draw on astrophysics, particle physics, evolutionary biology, plate tectonics and sociology. All projections must account for the [[Second law of thermodynamics]], under which entropy rises over time. The Sun will likely expand enough to overwhelm [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]], [[Venus]] and possibly Earth, then shrink to the size of a [[White dwarf]]. Long afterward, physicists expect matter itself to disintegrate through radioactive decay. Five spacecraft, including [[Voyager 1]], [[Voyager 2]] and [[New Horizons]], are on trajectories out of the Solar System and should persist indefinitely.

=== KEY: Timur_c37789
HEADWORD: Timur
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Delhi Sultanate | Genghis Khan | Golden Horde | Mamluk Sultanate | Ottoman Empire | Samarkand | Timurid Empire | Ulugh Beg
ENTRY:
Turco-Mongol conqueror, first ruler of the Timurid dynasty and founder of the [[Timurid Empire]], which ruled over modern-day Afghanistan, Iran, and Central Asia. Also known as Tamerlane, Timur was born into the Turkicized Mongol confederation of the Barlas in Transoxiana. By 1370 he controlled the western Chagatai Khanate, and from there led campaigns that defeated the Khans of the [[Golden Horde]], the [[Mamluk Sultanate]] in Egypt and Syria, the emerging [[Ottoman Empire]], and the [[Delhi Sultanate]] in the Indian subcontinent. He made [[Samarkand]] his capital.

His origins are debated. Historian Peter Jackson considers it generally accepted that Timur was of "unimpeachable Mongol ancestry," and Timurid histories trace his paternal descent to a male-line ancestor of [[Genghis Khan]]. No written evidence from Timur's lifetime or earlier, however, makes such a link to him directly. He sought to revive Genghis Khan's legacy and saw himself as restorer of the Mongol Empire, and by the end of his reign he controlled the remnants of the Chagatai Khanate, the Ilkhanate, and the Golden Horde and had even attempted to restore the Yuan dynasty in China.

Timur's armies were multi-ethnic and much feared, and his campaigns laid waste to large parts of Asia, Africa, and Europe. Scholars estimate that they caused the deaths of millions of people. He also patronized scholars and poets such as Ibn Khaldun and Hafez, and his reign led to the Timurid Renaissance. He died while on the way to invade Ming China, and his empire fragmented shortly afterward. He was the grandfather of [[Ulugh Beg]], an astronomer and mathematician who ruled Central Asia from 1411 to 1449, and the great-great-great-grandfather of Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire.

ENTRY:
Soft, silvery-white [[Metal|metal]] with the symbol Sn and atomic number 50. Tin is a post-transition metal in group 14 of the [[Periodic table]], obtained chiefly from the [[Mineral|mineral]] cassiterite. It is malleable and bends by hand, producing a "tin cry" from the twinning of its crystals. Tin has the largest number of stable [[Isotope|isotopes]] in the periodic table, 10 in all. The first large-scale alloy was [[Bronze]], made with copper as early as 3000 BC. [[Pewter]], an alloy of 85–90% tin, has been used for flatware since the Bronze Age. Inorganic tin has low toxicity and resists [[Corrosion]], so tin-plated steel is widely used for food packaging, as "tin cans."

=== KEY: Tinnitus_fbbaa3

ENTRY:
Perception of ringing or other sounds with no corresponding external source. The word comes from [[Latin]] *tinnire*, "to ring." It affects about 10–15% of people, but only 1–2% find it a severe problem. Tinnitus is a symptom rather than a disease, most often caused by hearing damage or age-related [[Hearing loss|hearing loss]], and it can also result from ear infections or [[Brain tumor|brain tumors]]. Diagnosis rests on the patient's description and an audiogram, with [[Magnetic resonance imaging]] used if other problems are suspected. No drug treats it; management relies on sound generators, hearing aids and counseling.

=== KEY: Tissue_biology_9097d9
HEADWORD: Tissue
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Connective tissue | Epidermis (botany) | Ground tissue | Muscle | Nervous tissue | Organ (biology) | Photosynthesis | Vascular tissue
ENTRY:
Group of similar cells and their extracellular matrix, from the same embryonic origin, that performs a specific function. Tissues lie between cells and [[Organ (biology)|organs]], which are formed by grouping several tissues. The word derives from the French *tissu*, "fabric." The study of tissues is called histology; Xavier Bichat is considered its father. In plants, tissues fall into three systems: the [[Epidermis (botany)|epidermis]], [[Ground tissue]] and [[Vascular tissue]]. Meristematic tissue, found at growing tips, consists of actively dividing cells that increase a plant's length and thickness. Ground tissue makes nutrients by [[Photosynthesis]] and stores reserves. Animal tissues are classified as epithelial, [[Connective tissue]], [[Muscle]] and [[Nervous tissue]].

=== KEY: Titan_moon_7dac51

ENTRY:
Largest moon of [[Saturn]] and second largest in the [[Solar System]], after Jupiter's [[Ganymede (moon)|Ganymede]]. [[Christiaan Huygens]] discovered it in 1655. Titan is the only moon known to have a dense atmosphere, denser than Earth's, and the only known object besides Earth with stable bodies of surface liquid. Its atmosphere is mainly nitrogen and [[Methane]], forming hydrocarbon clouds and haze. The Cassini–Huygens mission, which arrived in 2004, revealed hydrocarbon lakes in the polar regions. === KEY: Titanium_6c1aad

Chemical element with atomic number 22 and symbol Ti. Titanium can be reduced to a lustrous [[Transition metal|transition metal]] with a silver color, low density and high strength, resistant to corrosion in sea water, aqua regia and chlorine. It was discovered in [[Cornwall]], Great Britain, by William Gregor in 1791 and named by Martin Heinrich Klaproth after the [[Titans]] of Greek mythology. Alloyed with [[Aluminium|aluminum]], vanadium, molybdenum and iron, it is used in jet engines, missiles, spacecraft, desalination plants, golf clubs and consumer electronics. Its biocompatibility suits it to prostheses, orthopedic implants and dental implants.

=== KEY: Tobacco_mosaic_virus_ceee2e

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
RNA [[Virus|virus]] that infects many plants, especially [[Tobacco|tobacco]]. It causes mottled, mosaic-like discoloration of the leaves and was the first virus discovered. Adolf Mayer described the disease in 1886. In 1892 Dmitri Ivanovsky showed that its infectious agent passed through filters fine enough to trap [[Bacteria|bacteria]]. In 1898 Martinus Beijerinck showed that it reproduces in host cells and adopted the term virus. Wendell Meredith Stanley crystallized it in 1935, news that appeared on the front page of *[[The New York Times]]*. Stanley received one-fourth of the 1946 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, although some of his conclusions were later shown to be incorrect. [[Rosalind Franklin]] built a model of the virus for the 1958 World's Fair in [[Brussels]]. Its rod-shaped capsid is made of 2,130 coat-protein molecules around a single 6,400-base RNA strand.

Military government of Japan during the [[Edo period]], 1603–1868. Founded by [[Tokugawa Ieyasu]] after his victory at the [[Battle of Sekigahara]] in 1600, it ended the civil wars of the [[Sengoku period]]. The Tokugawa governed from Edo Castle in Edo, now Tokyo, together with daimyo lords who each administered a han, or feudal domain. Society was organized into a strict class system of samurai, farmers, artisans and merchants. Daimyo were required to keep their wives and children in the capital as hostages. Capital and most populous city of [[Japan]] (pop., 2023: over 14 million), at the head of Tokyo Bay on the central coast of [[Honshu]]. Officially the Tokyo Metropolis, it is Japan's economic center and the seat of its government and emperor. The Greater Tokyo Area, which includes parts of six neighboring prefectures, is the third most populous metropolitan area in the world, with 33 million residents as of 2025. It was greatly damaged by the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake and by Allied bombing in [[World War II]]. Rapid reconstruction beginning in the late 1940s fueled the Japanese economic miracle.

Tokyo was the first Asian city to host the Summer Olympics and Paralympics, in 1964, and hosted them again in 2021. Its gross domestic output was 120.2 trillion yen (US$887.9 billion) in fiscal year 2022, 21.2% of Japan's total. It remains a leading financial hub, and the Tokyo Stock Exchange is among the world's top five. The [[University of Tokyo]] is Japan's top-ranking university. Tokyo Station is the central hub of the [[Shinkansen]] high-speed railway, and Shinjuku Station is the world's busiest train station.

=== KEY: Tool_d421fd
HEADWORD: Tool
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Automation | Chimpanzee | Industrial Revolution | Interchangeable parts | Mesopotamia | Metalworking | Simple machine
ENTRY:
Object that extends an individual's ability to modify the surroundings or accomplish a task, typically solid, hand-operated, non-biological, and of a single broad purpose, unlike machines or computers. Humans make and use tools more than any other animal, including using tools to make other tools, but many animals also use tools. In 1981 Benjamin Beck published a widely used definition of tool use, later modified: the external employment of an unattached or manipulable attached object to alter the form, position, or condition of another object, another organism, or the user, with the user holding and directly manipulating it. By extension, concepts that support systematic thought are often called "tools" or "toolkits."

Anthropologists regard tool use as an important step in human evolution. Because humans and wild [[Chimpanzee|chimpanzees]] both use tools extensively, the first routine use is widely assumed to predate their divergence, though early tools were probably perishable, such as sticks. Stone artifacts date back about 2.5 million years, but a 2010 study suggests that *Australopithecus afarensis* carved animal carcasses with stone implements about 3.4 million years ago. Actual tools at least 2.6 million years old have been found in Ethiopia, and the hand axe is one of the earliest distinguishable forms. Early tools of stone, bone, and wood served for food preparation, hunting, weapons, clothing, pottery, and construction. The development of [[Metalworking|metalworking]] made new types possible, and harnessing animal power, wind, or steam allowed increasingly complex tools.

Several of the six classic [[Simple machine|simple machines]] (wheel and axle, lever, pulley, inclined plane, wedge, and screw) were invented in [[Mesopotamia]]. The wheel and axle first appeared with the potter's wheel in the 5th millennium BC, the lever in the shadoof c. 3000 BC, pulleys in the early 2nd millennium BC, and the screw in the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BC). Water wheels and watermills date to before 350 BC. Use of mechanical devices expanded in ancient Greece and Rome through waterwheels, and later windmills. Machine tools, with origins among 18th-century clock, watch, and instrument makers, surged during the [[Industrial Revolution]] and enabled precise, economical [[Interchangeable parts|interchangeable parts]]. Widespread [[Automation|automation]] in the 19th and 20th centuries let tools operate with minimal human supervision.

=== KEY: Topological_space_60ed22

=== KEY: Topology_99571e
HEADWORD: Topology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Graph theory | Henri Poincaré | Homotopy | Leonhard Euler | Metric space | Seven Bridges of Königsberg | Topological space
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerned with the properties of a geometric object that are preserved under continuous deformations such as stretching, twisting, crumpling, and bending, but not closing or opening holes, tearing, or gluing.

A [[Topological space]] is a set with a structure, called a topology, that allows continuous deformation to be defined. Examples include Euclidean spaces and, more generally, [[Metric space|metric spaces]], since any metric defines a topology. The deformations considered are homeomorphisms and homotopies, and a property invariant under them is a topological property. Basic examples are dimension, which distinguishes a line from a surface; compactness, which distinguishes a line from a circle; and connectedness, which distinguishes a circle from two non-intersecting circles. Intuitively, two spaces are homeomorphic if one can be deformed into the other without cutting or gluing, so a topologist cannot distinguish a coffee mug from a doughnut.

The underlying ideas go back to [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]], who in the 17th century envisioned *geometria situs* and *analysis situs*. [[Leonhard Euler]]'s 1736 paper on the [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg]] showed that no route through the town (now Kaliningrad) could cross each bridge exactly once, a result depending only on connectivity, and the problem led to [[Graph theory|graph theory]]. His polyhedron formula, V − E + F = 2, is regarded by some authorities as the first theorem of topology. Johann Benedict Listing introduced the term *Topologie* in 1847, and the English form appeared in 1883 in his obituary in *Nature*. [[Henri Poincaré]] consolidated and extended their work, and his 1895 paper *Analysis Situs* introduced the concepts now known as [[Homotopy|homotopy]] and homology. The idea of a topological space was developed only in the first decades of the 20th century.

ENTRY:
Japanese actor and film producer, noted for hypermasculine or heroic roles. He appeared in more than 180 screen credits, including 16 films with director [[Akira Kurosawa]], among them *Rashomon* (1950) and *[[Seven Samurai]]* (1954). He won Best Actor at the Venice Film Festival for *Yojimbo* (1961) and again for *Red Beard* (1965). He played [[Miyamoto Musashi]] in the *Samurai Trilogy* (1954–1956) and Admiral [[Isoroku Yamamoto]] in three films. In 1962 he established Mifune Productions, which achieved success with large-scale works such as *The Sands of Kurobe* (1968). He later worked in Hollywood, debuting in *Grand Prix* (1966). In 2016 his name was inscribed on the [[Hollywood Walk of Fame]].
