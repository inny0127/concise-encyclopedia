=== KEY: Whale_d157e6
HEADWORD: Whale
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blue whale | Cetacean | Krill | Plankton | Squid
ENTRY:
Large marine mammal, an informal grouping of [[Cetacean|cetaceans]] apart from dolphins and porpoises. Whales are fully aquatic placental mammals whose closest living relatives are hippopotamuses, from which cetaceans diverged about 54 million years ago. Baleen whales (Mysticeti) filter [[Krill|krill]] and [[Plankton|plankton]] through baleen plates, while toothed whales (Odontoceti) have conical teeth for catching fish or [[Squid|squid]]. The [[Blue whale]] reaches 29.9 m (98 ft) and 190 tonnes, the largest known animal, while the dwarf sperm whale is 2.6 m (8.5 ft) long. Whales breathe through blowholes, are warm-blooded and insulated by blubber. Calves are typically born in spring and summer. Once relentlessly hunted, whales are now protected by international law, but the North Atlantic right whale nearly became extinct in the 20th century, dropping to a low of 450. Whale watching is now a major form of tourism.

=== KEY: Whale_shark_290e6e
HEADWORD: Whale shark
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gujarat | Kerala | Krill | Plankton | Sardine | Whale
ENTRY:
Slow-moving, filter-feeding carpet shark, the largest living fish species. The only living species of the genus *Rhincodon* reaches 18.8 m (61.7 ft), the largest reliably recorded length, and is by far the largest living non-cetacean animal. It feeds on [[Plankton|plankton]], [[Krill|krill]], fish eggs and small schooling fish such as [[Sardine|sardines]], filtering over 6,000 liters of water an hour through its gill pads. It migrates across tropical oceans and gathers at coastal sites including Ningaloo Reef in Western Australia and off [[Gujarat]] and [[Kerala]] in India. It is docile toward humans, but the IUCN Red List lists it as Endangered after a population decline of more than 50% over the last 75 years, caused by targeted fishing, bycatch and ship collisions. Despite its name, it is a fish, not a mammal, and is not closely related to [[Whale|whales]].

=== KEY: Whaling_e8046a
HEADWORD: Whaling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Iceland | Industrial Revolution | Inuit | Japan | Norway
ENTRY:
Hunting of whales for meat, blubber and other products. Blubber could be rendered into oil that was important in the [[Industrial Revolution]]. The earliest well-documented industry dates to 1026 in the Basque coastal regions of Spain and France, and it spread through the North Atlantic. Subsistence whaling of small cetaceans by the [[Inuit]] and other peoples dates to at least 3000 BC, and a 2026 study dates whaling of baleen whales to at least 5,000 years ago in southern Brazil. Commercial fleets grew from the 17th century, and factory ships and explosive harpoons came in the first half of the 20th century; by the late 1930s more than 50,000 whales were killed annually. In 1982 the International Whaling Commission decided on a five-year pause on commercial whaling from 1986, which has since been extended indefinitely. [[Iceland]], [[Japan]] and [[Norway]] still hunt commercially, and whale watching has become a significant industry in many parts of the world.

=== KEY: Wheat_966d95
HEADWORD: Wheat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbohydrate | Common wheat | Fertile Crescent | Maize
ENTRY:
Group of wild and domesticated grasses of the genus *Triticum*, cultivated for their grain, a staple food worldwide. Species and hybrids include the most widely grown [[Common wheat]] (*T. aestivum*), spelt, durum, emmer, einkorn, and Khorasan or Kamut. The archaeological record suggests that wheat was first cultivated in the [[Fertile Crescent]] around 9600 BC.

Wheat is grown on a larger area than any other food crop (219.5 million hectares in 2024), and world trade in it exceeds that of all other crops combined. World production in 2024 was 799 million tonnes, second among cereals after [[Maize|maize]]. Since 1960 production of wheat and other grain crops has tripled.

The plant is a stout, medium to tall grass with a jointed, usually hollow stem. Each flower head holds some 20 to 100 flowers, over 99% of them self-pollinated, and each fertilized carpel develops into a grain. Roots are among the deepest of arable crops, reaching 2 meters.

Hunter-gatherers in West Asia harvested wild wheats for thousands of years before domestication, perhaps as early as 21,000 BC. Repeated harvesting and sowing favored mutant forms with larger grains and a toughened rachis that keeps seeds attached to the ear; as a result, highly domesticated strains cannot survive in the wild.

Wheat is an important source of [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]] and the leading source of vegetable protein in human food, with a protein content of about 13%, relatively high among major cereals but low in protein quality. Gluten, most of its protein, triggers coeliac disease, noncoeliac gluten sensitivity, gluten ataxia, and dermatitis herpetiformis in a small part of the population.

=== KEY: Wheel_a5df60
HEADWORD: Wheel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axle | Chariot | Friction | Potter's wheel | Simple machine | Uruk
ENTRY:
Rotating component, typically circular, intended to turn on an axle bearing. The wheel is a key part of the wheel and axle, one of the six [[Simple machine|simple machines]]. With [[Axle|axles]], wheels allow heavy objects to be moved easily, support loads, and perform work in machines. A wheel reduces [[Friction|friction]] by rolling. Other forms include the ship's wheel, steering wheel, [[Potter's wheel|potter's wheel]], and flywheel.

The English word comes from Old English *hwēol*, from a Proto-Indo-European root meaning "to revolve, move around." The wheel probably evolved from rollers, round logs used to move heavy objects. The solid wooden wheel dates to the late Neolithic and appeared alongside the early Bronze Age, after several wheel-less millennia that followed the invention of agriculture and pottery.

The oldest indirect evidence of wheeled movement is miniature clay wheels found north of the Black Sea from before 4000 BC. Pictographs of wheeled wagons at the Eanna district of [[Uruk]] date to c. 3500–3350 BC. A potter's wheel from western Ukraine dates to the middle of the 5th millennium BC, earlier than the earliest known use in Mesopotamia. The Ljubljana Marshes Wheel in Slovenia dates to 3340–3030 BC. One recent analysis instead points to copper miners in the Carpathian Mountains, around 3900 BC, as the origin.

The spoked wheel allowed lighter and swifter vehicles. Its earliest known wooden examples are from the Sintashta culture, c. 2000 BC, and horse cultures of the Caucasus soon used spoked-wheel war [[Chariot|chariots]]. Celtic chariots introduced an iron rim in the 1st millennium BC. In China, wheel tracks from around 2200 BC have been found at Pingliangtai, and wheeled vehicles were introduced there from the west.

=== KEY: Wheel_and_axle_00cce5
HEADWORD: Wheel and axle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axle | Bearing (mechanical) | Hero of Alexandria | Lever | Mesopotamia | Potter's wheel | Ur | Wheel
ENTRY:
Simple machine: a [[Wheel|wheel]] fixed coaxially to a smaller [[Axle|axle]]. The two parts rotate together and transfer force from one to the other, so the device can be viewed as a version of the [[Lever|lever]], with the drive force applied to the rim and the load on the axle, which is supported in a [[Bearing (mechanical)|bearing]] as fulcrum. Freely spinning [[Potter's wheel|potter's wheels]] were developed in [[Mesopotamia]] by 4200–4000 BC, and the oldest surviving example, from [[Ur]], dates to about 3100 BC. Wheeled vehicles appeared in the late 4th millennium BC. A wooden wheel and axle from the Ljubljana Marshes in Slovenia is between 5,100 and 5,350 years old, by radiocarbon dating. [[Hero of Alexandria]] identified the wheel and axle as one of the simple machines used to lift weights, and Renaissance scientists later counted it among six simple machines.

=== KEY: Wheelbarrow_23d916
HEADWORD: Wheelbarrow
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Construction | Gardening | Han dynasty | Lever | Middle Ages | Three Kingdoms | Wheel | Zhuge Liang
ENTRY:
Small hand-propelled vehicle, usually with a single [[Wheel|wheel]], guided by two handles at the rear. It spreads a load between the wheel and the operator, making it a second-class [[Lever|lever]] that carries heavier loads than a person could alone. Typical capacity is about 100 liters (3.53 cubic feet), and it is common in [[Construction|construction]] and [[Gardening|gardening]]. Two-wheeled types are more stable on level ground, while one-wheel types maneuver better in small spaces and on planks. The earliest archaeological evidence is a one-wheel cart in [[Han dynasty|Han dynasty]] tomb murals and reliefs, including a Chengdu, Sichuan mural dated to 118 AD of a man pushing one. The [[Three Kingdoms]] text Sanguozhi credits [[Zhuge Liang]] with a wooden ox used to transport military supplies in 231 AD. Chinese wheelbarrows with large central wheels were common, and the wheelbarrow did not reach Europe until the [[Middle Ages]].

=== KEY: Wheelchair_8826d7
HEADWORD: Wheelchair
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Disability | Electric motor | Injury
ENTRY:
Mobilized chair on two or more wheels, used when walking is difficult or impossible because of [[Injury|injury]], illness, [[Disability|disability]] or age-related conditions. Wheelchairs provide mobility, postural support and greater independence, and they come in many forms, including sports and beach models. The main distinction is between motorized wheelchairs, powered by batteries and [[Electric motor|electric motors]], and manual wheelchairs, which are propelled by the occupant or pushed by another person. In 1655 Stephan Farffler, a 22-year-old paraplegic watchmaker, built the world's first self-propelling chair, on a three-wheel chassis. The invalid carriage, or Bath chair, brought wheeled chairs into more common use from around 1760. In 1933 Harry C. Jennings Sr. and Herbert Everest, both mechanical engineers, introduced the first lightweight, steel, folding wheelchair; their "X-brace" design is still in common use.

=== KEY: Whirlpool_Galaxy_e3fe62
HEADWORD: Whirlpool Galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Active galactic nucleus | Binoculars | Cepheid variable | Constellation | Edwin Hubble | Light-year | Spiral galaxy
ENTRY:
Interacting grand-design [[Spiral galaxy|spiral galaxy]] in the [[Constellation|constellation]] Canes Venatici, 31 million [[Light-year|light-years]] away. Also called Messier 51a or NGC 5194, it is 76,900 light-years in diameter and has a Seyfert 2 [[Active galactic nucleus|active galactic nucleus]]. It interacts with its companion, NGC 5195, and the pair is among the most famous and relatively close interacting systems, a favorite subject of galaxy interaction models. Charles Messier discovered it on October 13, 1773. William Parsons, 3rd Earl of Rosse, found its spiral structure with a 72-inch telescope, the first spiral "nebula" known to have one. [[Edwin Hubble|Edwin Hubble's]] observations of [[Cepheid variable|Cepheid variables]] in some spiral nebulae showed that they were separate galaxies. Amateur astronomers can see the pair with [[Binoculars|binoculars]].

=== KEY: Whirlpool_fc6a7c
HEADWORD: Whirlpool
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Niagara Falls | Rapids | Strait | Tide
ENTRY:
Body of rotating water produced by opposing currents, or by a current running into an obstacle. Miniature whirlpools form in a draining bath or sink. More powerful ones in seas may be called maelstroms, and a whirlpool with a downdraft is a vortex. In narrow ocean [[Strait|straits]] with fast-flowing water, whirlpools are often caused by [[Tide|tides]]. Small whirlpools appear at river [[Rapids|rapids]] and downstream of weirs and dams, while large cataracts such as [[Niagara Falls]] produce strong ones. Many stories describe ships sucked into maelstroms, although only smaller craft are actually in danger.

=== KEY: Whiskers_3091af
HEADWORD: Whiskers
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Crustacean | Hair | Insect | Marsupial | Monotreme | Primate | Triassic
ENTRY:
Specialized sensory [[Hair|hairs]] that help most mammals sense their environment, also called *vibrissae*. Whiskers detect air currents and objects, aiding navigation, hunting and spatial awareness, and can trigger reflexes that protect the eyes. They also convey an animal's emotional state to other members of its species. Whiskers grow in clusters above the eyes, on the chin, on the forelegs, near the ears and above the upper lip. Most mammals have them, including all non-human [[Primate|primates]] and [[Marsupial|marsupials]], but [[Monotreme|monotremes]] lack them. Whiskers are stiffer, thicker and longer than body fur, with richly innervated follicles. Rapid whisking, a cyclic motion of the tips, can reach 25 Hz in mice. Similar structures occur in [[Insect|insects]], [[Arthropod|arthropods]], [[Crustacean|crustaceans]], fish and birds. It has been suggested that they evolved during the [[Triassic]] period.

=== KEY: White_25a817
HEADWORD: White
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Lightest [[Color|color]], achromatic, reflecting all visible wavelengths of light. It is the color of [[Snow|snow]], chalk and milk, and the opposite of [[Black|black]]. White pigments, especially [[Titanium dioxide|titanium dioxide]], produce it, while screens create it by mixing red, blue and green light. Priestesses in [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] and [[Ancient Rome|ancient Rome]] wore white as a symbol of purity, and Romans wore white togas as symbols of citizenship. From the 18th century white became the most common color of new churches and government buildings, especially in the United States. Surveys in Europe and the United States associate white most often with perfection, honesty, cleanliness and neutrality. The [[Pope|pope]] has worn white since 1566, and white is the color of mourning in many Asian cultures.

=== KEY: White_Nile_6096f9

ENTRY:
River in North and East Africa, the longer but less voluminous of the two major tributaries of the [[Nile]], the other being the [[Blue Nile]]. Its name comes from the clay sediment carried in its water, which gives it a pale color. Strictly, the White Nile begins at Lake No, where the Bahr al Jabal and Bahr el Ghazal rivers meet; more broadly it includes the course from [[Lake Victoria]] to the Blue Nile. In [[Uganda]] it flows as the Victoria Nile to [[Lake Albert (Africa)|Lake Albert]], then as the Albert Nile to the border with [[South Sudan]], where it continues as the Mountain Nile, or Bahr al Jabal.

=== KEY: White_Sea_Baltic_Canal_422ae1
HEADWORD: White Sea–Baltic Canal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baltic Sea | Gulag | Joseph Stalin | Lake Onega | White Sea
ENTRY:
Man-made ship canal in northwestern Russia, linking the [[White Sea]] with [[Lake Onega]], which connects to the [[Baltic Sea]]. Opened on August 2, 1933, it is 227 km (141 mi) long, but its minimum depth of 3.5 m (11.5 ft) is inadequate for most seagoing vessels. === KEY: White_blood_cell_738e66
HEADWORD: White blood cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood | Disease | Immune system | Infection | Inflammation | Lymphatic system | Red blood cell
ENTRY:
Leukocyte, or immune cell, of the [[Immune system|immune system]], which defends the body against [[Infection|infection]], other [[Disease|diseases]] and foreign entities. Unlike [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]], white blood cells have nuclei and are generally larger. They are produced from hematopoietic stem cells in the bone marrow and circulate through the [[Blood|blood]] and [[Lymphatic system|lymphatic system]]. The main types are granulocytes (neutrophils, eosinophils and basophils), lymphocytes (T cells and B cells) and monocytes. Neutrophils are the most abundant, at 50–70% of leukocytes. In the US the normal count is 4,000 to 11,000 cells per microliter of blood. An excess usually reflects [[Inflammation|inflammation]] or infection, while leukopenia, a count below normal, indicates a weakened immune system.

=== KEY: White_dwarf_824784
HEADWORD: White dwarf
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Heat | Neutron star | Nuclear fusion | Sirius | Solar mass | Sun
ENTRY:
Very dense stellar remnant, with a mass comparable to the [[Sun]] packed into an Earth-sized volume. A white dwarf shines from residual [[Heat|heat]] and slow crystallization, not from [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]]. Stars like the Sun, too light to collapse into a [[Neutron star|neutron star]] or [[Black hole|black hole]], are expected to become white dwarfs. The Chandrasekhar limit, about 1.44 times the mass of the Sun, is the maximum mass for a non-rotating white dwarf; observed masses are largely between 0.5 and 0.7 [[Solar mass|solar masses]]. The nearest known is [[Sirius]] B, 8.6 light years away. The first recognized, 40 Eridani B, was identified as white in 1910, and the name was coined in 1922.

=== KEY: White_hole_cd27ea
HEADWORD: White hole
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Hypothetical region of [[Spacetime|spacetime]] in [[General relativity|general relativity]] that cannot be entered from outside, although matter, energy, light and information can escape from it. It is the opposite of a [[Black hole|black hole]]. White holes appear in the theory of eternal black holes, as a region in the past of a black hole, but no physical process has been observed that could form one. Racial classification most often applied to people of [[Europe|European]] origin, and in a broader sense to anyone with light skin. The modern concept of a unified [[Race (human categorization)|White race]] entered the major European languages in the late 17th century, in the context of race-based [[Slavery|slavery]]. === KEY: Whitefly_288162
HEADWORD: Whitefly
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hemiptera | Leaf | Pesticide
ENTRY:
[[Hemiptera|Hemipteran]] insects of the family Aleyrodidae, which typically feed on the undersides of plant [[Leaf|leaves]]. More than 1550 species have been described. Adults are 1 mm to 2 mm long, and many species are dusted with a floury wax, which gave the family its name. Their small size lets them pass through fine screens that keep out their natural enemies, so unchecked greenhouse populations can rapidly become overwhelming. Whiteflies excrete honeydew, which promotes sooty mold, and vector more than 200 plant viruses. Control relies on biological agents such as the parasitoid *Encarsia formosa*, along with cultural practices and [[Pesticide|pesticides]], though some species resist insecticides.

=== KEY: Wind_52258b
HEADWORD: Wind
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric circulation | Atmospheric pressure | Coriolis force | Monsoon | Solar wind | Wind power
ENTRY:
Natural movement of air or other gases relative to a planet's surface. Winds range from thunderstorm flows lasting tens of minutes, through local breezes lasting a few hours, to global winds resulting from the difference in absorption of solar energy between climate zones. The study of wind is called anemology.

Wind is caused by differences in [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]], primarily due to temperature differences; air moves from higher to lower pressure. On a rotating planet it is also deflected by the [[Coriolis force|Coriolis effect]], except exactly on the equator. The two major drivers of large-scale [[Atmospheric circulation|atmospheric circulation]] are differential heating between the equator and the poles and the planet's rotation. Thermal lows over terrain can drive [[Monsoon|monsoon]] circulations, and sea and land breezes define local winds on coasts.

Wind direction is the direction from which it originates, so a westerly wind blows from the west. Weather vanes and windsocks indicate direction, and speed is measured by anemometers, most commonly with rotating cups or propellers. Short bursts are gusts, and strong winds of around one minute are squalls; long-duration winds are named by average strength, such as breeze, gale, storm, and hurricane.

In outer space, the [[Solar wind|solar wind]] is the movement of gases or charged particles from the Sun, and planetary wind is the outgassing of light elements from an atmosphere. The strongest observed winds in the Solar System are on Neptune and Saturn.

Wind shapes landforms through aeolian processes such as erosion and the formation of loess, spreads wildfires, and disperses seeds. Humans have used it for sailing ships, hot air balloons, and as a source of [[Wind power|power]].

Class of musical instruments in which a column of air inside a resonator, usually a tube, is set into vibration by the player blowing into or over a mouthpiece. === KEY: Wind_power_f858b0
HEADWORD: Wind power
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Electrical grid | Fossil fuel | Solar power | Transformer | Wind turbine
ENTRY:
Use of wind energy to generate useful work, today mostly electricity. Historically used by sails, windmills, and windpumps, it is now generated almost completely by [[Wind turbine|wind turbines]], generally grouped into wind farms and connected to the [[Electrical grid|electrical grid]].

Wind supplied about 2,700 TWh of electricity in 2025, over 8% of world electricity. About 100 GW was added during 2021, mostly in [[China]] and the United States, and global installed capacity exceeded 800 GW. In 2024, 30 countries generated more than a tenth of their electricity from wind, and generation has nearly tripled since 2015.

The power in moving air is proportional to the cube of wind speed, so available power increases eightfold when speed doubles. A turbine's theoretical efficiency is capped at 59.3% by the Betz limit. Almost all large turbines share one design: a horizontal-axis machine with an upwind rotor of 3 blades on a nacelle atop a tall tubular tower. Turbines in a farm are linked by a medium-voltage system, often 34.5 kV, and a substation [[Transformer|transformer]] raises the voltage for transmission.

Supporters describe wind as renewable, with a much smaller environmental impact than burning [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] and among the lowest costs per unit of energy; in many locations new onshore wind farms are cheaper than new coal or gas plants. Because output is variable, a reliable supply needs energy storage or dispatchable generation. Onshore farms have a greater visual impact than most other power stations per energy produced; offshore farms have less but generally cost more. Critics also cite wildlife effects, including bird and bat collisions. Wind output is often higher at night and in winter, when [[Solar power|solar]] output is low.

=== KEY: Wind_turbine_24fae5
HEADWORD: Wind turbine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electrical grid | Kinetic energy | Renewable energy
ENTRY:
Device for converting the [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] of wind into electrical energy. Turbines are built in a wide range of sizes, with horizontal or vertical axes, although horizontal is most common; commercial horizontal-axis turbines usually have three blades, upwind of their towers. Vertical-axis designs include the Darrieus, the Savonius, and floating and airborne types. Blades are most commonly made of glass-fiber composites, with carbon fiber also used. Larger turbines can contribute to a domestic power supply while selling unused power back to the [[Electrical grid|electrical grid]]. Wind turbines produce among the cheapest [[Renewable energy|renewable]] energy and are clean, emitting no greenhouse gases, but their output varies with the wind rather than with demand, so they are unreliable unless energy storage is available. As of 2024, hundreds of thousands of large turbines, in wind farms, were generating over 1,136 gigawatts, with 117 GW added each year.
