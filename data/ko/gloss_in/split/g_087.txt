=== KEY: Meditation_3ed75f
HEADWORD: Meditation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Christianity | Hinduism | Islam | Jainism | Judaism | Taoism | Upanishads
ENTRY:
Practice in which an individual uses a technique or combination of techniques to train attention and awareness and to detach from "discursive, ruminating thought," aiming at a mentally clear, emotionally calm, and stable state. Techniques are broadly classified as focused (concentrative) methods, which direct attention to an object such as the breath or a mantra, and open-monitoring methods, which include mindfulness and awareness of subjective experience in the present moment.

The earliest records of meditation (*dhyana*) are found in the [[Upanishads]], and it plays a salient role in [[Jainism]], [[Buddhism]], [[Hinduism]], and [[Taoism]]. Meditation-like techniques are also known in [[Judaism]], [[Christianity]], and [[Islam]] in the context of remembrance of, and prayer and devotion to, God. The English word comes from Latin *meditatio*, from *meditari*, "to think, contemplate, devise, ponder." In the Catholic tradition, *meditatio* as a formal, stepwise process dates to at least the 12th-century monk Guigo II.

Meditation is hard to define because it covers dissimilar practices in different traditions, and no definition of necessary and sufficient criteria has gained wide acceptance in the scientific community. A study by Bond and colleagues (2009), using a panel of 7 experts, identified three essential criteria: a defined technique, logic relaxation, and a self-induced state.

Asian techniques have spread to other cultures and are applied in non-spiritual contexts such as business and health. Jon Kabat-Zinn developed Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction in the late 1970s. Several meta-analyses of clinical studies of mindfulness have demonstrated mental and physical health benefits. Research, initially criticized for methodological weaknesses, now commonly employs randomized controlled trials and active control groups. Meditation can occasionally exacerbate anxiety or depression in vulnerable individuals.

=== KEY: Mediterranean_Sea_980a86
HEADWORD: Mediterranean Sea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Black Sea | Bosporus | Red Sea | Roman Empire | Strait of Gibraltar | Suez Canal
ENTRY:
Intercontinental sea between Europe, Asia, and Africa, almost completely enclosed by land. In the west it connects to the [[Atlantic Ocean]] through the [[Strait of Gibraltar]], only 14 km wide; in the northeast to the [[Black Sea]] through the [[Bosporus]]; and in the southeast to the [[Red Sea]] via the [[Suez Canal]].

The sea covers about 2,500,000 sq km, 0.7% of the global ocean surface, and includes fifteen marginal seas. Its average depth is 1,500 m, and the deepest recorded point is 5,109 ± 1 m in the Calypso Deep in the Ionian Sea. About 5.9 million years ago it was cut off from the Atlantic and was partly or completely desiccated over some 600,000 years during the Messinian salinity crisis, before the Zanclean flood refilled it about 5.3 million years ago.

Twenty countries border the sea or its marginal seas, from Spain, France, and Italy to Egypt, Libya, and Morocco; Cyprus and Malta are island countries within it. Sicily and Sardinia are its largest islands.

The Levant was among the first regions with permanent human habitation, as early as 12,000 BC, and Egypt, Greece, and the Fertile Crescent saw some of the earliest civilizations. The sea was an important route for trade, colonization, and conquest. The [[Roman Empire]], which called it *Mare Nostrum* ("Our Sea"), is the only state to have controlled all of its coasts. The name *Mare Mediterrāneum*, Latin for "in the middle of land," is first attested in the 6th century. Today the sea faces eutrophication, plastic pollution, and warming waters, and irregular crossings from North Africa to Europe have resulted in over 35,000 documented deaths since 2014.

=== KEY: Megabat_fd01db

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Family of fruit-eating [[Bat|bats]] (Pteropodidae), also called flying foxes. The family contains about 197 species, as of 2018, and is found in tropical and subtropical areas of [[Eurasia]], [[Africa]] and [[Oceania]]. It likely evolved in Australasia. Megabats include the largest bats, with some species weighing up to 1.45 kg and having wingspans of up to 1.7 m, although nearly a third of species weigh less than 50 g. Most species bear one offspring at a time after a pregnancy of four to six months, and a quarter of species are threatened, mainly by habitat destruction and overhunting. As natural reservoirs of several viruses that affect humans, megabats are of interest to [[Public health|public health]] researchers.

=== KEY: Megafauna_43c82b
HEADWORD: Megafauna
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Human | Pleistocene
ENTRY:
Large animals, commonly defined as those weighing about 45 kg or more. Megafauna suppress the growth of woody vegetation, reducing wildfire frequency, and help regulate the abundance of smaller animals. During the [[Pleistocene]], megafauna were diverse across the globe. From about 50,000 years ago, most large mammal species became extinct, including 80% of mammals over 1,000 kg, while small animals were largely unaffected. [[Human|Humans]] and climatic change are the most likely causes, though their relative importance is disputed. One of the earliest uses of the term appeared in [[Alfred Russel Wallace]]'s 1876 work on animal distribution.

=== KEY: Megalith_f909f1
HEADWORD: Megalith
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brittany | Bronze Age | Göbekli Tepe | Neolithic | Stonehenge | Sweden
ENTRY:
Large stone used, alone or with other stones, to construct a prehistoric structure or monument. The word was first used in 1849 by the British antiquarian Algernon Herbert in reference to [[Stonehenge]]; it derives from the Greek *mégas* ("great") and *líthos* ("stone"). More than 35,000 megalithic structures have been identified across Europe, from [[Sweden]] to the Mediterranean. Most were erected between the [[Neolithic]] and the [[Bronze Age]]. The oldest known ceremonial structures, at [[Göbekli Tepe]] in Turkey, date to c. 9000 BC. Common types include the menhir, a single upright stone; the dolmen, a stone table usually covered with earth; and alignments, such as the Carnac Stones in [[Brittany]], France, which consist of thousands of stones. Stone circles are called cromlechs in most languages.

=== KEY: Megathrust_earthquake_cdf9e3
HEADWORD: Megathrust earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Oceanic trench | Thrust fault | Tsunami
ENTRY:
Type of very powerful earthquake occurring at convergent plate boundaries, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another. It results from slip along a [[Thrust fault|thrust fault]] at the contact between the two plates. Megathrust earthquakes are the planet's most powerful, with moment magnitudes that can exceed 9.0; every earthquake of magnitude 9.0 or greater since 1900 has been one. Because the faults often lie beneath [[Oceanic trench|oceanic trenches]], they can abruptly displace the seafloor and generate [[Tsunami|tsunamis]] that are often more destructive than the earthquake itself, including teletsunamis that cross ocean basins.

=== KEY: Megatsunami_7f7600
HEADWORD: Megatsunami
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1883 eruption of Krakatoa | Alaska
ENTRY:
Extremely large wave created by a sudden displacement of material into water, such as a landslide, meteor impact or volcanic eruption. Ordinary tsunamis, caused by seafloor displacement from earthquakes, rarely exceed 30 m in height, whereas megatsunamis can have initial wave heights in the hundreds of meters because the displacement splashes water upward and outward. Examples include the wave from the [[1883 eruption of Krakatoa|1883 eruption of Krakatoa]] and the 1958 Lituya Bay landslide in [[Alaska]], which produced a runup of 524.6 m. The term has no precise definition but is commonly used for waves over 100 m high.

=== KEY: Meiosis_ed87e3
HEADWORD: Meiosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chromosome | Down syndrome | Fertilisation
ENTRY:
Cell division that produces haploid gametes, such as sperm and egg cells, in sexually reproducing organisms. Meiosis consists of two rounds of division that produce four cells, each with one copy of each [[Chromosome|chromosome]]. Before division, DNA is replicated and homologous chromosomes pair and exchange genetic material by recombination, which creates new combinations of genes. Because two haploid gametes fuse at [[Fertilisation|fertilization]], alternating meiosis and fertilization sustain sexual reproduction in animals, plants and fungi. Errors that produce an abnormal number of chromosomes, known as aneuploidy, are the leading known cause of miscarriage and the most frequent genetic cause of developmental disabilities, including [[Down syndrome]].

=== KEY: Melanin_448e72
HEADWORD: Melanin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Melanoma | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Family of biomolecules organized as oligomers or polymers, forming the pigments of many organisms. Melanin is produced by melanocytes in a process called melanogenesis, in which the [[Amino acid]] tyrosine is oxidized and then polymerized. There are five basic types: eumelanin, pheomelanin, neuromelanin, allomelanin and pyomelanin. Eumelanin is more abundant in tissues requiring photoprotection, such as the epidermis, and can dissipate over 99.9% of absorbed [[Ultraviolet|UV]] radiation. In human skin, melanogenesis is initiated by UV exposure, which is associated with increased risk of [[Melanoma]]. Pheomelanin is largely responsible for the red or yellow tint of some skin and hair. Neuromelanin, found in the brain, has been studied for possible treatment of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's.

=== KEY: Melanoma_ea6e07
HEADWORD: Melanoma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biopsy | Chemotherapy | Melanin | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Skin cancer arising from [[Melanin|melanin]]-producing cells called melanocytes. It typically occurs in the skin but may rarely occur in the mouth, intestines or eye. The primary cause is [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light exposure in people with low levels of skin pigment; risk is higher for those with many moles, a family history, or poor immune function. About 30% of melanomas develop from moles. Diagnosis is by [[Biopsy]]. Treatment is typically surgical removal, with lymph node testing for larger cancers. Immunotherapy, radiation or [[Chemotherapy]] may improve survival once the disease has spread. Five-year survival rates in the United States are 100% for localized disease, 76% when the disease has spread to lymph nodes, and 35% for distant spread. Melanoma is the most dangerous type of skin cancer; worldwide cases are expected to rise from 331,722 in 2022 to 510,000 in 2040, although mortality is decreasing.

=== KEY: Melody_ce2f3a

ENTRY:
Linear succession of musical tones that the listener perceives as a single entity. In its most literal sense a melody combines pitch and [[Rhythm]], while more figuratively it can include tonal color. It is the foreground to a background accompaniment, and is often built from motifs repeated in various forms. Melodies are described by their melodic motion, pitch range, tension and release, and cadence. In [[Classical music]], composers often introduce an initial melody or theme and then create variations, and several melodic layers may form polyphony, as in a [[Fugue]]. [[Jazz]] musicians use the "lead" or "head" as the starting point for improvisation. [[Rock music]] and folk styles tend to pick one or two melodies and stick with them. [[Indian classical music]] relies more on melody and rhythm than on [[Harmony]], while Balinese [[Gamelan]] often plays complicated variations of a single melody simultaneously.

=== KEY: Melting_bf4376

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Helium-3 | Internal energy | Liquid | Molecule | Solid | Viscosity
ENTRY:
Phase transition of a [[Solid]] to a [[Liquid]]. It occurs when the [[Internal energy]] of the solid increases, typically through heat or pressure, raising its temperature to the melting point. At that point the ordering of ions or [[Molecule|molecules]] breaks down. Melting is a first-order phase transition: at the melting point the change in Gibbs free energy is zero, while enthalpy and entropy change. Substances in the molten state generally have reduced [[Viscosity]] as temperature rises; elemental sulfur is an exception, because its viscosity increases between 130 °C and 190 °C due to polymerization. Water on a very clean glass surface can supercool several degrees below its freezing point. Low-temperature [[Helium-3]] has a negative enthalpy of fusion below 0.3 K, so heat must be removed to melt it.

=== KEY: Memory_4789f2
HEADWORD: Memory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alzheimer's disease | Amnesia | Brain | Hippocampus
ENTRY:
Faculty of the mind by which data or information is encoded, stored, and retrieved when needed. It is the retention of information over time for the purpose of influencing future action. Without memory of past events, language, relationships, and personal identity could not develop. Memory loss is usually described as forgetfulness or as a disorder such as [[Amnesia|amnesia]].

Memory is often understood as an information processing system made up of a sensory processor, short-term (or working) memory, and long-term memory. Working memory serves as an encoding and retrieval processor, and long-term memory stores information through various categorical models or systems.

Declarative, or explicit, memory is the conscious storage and recollection of data and includes semantic memory, encoded with specific meaning, and episodic memory, encoded along a spatial and temporal plane. Non-declarative, or implicit, memory is the unconscious storage and recollection of information, as in procedural memory, the slow and gradual learning of skills, and priming.

Sensory memory holds information from the senses for less than one second and cannot be prolonged by rehearsal. In experiments published in 1963, George Sperling showed that its capacity is approximately 12 items but that it degrades within a few hundred milliseconds. Its three types are iconic (visual), echoic (auditory), and haptic (touch). Short-term memory allows recall for several seconds to a minute without rehearsal, with very limited capacity.

Memory is not a perfect processor. Pain impairs it, as do reduced attention to new stimuli and physical damage to areas of the [[Brain|brain]] associated with storage, such as the [[Hippocampus|hippocampus]]. Retrieval can also be disrupted by decay within long-term memory, and disruptions in memory networks, as in [[Alzheimer's disease]], show their vulnerability.

=== KEY: Mental_disorder_494326
HEADWORD: Mental disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bipolar disorder | Dementia | Mental health | Psychiatric hospital | Psychotherapy | Schizophrenia
ENTRY:
Behavioral or mental pattern that causes significant distress or impairment of personal functioning; also called mental illness or psychiatric disability. It is characterized by a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior, often in a social context. Disturbances may be single episodes, persistent, or relapsing–remitting. A mental disorder is one aspect of [[Mental health|mental health]].

The causes are often unclear. Disorders are usually diagnosed by a mental health professional, such as a clinical psychologist, psychiatrist, psychiatric nurse, or clinical social worker, often relying on observation and questioning, with cultural and religious beliefs and social norms taken into account. In 2013 the American Psychiatric Association redefined a mental disorder in the DSM-5 as "a syndrome characterized by clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning."

Services are usually based in [[Psychiatric hospital|psychiatric hospitals]], outpatient clinics, or the community. Common treatments are [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] or psychiatric medication, along with lifestyle changes, social interventions, and peer support; in a minority of cases there is involuntary detention or treatment.

In 2019 major depression affected about 264 million people worldwide; [[Dementia|dementia]], about 50 million; [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]], about 45 million; and [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]] and other psychoses, about 20 million. Neurodevelopmental disorders, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, and intellectual disability, begin early in development. Stigma and discrimination add to the suffering, prompting social movements to challenge exclusion.

The biomedical framing is disputed. Critics such as Thomas Szasz have argued that many conditions are "problems in living" or value judgments rather than verifiable pathologies, and some analysts warn of overdiagnosis.

=== KEY: Mental_health_7bab96
HEADWORD: Mental health
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eugenics | Mental disorder | Psychiatric hospital | Taxonomy | Well-being | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Emotional, psychological, and social [[Well-being|well-being]], influencing cognition, perception, and behavior. The [[World Health Organization]] (WHO) defines it as a "state of well-being in which the individual realizes their abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and can contribute to their community." From the perspectives of positive psychology or holism, it is not merely the absence of mental illness but a broader state that includes the ability to enjoy life and to achieve psychological resilience. Cultural differences, personal philosophy, and competing professional theories all affect how it is defined.

Impairment is a risk factor for a [[Mental disorder|mental disorder]]; about 970 million people worldwide had one in 2019, anxiety and depression being the most common. Diet, exercise, stress, drug use, and social connections are associated with mental health. Constant online connection can increase stress, and cyberbullying has been linked to depression and anxiety.

In the mid-19th century William Sweetser coined the term mental hygiene. Dorothea Dix exposed sub-standard conditions for the mentally ill and, from 1840 to 1880, won federal support for over 30 state [[Psychiatric hospital|psychiatric hospitals]], which were nevertheless understaffed and accused of violating human rights. Emil Kraepelin developed a [[Taxonomy|taxonomy]] of mental disorders in 1896 that dominated the field for nearly 80 years. Clifford Beers, after publishing *A Mind That Found Itself* (1908), opened the first outpatient mental health clinic in the U.S. The mental hygiene movement was at times associated with advocating [[Eugenics|eugenics]] and sterilization. After World War II the term "mental health" gradually replaced it, shifting emphasis toward prevention.

Access to care remains uneven: the WHO African Region averages 2.2 mental health workers per 100,000 population against a global median of 13.5. Critics argue that diagnostic expansion has led to overmedicalization.

=== KEY: Mercury_planet_315bca

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Solar System | Sun | Terrestrial planet | Venus
ENTRY:
Innermost and smallest planet of the [[Solar System]], first from the [[Sun]]. It is a rocky [[Terrestrial planet|terrestrial planet]] with a trace atmosphere and a surface gravity slightly lower than that of Mars. The largest crater, Caloris Planitia, is 1,550 km across, about one-third the planet's diameter of 4,880 km. Because it orbits closest to the Sun, it always appears near the Sun in Earth's sky, as a "morning star" or an "evening star."

Mercury's sidereal year (88.0 Earth days) and sidereal day (58.65 Earth days) are in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, so one solar day, from sunrise to sunrise, lasts around 176 Earth days. Equatorial temperatures range from −170 °C at night to 420 °C in sunlight, making it the second hottest planet after [[Venus]], since it lacks an atmosphere to trap heat. The planet is about 70% metallic and 30% silicate material. Its density, 5.427 g/cm³, is the second highest in the Solar System, implying a large iron-rich core. The core radius is estimated at 2,020 ± 30 km and occupies about 57% of the volume, against 17% for Earth. Mercury formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago and is expected to be destroyed when the Sun becomes a red giant in approximately seven or eight billion years.

The ancient Greeks had realized by about 350 BC that the morning and evening "stars" were the same body. Mariner 10 made the first successful flyby in 1974, and the planet has since been explored by the MESSENGER and BepiColombo orbiters.

=== KEY: Mesoamerica_79a4a1
HEADWORD: Mesoamerica
PERSON: no

Historical region and cultural area of central and southern [[Mexico]] and northern Central America, home to indigenous civilizations before the Spanish conquest. It comprises central and southern Mexico, all of Belize, Guatemala, El Salvador, western Honduras, and the Greater Nicoya region of Nicaragua and Costa Rica. Societies flourished there for more than 3,000 years before [[Spanish colonization of the Americas]] began on Hispaniola in 1493. It is one of six regions where ancient civilizations arose independently (see [[Cradle of civilization]]) and one of five where writing developed independently.

Beginning as early as 7000 BCE, the domestication of cacao, [[Maize|maize]], beans, tomato, avocado, vanilla, squash, and chili, along with the turkey and dog, led hunter-gatherers to settle in agricultural villages. Shared traits included a complex mythological and religious tradition, a vigesimal numeric system, a complex calendar, ball playing, and a distinct architectural style. Ceremonial centers were linked by trade in obsidian, jade, cacao, cinnabar, hematite, and ceramics. The wheel and basic metallurgy were known but never became technologically relevant.

The Olmec were among the earliest complex civilizations. [[Teotihuacan]] built a military and commercial empire and collapsed around 600 CE. The [[Aztecs]] later built a tributary empire covering most of central Mesoamerica.

The Spanish conquest in the 16th century ended the distinct tradition; [[Smallpox|smallpox]] and measles caused the deaths of upwards of 90% of the indigenous people.

=== KEY: Metabolism_671281
HEADWORD: Metabolism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Cancer | Cellular respiration | Enzyme | Glucose | Glycolysis | Nucleic acid
ENTRY:
Set of life-sustaining chemical reactions occurring within living organisms. Its three main functions are converting the energy in food into a form usable for cellular processes, converting food into building blocks of macromolecules such as proteins, lipids, [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]], and some carbohydrates, and excreting metabolic wastes. These enzyme-catalyzed reactions allow organisms to grow, reproduce, maintain their structures, and respond to their environments.

Reactions are catabolic, the breaking down of compounds (for example, of [[Glucose|glucose]] to pyruvate by [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]]), or anabolic, the building up of compounds by biosynthesis. Usually catabolism releases energy and anabolism consumes it. Catabolism supplies [[Adenosine triphosphate|adenosine triphosphate]] (ATP), the primary energy currency, produced through pathways such as [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]] and, under aerobic conditions, mainly in the mitochondria.

Reactions are organized into metabolic pathways, in which one chemical is transformed through a series of steps, each facilitated by a specific [[Enzyme|enzyme]]. Enzymes allow organisms to drive reactions that require energy by coupling them to spontaneous reactions that release energy, and they allow regulation of reaction rates.

An organism's metabolic system determines which substances it finds nutritious and which poisonous: some prokaryotes use hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) as a nutrient, yet the gas is poisonous to some animals. The basal metabolic rate measures the energy consumed by all these reactions. Basic pathways are similar across vastly different species; the carboxylic acids of the citric acid cycle occur in all known organisms, from *Escherichia coli* to elephants, probably reflecting their early appearance in evolution. Normal metabolism is disrupted in diseases such as type II diabetes and [[Cancer|cancer]], whose cells metabolize differently from normal cells, a difference that can help identify therapeutic targets.

=== KEY: Metal_eaa57a
HEADWORD: Metal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Bronze | Chemical element | Copper | Hooke's law | Materials science | Metallurgy | Pauli exclusion principle
ENTRY:
Material that, when polished or fractured, shows a lustrous appearance and conducts electricity and heat relatively well. The English word derives from Ancient Greek *métallon*, "mine, quarry, metal." These properties are associated with having electrons available at the Fermi level, as opposed to nonmetallic materials, which do not. Metals are typically ductile, able to be drawn into a wire, and malleable, able to be shaped by hammering or pressing.

A metal may be a [[Chemical element|chemical element]], such as iron, an [[Alloy|alloy]], such as stainless steel, or a molecular compound, such as polymeric sulfur nitride. The general science of metals is [[Metallurgy|metallurgy]], a subtopic of [[Materials science|materials science]]; their electronic and thermal properties also fall within condensed matter physics and solid-state chemistry. In everyday use, steel alloys are called metals, while polymers, wood, and ceramics are nonmetallic. Many elements and compounds become metallic under high pressure; iodine gradually becomes a metal at a pressure between 40 and 170 thousand times atmospheric.

Most metals are shiny, at least when polished or fractured. Densities vary widely: lithium is the least dense of the elemental metals (0.534 g/cm³) and osmium the most dense (22.59 g/cm³). Magnesium, aluminum, and titanium are light metals of commercial importance, with densities of 1.7, 2.7, and 4.5 g/cm³, compared with 7.9 for iron and 8.9 for copper. Metals typically deform under stress without cleaving, and their reversible elastic deformation is described well by [[Hooke's law]]. The atoms of simple metals are often in one of three crystal structures: body-centered cubic, face-centered cubic, or hexagonal close-packed.

Metals conduct electricity because, when a voltage is applied, some electrons shift to slightly higher-momentum states, which are available at energies near the highest occupied levels; by the [[Pauli exclusion principle]], no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state. Semiconductors such as silicon instead have an energy gap between filled and empty states. The elemental metals have electrical conductivities from 6.9 × 10³ S/cm for manganese to 6.3 × 10⁵ S/cm for silver, and conductivity usually decreases with heating. All metallic alloys, as well as conducting ceramics and polymers, are metals by the same definition, as are liquids such as mercury.

The strength and resilience of some metals have led to frequent use in high-rise building and bridge construction, most vehicles, many home appliances, tools, pipes, and railroad tracks. Precious metals were historically used as coinage, and coinage metals have now extended to at least 23 of the chemical elements. The history of refined metals is thought to begin with the use of [[Copper|copper]] about 11,000 years ago. Gold, silver, iron (as meteoric iron), lead, and brass were likewise in use before the first known appearance of [[Bronze|bronze]] in the fifth millennium BCE. Sodium, the first light metal, was discovered in 1809, and since the end of World War II more sophisticated alloys have been developed.

=== KEY: Metallic_bonding_fb8118

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Type of [[Chemical bond|chemical bond]] in metals, arising from electrostatic attraction between delocalized conduction electrons and positively charged metal ions. Quantum mechanics later gave the picture a formal basis in the free electron model, which treats electrons as a gas moving through the solid.

=== KEY: Metallurgy_4a5cb6
HEADWORD: Metallurgy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Egypt | Materials science | Metalworking | Nanotechnology | Serbia | Smelting
ENTRY:
Domain of [[Materials science|materials science]] and engineering that studies the physical and chemical behavior of metallic elements, their intermetallic compounds, and their mixtures, known as [[Alloy|alloys]]. It covers both the science and the technology of metals, including their production. It is distinct from the craft of [[Metalworking|metalworking]] in providing a scientific foundation for it, much as medical science supports the practice of medicine.

The science divides into chemical metallurgy, concerned chiefly with the reduction and oxidation of metals and their chemical performance (mineral processing, extraction, thermodynamics, electrochemistry, corrosion), and physical metallurgy, which focuses on mechanical and physical properties (crystallography, material characterization, phase transformations, failure mechanisms). Metal production is subdivided into ferrous metallurgy, based on iron, and non-ferrous metallurgy, based on other metals; ferrous production accounts for 95% of world metal production. Emerging areas include [[Nanotechnology|nanotechnology]], superconductors, composites, and biomedical materials.

The earliest metal employed by humans appears to be gold, which occurs native; natural gold dating to the late Paleolithic, 40,000 BCE, has been found in Spanish caves. Early cold metallurgy using native copper is documented in Anatolia and at Tell Maghzaliyah in Iraq, from the 7th to 6th millennia BCE. The earliest evidence of [[Smelting|smelting]] in Eurasia comes from the Balkans and Carpathian Mountains, with objects dated to around 6,200 to 5,000 BCE; a smelted copper axe from Pločnik, in present-day [[Serbia]], dates to 5,500 BCE. The earliest documented use of lead in the Near East dates from the 6th millennium BCE. Copper smelting is documented in southeastern Iran from c. 5,000 BCE and in northern [[Egypt]] from c. 4,000 BCE.

=== KEY: Metalworking_a4f579
HEADWORD: Metalworking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gold | Iraq | Iron | Smelting | Trade | Welding
ENTRY:
Process of shaping and reshaping metals to create useful objects, parts, and large structures. Modern metalworking falls into three broad areas: forming, cutting, and joining. Its roots predate recorded history, when people worked soft native metals such as [[Gold|gold]] with simple hand tools. The oldest archaeological evidence of copper working is a pendant from [[Iraq|northern Iraq]] dated to 8,700 BCE. Later came [[Smelting|smelting]] of ores and hot forging of [[Iron|iron]], and modern techniques include machining and [[Welding|welding]]. Metalworking has been an industry, a driver of [[Trade|trade]], a hobby, and an art. Machine shops hold a wide range of machine tools for precise products, while blacksmithing is no longer economically competitive on a large scale in developed countries.

=== KEY: Metamaterial_444a0d
HEADWORD: Metamaterial
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antenna (radio) | Optics | Refractive index | Sports equipment | Wavelength
ENTRY:
Engineered material with properties arising from its designed structure rather than its chemical makeup. Metamaterials are typically fashioned from metals and plastics arranged in repeating patterns smaller than the [[Wavelength|wavelengths]] of the waves they affect. Their shape, geometry, size, and arrangement let them block, absorb, enhance, or bend electromagnetic, acoustic, or seismic waves. Those with a negative [[Refractive index|refractive index]] for particular wavelengths have drawn much research. Potential applications include [[Sports equipment|sports equipment]], optical filters, medical devices, sensor systems, and lenses for high-gain [[Antenna (radio)|antennas]]. Researchers have also demonstrated a form of invisibility using gradient-index materials. Metamaterial research is interdisciplinary, drawing on electrical engineering, [[Optics|optics]], solid-state physics, and nanoscience.

=== KEY: Metamorphic_rock_a7b4bd
HEADWORD: Metamorphic rock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's crust | Gneiss | James Hutton | Marble | Schist | Slate
ENTRY:
Rock altered by heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids without fully melting. Metamorphic rock forms from a parent rock called the protolith, which may be igneous, sedimentary, or metamorphic. Temperatures greater than 150 to 200 °C (300 to 400 °F) and elevated pressure of 100 megapascals (1,000 bar) or more cause the rock to recrystallize while it remains mostly solid. Metamorphic rocks make up a large part of the [[Earth's crust]] and form 12% of Earth's land surface. Common examples include [[Gneiss]], [[Slate]], [[Marble]], [[Schist]], and quartzite. Studying them reveals the temperatures and pressures that occur at great depths within the crust. The importance of heat was first noted by [[James Hutton]], who is often described as the father of modern geology.

=== KEY: Metamorphoses_3abe47
HEADWORD: Metamorphoses
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dante Alighieri | Geoffrey Chaucer | Giovanni Boccaccio | Julius Caesar | Ovid | Renaissance | William Shakespeare
ENTRY:
Latin narrative poem by [[Ovid]] in dactylic hexameter. Composed around 8 CE, *Metamorphoses* contains 15 books and over 250 myths, tracing the history of the world from its creation to the deification of [[Julius Caesar]]. The poem defies simple genre classification, though it is generally considered to meet the criteria for an epic. Ovid drew on earlier metamorphosis poetry but diverged significantly from his models, and the work is organized around thematic transformation rather than linear chronology. It inspired [[Dante Alighieri|Dante]], [[Giovanni Boccaccio]], [[Geoffrey Chaucer]], and [[William Shakespeare]], and many of its episodes appeared in sculpture, painting, and music, especially during the [[Renaissance]]. The first of many English translations, by William Caxton, appeared in 1480.

=== KEY: Metamorphosis_e231f7
HEADWORD: Metamorphosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Frog | Hormone | Insect | Pupa | Tadpole
ENTRY:
Conspicuous, relatively abrupt change in an animal's body structure during its development. Some [[Insect|insects]], fish, amphibians, mollusks, crustaceans, cnidarians, echinoderms, and tunicates undergo metamorphosis, often with a change in nutrition or behavior. Animals with complete metamorphosis pass through radically different stages, while those with incomplete metamorphosis change between somewhat similar stages. In insects, [[Hormone|hormones]] control the process: ecdysone induces molting, and juvenile hormone prevents adult features during molts. Holometabolous insects enter an inactive [[Pupa|pupal]] stage, called a chrysalis in butterflies, before emerging as adults. Amphibians such as [[Frog|frogs]] transform from [[Tadpole|tadpoles]] into terrestrial adults. The evolution of metamorphosis in insects is thought to have fueled their dramatic radiation.

=== KEY: Metaphysics_96628a
HEADWORD: Metaphysics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Free will | Immanuel Kant | Philosophy
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Philosophy|philosophy]] that examines the basic nature or most fundamental structure of reality. It is traditionally seen as the study of mind-independent features of the world, though some theorists view it as an inquiry into the conceptual framework of human understanding. Some philosophers, including [[Aristotle]], call it "first philosophy," suggesting that it is more fundamental than other forms of philosophical inquiry.

Its topics include existence, the features all entities share and their division into categories of being, possibility and necessity, space, time, and change, causation, the relation of mind and matter, and whether there is [[Free will|free will]]. An influential division is between particulars, unique individual entities such as a specific apple, and universals, general features such as the color red. Metaphysics differs from the individual sciences, which examine more specific features of particular classes of entities.

Starting with [[Immanuel Kant]], an alternative conception focused on conceptual schemes rather than external reality. P. F. Strawson contrasted descriptive metaphysics, which articulates the schemes commonly used to understand the world, with revisionary metaphysics, which aims to produce better ones. Metaphysicians typically rely on rational intuitions and abstract reasoning but also use empirical approaches linked to scientific theories; critics question the reliability of its methods and the meaningfulness of its theories.

The name comes from the Greek *ta meta ta phusika*, "what comes after the physics," possibly because an editor, likely Andronicus of Rhodes, placed Aristotle's treatise after his works on physics. Its roots lie in the Upanishads of ancient India, Taoism in ancient China, and pre-Socratic philosophy in ancient Greece. The modern period produced comprehensive systems, many of them idealist; in the 20th century, traditional metaphysics and idealism in particular faced criticism that prompted new approaches.

=== KEY: Metaphysics_Aristotle_17f113
HEADWORD: Metaphysics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Plato
ENTRY:
Collection of texts by [[Aristotle]] on substance, causation, and first philosophy. Many of Aristotle's works are highly compressed, and the treatises are often thought to be lecture notes. The title, from the Greek for those after the physics, reflects the work's place after the *Physics* in an arrangement by Andronicus of Rhodes. The text covers substance theory, different kinds of causation, form and matter, and the existence of mathematical objects. Book Alpha surveys earlier philosophers' views on first principles and causes, from the Ionian monists up to [[Plato]]. Book Lambda culminates in an eternal, unmoved mover that serves as the final cause of cosmic motion. The order in which the books were written is unknown, since later editors arranged them.

=== KEY: Metastasis_a5e887
HEADWORD: Metastasis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Breast cancer
ENTRY:
Spread of a pathogenic agent from a primary site to a secondary site in the body. The term usually refers to a cancerous tumor, and the new sites are called metastases. Cancer cells can penetrate blood or lymphatic vessels and travel to other tissues, where they form a secondary tumor resembling the original. [[Breast cancer]] that spreads to the lungs forms a tumor called metastatic breast cancer, not lung cancer. In the TNM staging system, metastasis is represented by the letter M and places a cancer in Stage IV. The chance of curative treatment is greatly reduced once a cancer has metastasized.
