=== KEY: Brass_ff6039
HEADWORD: Brass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Bronze | Copper | Corrosion | Musical instrument | Plumbing | Valve | Zinc
ENTRY:
[[Alloy]] of [[Copper|copper]] and [[Zinc|zinc]], in proportions that can be varied to achieve different colors and properties. Brass is similar to [[Bronze|bronze]], which uses tin instead of zinc. Its relatively low melting point (900 to 940 °C; 1,650 to 1,720 °F, depending on composition) and high workability make it easy to cast and machine. It is used for locks, hinges, gears, bearings, zippers, [[Plumbing|plumbing]] and [[Valve|valves]], where [[Corrosion|corrosion]] resistance and low friction are needed, and for horns and bells in [[Musical instrument|musical instruments]]. It is unsuitable for marine use because salt water leaches the zinc, but marine brass containing tin avoids this. The density of brass is 8.4 to 8.73 g/cm³, and almost 90% of brass alloys are recycled.

=== KEY: Brass_instrument_ae12e2
HEADWORD: Brass instrument
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Musical instrument | Saxophone | Trombone | Trumpet | Tuba | Woodwind instrument
ENTRY:
Class of [[Musical instrument|musical instruments]] that produce sound by sympathetic vibration of air in a tubular resonator, driven by the player's lips. Slides, valves, crooks or keys change the length of tubing, and the player's embouchure, lip tension and air flow select the harmonic produced. Classification follows how sound is made, not the material: wooden instruments such as the alphorn and didgeridoo are brass instruments, while the [[Saxophone|saxophone]] is a [[Woodwind instrument|woodwind]] made of brass. Valved instruments include the [[Trumpet|trumpet]], horn, euphonium and [[Tuba|tuba]], while the [[Trombone|trombone]] is a slide instrument. Natural instruments, such as the bugle, play only notes of the harmonic series and are used for period performances.

=== KEY: Brazil_42537f
HEADWORD: Brazil
PERSON: no

Largest country in South America (pop. over 214 million), with its capital at [[Brasília]]. Fifth-largest in the world by area (8,515,770 sq km) and seventh by population, Brazil is a federation of 26 states and a Federal District. Its most populous city is [[São Paulo]], followed by [[Rio de Janeiro]]. [[Portuguese language|Portuguese]] is the official language, and Brazil is the only Portuguese-speaking country in the Americas.

Bounded by the Atlantic Ocean on the east, with a coastline of 7,491 km, Brazil covers roughly half of the continent and borders every other South American country except Ecuador and Chile. It contains most of the Amazon basin, with the world's largest river system and most extensive virgin tropical forest, and ranks first among 17 megadiverse countries.

Indigenous peoples inhabited the territory before the Portuguese explorer [[Pedro Álvares Cabral]] landed in 1500. Portugal founded the first city, São Vicente, in 1532 and imported enslaved Africans to work plantations. Prince Pedro of Braganza declared independence in 1822 and established the [[Empire of Brazil]]. Slavery was finally abolished in 1888, and a military coup in 1889 made Brazil a presidential republic. A revolution in 1930 brought [[Getúlio Vargas]] to power. A military dictatorship ruled from 1964 until 1985, and the constitution of 1988 defines Brazil as a democratic federal republic.

Brazil has the largest economy in Latin America and one of the 10 largest in the world. It is a founding member of the United Nations and a member of the G20 and [[BRICS]].

Extensive region covering most of eastern, southern and central [[Brazil]]. It covers some 4,500,000 km² (1,930,511 sq mi), approximately half of the country's land area. === KEY: Bread_1296fc
HEADWORD: Bread
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Neolithic | Pliny the Elder | Rye | Sourdough | Sumer | Wheat | Yeast
ENTRY:
Baked food made from water, flour, and often yeast. A staple food across the world, particularly in Europe and the Middle East, bread is one of the oldest human-made foods. The oldest evidence of bread-making comes from a 14,500-year-old Natufian site in Jordan's northeastern desert, and grains became its mainstay around 10,000 BC with the dawn of the [[Neolithic]] age and the spread of agriculture.

Bread may be leavened by naturally occurring microbes, as in [[Sourdough|sourdough]], by chemicals, by industrially produced [[Yeast|yeast]], or by high-pressure aeration; it may also be unleavened. In yeast fermentation, carbon dioxide and ethanol vapors produce the air pockets of the crumb. An early leavened bread was baked as early as 6000 BC in southern Mesopotamia, home of the [[Sumer|Sumerian]] civilization, which may have passed the knowledge to the Egyptians around 3000 BC. [[Pliny the Elder]] reported that the Gauls and Iberians used the foam skimmed from beer, called barm, for a lighter bread, and that the most common leavening was a piece of dough kept from the previous day. The Chorleywood process, developed in 1961, uses intense mechanical working to shorten fermentation and is widely used in large factories.

Bread is the staple of the Middle East, Central Asia, North Africa, Europe, and European-derived cultures, in contrast to parts of South and East Asia, where rice or noodles are the staple. Most is made from [[Wheat|wheat]], whose gluten gives dough sponginess and elasticity. Other cereals, including [[Rye|rye]], barley, maize, oats, sorghum, millet, and rice, have been used, usually mixed with wheat flour because they contain less gluten. Gluten-free breads use flours such as almond, rice, sorghum, corn, legumes, and cassava.

Edible fruit-bearing tree in the [[Moraceae|mulberry and jackfruit family]], *Artocarpus altilis*. The fruit is a staple in many cultures, and the light, sturdy timber is used for furniture, houses and surfboards. Most selectively bred [[Cultivar|cultivars]] are seedless, and large-scale propagation uses root cuttings. The tree is closely related to the [[Jackfruit|jackfruit]].

First meal of the day, usually eaten in the morning. === KEY: Breathing_bd2879
HEADWORD: Breathing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cellular respiration | Circulatory system | Diffusion | Lung | Oxygen | Thermoregulation | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Rhythmic process of inhaling [[Oxygen|oxygen]]-rich air into the [[Lung|lungs]] and exhaling carbon dioxide, enabling gas exchange. All aerobic organisms require oxygen for [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]], which produces carbon dioxide as a waste product. In [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] with lungs, breathing consists of repeated cycles of inhalation and exhalation through a branched system of airways that lead to the alveoli, where gases move by [[Diffusion|diffusion]]. The [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]] transports the gases between the lungs and the tissues. Under normal conditions, homeostatic mechanisms control the depth and rate of breathing unconsciously, maintaining arterial partial pressures of carbon dioxide and oxygen and thereby keeping extracellular fluid pH stable. Breathing also supports speech and reflexes such as yawning and coughing, and contributes to [[Thermoregulation|thermoregulation]], as in panting. The lungs expand mainly through contraction of the diaphragm and, to a lesser extent, the intercostal muscles, while exhalation at rest is largely passive.

=== KEY: Breccia_08bf17
HEADWORD: Breccia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caldera | Conglomerate (geology) | Gravel | Impact event | Landslide
ENTRY:
Clastic rock of angular, coarse fragments bound by a finer-grained matrix or cement. Unlike [[Conglomerate (geology)|conglomerate]], whose clasts are rounded, breccia contains at least 30 percent [[Gravel|gravel]]-sized particles over 2 mm with sharp edges that have not been worn down, indicating deposition close to the source. Sedimentary breccias form by sedimentary processes, such as scree cemented at the base of a cliff. Fault or tectonic breccias result from the grinding of two fault blocks as they slide past each other. Volcanic breccias form by explosive eruption of lava and other rock. Megabreccias, with fragments from at least 1 meter to more than 400 meters, can form through [[Landslide|landslides]], [[Impact event|impact]] events or [[Caldera|caldera]] collapse.

=== KEY: Breed_79adaa
HEADWORD: Breed
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cultivar | Hybrid (biology) | Inbreeding | Selective breeding | Species
ENTRY:
Group of domestic animals within a [[Species|species]] that share homogeneous appearance, behavior or other characteristics distinguishing them from other members of the species. Breeds form through genetic isolation and either natural adaptation or [[Selective breeding|selective breeding]], or both. No single scientifically accepted definition of the term exists; a breed is a term used among breeders for a nameable subset of a species. Individuals of a breed pass predictable traits to their offspring, a property called breeding true. Plant breeds are more commonly called [[Cultivar|cultivars]]. Offspring of animals from different breeds are crossbreeds or mixed breeds, and crosses above the breed level, such as between species, are [[Hybrid (biology)|hybrids]]. Breeders select foundation stock and mate the most desirable animals, and a breed standard describes desirable and undesirable traits. Populations must be large enough to maintain a breed without forced [[Inbreeding|inbreeding]].

=== KEY: Bremsstrahlung_6076d3
HEADWORD: Bremsstrahlung
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Conservation of energy | Electromagnetic radiation | Electron | Kinetic energy | Photon | Plasma (physics) | X-ray
ENTRY:
[[Electromagnetic radiation|Electromagnetic radiation]] produced when a charged particle, typically an [[Electron|electron]], is deflected and decelerated by the field of another charged particle, usually an [[Atomic nucleus|atomic nucleus]]. The particle loses [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]], which is converted into [[Photon|photons]], in accordance with [[Conservation of energy|conservation of energy]]. The spectrum is continuous, becoming more intense and shifting toward higher frequencies as the energy change of the decelerated particles increases. More broadly, the term covers any radiation produced by acceleration of a charged particle, such as synchrotron radiation. Radiation from [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] is called free-free radiation. In the narrower sense used most often, the term refers to radiation produced when electrons decelerate in matter, as in [[X-ray|X-ray]] tubes.

=== KEY: Bridge_de8504
HEADWORD: Bridge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andes | Concrete | Danube | Foundation (engineering) | Golden Gate Bridge | Industrial Revolution | Steel | Sui dynasty
ENTRY:
Structure designed to span an obstacle, such as a river or railway, and carry vehicles, pedestrians, and other loads across it. Most bridges consist of a flat deck supported by beams, arches, or cables, resting on a [[Foundation (engineering)|foundation]] designed to transfer the bridge's weight to the subsoil without settling.

The earliest bridges were wooden boardwalks or logs across wetlands and creeks. Corbel arch bridges were built c. 13th century BC in Mycenaean Greece, and the Arkadiko Bridge still exists. The Romans and ancient Chinese built major arch bridges of timber, stone, and brick; the Romans also used cement, mixed with small rocks to form [[Concrete|concrete]]. Trajan's Bridge (c. 105 AD) crossed the [[Danube]] and was over 900 m long. The Anji Bridge, built from 595 to 605 AD during the [[Sui dynasty]], is the oldest surviving stone bridge in China. The Inca built rope suspension bridges in the [[Andes]]. In Renaissance Europe, scientific and engineering advances produced wider spans, truss bridges, and stone segmental arches.

The [[Industrial Revolution]] brought mass-produced [[Steel|steel]], which enabled truss and cantilever bridges able to cross wide rivers and deep valleys. The longest spans use suspension or cable-stayed designs, both relying on high-strength steel cables, and the maximum span reached 2 km in 2022. Other forms include viaducts, trestles, and movable bridges such as drawbridges and swing bridges.

A bridge must support its own weight and that of its traffic, and tolerate stresses from winds, floods, and earthquakes; engineers typically use limit state design and the finite element method. Bridges can shorten transport times and raise gross domestic product, but can also increase pollution. Some serve as landmarks, such as the [[Golden Gate Bridge]].

Satellite broadcasting began in the 1960s, and in the 2000s broadcasters switched from analog to digital signals.

=== KEY: Bronze_Age_b9e183
HEADWORD: Bronze Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze | Chalcolithic | Iron Age | Late Bronze Age collapse | Neolithic | Stone Age | Sumer
ENTRY:
Period in the development of material culture among ancient societies in Asia, the Near East, and Europe, marked by the use of [[Bronze|bronze]]. It is the middle period of the three-age system, following the [[Stone Age]] and preceding the [[Iron Age]]; a transition, the [[Chalcolithic]], separates it from the [[Neolithic]]. The Bronze Age had begun in much of the Old World by 3,000 BC. In the Near East it ran c. 3300–1200 BC, in Europe from about 2500 BC to 800 BC, and in East Asia it began around 2000 BC.

Bronze Age cultures were the first to develop writing: cuneiform in Mesopotamia and hieroglyphs in Egypt. The Americas are conventionally studied under a five-period system with no Bronze Age.

Bronze was harder and more durable than other metals then available. Iron is abundant, but smelting it requires 1,250 °C, and it was beyond common use until the end of the 2nd millennium BC. Tin occurs in few places, unlike copper, so bronze often stayed rare and expensive, and some societies used it mainly for weapons or elite art. The Maykop culture of the North Caucasus produced the oldest-known bronze, an arsenical bronze, by the mid-4th millennium BC.

The Near East entered the Bronze Age first, with the rise of [[Sumer]] in the mid-4th millennium BC. Its cultures developed city-states, written law codes, and the potter's wheel. The Hittites founded an empire in the 18th century BC at Hattusa, reaching its height in the 14th century BC. Widespread disruption in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC, the [[Late Bronze Age collapse]], ended the age there.

=== KEY: Bronze_a0df72
HEADWORD: Bronze
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Arsenic | Bronze Age | Copper | Cornwall | Iron Age | Tin
ENTRY:
[[Alloy]] consisting primarily of [[Copper|copper]], commonly with about 12–12.5% [[Tin|tin]]. Added metals such as aluminum, manganese, nickel, or zinc, and sometimes phosphorus, arsenic, or silicon, give alloys harder than copper alone or with other useful properties.

The period in which bronze was the hardest metal in widespread use is the [[Bronze Age]]. It began in western Eurasia in the mid-4th millennium BC and in China in the early 2nd millennium BC, and was followed by the [[Iron Age]], which started about 1300 BC. The earliest known tin-copper artifact, dated to c. 4650 BC, comes from the Vinča culture site at Pločnik, Serbia. Copper–[[Arsenic|arsenic]] alloys, stronger and with a lower melting point than pure copper, were made earlier; the earliest known artifacts, from Tal-i-Iblis on the Iranian plateau, date to 3800–3400 BC. Tin became the major non-copper ingredient in the late 3rd millennium BC; tin bronze was easier to control and cast and, unlike arsenic, not toxic.

Copper and tin ores are seldom found together, with exceptions such as [[Cornwall]], so bronze work has always involved long-distance trade. Cornish tin was traded as far as Phoenicia. An analysis of 324 metallic finds of the Indus Valley Civilisation (2600–1900 BC) found 67 (20%) with some tin content.

Bronze has a Vickers hardness of 60–258, against 30–80 for wrought iron, yet the Bronze Age gave way to iron after migrations around 1200–1100 BC disrupted tin supplies and raised prices. Bronze remains in use for sculpture, bells, bearings, and marine hardware.
