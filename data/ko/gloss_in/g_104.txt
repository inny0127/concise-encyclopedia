=== KEY: Persepolis_141a17
HEADWORD: Persepolis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Alexander the Great | Cuneiform | Iran | Nowruz | UNESCO | Zagros Mountains
ENTRY:
Ceremonial capital of the [[Achaemenid Empire|Achaemenid Empire]], in the plains of Marvdasht, Fars province, [[Iran|Iran]]. Encircled by the southern [[Zagros Mountains|Zagros Mountains]], it is a [[UNESCO|UNESCO]] World Heritage Site. Its earliest remains date to 515 BC. The complex, raised on a walled platform, included five halls and grand entrances, and was occupied seasonally rather than as a city. Its exact function remains unclear. Most archaeologists long held that it was used for celebrating [[Nowruz|Nowruz]], the Persian New Year, and Iranian nobility and tributary peoples came to present gifts to the king, as shown in the stairway reliefs. In 330 BC the army of [[Alexander the Great|Alexander the Great]] took the complex, and its wooden parts were destroyed by fire. Exploration from the early 17th century led to the rediscovery of [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing.

=== KEY: Persian_Constitutional_Revolution_5d6a23
HEADWORD: Persian Constitutional Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Iran
ENTRY:
Uprising in [[Iran|Qajar Iran]] against absolute monarchy, 1905–1911. Driven by merchants, intellectuals and parts of the Shia clergy, it led to a 1906 constitution and a parliament, the Majlis. Protests in 1905 targeted a foreign customs director enforcing tariffs that repaid a loan financing the shah's European tour. Mohammad Ali Shah abolished the constitution and bombarded the parliament in 1908 with Russian and British support. Constitutionalist forces marched on Tehran, forced his abdication in favor of his young son, and reestablished the constitution in 1909. The revolution ended in December 1911 when the Second Majlis deputies were expelled with the support of 12,000 Russian troops. The constitution, though not adhered to, remained until the Islamic Revolution.

Inland sea of West Asia, between [[Iran|Iran]] and the [[Arabian Peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]], sometimes called the Arabian Gulf. === KEY: Persian_language_ca75ca

Western Iranian language, also known as Farsi, of the Indo-European family. Body of written texts in the Persian language, spanning over two and a half millennia. === KEY: Personal_name_c8c233
HEADWORD: Personal name
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Björk | Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky | Surname | United Nations
ENTRY:
Set of names by which an individual person or animal is known.

Also called a full name or prosoponym (from [[Ancient Greek]] *prósōpon*, "person," and *onoma*, "name"), it is in many cultures synonymous with the birth name or legal name.

In Western culture, nearly everyone has at least one given name (first name, forename, or Christian name) and a [[Surname|surname]] (last name or family name), which generally indicates membership in a family, tribe, or clan. In Arabic culture and in parts of Africa and Asia, a person's name is joined to a chain of names that begins with the father's name and usually ends with a family or tribe name. Some cultures add patronymics or matronymics, as in the middle name of [[Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky]] (whose father's given name was Ilya) or the last name of [[Björk]] Guðmundsdóttir. Some people are known by a single name and are called mononymous.

Surnames came into common use in China beginning in the 3rd century BC, in France in the 13th century, and in Germany in the 16th century; in Japan they did not become prevalent until the 19th century. Spain and most Latin American countries use two surnames. In Russia the middle name is a patronymic. Iceland still does not use surnames for its native inhabitants. The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Rights of the Child declares that a child has the right to a name from birth.

=== KEY: Personal_protective_equipment_9e468a

ENTRY:
Protective [[Clothing|clothing]], [[Helmet|helmets]], goggles and other gear designed to protect the wearer from injury or infection. Hazards include physical, electrical, heat, chemical, biological and airborne particulate matter. PPE is worn for [[Occupational safety and health|occupational]] safety and for [[Sport|sports]] and recreation. === KEY: Personality_4e9837
HEADWORD: Personality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abraham Maslow | Carl Rogers | Gordon Allport | Identity (social science) | Motivation | Personality psychology | Sigmund Freud
ENTRY:
Behavioral, cognitive, and emotional patterns that make up an individual's unique, time-dependent adjustment to life.

Personality is relatively stable but can change over time through experience and developmental processes. There is no consensus on its definition; most theories focus on traits, [[Motivation|motivation]], skills, and [[Identity (social science)|identity]]. [[Personality psychology]] seeks to explain the characteristics that underlie differences in behavior, and personality is related to life outcomes such as work and relationship success, mental health, well-being, and longevity. Approaches to its study can be organized across dispositional, biological, intrapsychic (psychodynamic), cognitive-experiential, social and cultural, and adjustment domains. They reflect the influence of early theorists including [[Sigmund Freud]], Alfred Adler, [[Gordon Allport]], Hans Eysenck, [[Abraham Maslow]], and [[Carl Rogers]]. Psychodynamic theory stresses unconscious conflicts and early childhood experiences, humanistic theory emphasizes personal growth and self-actualization, and trait theory treats stable dispositional characteristics as the core of personality.

Personality is measured with objective tests and projective measures, among them the Big Five Inventory, the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI-2), and the Rorschach Inkblot test. The approach with the most support in the field is the Big Five: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (or emotional stability). These components are generally stable over time, and about half of the variance appears attributable to a person's genetics rather than environment. Personality changes much more quickly during childhood, so personality constructs in children are called temperament, regarded as the precursor to personality.

=== KEY: Personality_disorder_0465da
HEADWORD: Personality disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Borderline personality disorder | Psychotherapy | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Mental disorder characterized by an enduring, pervasive pattern of maladaptive behavior, emotions, cognition and inner experience that deviates from social norms. The pattern develops early, is inflexible and causes significant distress or impairment, especially in interpersonal relationships. The [[World Health Organization|World Health Organization]] ICD-11 classifies personality disorders dimensionally, by severity and traits, while the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-5-TR) uses categories. Categorical models have been criticized for weak evidence, heavy comorbidity and stigma. Among psychiatric patients, prevalence is estimated between 40 and 60%. Treatment is primarily [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapeutic]], including dialectical behavior therapy for [[Borderline personality disorder|borderline personality disorder]]. Some experts contest the validity of diagnoses, arguing that they reflect social and cultural judgments.

=== KEY: Personality_psychology_5cca02
HEADWORD: Personality psychology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Extraversion and introversion | Free will | Gordon Allport | Latin | Psychology
ENTRY:
[[Psychology|Branch of psychology]] that examines personality and how it varies among individuals. Its areas of focus include describing what personality is, documenting how it develops, explaining the mental processes behind it, and providing a framework for understanding individuals. The word personality comes from the [[Latin|Latin]] *persona*, meaning "mask." In 1937 [[Gordon Allport|Gordon Allport]] distinguished nomothetic psychology, which seeks general laws such as the traits of [[Extraversion and introversion|extraversion and introversion]], from idiographic psychology, which studies the unique individual. Major traditions include trait, psychodynamic, humanistic, biological, behaviorist, evolutionary and social learning perspectives. Many researchers take an eclectic approach. Debates include [[Free will|free will]] versus determinism and heredity versus environment.

=== KEY: Perspective_graphical_1e2f74
HEADWORD: Perspective
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Democritus | Euclid | Filippo Brunelleschi | Leon Battista Alberti | Leonardo da Vinci | Masaccio
ENTRY:
Method of representing three-dimensional objects on a flat surface so that they appear to recede in depth. In linear perspective, objects appear smaller as their distance from the observer increases, and receding lines converge toward vanishing points, usually on the horizon line. Systematic attempts date to around the fifth century BC in ancient Greece, where [[Aristotle]]'s *Poetics* described *skenographia*, the use of flat stage panels to give an illusion of depth. The philosophers Anaxagoras and [[Democritus]] developed geometric theories of perspective for it, and [[Euclid]] argued in his *Optics* (c. 300 BC) that perceived size is not a simple proportion of distance. Italian Renaissance artists, including [[Filippo Brunelleschi]], [[Leon Battista Alberti]], [[Masaccio]] and [[Leonardo da Vinci]], studied linear perspective, wrote treatises on it and incorporated it into their work. Chinese artists used oblique projection from the first or second century until the 18th century.

Dutch-American physicist and physical chemist, and Nobel laureate in [[Chemistry]]. === KEY: Petroleum_592220
HEADWORD: Petroleum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baku | Bitumen | Climate change | Fossil fuel | Gasoline | Mesozoic | Sumer
ENTRY:
Naturally occurring yellowish-black liquid mixture consisting primarily of hydrocarbons, found in geological formations.

Also called crude oil or simply oil, the term covers both unprocessed crude and refined petroleum products. Petroleum is a [[Fossil fuel]] formed over millions of years from the anaerobic decay of buried prehistoric organisms, particularly plankton and algae. An estimated 70% of the world's deposits formed during the [[Mesozoic]]. Reserves are recovered mainly by drilling; unconventional reserves such as oil sands and oil shale are recovered by methods such as fracking. Oil is then refined, most easily by distillation, into products including [[Gasoline|gasoline]] (petrol), diesel, kerosene, jet fuel, [[Bitumen|bitumen]], lubricants, and thousands of other petrochemicals. The world consumes about 100 million barrels (16 million cubic meters) each day.

The word comes from Medieval Latin *petroleum* ("rock oil"), used in southern Italian monasteries by the end of the first millennium. Use of petroleum products is ancient: more than 4,300 years ago the [[Sumer|Sumerians]] used bitumen to make boats. By 347 CE oil was produced from bamboo-drilled wells in China, and in the 9th century oil fields were exploited around modern [[Baku]], Azerbaijan.

Petroleum production played a key role in industrialization after the Second Industrial Revolution, and some petroleum-rich countries, known as petrostates, gained significant economic and international influence in the latter half of the 20th century. Petroleum is non-renewable, and its extraction, refining, and burning release large quantities of greenhouse gases, making it a major contributor to anthropogenic [[Climate change|climate change]]. Production is estimated to reach peak oil before 2035, while the transition to renewable energy and increased electrification is expected to reduce dependency on petroleum.

=== KEY: Pharmacokinetics_0273c7
HEADWORD: Pharmacokinetics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cosmetics | Liver | Pesticide | Pharmacology
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Pharmacology|pharmacology]] that studies how the body affects a substance after administration, often abbreviated PK. The substances include pharmaceutical drugs, [[Pesticide|pesticides]], food additives and [[Cosmetics|cosmetics]]. PK traces the fate of a chemical from administration until it is completely eliminated, using mathematical models of the relationship between drug plasma concentration and time. Pharmacokinetics describes what the organism does to a drug, whereas pharmacodynamics describes what the drug does to the body. The processes are summarized as ADME: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion, with liberation sometimes added as a first step. Metabolism, mainly in the [[Liver|liver]], breaks the drug down through enzymes such as cytochrome P450. With regular dosing, steady state is generally reached after 3 to 5 times the drug's half-life.

=== KEY: Phase_diagram_7354b8
HEADWORD: Phase diagram
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Critical point (thermodynamics) | Phase (matter) | Triple point
ENTRY:
Chart used to show the conditions of pressure, temperature and other variables under which thermodynamically distinct [[Phase (matter)|phases]] occur and coexist at equilibrium. Lines on the diagram, called phase boundaries, mark conditions under which phases coexist, and phase transitions occur along them. Where lines intersect, a [[Triple point]] marks conditions at which three phases coexist; for water this is 273.16 K and a partial vapor pressure of 611.657 Pa. The liquid–gas boundary ends at the [[Critical point (thermodynamics)|critical point]], beyond which the liquid and gaseous phases become indistinguishable. Water's critical point is at Tc = 647.096 K (373.946 °C), pc = 22.064 MPa (217.75 atm). The solidus is the temperature below which a substance is stable as a solid, and the liquidus is the temperature above which it is stable as a liquid. Phase diagrams are essential in metallurgy and materials science, where they guide the design of alloys.

=== KEY: Phase_matter_c9644a
HEADWORD: Phase
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Colloid | Emulsion | Plasma (physics) | State of matter
ENTRY:
Region of a material that is chemically uniform, physically distinct and often mechanically separable. In a glass jar holding ice and water, the ice, the water and the humid air above them are each a separate phase, and the glass is a fourth. The term is sometimes used as a synonym for [[State of matter|state of matter]], but several immiscible phases can exist within one state, as with oil and water both in the liquid state. Distinct phases may be gas, liquid, solid, [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] or Bose–Einstein condensate, and useful mesophases lie between solid and liquid. As many as eight immiscible liquid phases have been observed. Phases do not always separate spontaneously: [[Emulsion|emulsions]] and [[Colloid|colloids]] are immiscible combinations that stay mixed.

=== KEY: Phase_transition_1e167e
HEADWORD: Phase transition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Allotropy | Boiling point
ENTRY:
Physical process of transition between one state of a medium and another. The term commonly refers to changes among the basic states of matter: solid, liquid and gas, and in rare cases plasma. During a transition, certain properties of the medium change as external conditions such as temperature or pressure change, often abruptly. A liquid may become a gas on heating to its [[Boiling point|boiling point]], with an abrupt change in volume. The conditions at which the transformation occurs define the transition point. Below the boiling point the liquid is the more stable state, whereas above it the gas is. Changes can also occur within a single state, such as when a solid changes crystal structure; this is called [[Allotropy|allotropy]] in elements and polymorphism in compounds. The best-known magnetic transition is the change between ferromagnetic and paramagnetic phases at the Curie point.

=== KEY: Phenomenology_philosophy_2189f5
HEADWORD: Phenomenology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edmund Husserl | Martin Heidegger
ENTRY:
Philosophical method and movement of the early 20th century, concerned with the structures of conscious experience as they appear. The tradition was inaugurated by [[Edmund Husserl|Edmund Husserl]], whose transcendental phenomenology sought universal logical structures in subjective experience. Phenomenology seeks to describe the universal features of consciousness while avoiding assumptions about the external world. Its central concept is intentionality, the idea that consciousness is always directed toward an object. The term first appeared in direct connection with Husserl's philosophy in a 1907 article in *The Philosophical Review*. [[Martin Heidegger|Martin Heidegger]] argued that truths are contextually situated and depend on historical and cultural context, and other branches include hermeneutic, genetic and embodied phenomenology. The approach has been applied to qualitative research in psychology, the social sciences, the humanities and cognitive science.

=== KEY: Philosophy_e5348e
HEADWORD: Philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Epistemology | Ethics | Immanuel Kant | Logic | Metaphysics | Natural philosophy | Pythagoras
ENTRY:
Systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value.

The word comes from the [[Ancient Greek]] *philosophía*, "love of wisdom," from *phílos* ("loving, friend of") and *sophía* ("wisdom"). Some sources say the term was coined by the pre-Socratic philosopher [[Pythagoras]], but this is not certain. It entered English from Old French and Anglo-Norman around 1175 CE. Philosophy is a rational and critical inquiry that reflects on its own methods and assumptions. Socrates stated that "the unexamined life is not worth living" to highlight its role in understanding one's own existence.

Before the modern age the term was used in a wide sense that included most forms of rational inquiry, with [[Natural philosophy]] encompassing physics, chemistry, and biology. Isaac Newton's 1687 *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* referred to natural philosophy in its title but is today considered a book of physics. Many individual sciences, such as physics and psychology, once formed part of philosophy but are now separate academic disciplines. Toward the end of the modern period the term acquired its narrower meaning, associated mainly with metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics, and distinguished from the empirical sciences and mathematics.

Influential traditions include Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian, and Chinese philosophy. Western philosophy originated in ancient Greece. A central topic in Arabic–Persian philosophy is the relation between reason and revelation. Indian philosophy combines the spiritual problem of how to reach enlightenment with the exploration of the nature of reality and the ways of arriving at knowledge. Chinese philosophy focuses principally on practical issues about right social conduct, government, and self-cultivation.

The major branches are [[Epistemology]], which studies what knowledge is and how to acquire it; [[Ethics]], which investigates moral principles and right conduct; [[Logic]], the study of correct reasoning; and [[Metaphysics]], which examines the most general features of reality, existence, objects, and properties. Other subfields include aesthetics, philosophy of language, mind, mathematics, science, religion, and history, and political philosophy. Within each branch, competing schools promote different principles, theories, or methods. Philosophers use conceptual analysis, reliance on common sense and intuitions, thought experiments, analysis of ordinary language, description of experience, and critical questioning. Philosophy also informs fields such as law, business, and journalism by addressing their ethical questions.

Attempts to define philosophy precisely are controversial and are studied in metaphilosophy. Some definitions focus on method, such as pure reasoning, and others on topic. [[Immanuel Kant]] held that its task is united by four questions: "What can I know?", "What should I do?", "What may I hope?", and "What is the human being?" Naturalistic philosophers such as W. V. O. Quine see it as an empirical yet abstract science, and it has been described as "the midwife of the sciences." Others regard it as conceptual analysis, finding the necessary and sufficient conditions for applying concepts, or as thinking about thinking.

=== KEY: Philosophy_of_language_3426a1

ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy that studies the nature of language and its relation to meaning, reference and the world. Inquiry dates to ancient Greece, where [[Plato]] asked in the dialogue *Cratylus* whether names are conventional or natural. Major subfields include [[Semantics]], which studies truth-conditional meaning, and [[Pragmatics]], which examines how context and speaker intentions shape interpretation.

Paradoxes discovered around the end of the 19th century, including [[Russell's paradox]], made the logical foundations of mathematics questionable. The study of infinite sets by [[Georg Cantor]] led to the idea of several sizes of infinity. [[Gottlob Frege]] and [[Bertrand Russell]] championed logicism, which holds that mathematics derives from logic, while [[David Hilbert]] treated mathematics as a formal system of symbols manipulated by syntactic rules. Later work in [[Mathematical logic]] provided a framework in which theorems follow from axioms by inference rules.

=== KEY: Philosophy_of_mind_2f2beb
HEADWORD: Philosophy of mind
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artificial intelligence | Baruch Spinoza | Behaviorism | Neuroscience | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy concerned with the nature of the mind and its relation to the body and the external world. Its central issue is the mind–body problem, and it also addresses the hard problem of consciousness and the nature of particular mental states. Dualism entered Western philosophy through [[René Descartes]] in the 17th century, who held that the mind is an independent substance. [[Baruch Spinoza]] instead held that mind and body are properties of one underlying substance. Most modern philosophers hold that the mind is not separate from the body, and [[Behaviorism]] is one physicalist position. Questions about how subjective experience and intentionality can be explained in naturalistic terms remain unresolved, and the field has strong ties to [[Neuroscience]] and [[Artificial intelligence]].

=== KEY: Philosophy_of_religion_3eb3bb
HEADWORD: Philosophy of religion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Medieval philosophy | Metaphysics | Miracle | Religious studies | Stoicism | Theology | Upanishads
ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy that critically examines the central themes and concepts of religious traditions, such as God, souls, miracles and salvation. It differs from [[Theology]] by evaluating the logical coherence and truth of religious concepts independently of any particular tradition, and from [[Religious studies]] by not being limited to historical or sociological perspectives. The field overlaps with [[Metaphysics]], epistemology, logic, ethics and aesthetics. Philosophical discussions of religion appear in ancient texts, including the Hindu [[Upanishads]], and [[Stoicism]] included theories of deities, while [[Aristotle]] described an unmoved mover in his metaphysics. [[Medieval philosophy]] was strongly shaped by the Abrahamic religions. The term came into general use in the West only in the 19th century. Classic topics include the existence of God, the problem of evil and [[Miracle|miracles]].

=== KEY: Philosophy_of_science_724040
HEADWORD: Philosophy of science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Francis Bacon | Karl Popper | Positivism
ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy concerned with the foundations, methods and implications of science. Central questions include the difference between science and non-science, whether science can infer truth about unobservable entities, and whether inductive reasoning can justify definite knowledge. [[Francis Bacon]] advocated systematic empirical methods in the 17th century, but the field emerged as a distinct discipline only in the 20th century, after the [[Positivism|logical positivist]] movement. [[Karl Popper]] criticized logical positivism and helped establish modern standards of scientific methodology. Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) argued that scientific progress is relative to a paradigm, the set of questions, concepts and practices that define a discipline in a given period. Paul Feyerabend and others argued against the existence of a single scientific method.
