Subphylum of [[Arthropod|arthropods]], named from the Greek for six legs, that comprises the largest clade of arthropods and includes most extant arthropod species. === KEY: Hibernation_c4bbd5
HEADWORD: Hibernation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bear | Mammal | Rodent | Winter
ENTRY:
Seasonal state of minimal activity and reduced metabolism, entered by some animals to pass through the [[Winter|winter]] months. Hibernation is a form of heterothermy in which body temperature, breathing and heart rate are lowered, so that the animal conserves energy when sufficient food is unavailable. Before entering dormancy, animals must store enough energy to last the entire period: larger species become hyperphagic and build fat deposits, while many small species cache food instead. Some [[Mammal|mammals]] hibernate while pregnant; female [[Bear|bears]] give birth during or shortly after the dormancy. Although the term was traditionally reserved for deep hibernators such as [[Rodent|rodents]], it is now applied according to metabolic suppression rather than an absolute drop in body temperature. The summer equivalent is aestivation.

=== KEY: Higgs_boson_7d7c32

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Massive elementary particle in the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics, produced by quantum excitation of the Higgs field. The boson has zero spin and no electric or color charge, and it decays almost immediately into other particles. The Higgs field takes a nonzero value everywhere, giving rest mass to elementary particles through the Higgs mechanism, which Peter Higgs and five other scientists in three teams proposed in 1964. After a 40-year search, the particle was discovered in 2012 by the ATLAS and CMS experiments at the [[Large Hadron Collider|Large Hadron Collider]] at [[CERN]] near [[Geneva]]. Peter Higgs and François Englert were awarded the [[Nobel Prize]] in Physics in 2013. The particle has often been called the God particle in the media, a name that physicists, including Higgs, have criticized.

=== KEY: High_pressure_area_52f21b
HEADWORD: High-pressure area
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coriolis force | Francis Galton | Weather | Wind
ENTRY:
Atmospheric region where air pressure is higher than in surrounding areas, also called an anticyclone. Highs are middle-scale features that result from interplay between larger patterns of atmospheric circulation. The strongest form when cold air from polar regions spreads into cooler neighboring regions, while more frequent weaker highs result from subsidence, in which cool, drier air descends. [[Wind|Winds]] flow outward from the center and are curved by the [[Coriolis force]], rotating clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern. High pressure typically brings clear skies, and in summer and winter it brings warm and cold weather respectively in temperate latitudes. On English-language [[Weather|weather]] maps, high-pressure centers are marked H. The term anticyclone was coined in 1877 by [[Francis Galton]].

=== KEY: Hilbert_s_problems_e74300
HEADWORD: Hilbert's problems
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hilbert | Henri Poincaré | Paris | Riemann hypothesis
ENTRY:
Set of 23 mathematical problems proposed by German mathematician [[David Hilbert]] in 1900. Hilbert intended to rival [[Henri Poincaré]], the master of French mathematics, and presented ten of the problems at the [[Paris]] conference of the International Congress of Mathematicians on August 8. The problems range from the cardinal number of the continuum to the axioms of physics. All were unsolved when published, and several proved highly influential for 20th-century mathematics. === KEY: Hilbert_space_e3e511
HEADWORD: Hilbert space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hilbert | Functional analysis | John von Neumann | Partial differential equation | Pythagorean theorem | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Type of vector space that generalizes Euclidean space to any finite or infinite dimension. A Hilbert space has an inner product that allows length and angle to be measured, and it is complete, so that enough limits exist for the techniques of calculus. It was studied from the first decade of the 20th century by [[David Hilbert]], Erhard Schmidt and Frigyes Riesz, and it is indispensable in [[Partial differential equation|partial differential equations]], [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], Fourier analysis and ergodic theory. [[John von Neumann]] coined the term for the abstract concept. Examples include spaces of square-integrable functions and of sequences. Exact analogs of the [[Pythagorean theorem]] hold, and perpendicular projection onto a subspace is central to optimization. These methods helped establish [[Functional analysis|functional analysis]] as a fruitful field.

=== KEY: Himalayas_febe1d
HEADWORD: Himalayas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: India | Indian subcontinent | Kashmir | Mount Everest | Nanga Parbat | Nepal | Tibetan Plateau
ENTRY:
Mountain range in Asia separating the plains of the [[Indian subcontinent]] from the [[Tibetan Plateau]]. It contains the highest peaks on Earth, including [[Mount Everest]], at 8,848.86 m, and more than 100 peaks exceeding 7,200 m (23,600 ft). The range runs west-northwest to east-southeast in an arc 2,400 km (1,500 mi) long, varying in width from 350 km (220 mi) in the west to 151 km (94 mi) in the east. [[Nanga Parbat]] is its western anchor and Namcha Barwa its eastern.

The range spans five countries: [[Nepal]], [[India]], China, Bhutan, and Pakistan. Sovereignty over the range in the [[Kashmir]] region is disputed among India, Pakistan, and China. The name comes from the Sanskrit *Himālaya*, "abode of snow." The Himalayas consist of four parallel ranges from south to north: the Sivalik Hills, the Lower Himalayan Range, the Great Himalayas (the highest and central range), and the Tibetan Himalayas.

The mountains rose after the collision of the Indian tectonic plate with the Eurasian plate, which began about 50 million years ago and continues to drive uplift. The 8,000 m peaks include Dhaulagiri, Annapurna, Manaslu, Shishapangma, Cho Oyu, Lhotse, Makalu, and Kangchenjunga, the third-highest mountain in the world. Kathmandu, capital of Nepal, is the largest city in the range.

The Indus, the Ganges, and the Tsangpo–Brahmaputra rise near the Himalayas; their combined drainage basin is home to some 600 million people, and 53 million people live in the Himalayas themselves. The range has shaped the cultures of South Asia and Tibet, and many peaks are sacred in Hinduism and Buddhism. The summits of several, including Gangkhar Puensum and Nanda Devi, are off-limits to climbers.

=== KEY: Hindu_philosophy_a32e8a
HEADWORD: Hindu philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Jainism | Vedanta | Vedas | Yoga
ENTRY:
Philosophical systems that developed alongside the first Hindu religious traditions in ancient and medieval India. The six orthodox (*āstika*) schools accept the [[Vedas]] as authoritative. Samkhya is the earliest school of dualism, and [[Yoga]] combines its metaphysics with meditation and breath techniques. Nyaya emphasizes logic and direct realism, and its offshoot Vaisheshika is concerned with atomism and naturalism. Mimamsa justifies ritual, faith and religious obligation, while [[Vedanta]] contains traditions that mostly embrace nondualism. They are regarded as Indian but almost never as Hindu. Theistic sub-schools, such as Pashupata Shaiva and Vaishnava, blend ideas from several orthodox systems, and each school has an epistemological literature called *pramana*.

=== KEY: Hinduism_fb859c
HEADWORD: Hinduism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dharma | India | Karma | Mahabharata | Nepal | Ramayana | Vedas | Yoga
ENTRY:
Umbrella term for a range of Indian religious and spiritual traditions unified by adherence to [[Dharma]], a cosmic order maintained through rituals and righteous living, as expounded in the [[Vedas]]. With approximately 1.17 billion followers, around 15% of the global population, it is the world's third-largest religion and the largest ethnic religion. Hindus live largely in [[India]], [[Nepal]], Mauritius, and Bali, Indonesia.

Hinduism has no single founder and no central doctrinal authority, and many Hindus do not claim to belong to any denomination. Scholars recognize four major denominations: Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, and Smartism. Six Āstika schools of philosophy accept the authority of the Vedas, among them Vedanta. Texts are classified as Śruti ("heard") and Smṛti ("remembered"); the major scriptures are the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Puranas, the [[Ramayana]], the [[Mahabharata]] (including the Bhagavad Gita), and the Agamas.

Prominent themes include [[Karma|karma]] (action, intent, and consequences), saṃsāra (the cycle of death and rebirth), and four Puruṣārthas, the proper aims of human life: dharma (ethics and duties), artha (prosperity and work), kama (desires), and moksha (liberation). Practices include devotion (bhakti), worship (puja), sacrificial rites (yajna), meditation, and [[Yoga]].

The word *Hindu* is an exonym derived from the Sanskrit *Sindhu*, the name of the Indus River, and was originally a geographical term. The traditional chronology presents Hinduism as existing for thousands of years, whereas scholars regard it as a synthesis of Brahmanical orthopraxy with various Indian cultures that emerged after the Vedic period, between c. 500–200 BC and c. AD 300. It flourished in the medieval period as Buddhism declined in India. Since the 19th century, modern Hinduism has acquired wide appeal in the West, reflected in the popularization of yoga and movements such as ISKCON.

=== KEY: Hindustani_language_537cb9
HEADWORD: Hindustani language

ENTRY:
Indo-Aryan language native to the Hindi-Urdu Belt, spoken across northern, central, western, and eastern India and in Pakistan as a lingua franca. Also called Hindi–Urdu, it is pluricentric, with two standard registers: Hindi, a Sanskritized register written in the Devanagari script, and Urdu, a Persianized and Arabized register written in the Perso-Arabic Nastaliq script. Both are official languages of India, and Urdu is also the national language of Pakistan. Colloquial speech falls on a spectrum between the two, and conversion between them is generally done by transliteration; translation is usually needed only for religious and literary texts.

The core vocabulary derives from Prakrit and Sanskrit, with substantial loanwords from Persian and Arabic. Early forms developed from Apabhraṃśa vernaculars in the 7th–13th centuries, and the language emerged as a contact language around the Ganges-Yamuna Doab (Delhi, Meerut, and Saharanpur), based on the Khariboli dialect. The campaigns of [[Alauddin Khalji]] carried it into the Deccan, where the dialect Deccani developed. It took shape as a Persianized vernacular under the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and the Mughal Empire (1526–1858).

Hindi and Urdu diverged as standardized forms in the 19th century, largely along religious and cultural lines. [[Mahatma Gandhi]] endorsed Hindustani as a unifying language that could transcend communal divisions, and it became an expression of national unity during the [[Indian independence movement]]. It remains prominent in Bollywood films and songs.

According to Ethnologue, Hindi and Urdu together are the third-most-spoken language in the world after English and Mandarin, with 855 million native and second-language speakers, though this includes many who reported their language as Hindi on the Indian census but speak other Hindi languages.

=== KEY: Hippocrates_35abef
HEADWORD: Hippocrates
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Democritus | Humorism | Plato | Sea of Marmara | Surgery
ENTRY:
Greek physician and philosopher of the classical period, born on the island of Kos and traditionally called the "Father of Medicine." He is credited with the use of prognosis and clinical observation, the systematic categorization of diseases, and the later discredited formulation of humoral theory ([[Humorism]]). His studies set out the basic ideas of specialties including [[Surgery|surgery]], urology, neurology, and acute medicine.

Little is known concretely about what Hippocrates himself thought, wrote, and did, because his achievements were conflated with those of later practitioners of Hippocratic medicine and the writers of the Hippocratic Corpus. The 2nd-century physician Soranus of Ephesus, his first biographer, wrote that his father, Heraclides, was a physician and that he learned medicine from his father and grandfather and studied other subjects with [[Democritus]] and Gorgias. Other biographical details are likely untrue. He taught and practiced medicine throughout his life, traveling at least as far as Thessaly, Thrace, and the [[Sea of Marmara]]. [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] mention him in passing.

Hippocrates is credited as the first to hold that diseases have natural causes rather than being punishments from the gods, attributing them to environmental factors, diet, and living habits. The Hippocratic or Koan school emphasized patient care and prognosis, whereas the Knidian school focused on diagnosis. Because Greek taboo forbade dissection of humans, medicine of the period knew almost nothing of anatomy and physiology, and many Hippocratic convictions rested on incorrect anatomy. A central concept was the crisis, the point in a disease at which the patient either succumbed or recovered.

The Hippocratic Corpus, a collection of about 60 treatises, summarized earlier medical knowledge and prescribed acceptable practices for physicians; most of it dates to the late 5th and early 4th centuries BC and reflects multiple authors. Hippocrates is credited with the Hippocratic Oath, still in use today, though its direct authorship is debated.

=== KEY: Historical_linguistics_db94cc
HEADWORD: Historical linguistics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comparative linguistics | Etymology | Indo-European languages
ENTRY:
Branch of linguistics that studies how languages change over time, also called diachronic linguistics. It seeks to explain the nature and causes of linguistic change and to trace the evolution of languages. Its main areas include the reconstruction of ancestral languages, the classification of languages into families ([[Comparative linguistics|comparative linguistics]]), and the study of social and cultural influences on language. Historical linguists also study the origins and meanings of words, or [[Etymology|etymology]].

=== KEY: History_16d2b3
HEADWORD: History
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient history | Archaeology | History of Africa | Marxism | Military history | Positivism | Postmodernism
ENTRY:
Systematic study of the past, focusing primarily on the human past. As an academic discipline, history analyzes and interprets evidence to construct narratives about what happened and to explain why, including the contexts, causal mechanisms, and human motives involved. In a more general sense, the term refers to the past itself, to periods in the past, or to individual texts about the past. A history in this last sense is a representation of the past that involves interpretation and reconstruction, and its narrative can change as historians discover new evidence or reinterpret known sources.

Historical research relies on primary and secondary sources to reconstruct past events and validate interpretations. Source criticism assesses the authenticity, content, and reliability of sources, and historians try to integrate the perspectives of several sources into a coherent narrative. Schools of thought, including [[Positivism|positivism]], the Annales school, [[Marxism]], and [[Postmodernism|postmodernism]], take distinct methodological approaches. Pseudohistory, by contrast, deviates from historiographical standards by relying on disputed evidence, selectively ignoring genuine evidence, or otherwise distorting the record, often for ideological purposes. Holocaust denial and Armenian genocide denial are prominent examples of historical denialism.

The discipline has many branches. Some focus on time periods, such as [[Ancient history|ancient history]]; others on regions, such as the [[History of Africa|history of Africa]]; others on themes, such as political, [[Military history|military]], social, and economic history. Branches defined by method or source include quantitative, comparative, and oral history. Historians have traditionally been concerned with written documents, leaving prehistory to fields such as [[Archaeology|archaeology]], but their scope broadened in the 20th century to include the human past before writing.

Historians debate whether history is a social science, part of the humanities, or a hybrid. Like social scientists, they formulate hypotheses and use objective evidence, but the reliance on interpretation, storytelling, and cultural heritage aligns the field with the humanities. Views on its purpose also differ. Some hold that its aim is the disinterested discovery of truth; others stress the lessons it offers for present decisions, an understanding of the human condition, or the social cohesion that comes from a shared past. For some scholars, including Whig historians and the Marxist E. H. Carr, history is a key to understanding the present and, for Carr, to shaping the future.

History emerged as a field of inquiry in antiquity to replace myth-infused narratives, with influential early traditions in Greece, China, and later the Islamic world. Historical writing became increasingly professional in the 19th century, when rigorous methodology and academic institutions were established. The English word comes from the Ancient Greek *historía*, meaning inquiry and testimony; it entered Middle English in the 14th century via Old French *histoire*, meaning "story, tale."

=== KEY: History_of_Africa_22849c
HEADWORD: Africa, History of
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: African Union | Ancient Egypt | Bantu expansion | Decolonization | Great Zimbabwe | Oral tradition | Sahara | Scramble for Africa
ENTRY:
Account of human development on the African continent from the earliest humans to the present. Africa is considered by most paleoanthropologists to be the oldest inhabited territory on Earth. Archaic humans emerged there between 0.5 and 1.8 million years ago, and modern humans (*Homo sapiens*) appeared in East Africa around 300,000–250,000 years ago and spread across the world. Most African cultures revere the spoken word, and history has predominantly been passed down through [[Oral tradition|oral tradition]].

In the 4th and 3rd millennia BC, [[Ancient Egypt]] and Kerma arose in the northeast, while the Tichitt Tradition flourished in West Africa. Between about 3000 BC and AD 500 the [[Bantu expansion]] spread from what is now Cameroon across much of central, eastern, and southern Africa, displacing or absorbing groups such as the Khoisan and Pygmies. At its peak, Africa is estimated to have had around 10,000 polities. Prominent states included Kush, Carthage, Aksum, Mali, Songhai, Benin, Kongo, and [[Great Zimbabwe]].

From the 7th century AD, Islam spread west with the Arab conquest of North Africa and into the Horn of Africa, later reaching the Swahili coast and crossing the [[Sahara]] into the western Sahel. When the trans-Saharan, Red Sea, Indian Ocean, and Atlantic slave trades began, local slave systems started supplying captives for markets outside Africa.

Between 1870 and 1914, European colonization grew rapidly in the [[Scramble for Africa]]. The major powers partitioned the continent at the 1884 Berlin Conference, and territory under European control rose from one-tenth of the continent to over nine-tenths.

Nationalism gave rise to independence movements, and after World War II a wave of [[Decolonization|decolonization]] followed, culminating in the 1960 Year of Africa and the founding of the Organisation of African Unity in 1963, predecessor of the [[African Union]]. Countries kept their colonial borders, and many continue to face internal conflict, neocolonialism, and climate change.

=== KEY: History_of_Asia_099fc9
HEADWORD: History of Asia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Eurasian Steppe | Genghis Khan | Göbekli Tepe | Indus Valley Civilisation | Islamic Golden Age | Silk Road | Vedic period
ENTRY:
Collective past of the continent's East, South, Southeast, Central, North, and West Asian regions.

Asia is home to two of the world's oldest continuous civilizations, the Chinese and the Indian, and was also the site of the Mesopotamian, [[Indus Valley Civilisation|Indus Valley]], and Yellow River civilizations. These arose in fertile river valleys suited to agriculture, likely developed writing, cities, states, and empires independently. [[Göbekli Tepe]] in Anatolia (c. 9500–8000 BC) has the world's oldest known megaliths, and Lahuradewa in India yielded rice-associated dates of 9000–8000 BC, which an excavation report called the earliest Neolithic site in South Asia. The Indus Valley civilization, a Bronze Age culture of 3300–1300 BC, built planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro. The [[Vedic period]] followed, lasting roughly 1500 to 500 BC, when Sanskrit developed and the Vedas were composed.

Geography shaped events. Nomads of the [[Eurasian Steppe|Eurasian steppe]] could reach most of the continent, but Siberian forests and tundra, and mountains and deserts formed barriers. Nomads who conquered West Asian states were soon forced to adopt local customs. The [[Achaemenid Empire]], founded by Cyrus the Great, ruled from Greece to the Indus River from the 6th to 4th centuries BC.

Asia was the birthplace of the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, and Islam), the Indian religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism), and the Iranian religions, including Zoroastrianism and the now extinct Manichaeism. The spread of Islam ushered in the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. Gunpowder, invented in medieval China, was developed further by the Mughals and Safavids. The [[Silk Road]] spread cultures, languages, religions, and diseases across Asia and Europe, and the Mongol conquests under [[Genghis Khan]] connected much of Eurasia at a very high human cost.

=== KEY: History_of_Earth_5f53f3
HEADWORD: History of Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian explosion | Great Oxidation Event | Hadean | Phanerozoic | Plate tectonics | Theia (hypothetical planet)
ENTRY:
Development of the planet from its formation to the present, reconstructed by nearly all branches of natural science.

Earth formed around 4.54 billion years ago by accretion from the solar nebula, a rotating cloud of dust and gas made of hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang and heavier elements ejected by supernovae. A giant impact with a planet-sized body named [[Theia (hypothetical planet)|Theia]] is thought to have formed the Moon. Earth then cooled, forming a solid crust and allowing liquid water on the surface.

Geologists divide this history into four eons, starting 4,540 Ma (million years ago). The [[Hadean]] eon, the time before a reliable fossil record, ended about 4.03 billion years ago. The Archean and Proterozoic eons saw the beginnings of life. The earliest undisputed evidence dates from at least 3.5 billion years ago, including stromatolites in 3.48 billion-year-old sandstone from Western Australia. Photosynthetic organisms appeared between 3.2 and 2.4 billion years ago and began enriching the atmosphere with oxygen, culminating in the [[Great Oxidation Event]] around 2.4 billion years ago. Complex multicellular life arose about 580 million years ago and diversified in the [[Cambrian explosion]] about 538.8 million years ago, which opened the [[Phanerozoic]] eon.

The Phanerozoic has three eras: the Paleozoic, an era of arthropods, fishes, and the first life on land; the Mesozoic, which spanned the rise and climactic extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs; and the Cenozoic, which saw the rise of mammals. Recognizable humans emerged at most 2 million years ago. An estimated 99 percent of all species that ever lived, over five billion, have gone extinct. [[Plate tectonics]] continues to reshape the continents and oceans.

Record of the continent's peoples and states from the first modern humans to the present.

The ancestors of contemporary Europeans appeared about 37,000 years ago during the Paleolithic. Farmers from Anatolia spread agriculture during the Neolithic (7000–2000 BC), and from about 4000 BC steppe pastoralists spread [[Indo-European languages]] westward. The city-states of ancient Greece mark the start of classical antiquity (c. 800 BC–AD 500). Christianity emerged under Roman rule in the 1st century AD.

The abdication of the last Western Roman Emperor in 476 traditionally begins the [[Middle Ages]] (c. 500–1500). The 8th-century Umayyad conquest of Hispania established Al-Andalus. The High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300) brought the split between the Latin and Eastern Orthodox churches and the Crusades.

The 15th century brought the [[Renaissance]] and revolutions in printing and seafaring, and the Age of Discovery led to colonization, the Atlantic slave trade, and the Columbian exchange. The 16th-century Reformation challenged the Catholic Church. Modern science emerged in the 17th-century [[Scientific Revolution]]. The [[Industrial Revolution]] and liberal and nationalist upheavals shaped the 19th century. World War I (1914–1918) ended with multi-ethnic empires fragmented into nation states. World War II (1939–1945) began with Nazi Germany's invasion of Poland, and the regime perpetrated the Holocaust. Europe was divided during the Cold War (1947–1991) between a U.S.-led Western bloc and the Soviet-led Eastern bloc. The Soviet Union dissolved after the Revolutions of 1989, and the [[European Union]] was established in 1993.

Development of the civilizations of the [[Iranian plateau]], also known as Persia, from prehistory to the present. Settlements date to the 5th millennium BC. The Medes unified the region as a kingdom in the 7th century BC, and [[Cyrus the Great]] founded the [[Achaemenid Empire]] by 550 BC, the largest empire yet seen. After Alexander's conquest came Seleucid rule and then the [[Parthian Empire]], succeeded by the [[Sasanian Empire]] in the 2nd century. The Muslim conquest of Persia in the 7th century brought Islamization, and the [[Safavid dynasty]] reunified the country by 1501 and converted it to Shia Islam. After a 1953 coup aided by the US and UK removed an elected prime minister, Iran was ruled as an autocracy under the Shah until the [[Iranian Revolution]] of 1979, which created an Islamic republic. Foreign relations have been shaped by regional conflicts, beginning with the [[Iran–Iraq War]], and by the contentious nuclear program.

=== KEY: History_of_Iraq_97d01b
HEADWORD: History of Iraq
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 2003 invasion of Iraq | Abbasid Caliphate | Cyrus the Great | Islamic Golden Age | Islamic State | Mesopotamia | Saddam Hussein
ENTRY:
Development of the region of [[Mesopotamia]], between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, from prehistory to the present. Sumer, which emerged between 6000 and 5000 BC, is recognized as the world's earliest civilization, with urban development, written language and monumental architecture. The region produced early literature and advances in astronomy and mathematics before the Neo-Babylonian Empire was conquered by [[Cyrus the Great]] in 539 BC. Under the [[Abbasid Caliphate]], Baghdad, founded in 762, became a center of the [[Islamic Golden Age]] until the Mongol invasion of 1258. Ottoman rule lasted until the end of World War I, after which Mandatory Iraq was established under the British. Iraq gained independence in 1932 as a kingdom, which became a republic in 1958. Later challenges included the rule of [[Saddam Hussein]], the US-led [[2003 invasion of Iraq]], sectarian violence and the rise of the [[Islamic State]].

=== KEY: History_of_Islam_f8c4d2
HEADWORD: History of Islam
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abbasid Caliphate | Black Death | Delhi Sultanate | Islamic Golden Age | Mecca | Medina | Muhammad | Umayyad Caliphate
ENTRY:
Development of the faith that began with the mission of [[Muhammad]] in [[Mecca]] and [[Medina]] in the early 7th century. After Muhammad's death in 632, the first caliphs unified Arabia and launched conquests that extended Islamic rule from Spain to India by the mid-8th century. The [[Umayyad Caliphate]] ruled from Damascus, and the [[Abbasid Caliphate]] from Baghdad, where the House of Wisdom translated classical learning during the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. A dispute over succession divided Sunnis from Shiites, whose leader Husayn was killed at Karbala in 680. The [[Delhi Sultanate]] conquered northern India in the early 13th century, while Mongol invasions and the [[Black Death]] weakened the traditional centers of the Muslim world. In the early modern period the Ottomans, Timurids, Mughals and Safavids emerged as gunpowder empires. Most of the Muslim world fell under European control in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

=== KEY: History_of_Israel_be6e36
HEADWORD: History of Israel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1948 Arab–Israeli War | Assyria | Babylon | Balfour Declaration | Crusades | David Ben-Gurion | Israeli–Palestinian conflict | Zionism
ENTRY:
Development of the Southern Levant as the land of the Jewish people, from prehistory to the present. Kingdoms of Israel and Judah formed in the Iron Age, and the region was then ruled by [[Assyria]], [[Babylon]], Persia and Greece, followed by the Hasmonean dynasty and the Roman Republic. A Christian majority later emerged under Byzantine rule. After the 7th-century Muslim conquest the region came under caliphates, the [[Crusades]] and Mamluk rule, and four centuries of Ottoman administration. In the 19th century, [[Zionism]] drove increased Jewish immigration. After World War I, Britain administered Palestine under a mandate and committed to a Jewish homeland through the [[Balfour Declaration]]. Tensions led to the 1947 UN Partition Plan and civil war. On May 14, 1948, [[David Ben-Gurion]] proclaimed independence, and the [[1948 Arab–Israeli War]] followed. Peace treaties with Egypt (1979) and Jordan (1994) were signed, and the Oslo I Accord of 1993 established the Palestinian Authority, but the [[Israeli–Palestinian conflict]] remains unresolved.

=== KEY: History_of_Italy_74d405
HEADWORD: History of Italy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benito Mussolini | Genoa | Renaissance | Roman Empire | Roman Republic | Venice | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Development of the Italian peninsula and adjacent islands, from the Paleolithic to the present. The [[Roman Republic]] unified the peninsula before the [[Roman Empire]], established in 27 BC, ruled the Mediterranean for centuries. After the fall of the [[Western Roman Empire]], Italy fragmented into city-states and regional polities, and maritime republics such as [[Venice]] and [[Genoa]] prospered. The [[Renaissance]] spread from these states across Europe. In the 19th century, unification produced a nation-state under the House of Savoy, and the Kingdom of Italy built a colonial empire. Italy was victorious in World War I and acquired Trento and Trieste. The fascist dictatorship of [[Benito Mussolini]] took power in 1922. Italy fought as an Axis power until its surrender and occupation by Nazi Germany, then as a co-belligerent of the Allies. A 1946 referendum abolished the monarchy, and the republic co-founded the European Union and NATO.

=== KEY: History_of_Japan_db661a
HEADWORD: History of Japan
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki | Attack on Pearl Harbor | Boshin War | Heian period | Liberal Democratic Party (Japan) | Meiji Restoration | Tokugawa shogunate
ENTRY:
Development of the island nation of Japan, from the Paleolithic to the present. The Jōmon period of hunter-gatherers was followed by the Yayoi period, when migrants from the continent introduced iron and agriculture. Between the 4th and 9th centuries, Japan's kingdoms were gradually unified under the emperor. The [[Heian period]] (794–1185) was a golden age of classical culture. The [[Tokugawa shogunate]] ruled the Edo period (1600–1868), cutting off almost all contact with the outside world. The American Perry Expedition of 1853–54 ended that seclusion, and the [[Boshin War]] of 1868 returned power to the emperor. The [[Meiji Restoration]] transformed Japan into a great power. Japan's military dominated politics in the 1920s and 1930s. After the [[Attack on Pearl Harbor]] in 1941, Japan surrendered in 1945 after the [[Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki]]. The 1947 constitution made Japan a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy, and under the [[Liberal Democratic Party (Japan)|Liberal Democratic Party]] Japan enjoyed high economic growth after 1955.

=== KEY: History_of_Korea_b70058
HEADWORD: History of Korea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Goryeo | Hangul | Joseon | Korean War | Mongol Empire | Sejong the Great | Three Kingdoms of Korea | Yi Sun-sin
ENTRY:
Development of the Korean Peninsula and southern Manchuria, from the Lower Paleolithic to the present. According to a mythic account, the Gojoseon kingdom was founded in 2333 BC, and it fell to the Han dynasty of China in the late 2nd century BC. The [[Three Kingdoms of Korea]] of Goguryeo, Baekje and Silla controlled the peninsula from 57 BC until Silla unified it in 676. The [[Goryeo]] dynasty unified the peninsula, and Mongol invasions in the 13th century brought it under the [[Mongol Empire]]. In 1392 General Yi Seong-gye founded the [[Joseon]] dynasty, under which King [[Sejong the Great]] personally created [[Hangul]]. Joseon repelled Japanese invasions from 1592 to 1637 with Ming help and the naval fleet of [[Yi Sun-sin]]. Japan annexed Korea in 1910. Division along the 38th parallel after 1945 led to the [[Korean War]] (1950–1953), after which the Republic of Korea industrialized rapidly, while the Democratic People's Republic of Korea became a hereditary dictatorship.

=== KEY: History_of_Malaysia_08d947
HEADWORD: History of Malaysia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Borneo | Islam | Mahathir Mohamad | Malacca Sultanate | Malay Peninsula | Malaysia | Singapore | Srivijaya
ENTRY:
Political and cultural history of the [[Malay Peninsula]] and [[Borneo]], now the federation of [[Malaysia]]. Human occupation extends back at least 1.83 million years. Hinduism and Buddhism dominated early regional history under the Sumatra-based [[Srivijaya]] civilization, which peaked from the 7th to the 13th centuries. [[Islam]] took firm root in the 15th century, creating the [[Malacca Sultanate|Sultanate of Malacca]]. The Portuguese captured Malacca in 1511, and the Dutch did so in 1641 with the aid of Johor. Malayan independence came on August 31, 1957. The Federation of Malaysia formed on September 16, 1963, but [[Singapore]] was expelled in August 1965. A racial riot in 1969 led to emergency rule and the New Economic Policy of 1971. Rapid growth and urbanization followed under [[Mahathir Mohamad]] from the 1980s. The 1MDB scandal significantly influenced the 2018 general election, which brought the first change of ruling party since independence.

=== KEY: History_of_Mesopotamia_c55992
HEADWORD: History of Mesopotamia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cradle of civilization | Euphrates | Tigris | Ur | Uruk
ENTRY:
Civilizations of the region between the [[Tigris]] and [[Euphrates]], from Paleolithic occupation to late antiquity. The history is pieced together from archaeological excavations and, after writing appeared in the late 4th millennium BC, from written sources. Mesopotamia is often called a [[Cradle of civilization|cradle of civilization]], having been home to many of the oldest major civilizations, which enter history from the Early Bronze Age. The name, meaning land between the rivers, refers to the territory between the Euphrates and Tigris and is first attested in the 4th century BC. Sumerian city-states such as [[Uruk]] and [[Ur]] emerged in the south by about 3500 BC. In modern usage the term usually covers the period until the Arab Muslim conquests of the 7th century AD. Chronologies for the third and second millennia BC remain debated, with proposed schemes varying by as much as 150 years.

=== KEY: History_of_Mexico_f77a9a
HEADWORD: History of Mexico
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztec Empire | Christianity | Mesoamerica | Mexican Revolution | Mexican drug war | Mexican–American War | New Spain | Tenochtitlan
ENTRY:
Political and cultural development of Mexico, from the first settlers more than 13,000 years ago to the present. Central and southern Mexico, known as [[Mesoamerica]], saw the rise and fall of civilizations such as the Olmecs, Mayas and [[Aztec Empire|Aztecs]]. Mesoamerican peoples developed glyph-based writing systems. After the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire in the 16th century, which included the fall of [[Tenochtitlan]] in 1521, the territory became [[New Spain]] and [[Christianity]] was introduced. Mexico gained independence from Spain in 1821 but faced conflicts between caudillos, the [[Mexican–American War]] and a French invasion. The [[Mexican Revolution]] led to the rise of the Institutional Revolutionary Party (PRI). Nationalization, reforms and development projects produced the Mexican miracle of the 1940s to 1970s. Since joining the North American Free Trade Agreement in 1994, Mexico has also faced the peso crisis and the [[Mexican drug war]].

=== KEY: History_of_Morocco_b51cdc
HEADWORD: History of Morocco
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Almoravid dynasty | Berbers | Byzantine Empire | Carthage | Casablanca | Phoenicia | Roman Empire | Umayyad Caliphate
ENTRY:
Political and cultural development of present-day Morocco, from early hominin activity to the Alawi dynasty. Hominins were active at Thomas Quarry I in [[Casablanca]] about 1.3 million years ago. [[Phoenicia|Phoenician]] traders colonized the coast between the 8th and 6th centuries BCE, and [[Carthage]] later extended its hegemony over the coastal areas. Indigenous [[Berbers|Berber]] monarchs ruled from the 3rd century BCE until 40 CE, when the territory was annexed to the [[Roman Empire]]. Vandals overran it in the mid-5th century CE, and the [[Byzantine Empire]] recovered it in the 6th century. Muslims conquered the region in the early 8th century, but it broke away from the [[Umayyad Caliphate]] after the Berber Revolt of 740. The Idrisid dynasty established the Moroccan state about half a century later, followed by the [[Almoravid dynasty|Almoravid]], Almohad, Marinid and Wattasid dynasties. The Alawi dynasty has ruled Morocco since the 17th century and remains the reigning dynasty.

=== KEY: History_of_Myanmar_bee7f3
HEADWORD: History of Myanmar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aung San Suu Kyi | Konbaung dynasty | Pagan Kingdom | Theravada | Toungoo dynasty
ENTRY:
Political and cultural development of present-day Myanmar, from prehistoric settlement to the present. The Pyu city-states, founded by Tibeto-Burman speakers, adopted [[Theravada]] Buddhism. The Bamar people entered the upper Irrawaddy valley in the early 9th century and founded the [[Pagan Kingdom]] (1044–1297), the first unification of the valley. After the Mongol invasion of 1287, several rival kingdoms competed. The [[Toungoo dynasty]] (1510–1752) reunified the country and briefly ruled the largest empire in Southeast Asian history. The [[Konbaung dynasty]] restored the kingdom in the 18th century, but the Anglo-Burmese wars (1824–85) led to British colonial rule. Independence came on January 4, 1948, and one of the longest-running civil wars followed. The military ruled in various forms from 1962 to 2010, and again from 2021, when it overturned the government of [[Aung San Suu Kyi]]. Myanmar has become one of the least developed nations in the world.

=== KEY: History_of_Nigeria_3d8c9d
HEADWORD: History of Nigeria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic slave trade | Benin | Boko Haram | Lagos | Nok culture | Oyo Empire | Sokoto Caliphate
ENTRY:
Political and social development of Nigeria, from early settlement to the present democratic republic. The [[Nok culture]] began around 1500 BC, followed by ancient civilizations such as the Ife Empire, the [[Benin|Benin Kingdom]] and the [[Oyo Empire]]. Islam reached the region through the Bornu Empire and Hausa kingdoms in the 11th century. After 1804, Usman dan Fodio unified a large territory in a jihad, creating the [[Sokoto Caliphate]]. Coastal towns took part in the [[Atlantic slave trade|transatlantic slave trade]], and British expansion began from [[Lagos]]. Great Britain controlled almost all of present-day Nigeria from 1903, and the territory was united under a single administration in 1914. Nigeria gained independence in 1960, and the Biafran War of 1967 to 1970 became one of the worst humanitarian disasters of the late twentieth century. The country transitioned to a democratic federal republic in 1999. The [[Boko Haram]] insurgency drew international attention around 2014.

=== KEY: History_of_North_Africa_451ce1
HEADWORD: History of North Africa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Arab Spring | Carthage | Phoenicia | Punic Wars | Sahara
ENTRY:
Political and cultural history of the strip of northern Africa between the [[Sahara]] and the Mediterranean Sea. The earliest known humans lived in the region around 260,000 BC. Green Sahara conditions, with lakes and savanna, ended between 5500 and 4000 BP. In the 1st millennium AD, the Sahara became a major trade zone for camel caravans. The Nile Valley gave rise to [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]], one of the world's first civilizations. The [[Phoenicia|Phoenician]]-founded city of [[Carthage]] challenged Rome in the [[Punic Wars]]. Arab Muslim conquests from the 7th century brought Islamization and Arabic dominance, and the Berber-led Almoravids and Almohads later ruled. France invaded Algeria in 1830, beginning systematic colonization, and most states gained independence after World War II. Oil wealth in Libya and Algeria fueled development but also corruption. Popular uprisings, including the [[Arab Spring]] of 2011, followed.
