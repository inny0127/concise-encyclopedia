=== KEY: Suleiman_the_Magnificent_d6bab3
HEADWORD: Suleiman the Magnificent
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Belgrade | Kingdom of Hungary | Knights Hospitaller | Mehmed II | Ottoman Empire | Selim I | Sharia
ENTRY:
Ottoman sultan (r. 1520–66), the longest-reigning of the dynasty. His rule marked a peak in the [[Ottoman Empire]]'s economic, military, and political power and raised its subjects to at least 25 million. He succeeded his father [[Selim I]] on September 30, 1520.

Suleiman opened his reign with campaigns against the Christian powers of Central and Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean. [[Belgrade]] fell in 1521 after he encircled it with 250,000 soldiers and over 100 ships, and Rhodes, base of the [[Knights Hospitaller]], fell in 1522 after a five-month siege. His great-grandfather [[Mehmed II]] had failed to take Belgrade. At Mohács in 1526 he defeated Louis II of Hungary and broke the strength of the [[Kingdom of Hungary]], much of which was later incorporated into the empire. His defeat at the siege of Vienna in 1529 halted further advance into Europe. He fought for years against the Shia Safavid Empire of Iran and annexed Iraq. In North Africa, Ottoman Tripolitania was established, and the fleet dominated the seas from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf.

He personally instituted major judicial changes concerning society, education, taxation, and criminal law. With the chief judicial official Ebussuud Efendi he brought together the two forms of Ottoman law: sultanic (Kanun) and Islamic ([[Sharia]]).

Suleiman was a distinguished poet and goldsmith and a patron of the arts, overseeing what is called the Ottoman "Golden Age" of art, literature, and architecture. In 1534 he broke with tradition by marrying Hürrem, a woman of his harem. After his death in 1566 he was succeeded by his son Selim II. Scholars typically regard the following period as one of crisis and adaptation rather than simple decline.

=== KEY: Sulfate_749805

ENTRY:
Polyatomic anion, SO₄²⁻, found in salts of [[Sulfuric acid]]. Its sulfur atom sits at the center of four equivalent oxygen atoms in a [[Tetrahedral molecular geometry|tetrahedral]] arrangement, as predicted by VSEPR theory. Sulfate is the conjugate base of hydrogensulfate (HSO₄⁻). Organic sulfate esters, such as dimethyl sulfate, are covalent compounds. === KEY: Sumer_0f47b8
HEADWORD: Sumer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Akkadian Empire | Epic of Gilgamesh | Iraq | Mesopotamia | Sumerian language | Uruk
ENTRY:
Earliest known civilization, in southern [[Mesopotamia]] (now south-central [[Iraq]]), emerging during the Chalcolithic and early Bronze Age between the 5th and 4th millennium BC. Sumerian farmers along the Tigris and Euphrates grew abundant grain and other crops, and the surplus enabled urban settlements. The world's earliest known texts come from the Sumerian cities of [[Uruk]] and Jemdet Nasr and date between c. 3350 and c. 2500 BC, following proto-writing from c. 4000 to c. 2500 BC.

The name Sumer comes from the Akkadian term for the non-Semitic-speaking inhabitants. The Sumerians called their land Kengir, "Country of the noble lords," and their language Emegir. The [[Sumerian language]] is a language isolate.

Their origins are disputed. Most historians suggest permanent settlement between c. 5500 and c. 3300 BC by a West Asian people. Other proposals include North African, Caucasian, and Dilmun (Bahrain) origins, or a homeland on the coast of Eastern Arabia before it flooded at the end of the Ice Age; none is established. A prehistoric people termed Ubaidians, never mentioned by the Sumerians, are assumed to have been the first civilizing force, draining marshes for agriculture and developing trade, weaving, leatherwork, metalwork, masonry, and pottery.

Sumerian civilization took form in the Uruk period (4th millennium BC), continuing into the Jemdet Nasr and Early Dynastic periods. The Sumerians progressively lost control to Semitic states from the northwest, and Sumer was conquered by the [[Akkadian Empire]] around 2270 BC.

One later account lists city-states such as Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Lagash, and Nippur, governed by priest-kings or assemblies, and gods such as Enki and Inanna. It credits the Sumerians with cuneiform writing, the wheel, and the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]].

Ancient language of [[Mesopotamia]], the oldest attested language, dating back to at least 3100 BC. [[Akkadian language|Akkadian]], a Semitic language, gradually replaced Sumerian as the primary spoken language around c. 2000 BC, but Sumerian remained a sacred, ceremonial, literary and scientific language in Akkadian-speaking states such as [[Assyria]] and [[Babylonia]] until the 1st century AD. The cuneiform script, originally developed for Sumerian, was adopted by many regional languages, and Akkadian's grammar and vocabulary were significantly influenced by Sumerian. Scholars began deciphering its inscriptions in the 19th century.

Unfinished theological treatise by Thomas Aquinas, a compendium of the main teachings of the [[Catholic Church]]. Astronomical moment when one of Earth's poles reaches its maximum tilt toward the Sun. It occurs twice yearly, in June in the Northern Hemisphere and in December in the Southern, and marks the longest day of daylight in that hemisphere. Atmospheric optical phenomenon, also called a parhelion, in which bright spots appear on either side of the Sun. Sun dogs typically appear as a pair of patches about 22° to the left and right of the Sun. === KEY: Sun_ef6572
HEADWORD: Sun
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomical unit | Galactic Center | Molecular cloud | Nuclear fusion | Red giant | Solar System | Solar wind | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Star at the center of the [[Solar System]], a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) and the main source of energy for life on Earth.

The Sun is a massive sphere of hot plasma, heated to incandescence by [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]] reactions in its core, radiating energy from its surface mainly as visible light and infrared radiation, with 10% at ultraviolet energies. It has been an object of veneration in many cultures and a central subject of astronomical research since antiquity. It orbits the [[Galactic Center]] at a distance of 24,000 to 28,000 light-years. Its mean distance from Earth is about 1.496×10⁸ km, or about 8 light-minutes; this distance was used to define the [[Astronomical unit|astronomical unit]] (au), now defined as 149.5978707×10⁶ km.

The Sun is the largest and most massive object in the Solar System. Its diameter is about 1,391,400 km (864,600 mi), around 109 times that of Earth, and its mass is around 330,000 times Earth's, about 99.86% of the system's total. It has an absolute magnitude of +4.83, estimated to be brighter than about 85% of the stars in the Milky Way, most of which are red dwarfs. It has no definite boundary; its radius is measured to the edge of the photosphere, the apparent visible surface. Its oblateness is 8.2×10⁻⁶, or 8 parts per million, making it the natural object closest to a perfect sphere ever observed. The photosphere is 74.9% hydrogen and 23.8% helium by mass; heavier elements account for less than 2%, chiefly oxygen (roughly 1%), carbon (0.3%), neon (0.2%), and iron (0.2%).

The Sun rotates faster at its equator than at its poles, with a period of approximately 25.6 days at the equator and 33.5 days at the poles relative to the stars, and about 28 days at the equator as seen from Earth. The early Sun rotated up to ten times faster; magnetic braking, as its magnetic field interacted with the outflowing [[Solar wind|solar wind]], slowed it.

The Sun formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of matter within a large [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]]; most of the matter gathered in the center, and the rest flattened into a disk that became the Solar System. Every second, its core fuses about 600 billion kg of hydrogen into helium and converts four billion kg of matter into energy. About four to seven billion years from now, as core hydrogen fusion diminishes, the Sun will expand into a [[Red giant|red giant]]. Models suggest it will then shed its outer layers and become a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]], which may glow for perhaps trillions of years before becoming an extremely dense black dwarf. The English word *sun* derives from Old English *sunne*; the adjective *solar* comes from Latin *sol*. The astronomical symbol is ☉.

ENTRY:
Property of certain materials with exactly zero electrical resistance and expelled magnetic fields. Superconductivity was discovered in solid [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] on April 8, 1911, by Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes. === KEY: Supernova_8776b7
HEADWORD: Supernova
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Kepler's Supernova | Large Magellanic Cloud | Milky Way | Neutron star | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Powerful and luminous explosion of a star, occurring in the last evolutionary stages of a massive star or when a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]] undergoes runaway nuclear fusion.

The original object, the progenitor, can collapse to form a [[Neutron star|neutron star]] or [[Black hole|black hole]], or disperse completely into a diffuse nebula with no stellar remnant. Peak optical luminosity can be comparable to that of an entire galaxy before fading over several weeks or months.

Most supernovae are triggered by one of two mechanisms. In the first, a white dwarf re-ignites runaway fusion, thought to follow accretion from a binary companion or a stellar merger. In the second, a massive star's core collapses once radiation pressure no longer balances gravity, classically when the star begins producing iron. Supernovae can expel several solar masses of material at speeds up to several percent of the speed of light, driving a shock wave into the interstellar medium and sweeping up a shell observed as a supernova remnant. They are a major source of heavier elements, from oxygen to rubidium, and of cosmic rays, and may produce gravitational waves.

Supernovae occur in the [[Milky Way]] approximately 1.6 to 4.6 times per century on average, and several thousand are typically seen in distant galaxies every year. The most recent in the Milky Way to be observed were Tycho's Supernova in 1572 and [[Kepler's Supernova]] in 1604, both visible to the naked eye; they were used to argue against the Aristotelian idea of an unchanging universe beyond the Moon and planets. SN 1987A, in the [[Large Magellanic Cloud]], provided the only measurements of astronomical neutrinos other than the Sun's.

Theoretical framework in [[Particle physics|particle physics]] proposing a symmetry between [[Boson|bosons]] and [[Fermion|fermions]]. === KEY: Supply_and_demand_7df86c

ENTRY:
Economic model of price determination in a [[Market (economics)|market]]. In [[Microeconomics|microeconomics]], it holds that the price of a good in a perfectly competitive market settles where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The concept forms the theoretical basis of modern economics. Under perfect competition, supply is determined by marginal cost: firms produce additional output while its cost is below the market price, and a rise in raw material costs shifts supply to the left. In [[Macroeconomics|macroeconomics]], an aggregate demand–aggregate supply model describes total output and the price level. Alfred Marshall presented the framework in his 1890 *Principles of Economics*.

=== KEY: Surface_tension_cc681e

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Energy per unit [[Area|area]] of a liquid's surface, equivalently a force per unit length. Cohesive forces pull surface molecules inward, so liquid surfaces tend to contract to the smallest possible area; this is why droplets form spheres. [[Water]] has a surface tension of 72.8 millinewtons per meter at 20 °C, high because of its [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonds]]; this lets objects denser than water, such as razor blades and water striders, float. === KEY: Surgery_4cc2ab

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Medical specialty that uses manual and instrumental techniques to diagnose or treat pathological conditions, alter bodily functions, reconstruct or alter appearance, or remove unwanted tissues, neoplasms, and foreign bodies.

A surgical procedure is generally one that involves cutting tissue to treat disease or injury, though procedures such as [[Endoscopy|endoscopy]] may count if they use surgical settings, such as sterile fields, anesthesia, or suturing. He premiered several works by [[Sergei Prokofiev]], including the Sixth Piano Sonata in 1940, and the Ninth Sonata was dedicated to him. His 1960 American debut at Carnegie Hall consisted of five sold-out recitals. He favored live recordings over studio sessions and founded festivals in La Grange de Meslay, France, and at the Pushkin Museum in Moscow devoted to opera, vocal and [[Chamber music|chamber music]]. He received the Stalin Prize in 1949 but avoided Communist Party membership amid political suspicion linked to his German heritage.

=== KEY: Swahili_9af95f

ENTRY:
Bantu language of the Atlantic–Congo family, originally spoken by the Swahili people along the East African coast and adjacent islands.

Also called Kiswahili, it is spoken by an estimated 40 million to 200 million people, native and second-language speakers combined; most native speakers live in [[Tanzania]] and [[Kenya]]. The name comes from a plural adjectival form of an [[Arabic]] word meaning "of the coasts." Arabic loanwords date from contact between Arab traders and the Bantu inhabitants of the coast, when Swahili emerged as a [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]]. Estimates of the Arabic share of the vocabulary vary, with about 40% commonly cited. Swahili also has loanwords from Portuguese, English, German, and was originally written in Arabic script. The earliest known Swahili documents are letters written in Kilwa in 1711 and sent to the Portuguese of Mozambique.

The dialect Kiunguja, from Zanzibar, became the basis of the modern standard language through missionary and colonial efforts. Missionaries adopted the Latin script in 1844, and the British Inter-Territorial Language Committee formalized Standard Swahili in 1930. Swahili played a role in anticolonial movements, and [[Julius Nyerere]] adopted it as a unifying language during the Tanganyikan independence struggle.

Swahili is one of three official languages (alongside English and French) of the East African Community and the lingua franca of much of the African Great Lakes region and East and Southern Africa. It is a working language of the [[African Union]]. July 7 is observed as Swahili Language Day, declared in 2022.

Family of [[Passerine|passerine]] songbirds (*Hirundinidae*) found on all continents, occasionally including [[Antarctica]]. Swallows are highly adapted to catching insects in flight, with slender bodies and long, pointed wings that allow great maneuverability and endurance. The family includes about 90 species in 21 [[Genus|genera]], with its greatest diversity in Africa, where it is thought to have evolved. Many European and North American species are long-distance migrants, while West and South African swallows do not migrate. The oldest known fossil swallow, *Miochelidon eschata*, comes from the Early [[Miocene]] of [[Siberia]]. In the [[United Kingdom]] and Ireland, *Hirundo rustica*, the barn swallow, is called the swallow.

Large, colorful [[Butterfly|butterfly]] of the family *Papilionidae*, with over 550 [[Species|species]]. Most are tropical, but the family occurs on every [[Continent|continent]] except Antarctica and includes the largest butterflies in the world, the birdwings of the genus *Ornithoptera*. Caterpillars carry an osmeterium, a forked organ behind the head that releases a foul-smelling secretion when threatened. Many species sequester toxic alkaloids from their host plants, making them unpalatable or poisonous to vertebrates. The common name refers to the forked hindwings of some species. Linnaeus chose *Papilio*, Latin for butterfly, as the type genus. The family is usually divided into three subfamilies: Baroniinae, Parnassiinae and Papilioninae. The Taira clan of [[Japan]] used the swallowtail, called *agehachō*, as its mon, or crest.
