=== KEY: Hydrogen_bond_b93e9d

ENTRY:
Intermolecular attraction between a hydrogen atom bonded to an [[Electronegativity|electronegative]] donor and a lone pair on an acceptor atom. === KEY: Hydrogen_e4ce2a
HEADWORD: Hydrogen
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antoine Lavoisier | Chemical element | Electrolysis | Fertilizer | Henry Cavendish | Isotope | Quantum mechanics | Tritium
ENTRY:
[[Chemical element]] with symbol H and atomic number 1, the lightest and most abundant element in the universe, constituting about 75% of all normal matter. Under standard conditions it is a colorless, odorless, non-toxic, highly combustible gas of diatomic molecules, H₂. On Earth it occurs as H₂ and in compounds such as water and organic compounds.

The common [[Isotope|isotope]] ¹H, with one proton, one electron, and no neutrons, has an abundance above 99.98%. The other naturally occurring isotopes are stable deuterium (²H), used in heavy water as a neutron moderator and coolant for nuclear reactors, and radioactive [[Tritium|tritium]] (³H), which decays to helium-3 by beta decay with a half-life of 12.32 years. The colors of light that hydrogen absorbs and emits were crucial to the development of [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].

Hydrogen is typically nonmetallic except under extreme pressure and readily forms covalent bonds with most nonmetals. It can also form the negatively charged hydride ion, H⁻, or the positively charged hydron, H⁺, whose exchange drives acid–base reactions and underlies the importance of pH.

Hydrogen gas was first produced artificially in the 17th century by reacting acids with metals. [[Henry Cavendish]], in 1766–1781, identified it as a distinct substance and found that it produces water when burned, the origin of its name, "water-former." [[Antoine Lavoisier]] named it in 1783.

Nearly all hydrogen is produced from fossil fuels, particularly by steam reforming of natural gas, with almost 100 million tonnes made annually as of 2024. Production by [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]] of water is more expensive. Main uses are fossil fuel processing and ammonia production for [[Fertilizer|fertilizer]]; fuel cells for generating electricity are an emerging use.

=== KEY: Hydrography_62bbb6

ENTRY:
Branch of applied sciences concerned with measuring and describing the physical features of [[Ocean|oceans]], [[Sea|seas]], coastal areas, [[Lake|lakes]] and [[River|rivers]], and predicting how they change over time. Its primary purpose is navigational safety, though it also supports economic development, security, scientific research and environmental protection. === KEY: Hydrology_5db492

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Scientific study of the movement, distribution and management of water on [[Earth]] and other planets. A practitioner is called a hydrologist and draws on earth science, civil or environmental engineering, and physical geography. === KEY: Hydropower_db31eb
HEADWORD: Hydropower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dam | Fossil fuel | Hydroelectricity | Methane | Reservoir | Watermill
ENTRY:
Use of falling or fast-running water to produce electricity or to power machines, by converting the gravitational potential or kinetic energy of a water source. It is now used mainly for [[Hydroelectricity|hydroelectric]] generation and also as one half of pumped-storage hydroelectricity, an energy storage system.

Since ancient times, [[Watermill|watermills]] have supplied renewable energy for irrigation and for machines such as gristmills, sawmills, textile mills, trip hammers, dock cranes, domestic lifts, and ore mills. The first commercial hydroelectric plant began operating in 1882 in Appleton, Wisconsin. Hydropower supplied over 4,300 terawatt-hours in 2022, about 15% of global electricity, and installed capacity reached approximately 1,283 gigawatts by 2024, excluding pumped storage.

Available power depends on the hydraulic head and the volumetric flow rate. A turbine that is 85% efficient, with a flow of 80 cubic meters per second and a head of 145 m, produces about 97 MW. Because stream flow varies seasonally, sites are assessed using flow records sometimes spanning decades, and [[Dam|dams]] and [[Reservoir|reservoirs]] give a more dependable supply by smoothing seasonal changes.

Hydropower does not directly produce carbon dioxide or other atmospheric pollutants and is an alternative to [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]]; institutions such as the World Bank view it as a low-carbon route to economic development. It also has costs. Dam failures can cost lives and cause pollution. Dams and reservoirs block animals from traveling upstream, alter water temperature and oxygen levels downstream, trap sediment, and cause habitat loss for some aquatic species. Decaying organic matter in reservoirs releases [[Methane|methane]], estimated at almost a billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent per year, and people living near plants may be displaced during construction or when reservoir banks become unstable.

=== KEY: Hydrosphere_6b6a96
HEADWORD: Hydrosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Glacier | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Combined body of water found on, under and above the surface of a planet, minor planet or natural satellite. [[Earth|Earth's]] hydrosphere has existed for about 4 billion years and continues to change as seafloor spreading and continental drift rearrange land and ocean. It has been estimated at 1.386 billion cubic kilometers (333 million cubic miles) of water, including water in the atmosphere, in [[Glacier|glaciers]], lakes and rivers, and in living organisms. Saltwater accounts for 97.5% of this total. Of the fresh water, 68.9% is ice and permanent snow cover, 30.8% is groundwater, and only 0.3% is in easily accessible lakes, reservoirs and river systems. Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface. Water moves between reservoirs through the [[Water cycle|water cycle]], driven by solar energy and gravity.

=== KEY: Hydrostatic_equilibrium_b75088
HEADWORD: Hydrostatic equilibrium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dwarf planet | Gravity | Outer space | Solar System
ENTRY:
State of balance in a fluid or plastic solid at rest, in which external forces such as [[Gravity|gravity]] are offset by a pressure-gradient force. On Earth it keeps the atmosphere from collapsing into a thin, dense shell, while gravity keeps it from diffusing into [[Outer space|outer space]]. === KEY: Hydrothermal_vent_d7b931

PERSON: no

=== KEY: Hyena_f31b72

ENTRY:
Feliform [[Carnivora|carnivoran]] mammal of the family Hyaenidae, with just four living species. Despite their low diversity, hyenas are vital components of most [[Africa|African]] ecosystems. === KEY: Hygiene_38a12a
HEADWORD: Hygiene
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cholera | Ignaz Semmelweis | Louis Pasteur | Pathogen | Sanitation | Soap | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Set of practices performed to preserve health and prevent the spread of disease. The [[World Health Organization]] defines it as "conditions and practices that help to maintain health and prevent the spread of diseases." Its activities are grouped into home and everyday, personal, medical, sleep, and food hygiene, and practices vary from one culture to another.

The term is often used interchangeably with cleanliness, but the two differ: hygiene refers to practices that prevent the spread of disease-causing organisms, and cleaning, such as handwashing, removes microbes along with dirt and is often the means of achieving it.

In the home, the aim is to break the chain of infection transmission. The main sources of infection are people who are carriers or are infected, raw foods, water, and pets. The main routes of spread are hands, food-contact surfaces, and cleaning cloths and utensils. Because the infectious dose for some [[Pathogen|pathogens]] can be as small as 10–100 viable units, critical surfaces need hygienic cleaning. Regular handwashing with [[Soap|soap]] is one of the most effective ways to prevent transmission and significantly reduces gastrointestinal and respiratory infections; in healthcare settings, hand hygiene has been reported to reduce infection rates by up to 50%. Poor [[Sanitation|sanitation]] is a primary cause of diarrheal disease in low-income communities.

Hygiene practices go back to prehistoric grooming, and the Romans built aqueducts and public baths. The modern framework emerged in the 19th century amid urban epidemics of [[Cholera|cholera]] and typhoid, when reformers advocated separating sewage from water supplies. [[Ignaz Semmelweis]], whose handwashing protocols in maternity wards are reported to have halved mortality from puerperal fever, and [[Louis Pasteur]] helped establish germ theory.

=== KEY: Hyperbola_2af03c
HEADWORD: Hyperbola
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollonius of Perga | Conic section | Escape velocity
ENTRY:
Smooth plane curve and one of three kinds of [[Conic section|conic section]], with two branches that are mirror images of each other. It forms when a plane cuts both halves of a double cone without passing through the apex. It is also the locus of points whose distances to two fixed foci differ by a constant. === KEY: Hyperbolic_functions_c1caef
HEADWORD: Hyperbolic functions
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hyperbola | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Analogues of the trigonometric functions, defined using the [[Hyperbola|hyperbola]] rather than the circle. The basic functions are hyperbolic sine (sinh) and hyperbolic cosine (cosh), from which tangent, cotangent, secant and cosecant are derived. Just as (cos t, sin t) traces a circle, (cosh t, sinh t) traces the right half of the unit hyperbola. The derivatives of sinh and cosh are cosh and sinh. Vincenzo Riccati introduced them formally in 1757, and Johann Heinrich Lambert systematized their use during the 1760s.

=== KEY: Hypertension_01da3e

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Long-term medical condition of persistently elevated blood pressure in the [[Artery|arteries]]. Usually symptomless, it is a major risk factor for [[Stroke|stroke]], coronary artery disease, [[Heart failure|heart failure]], atrial fibrillation and [[Dementia|dementia]], and a major cause of premature death worldwide. === KEY: Hypha_2e180d

ENTRY:
Long, branching filamentous structure of a [[Fungus|fungus]], [[Oomycete|oomycete]] or actinobacterium. In most fungi, hyphae are the main mode of vegetative growth and collectively form a [[Mycelium|mycelium]]. Each hypha consists of one or more cells within a tubular [[Cell wall|cell wall]], usually made of [[Chitin|chitin]], unlike the cellulose walls of plants and oomycetes. === KEY: Hypothermia_fc041c
HEADWORD: Hypothermia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcohol intoxication | Anorexia
ENTRY:
Drop in human core body temperature below 35.0 °C (95.0 °F). Mild hypothermia causes shivering and mental confusion. In moderate cases shivering stops and confusion increases, and in severe cases hallucinations, paradoxical undressing and an increased risk of the heart stopping may occur. It classically results from exposure to cold weather or cold-water immersion, but any condition that reduces heat production or increases heat loss can cause it, including [[Alcohol intoxication|alcohol intoxication]], low blood sugar, [[Anorexia]] and advanced age. Mild cases are treated with warm drinks and clothing, moderate cases with heating blankets and warmed intravenous fluids, and severe cases may require extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or cardiopulmonary bypass. Hypothermia causes at least 1,500 deaths a year in the United States alone. The name derives from Greek words meaning under and heat.

Diagnosis uses blood tests of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) and thyroxine (T4). Treatment is levothyroxine, a thyroid hormone replacement, with doses adjusted to symptoms and TSH levels. Overt hypothyroidism affects about 0.3–0.4% of people in the United States and is more common in women.

=== KEY: Hypoxia_medicine_5867da

Insufficient [[Oxygen|oxygen]] supply at the tissue level, affecting the whole body or a local region. Hypoxia differs from hypoxemia and anoxemia, which refer specifically to low or absent oxygen in the blood. === KEY: Hysteresis_f2094d

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ferromagnetism | Hard disk drive
ENTRY:
Dependence of a system's state on its history. A magnet, for example, may have more than one possible magnetic moment in a given field, depending on how the field changed in the past. === KEY: Ibn_Khaldun_625851
HEADWORD: Ibn Khaldun
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Arab Tunisian scholar, historian, philosopher, and sociologist.

Born in [[Tunis]] into an upper-class Andalusian family of Yemeni Arab descent, the Banū Khaldūn, he came from a line that had held government and scholarly posts in [[Seville]] until the family left in 1232, before the city's occupation in 1248. His father, who devoted himself to [[Fiqh|fiqh]], literature, and piety, was his first and most influential teacher. The [[Black Death]] killed both his parents and much of his extended family; some scholars argue it shaped his views on urban life and inspired his major work. His career combined diplomatic and judicial posts with teaching, including at the Al-Qarawiyyin mosque and at Cairo's Al-Azhar, amid the politics of the Marinid, Hafsid, and Mamluk realms. He also met Timur, founder of the Timurid Empire.

His best-known book is the *Muqaddimah* (*Prolegomena*, "Introduction"), which he states he wrote in six months. Completed around 1377 as the preface to his universal history *Kitāb al-ʿIbar*, it analyzes the rise and decline of civilizations through ʿaṣabiyyah (group solidarity), environmental influences, and cycles of dynastic power driven by economic production and social cohesion rather than supernatural intervention. It also contains early reflections on supply and demand and taxation, and insists on critiquing historical sources through observation and comparison. It later influenced Ottoman historians such as Kâtip Çelebi, Mustafa Naima, and Ahmed Cevdet Pasha, who used its theories to analyze the growth and decline of the [[Ottoman Empire]]. Scholars have compared him with [[Niccolò Machiavelli|Machiavelli]], Hume, Hegel, Marx, and Comte, and with the economists Ricardo and Adam Smith, whose ideas found precedent, though not direct influence, in his. Many regard him as a forerunner of historiography, sociology, economics, and demography.

=== KEY: Ice_age_99a5c9
HEADWORD: Ice age

ENTRY:
Period of long-term cooling of Earth's surface and atmosphere, marked by prolonged expansion of continental [[Glacier|glaciers]] and polar ice caps. Ice ages contain colder glacial periods alternating with warmer interglacials. Earth is in the Late Cenozoic Ice Age, which began 34 million years ago, and the Quaternary glaciation began 2.58 million years ago. The most recent glacial period, the [[Last Glacial Period]], was followed by the warm [[Holocene]], which began 11,700 years ago. ENTRY:
Large platform of glacial ice floating on the ocean, fed by one or more tributary glaciers. === KEY: Ideology_d25ceb
HEADWORD: Ideology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cognition | Karl Marx | Myth | Reign of Terror | Utopia | Worldview
ENTRY:
Set of ideals, values, or beliefs attributed to a person or group, especially those held for reasons not purely about belief in certain knowledge and in which "practical elements are as prominent as theoretical ones."

It is related to, but distinct from, a [[Worldview|worldview]]. The term was coined in 1796 by the French philosopher Antoine Destutt de Tracy, who proposed a "science of ideas" grounded in sensations and the ideas they produce. He formed the concept while imprisoned during the [[Reign of Terror]], envisioning a liberal philosophy defending individual liberty, property, free markets, and constitutional limits on state power. His *Elements of Ideology* appeared in 1804–15. The word acquired a negative sense when Napoleon described his opponents as "the ideologues," and [[Karl Marx]] adopted that sense, situating ideology within class struggle and domination. Others regarded it as a necessary part of institutional functioning and social integration. By 1907 the English term also meant a comprehensive framework of beliefs through which the world is interpreted, and it has since become largely neutral in the analysis of political opinions and social groups.

Ideologies may be political, social, epistemological, or ethical. Many analysts describe them as coherent systems of ideas resting on a few basic assumptions about reality that may or may not have any factual basis. Manfred Steger and Paul James define them as "patterned clusters of normatively imbued ideas and concepts, including particular representations of power relations." Willard A. Mullins contrasts ideology with [[Utopia|utopia]] and historical [[Myth|myth]], holding that an ideology must have power over [[Cognition|cognition]], guide evaluations and action, and be logically coherent. Belief can range from passive acceptance to fervent advocacy.
