HEADWORD: Man

Adult male human. Before adulthood, a male child or adolescent is called a boy. A man's genome usually inherits an X chromosome from the mother and a Y chromosome from the father, and sex differentiation of the male fetus is governed by the *SRY* gene on the Y chromosome. During [[Puberty|puberty]], hormones that stimulate androgen production produce secondary sexual characteristics, including greater muscle mass, greater height, facial hair growth, and lower body fat composition. Male anatomy is distinguished by the male reproductive system, which includes the testicles, sperm ducts, prostate gland, epididymides, and penis. Men tend to be taller and heavier than women and, adjusted for height, to have greater lean and bone mass and lower fat mass.

The English word derives from Old English *mann*, which primarily meant "person" or "human being" and referred to men, women, and children alike; the distinct word for an adult male was *wer*, which survives today only in compounds such as "werewolf."

Throughout history, traditional gender roles have often defined masculinity and men's activities and opportunities, and many societies have been patriarchal, giving men considerable power, particularly over women. Men are over-represented as both perpetrators and victims of violence, are often directed into professions with high mortality rates, and are most often subject to conscription into military service. Many religious doctrines set rules for men, such as religious circumcision. Critiques of the impact of these roles have prompted movement toward [[Gender equality|gender equality]] and the diverse men's movement. Trans men have a gender identity that does not align with the female sex they were assigned at birth, while intersex men may have sex characteristics that do not fit typical notions of male biology.

HEADWORD: Man Ray

ENTRY:
American-born, French-naturalized visual artist who spent most of his career in [[Paris]]. Born Emmanuel Radnitzky in South [[Philadelphia]] to Russian Jewish immigrants, he adopted the name Man Ray. He contributed to the [[Dada]] and [[Surrealism|Surrealist]] movements, although his ties to each were informal, and considered himself a painter above all. He is best known for photograms he called *rayographs*, after himself, made by placing objects directly on photosensitive paper and exposing them to light. He also worked as a fashion and portrait photographer and made films and readymades, such as *Cadeau* (1921), an iron fitted with tacks. Items linked to his father's tailoring trade, such as [[Sewing machine|sewing machines]] and mannequins, appear throughout his work. In Paris he was associated with [[Tristan Tzara]].

=== KEY: Management_fe4dbc
HEADWORD: Management
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin
ENTRY:
Administration of organizations, whether businesses, nonprofit organizations, or government bodies, through business administration, nonprofit management, or public administration respectively. It is the process of managing the resources of businesses, governments, and other organizations.

Larger organizations generally have three hierarchical levels of managers. Senior management, including the board of directors and a chief executive officer (CEO) or president, sets strategic goals and policies. Middle management, including branch, regional, department, and section managers, directs front-line managers and communicates senior management's goals to them. Line management, made up of supervisors and frontline managers or team leaders, oversees regular employees or volunteers and performs the functions traditionally considered the core of management.

The English verb derives from the fifteenth-century French *mesnager*, which referred in equestrian language to holding a horse's reins, and ultimately from the [[Latin]] *manus* (hand) and *agere* (to act). Definitions vary. Henri Fayol stated: "To manage is to forecast and to plan, to organize, to command, to co-ordinate and to control." Peter Drucker saw the basic task of management as twofold: marketing and innovation. Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management applied empirical analysis of workflows through time-motion studies to improve efficiency, and it also prompted debate over worker autonomy.

Management is taught as both theory and practice at colleges and universities, with prominent degree programs in management, business administration, and public administration. Social scientists study it as an academic discipline, investigating social organization, organizational adaptation, and organizational leadership. In recent decades there has been a movement for evidence-based management.

=== KEY: Managua_d98bf2
HEADWORD: Managua
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
City (pop., 2023: 1,063,815), capital and largest city of [[Nicaragua]], on the shore of Lake Managua. It is one of the largest cities in [[Central America]] and the seat of Managua Department. The area was inhabited by [[Paleo-Indians]] as far back as 12,000 BC. Founded in 1819, Managua became the national capital in 1852, having been a small fishing village before then. Between 1842 and 1930 it grew rapidly and became one of the most developed cities in Central America. A 1931 earthquake killed between 1,000 and 2,450 people, and the 1972 earthquake destroyed 90% of the downtown and killed more than 19,120 people. The city has been rebuilt several times and remains a major economic hub for the country and Central America.

=== KEY: Manatee_5a670e
HEADWORD: Manatee
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Genus of large, fully aquatic, mostly herbivorous marine mammals, also called sea cows. Three living species, members of the order [[Sirenia]], inhabit shallow, marshy coastal areas and rivers of the [[Caribbean Sea]], the [[Gulf of Mexico]], the Amazon basin, and [[West Africa]]. They eat more than 60 freshwater and saltwater plants and can grow up to 4.0 m (13 ft 1 in) long. Human activity is the main cause of death, through habitat destruction and collisions with propeller-driven boats; some have been found with over 50 propeller scars.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
City (pop., 2024: 2,279,686), capital of the Brazilian state of Amazonas, near the confluence of the [[Rio Negro (Amazon)|Negro]] and [[Amazon River|Amazon]] rivers. It is Brazil's seventh-largest city and one of two cities in the [[Amazon rainforest]] with more than 1 million people, alongside [[Belém]]. The Portuguese built the Fort of São José do Rio Negro there in 1669, and the settlement was named Manaus in 1832. Rubber made it the richest city in South America in the late 1800s and earned it the nickname Paris of the Tropics. Its economy centers on the Industrial Park of Manaus, a free economic zone whose factories make electronics, chemical products, and soap. The city was one of 12 Brazilian host cities for the 2014 World Cup.

=== KEY: Mancala_c2e395
HEADWORD: Mancala
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Family of two-player count-and-capture [[Board game|board games]] played with seeds, stones, or beans in rows of pits. A turn consists of taking all the seeds from one pit and sowing them one per pit in sequence. A player who ends a turn in an empty pit on their own side captures the opponent's seeds in the pit directly across. The word derives from the Arabic root *naqala*, meaning to move. Its origins are disputed. Claimed boards from Jordan date to about 5,870 BC, a claim others dispute, while excavated boards and rock cuts in Eritrea and Ethiopia date to the 6th and 7th centuries AD. The game reached the Americas through the [[Atlantic slave trade]], and the traditional game Warra was still played in [[Louisiana]] in the early 20th century.

HEADWORD: Manchester
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
City (pop., 2024: over 589,000) and metropolitan borough in North West [[England]]. It forms the core of the Greater Manchester Built-up Area, which has 2.87 million people and is one of the most populous conurbations in the [[United Kingdom]]. The city's history began with a civilian settlement associated with a Roman fort, Mamucium, established around AD 79 near the confluence of the Medlock and Irwell rivers. Manchester became the world's first industrialized city during the [[Industrial Revolution]], driven by textile manufacture, and gained city status in 1853. The Manchester Ship Canal, opened in 1894, linked the city to the [[Irish Sea]], 36 miles (58 km) west. Deindustrialization after the Second World War reduced its fortunes, and extensive regeneration followed the 1996 bombing. Manchester hosted the 2002 Commonwealth Games. It is noted for its architecture, music, media, and sports clubs.

City (pop., 2020: 115,644), largest city in [[New Hampshire]], United States, on the banks of the Merrimack River. It is the tenth-most populous city in [[New England]] and, with Nashua, is one of two seats of Hillsborough County. Samuel Blodget is credited with the name, reflecting his hope of building an industrial center like the English city of [[Manchester]]. During the [[Industrial Revolution]], the Amoskeag Manufacturing Company operated the largest cotton textile plant in the world. Many textile jobs left after [[World War II]], but new industries, including DEKA, arrived.

ENTRY:
[[Association football]] club in the [[Premier League]], based at Old Trafford, Greater Manchester, England. Founded in 1878 as Newton Heath LYR Football Club and renamed in 1902, it is nicknamed the Red Devils. Under [[Alex Ferguson]], manager from 1986 to 2013, the club won 13 league titles and two [[UEFA Champions League]] titles, and in 1998–99 became the first English club to win the continental treble. Its 20 top-flight league titles are a joint record. The Glazer family has controlled the club since Malcolm Glazer's 2005 purchase, and some shares have been listed on the New York Stock Exchange since 2012.
