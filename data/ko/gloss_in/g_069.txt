=== KEY: James_Clerk_Maxwell_9a8628
HEADWORD: Maxwell, James Clerk
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Control theory | Isaac Newton | Maxwell's demon | Maxwell's equations | Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution | Quantum mechanics | Radio wave | Statistical mechanics
ENTRY:
Scottish physicist and mathematician who formulated the classical theory of electromagnetic radiation, the first theory to describe electricity, magnetism, and light as different manifestations of the same phenomenon. Born in Edinburgh, he graduated from Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1854, earning distinction in mathematics and the Smith's Prize. He later held the chair of natural philosophy at Marischal College in Aberdeen, where he proposed that the rings of Saturn were composed of numerous small particles, work that earned him the Adams Prize. From 1860 to 1865 he was professor of natural philosophy at King's College London. His 1865 paper "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field" showed that electric and magnetic fields travel through space as waves at the speed of light, and led to his prediction of the existence of [[Radio wave|radio waves]]. The four equations now known as [[Maxwell's equations]] appear in his 1873 *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* and are regarded as the second great unification in physics, after that achieved by [[Isaac Newton]].

Maxwell was the first to derive the [[Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution]], describing the speeds of gas molecules, and was key in the creation of [[Statistical mechanics|statistical mechanics]]. In 1861 he presented the first durable color photograph, and showed that any color can be produced by mixing three primary colors: red, green, and blue. His 1863 paper "On Governors" is a foundation of [[Control theory|control theory]] and cybernetics. In 1867 he proposed the thought experiment known as [[Maxwell's demon]].

In 1871 he returned to Cambridge as the first Cavendish Professor of Physics and oversaw construction of the Cavendish Laboratory. He is regarded as a founder of modern electrical engineering, and his work laid foundations for relativity and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].

=== KEY: James_Hutton_8ec674

PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Scottish geologist, agriculturalist, and chemical manufacturer, often called the "Father of Modern Geology." Hutton inferred the remote history of the Earth from evidence in present-day rocks, studying features such as Salisbury Crags and Siccar Point. He argued that geological features underwent continuing transformation over indefinitely long periods, and he was one of the earliest proponents of uniformitarianism, the view that continuing natural processes shape the Earth's crust. He proposed a "system of the habitable Earth," a deistic mechanism to keep the world suitable for humans, an early form of the [[Anthropic principle]]. He earned a medical doctorate at Leiden University and co-owned a profitable chemical works that made sal ammoniac from [[Soot|soot]]. In Berwickshire he farmed and introduced innovative farming practices.

=== KEY: James_Watt_44fbba

PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Horsepower | Industrial Revolution | Steam engine | Watt
ENTRY:
Scottish inventor, engineer and chemist. While working as an instrument maker at the University of Glasgow, Watt improved the [[Steam engine|steam engine]] of Thomas Newcomen. Newcomen's design repeatedly cooled and reheated the cylinder, wasting energy. Watt's separate condenser avoided this waste and greatly improved the power and efficiency of steam engines. He adapted the engine to produce rotary motion, broadening its use beyond pumping water, and his steam engine of 1776 was fundamental to the [[Industrial Revolution]]. In 1775 he formed a partnership with Matthew Boulton, and the firm of Boulton and Watt became highly successful. As Watt developed the concept of [[Horsepower|horsepower]], the SI unit of power, the [[Watt|watt]], was named after him.

=== KEY: Japan_53a577

Island country (pop. almost 123 million) in East Asia, in the Pacific Ocean off the northeast coast of the Asian mainland, with Tokyo as its capital and largest city. It invaded China in 1937 and attacked the U.S. and European colonial powers in 1941, entering World War II as an Axis power. After the [[Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki|atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki]], it surrendered in 1945 and was under Allied occupation until 1952.

Japan experienced rapid economic growth in the following decades, but since the collapse of its asset price bubble in the early 1990s has had a prolonged stagnation known as the Lost Decades. It is a constitutional monarchy with a bicameral legislature, the National Diet, and the only Asian member of the G7. It is a leader in automotive, electronics, and robotics industries and has one of the world's highest life expectancies, but its population is declining.

=== KEY: Japanese_language_825bc2
HEADWORD: Japanese language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Japan
ENTRY:
Principal language of the Japonic family, with around 123 million speakers, primarily in [[Japan]], the only country where it is the national language. Proposals linking it to Ainu, Austronesian, Koreanic, and the discredited Altaic have not gained wide acceptance. Proto-Japonic is thought to have been brought from the Korean peninsula in the early to mid-4th century BC, during the Yayoi period.

Chinese documents of the 3rd century AD recorded a few Japanese words, but substantial Old Japanese texts date from the 8th century. The *Kojiki*, the earliest text, was written entirely in Chinese characters. Early Middle Japanese is the language of the Heian period (794–1185), when waves of Sino-Japanese vocabulary entered the language and made it mora-timed. Late Middle Japanese (1185–1600) saw extensive grammatical change and the first European loanwords. The basis of the standard dialect moved from the Kansai region to the Edo region (modern Tokyo) in the early modern period. After the end of Japan's isolation in 1853, loanwords from European languages increased significantly, and words from English roots have proliferated.

Japanese is agglutinative and mora-timed, with a pure vowel system, phonemic vowel and consonant length, and a lexically significant pitch accent. Word order is normally subject–object–verb, with particles marking grammatical function and sentence structure that is topic–comment. Nouns have no grammatical number or gender, and there are no articles. Verbs and adjectives are conjugated, verbs primarily for tense and voice but not person. A complex system of honorifics indicates the relative status of the speaker, the listener, and persons mentioned.

The writing system combines Chinese characters, called *kanji*, with two syllabaries derived from them, *hiragana* and *katakana*. Latin script (*rōmaji*) is used in a limited fashion, for example for imported acronyms.

=== KEY: Java_d52387
HEADWORD: Java
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dutch East Indies | Jakarta
ENTRY:
Island of Indonesia, one of the Greater Sunda Islands, bordered by the Indian Ocean to the south and the Java Sea to the north. It is the world's most populous island, with about 56% of the Indonesian population on only 7% of its land area. It is the world's 13th largest island. [[Jakarta]], Indonesia's capital, lies on its northwestern coast. Java dominates Indonesia politically, economically, and culturally.

The island was formed by volcanic eruptions caused by the subduction of the Australian Plate under the Sunda Plate. A chain of volcanic mountains forms its east–west spine, and the highest, Mount Semeru, reaches 3,676 m. Mount Merapi is the most active volcano in Java and in Indonesia. The mountains split the interior into relatively isolated regions suited to wet-rice cultivation, and its rice lands are among the richest in the world. Indonesian coffee was first grown on Java, beginning in 1699. Java is divided into four provinces, Banten, West Java, Central Java, and East Java, and two special regions, Jakarta and Yogyakarta.

Four main languages are spoken: Javanese, Sundanese, Madurese, and Betawi. Javanese and Sundanese are the most widely spoken; the Javanese live in the central and eastern parts and the Sundanese in the west. The majority are Muslim, but the population includes diverse religious beliefs.

Java was the center of powerful Hindu-Buddhist empires and Islamic sultanates, and the core of the colonial [[Dutch East Indies]]. It was also the center of the Indonesian struggle for independence in the 1930s and 1940s. Five of Indonesia's ten UNESCO World Heritage Sites are on Java, among them Borobudur, Prambanan, Ujung Kulon National Park, and Yogyakarta.

High-level, general-purpose, memory-safe, object-oriented programming language. Java was designed by James Gosling at Sun Microsystems and released in May 1995. === KEY: Jazz_9a284e
HEADWORD: Jazz
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blues | Duke Ellington | Ragtime
ENTRY:
Music genre that originated in the African-American communities of New Orleans, Louisiana, in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Its roots lie in [[Blues|blues]], [[Ragtime|ragtime]], European harmony, African rhythmic rituals, spirituals, hymns, marches, vaudeville song, and dance music. It is characterized by swing and blue notes, complex chords, call-and-response vocals, polyrhythms, and improvisation. Since the 1920s Jazz Age it has been recognized as a major form of musical expression in both traditional and popular music.

The origin of the word is much researched. It is believed to be related to *jasm*, a slang term dating to 1860 meaning "pep, energy." The earliest written record, in a 1912 *Los Angeles Times* article, concerns a baseball pitch called a "jazz ball." The American Dialect Society named it the Word of the 20th Century.

Jazz is difficult to define because it spans more than 100 years, from ragtime to rock-infused fusion. [[Duke Ellington]] said, "It's all music."

Jazz did not begin as a single tradition. New Orleans jazz began in the early 1910s, combining brass band marches, French quadrilles, biguine, ragtime, and blues with collective polyphonic improvisation. In the 1930s the prominent styles were swing big bands, Kansas City jazz, and gypsy jazz. Bebop emerged in the 1940s, moving jazz from danceable popular music to a more challenging "musician's music" played at faster tempos. The mid-1950s brought hard bop, and the late 1950s modal jazz and free jazz, which explored playing without regular meter, beat, and formal structures. Jazz fusion, combining improvisation with rock's rhythms and electric instruments, appeared in the late 1960s and early 1970s; its commercial form, smooth jazz, succeeded in the early 1980s.
