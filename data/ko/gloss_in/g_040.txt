=== KEY: Eddy_current_a5f56e
HEADWORD: Eddy current
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric motor | Léon Foucault | Transformer
ENTRY:
Loop of electric current induced within conductors by a changing magnetic field, also called Foucault's current. Eddy currents flow in closed loops perpendicular to the magnetic field and arise from time-varying fields, as in an AC electromagnet or [[Transformer|transformer]], or from relative motion between a magnet and a conductor. By Lenz's law, an eddy current creates a field that opposes the change that produced it, so a nearby conductive surface drags on a moving magnet, a principle used in eddy current brakes. Their resistance also dissipates energy as heat, causing losses in transformers, [[Electric motor|motors]] and generators that laminated or ferrite cores reduce. Eddy currents also heat metal in induction furnaces and reveal cracks in metal parts through eddy-current testing. [[Léon Foucault]] is credited with discovering them in 1855.

=== KEY: Education_de7a22
HEADWORD: Education
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Critical thinking | Early childhood education | Economic growth | Primary education | Printing press | Secondary education | Tertiary education | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Transmission of knowledge and skills and the development of character traits. The term derives from the Latin *educare* ("to bring up") and *educere* ("to bring forth"), and it can refer to a process (schooling, teaching, and learning), to the mental states and qualities of educated people, or to the academic field that studies the methods, processes, and social institutions involved. Its exact definition is disputed, as are its aims.

Education is classified in several ways. Formal education happens in a complex institutional framework, such as public schools, with classes based on a student's age and progress. Non-formal education is also structured but occurs outside the formal schooling system, and informal education is unstructured learning through daily experiences. Formal and non-formal education are divided into levels: [[Early childhood education|early childhood]], [[Primary education|primary]], [[Secondary education|secondary]], and [[Tertiary education|tertiary]] education. Further classifications are based on teaching method (teacher-centered or student-centered), subject, medium, and funding.

Theorists have defined education in different ways. R. S. Peters outlined three essential features: that knowledge and understanding are imparted, that the process is beneficial, and that it is done in a morally appropriate manner. Critics note that such precise definitions exclude less common types of education, and alternatives rely on family resemblance among forms of education instead. Evaluative or thick conceptions hold that education by nature leads to some improvement, whereas thin conceptions are value-neutral. Many scholars stress [[Critical thinking|critical thinking]] as what distinguishes education from indoctrination, which instills beliefs regardless of whether they are rational. Others argue that the two cannot always be clearly separated, since young children must sometimes learn things, such as safety rules and hygiene practices, without understanding the reasons.

Education socializes children into society by teaching cultural values and norms and equips them with skills to become productive members of society. It stimulates [[Economic growth|economic growth]] and raises awareness of local and global problems; studies estimate that each additional year of schooling raises lifetime earnings by about 10%. Governments set education policy, determining when classes happen, what is taught, and who can or must attend, and international organizations such as [[UNESCO]] have promoted primary education for all children. Success depends on psychological factors (motivation, intelligence, and personality), social factors (socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and gender, which are often linked to discrimination), access to educational technology, teacher quality, and parent involvement. Some analyses find no systematic link between increased spending and student performance, attributing gaps to instructional quality, family background, and institutional incentives.

In prehistory, education was informal, taking place through oral communication and imitation. With the rise of ancient civilizations and the invention of writing, education shifted toward formal instruction, initially available mainly to elites and religious groups. The [[Printing press|printing press]] of the 15th century made books more widely available and increased literacy. From the 18th and 19th centuries public education became more important, leading to the worldwide effort to make primary education free and compulsory up to a certain age. Over 90% of primary-school-age children worldwide now attend primary school.

=== KEY: Egg_cell_f4969a

Female reproductive cell, or gamete, of most anisogamous organisms, which reproduce sexually with a larger female gamete and a smaller male one. === KEY: Egg_f04ba3
HEADWORD: Egg
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chicken | Egg cell | Embryo | Fish | Monotreme | Ostrich | Whale shark
ENTRY:
Organic vessel grown by an animal to carry a possibly fertilized [[Egg cell|egg cell]], or zygote, within which an [[Embryo|embryo]] is incubated until it can survive on its own and hatches. Similar reproductive structures in other kingdoms are called spores or, in seed plants, seeds.

Most arthropods, vertebrates (excluding live-bearing mammals), and mollusks lay eggs, although some animals, such as scorpions, do not. Reptile, bird, and monotreme eggs are laid out of water and are surrounded by a protective shell.

The largest recorded egg came from a [[Whale shark|whale shark]] and measured 30 cm × 14 cm × 9 cm. The [[Ostrich|ostrich]] egg, at 1.5 kg, is the largest of any living bird. The bee hummingbird lays the smallest known bird egg, 6.35–11.4 mm long.

Most [[Fish|fish]] lay undeveloped eggs that are fertilized externally; large fish can produce over 100 million eggs in one spawning. Fish and amphibian eggs are jellylike and shell-less, and would dry out in air. Rays and most sharks retain the eggs, which develop internally.

Reptiles, birds, and mammals are amniotes, whose complex eggs or embryos include an amniotic membrane. Reptile eggs are leathery in snakes and most lizards and calcareous in turtles. Dinosaurs laid eggs, some preserved as fossils. Among mammals, only the [[Monotreme|monotremes]], the platypus and two genera of echidna, lay eggs.

A typical [[Chicken|chicken]] egg has a hard calcium carbonate shell with about 17,000 microscopic pores for gas exchange, and fertilized eggs need about 21 days of incubation to produce a chick.

Eggs are a popular food worldwide, with global production of approximately 91 million metric tons in 2023. They symbolize life, healing, and rebirth in folklore and mythology.

=== KEY: Egyptian_hieroglyphs_4feb4f

ENTRY:
Formal writing system of [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] for writing the [[Egyptian language|Egyptian language]], combining ideographic, logographic, syllabic and alphabetic elements. In the Middle Kingdom the system used about 900 distinct signs, and cursive forms were used for religious literature on [[Papyrus|papyrus]] and wood. Hieroglyphs are the ultimate ancestor of the [[Phoenician alphabet]], the first widely used phonetic writing system. They are thought to be one of four writing systems developed without outside influence, along with [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]], [[Chinese characters]] and Mayan script. The first decipherable sentence dates to the 28th century BC. Late survivals of hieroglyphic use extend into the 4th century AD, but after pagan temples closed in the 5th century, fluent readers were lost. Jean-François Champollion deciphered the script in the 1820s with the help of the [[Rosetta Stone]].

Collection of myths from [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] that describe the actions of the gods as a means of understanding the world. Myths appear in writings and art, particularly in [[Hymn|hymns]], ritual texts, funerary texts and temple decoration, though sources rarely contain a complete account of a myth. PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Colombia | Voltaire
ENTRY:
Mythical city of gold supposedly located somewhere in South America. The legend originated in 16th-century Spanish accounts of a ruler called El Dorado, or the Golden One, who covered himself in gold dust before washing it off in a sacred lake. It is associated with the Muisca people of present-day [[Colombia]], and later accounts linked it to the installation of a new *zipa*, who made offerings of gold at Lake Guatavita. Spanish expeditions under Gonzalo Jiménez de Quesada conquered Muisca territory in 1537. Later expeditions, including those of Gonzalo Pizarro, Francisco Orellana and Walter Raleigh, moved the supposed location to the Amazon and the Guianas. The legend then became associated with Lake Parime, a supposed inland lake that appeared on 17th-century maps before its existence was rejected. Although belief in El Dorado as a real place declined, it became a symbol of fabulous wealth, appearing in *Candide* by [[Voltaire]].

=== KEY: El_Ni_o_Southern_Oscillation_115a9e
HEADWORD: El Niño–Southern Oscillation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric pressure | Christmas | Lima | Pacific Ocean | Tropics
ENTRY:
Global climate phenomenon of fluctuating sea surface temperatures over the tropical [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific Ocean]]. Its warming phase is called El Niño and its cooling phase La Niña, and the Southern Oscillation is the accompanying shift in [[Atmospheric pressure|surface air pressure]]. The phases recur irregularly about every two to seven years and are not easily predictable. El Niño causes short-term spikes in global average surface temperature, lasting about one year, while La Niña causes short-term cooling. The phenomenon disrupts weather across the [[Tropics|tropics]] and beyond, bringing intense storms to some places and droughts to others. The countries most affected are developing countries bordering the Pacific that depend on agriculture and fishing. The term was first recorded in a climate context in 1892, when Captain Camilo Carrillo reported to a geographical congress in [[Lima]] that Peruvian sailors had named the warm current after the Christ Child, as it typically appeared around [[Christmas]].

=== KEY: Elastic_modulus_2d5ed4
HEADWORD: Elastic modulus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Stiffness | Stress (mechanics) | Viscosity | Young's modulus
ENTRY:
Measure of a material's resistance to elastic (non-permanent) deformation under [[Stress (mechanics)|stress]]. It is defined as the slope of the stress–strain curve in the elastic region, so a [[Stiffness|stiffer]] material has a higher elastic modulus. Several types describe different modes of deformation. [[Young's modulus]] (E) describes tensile and compressive elasticity and is defined as the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain; it is often called simply the elastic modulus. The shear modulus (G) describes deformation of shape at constant volume, and the bulk modulus (K) describes volumetric elasticity and is the inverse of compressibility. Fluids at rest cannot support shear stress, so their shear modulus is zero; moving fluids experience shear stresses that give rise to [[Viscosity|viscosity]]. Steel has a Young's modulus of around 200 GPa, while rubber's is about 0.01–0.1 GPa.

=== KEY: Elasticity_physics_83352a
HEADWORD: Elasticity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Continuum mechanics | Elastic modulus | Hooke's law | Materials science | Metal | Plasticity (physics) | Polymer | Stress (mechanics)
ENTRY:
Physical property by which a body returns to its original size and shape after deformation. Studied in [[Continuum mechanics|continuum mechanics]] and [[Materials science|materials science]], elasticity is quantified in engineering by [[Elastic modulus|elastic moduli]], which relate stress to strain. Solids deform under adequate loads and recover their shape if they are elastic, in contrast to [[Plasticity (physics)|plastic]] behavior, in which the deformation is permanent. In [[Metal|metals]], the atomic lattice changes size and shape under force and returns to its lower-energy state when the force is removed; in rubbers and other [[Polymer|polymers]], elasticity arises from the stretching of polymer chains. [[Hooke's law]] states that the force needed to deform an elastic object is proportional to the distance of deformation, an ideal that most materials follow only for small deformations. The elastic limit, or yield strength, is the maximum [[Stress (mechanics)|stress]] a material can bear before plastic deformation begins.

=== KEY: Electric_battery_8201bd
HEADWORD: Electric battery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Benjamin Franklin | Data center | Michael Faraday
ENTRY:
Source of electric power consisting of one or more electrochemical cells with external connections for powering electrical devices. When a battery supplies power, its positive terminal is the cathode and its negative terminal the anode; electrons flow from the negative terminal through the external circuit to the positive terminal, driving a redox reaction in which higher-energy reactants become lower-energy products. "Battery" once meant multiple cells, but the term now includes single cells.

Primary batteries are used once and discarded, because their electrode materials change irreversibly during discharge; the alkaline battery is a common example. Secondary batteries can be recharged by an applied current; examples are the lead–acid automotive battery and the lithium-ion batteries in portable electronics and electric cars. Sizes range from cells for hearing aids and wristwatches to room-sized banks providing standby power for telephone exchanges and [[Data center|computer data centers]]. Batteries have much lower specific energy than fuels such as gasoline.

[[Benjamin Franklin]] first used the term "battery" in 1749 for linked Leyden jar capacitors. [[Alessandro Volta]] built the first electrochemical battery, the voltaic pile, in 1800: a stack of copper and zinc plates separated by brine-soaked paper disks. Volta did not realize that the voltage came from chemical reactions; [[Michael Faraday]] showed in 1834 that the associated corrosion was unavoidable. The Daniell cell, invented in 1836 by John Frederic Daniell, was the first practical source of electricity and powered telegraph networks. Dry cells, which replaced liquid electrolyte with a paste, made portable devices practical near the end of the 19th century.

Between 2010 and 2018, battery demand grew by 30% annually, reaching 180 GWh in 2018. Production involves mining of lithium, cobalt, and nickel, which can contaminate water sources and ecosystems.

=== KEY: Electric_charge_a68451
HEADWORD: Electric charge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coulomb | Coulomb's law | Electric field | Electron | Matter | Michael Faraday | Proton | Robert Millikan
ENTRY:
Physical property of [[Matter|matter]] that causes it to experience a force in an electromagnetic field. Charge is positive or negative: like charges repel and unlike charges attract. In ordinary matter, negative charge is carried by [[Electron|electrons]] and positive charge by the [[Proton|protons]] in atomic nuclei. Charge is quantized in integer multiples of the elementary charge, about 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, and in an isolated system the total charge does not change. Electric charges produce [[Electric field|electric fields]], and their interaction with the electromagnetic field is the source of the electromagnetic force. [[Coulomb's law]] makes that force proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The SI unit is the [[Coulomb|coulomb]] (C). [[Michael Faraday]] was the first to note the discrete nature of charge, in his electrolysis experiments, and [[Robert Millikan]]'s oil drop experiment measured the elementary charge.

=== KEY: Electric_current_1d557e
HEADWORD: Electric current
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ampere | Coulomb | Electric charge | Incandescent light bulb | Ion | Magnetic field | Semiconductor
ENTRY:
Flow of charged particles, such as electrons or [[Ion|ions]], through a conductor or space. It is defined as the net rate at which [[Electric charge|electric charge]] flows through a surface. In wires the charge carriers are usually electrons, in [[Semiconductor|semiconductors]] they can be electrons or holes, and in electrolytes they are ions. The SI unit is the [[Ampere|ampere]] (A), equal to one [[Coulomb|coulomb]] per second. Currents create [[Magnetic field|magnetic fields]] used in motors, generators, inductors and transformers. In ordinary conductors they cause Joule heating, which produces light in [[Incandescent light bulb|incandescent bulbs]]. By convention, current runs in the direction of positive charge, opposite to electron flow in wires.

=== KEY: Electric_field_64b665
HEADWORD: Electric field
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Chemical bond | Coulomb's law | Electric charge | Electromagnetic field | Michael Faraday
ENTRY:
Physical field that surrounds [[Electric charge|electrically charged]] particles and exerts attractive or repulsive forces on other charges. Two charges must be present for these forces to act. The force follows [[Coulomb's law]], growing with the magnitude of the charges and weakening with distance. Defined as the force per unit charge on a positive test charge at rest, the field is measured in volts per meter (V/m), equal to newtons per coulomb (N/C). Electric fields originate from electric charges and time-varying currents, and together with magnetic fields they form the [[Electromagnetic field|electromagnetic field]]. In atoms, the field between the [[Atomic nucleus|nucleus]] and electrons holds them together, and the interaction between atoms gives rise to [[Chemical bond|chemical bonding]]. [[Michael Faraday]] introduced field lines to visualize the field.

ENTRY:

=== KEY: Electric_motor_6dc9eb
HEADWORD: Electric motor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Lorentz force | Michael Faraday | Nikola Tesla
ENTRY:
Machine that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. Most electric motors work through the interaction between the motor's magnetic field and current in a wire winding, which generates [[Lorentz force]] as torque on the motor's shaft. An electric generator is mechanically identical but operates inversely.

Motors may be powered by direct current sources such as batteries or by alternating current sources such as a power grid, and may be brushed or brushless, single-phase or three-phase, air-cooled or liquid-cooled. They produce linear or rotary force and so are a type of actuator. The largest, used for marine propulsion, pipeline compression, and pumped storage, exceed 100 megawatts. Other uses include fans, pumps, machine tools, household appliances, power tools, vehicles, and disk drives. In regenerative braking, traction motors run in reverse as generators to recover energy otherwise lost as heat and friction.

A motor has two mechanical parts: the rotor, which moves, and the stator, which does not. Electrically it consists of field magnets, electromagnets or permanent magnets, and the armature, wire windings on a ferromagnetic core; together they form a magnetic circuit. The stator core is made of thin insulated laminations, which reduce losses from circulating eddy currents. In commutated motors, a commutator, a rotary switch contacted by carbon brushes, reverses the current in the rotor windings every half turn (180°) so the torque keeps one direction; such motors have been mostly replaced by brushless designs.

British scientist [[Michael Faraday]] constructed the first primitive electric motor in 1821 using a mercury bath and wire. Moritz Hermann von Jacobi created the first practical rotating motor in 1834, and Thomas Davenport patented a practical direct-current motor in 1837. [[Nikola Tesla]] patented the induction motor in 1888.

=== KEY: Electric_potential_3adf5f
HEADWORD: Electric potential
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coulomb | Electric field | Volt | Work (physics)
ENTRY:
Electric potential energy per unit charge at a point in an [[Electric field|electric field]], also called electrostatic potential. It is measured in joules per [[Coulomb|coulomb]], or [[Volt|volts]] (V), and is often denoted V or φ. It is the [[Work (physics)|work]] needed to move a test charge from a reference point to a specific point in a static field, normalized to unit charge. The reference point is conventionally the Earth or a point at infinity, where the potential is zero. The potential difference between two points is the work done per unit charge when a charge moves between them. In classical electrostatics the field is the gradient of the potential. In time-varying fields, the potential combines with the magnetic vector potential to describe the field.

=== KEY: Electric_power_distribution_b174b7
HEADWORD: Electric power distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circuit breaker | Power station | Transformer
ENTRY:
Final stage in the delivery of electricity, from the transmission system to individual consumers. Distribution substations lower transmission voltage to medium voltage, usually between 600 V and 35 kV, using [[Transformer|transformers]]. Primary distribution lines carry this power to distribution transformers near customers, which step it down to the utilization voltage used by lighting and appliances; in the United States, residential customers usually receive 120/240 V. Urban distribution is mainly underground, while rural distribution is mostly overhead on utility poles. Distribution became necessary in the 1880s, when electricity began to be generated at [[Power station|power stations]] rather than where it was used. Substations use [[Circuit breaker|circuit breakers]] and switches to disconnect parts of the network.

=== KEY: Electric_power_transmission_11782e
HEADWORD: Electric power transmission
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Direct current | Electrical grid
ENTRY:
Bulk movement of electrical energy from a generating site, such as a power plant, to an electrical substation, carried by transmission lines. Lines carry either [[Alternating current|alternating current]] (AC) or [[Direct current|direct current]] (DC). Voltage is raised for long-distance transmission and reduced for local distribution, because higher voltages mean lower currents and lower losses. Most North American lines are three-phase AC, while high-voltage direct current (HVDC) is used for long distances, submarine cables and links between grids that are not synchronized. Underground transmission costs more to install but lowers maintenance costs. Interconnected lines form networks that offer redundant routes; the U.S. Northeast experienced blackouts in 1965, 1977 and 2003. The combined transmission and distribution network is known as the [[Electrical grid|electrical grid]].

=== KEY: Electrical_engineering_e0673f
HEADWORD: Electrical engineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Hans Christian Ørsted | James Clerk Maxwell | Michael Faraday | Thomas Edison
ENTRY:
Engineering discipline concerned with the study, design, and application of equipment, devices, and systems that use electricity, electronics, and electromagnetism. It emerged as an identifiable occupation in the latter half of the 19th century, after the commercialization of the electric telegraph, the telephone, and electrical power generation, distribution, and use. Engineers typically hold a degree in the subject and may belong to professional bodies such as the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE).

The field encompasses computer engineering, power engineering, telecommunications, radio-frequency engineering, signal processing, instrumentation, control engineering, photovoltaic cells, electronics, and optics and photonics. Many of these overlap with other branches of engineering. Machine learning and computer science were historically developed as fields of electrical engineering.

William Gilbert drew the first clear distinction between magnetism and static electricity. By 1800 [[Alessandro Volta]] had developed the voltaic pile. In 1820 [[Hans Christian Ørsted]] discovered that an electric current produces a magnetic field; Georg Ohm quantified the relation between current and potential difference in 1827; [[Michael Faraday]] discovered electromagnetic induction in 1831; Joseph Henry and Edward Davy invented the electrical relay in 1835; and [[James Clerk Maxwell]] published a unified theory of electricity and magnetism in 1873. Electrical telegraphy may be considered the first example of electrical engineering. By the late 19th century practitioners had built a global telegraph network, and the first professional institutions were founded in the U.K. and the U.S.

An international conference in Chicago in 1893 standardized the units volt, ampere, coulomb, ohm, farad, and henry. The Technische Universität Darmstadt founded the world's first department of electrical engineering in 1882 and introduced the first degree course in 1883. [[Thomas Edison]] opened a commercial power plant in lower Manhattan in 1882, serving 59 customers.

=== KEY: Electrical_grid_7d012d
HEADWORD: Electrical grid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Electric power distribution | Electric power transmission | Power station | Voltage
ENTRY:
Interconnected network for delivering electricity from producers to consumers. Grids consist of [[Power station|power stations]], substations that step [[Voltage|voltage]] up or down, [[Electric power transmission|transmission]] lines over long distances, and [[Electric power distribution|distribution]] networks to customers. Power stations are typically built close to energy sources and far from densely populated areas. Grids are nearly always synchronous, with three-phase [[Alternating current|alternating current]] frequencies synchronized. Grids range from microgrids, which can disconnect and operate autonomously during outages, to wide-area synchronous grids covering whole countries or continents. North America has four major interconnections, and most of continental Europe is served by one grid. About 840 million people, mostly in Africa, had no access to grid electricity in 2017, down from 1.2 billion in 2010.

=== KEY: Electrical_impedance_1fc76e
HEADWORD: Electrical impedance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Capacitance | Inductance | Ohm | Siemens (unit)
ENTRY:
Opposition of a circuit to [[Alternating current|alternating current]], combining resistance and reactance. Impedance is a complex quantity, usually denoted Z and measured in [[Ohm|ohms]] (Ω), the same unit as resistance. It is the ratio of the complex voltage across a two-terminal element to the complex current through it, and it generally depends on the frequency of the sinusoidal voltage. Resistance forms the real part of impedance, while reactance, arising from [[Inductance|inductance]] and [[Capacitance|capacitance]], forms the imaginary part. Impedance allows AC networks to be analyzed with a linear law, and its reciprocal, admittance, is measured in [[Siemens (unit)|siemens]]. The term was coined by Oliver Heaviside in July 1886.

=== KEY: Electrical_network_0c0172
HEADWORD: Electrical network
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Resistor | Transistor
ENTRY:
Interconnection of electrical components, or a model of one, built from elements such as voltage sources, current sources, resistances, inductances and capacitances. A circuit is a network with a closed loop that gives current a return path; networks without a closed loop are often called open circuits. A resistive network contains only [[Resistor|resistors]] and ideal current and voltage sources, and is simpler to analyze than a network containing capacitors and inductors. A network containing active electronic components, such as [[Transistor|transistors]], is an electronic circuit, which is generally nonlinear and needs more complex design tools. Analysis applies Kirchhoff's current law, under which the currents at any node sum to zero, and Kirchhoff's voltage law, under which the voltages around any closed loop sum to zero. Theorems such as superposition, Thévenin's theorem and Norton's theorem simplify linear networks.
