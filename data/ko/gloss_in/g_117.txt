=== KEY: Rule_of_law_45b076

ENTRY:
Political and legal principle holding that law is clear, consistent and open, that people have access to justice, and that government institutions are subject to the law. The principle is often summarized as "no one is above the law" or "all are equal before the law." Legal scholars stress that laws must apply equally to everyone; "formalists" add that they must be stable, accessible and clear, while "substantivists" include rights such as [[Human rights|human rights]] and compliance with [[International law|international law]]. === KEY: Rumi_2251ef
HEADWORD: Rumi
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Baghdad | Byzantine Empire | Persian literature | Quran | Tajikistan
ENTRY:
Persian-language Sufi mystic and poet, founder of the Mevlevi Order.

Jalāl al-Dīn Muḥammad Balkhī, known as Rumi, came from a family of Balkh and was born to Persian parents in Wakhsh, in present-day [[Tajikistan]]. His name means "from Rum", the Anatolian region once held by the [[Byzantine Empire]]; in Iran he is also called Mawlānā, "our master". Mongol advances forced the family west, and after stays in cities across Iran, [[Baghdad]], and Damascus, Rumi settled in Konya, in the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum, at age 19.

Rumi was expected to follow his father as an Islamic scholar, but the arrival in Konya of the wandering dervish Shams Tabrīzī changed his life. The two became deeply attached, and Rumi neglected his duties. When Shams disappeared, Rumi's intense grief found expression in the *Divan of Shams Tabrīzī*, which marked the beginning of his poetic output.

His six-volume *Masnavi*, of over 25,000 rhyming couplets, is often called a "Qur'an in Persian" and is considered one of the greatest poems of the [[Persian literature|Persian language]]. Many Muslims rank it among the most important works of Islamic literature, behind only the [[Quran]]. His teachings present adversity, pain, and loss as paths to spiritual growth.

Rumi died in Konya and was buried beside his father, and his shrine became a place of pilgrimage. After his death his followers and his son Sultan Walad founded the Mevlevi Order, known as the Whirling Dervishes for its *Sama* ceremony. His poems have been translated into many languages, and he has been described as the "world's most popular poet" and, in recent years, as the best-selling poet in the United States.

Hoofed herbivorous grazing or browsing mammal of the suborder Ruminantia. Ruminants digest plant fibers such as [[Cellulose|cellulose]] by fermenting them in a specialized stomach, principally through microbial action, a process called foregut fermentation. The fermented ingesta, known as cud, is regurgitated and chewed again in a process called rumination. The word comes from Latin rūminārī, "to ruminate," from rūmen, the first stomach.

=== KEY: Russell_s_paradox_9b7cf1
HEADWORD: Russell's paradox
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bertrand Russell | Gottlob Frege
ENTRY:
Set-theoretic paradox published by the British philosopher and mathematician [[Bertrand Russell]] in 1901. It shows that every set theory with an unrestricted comprehension principle leads to contradictions. Let R be the set of all sets that are not members of themselves. If R is not a member of itself, then by definition it is a member of itself; if it is, then by definition it is not. Russell also showed that a version of the paradox could be derived in the axiomatic system of the German philosopher and mathematician [[Gottlob Frege]], undermining Frege's attempt to reduce mathematics to logic. Two influential ways of avoiding it were proposed in 1908: Russell's own type theory and Zermelo set theory, which restricted the unlimited comprehension principle. Ernst Zermelo had discovered the paradox independently by 1902, but he did not publish it.

Final phase of the Russian monarchy, spanning most of northern Eurasia from 1721 until the proclamation of the Russian Republic in 1917. Autocephalous Eastern Orthodox church, also officially known as the Moscow Patriarchate. First Russian Revolution, a wave of political and social unrest in the Russian Empire beginning in 1905. === KEY: Russian_language_1c01d4
HEADWORD: Russian language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cyrillic script | Mikhail Lomonosov
ENTRY:
East Slavic language of the Balto-Slavic branch of the Indo-European family.

Russian is the native language of the Russian people and one of four extant East Slavic languages, alongside Belarusian, Rusyn, and Ukrainian. It was the de facto official language of the Soviet Union and remains official in Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan, and Kyrgyzstan. It is also a lingua franca in Ukraine, Moldova, the Caucasus, and Central Asia. It has over 210 million total speakers. It is the most spoken native language in Europe, the most spoken Slavic language, and the most geographically widespread language of Eurasia; it ranks seventh worldwide by native speakers. Russian is one of the six official languages of the UN and one of two on the International Space Station.

It is written in the Russian alphabet of the [[Cyrillic script]]. Most consonants have "hard" and "soft" counterparts, a distinction usually shown in writing by changing the following vowel. Unstressed vowels are reduced, and stress, often unpredictable, is not normally marked, though an optional acute accent can distinguish *замо́к* ("lock") from *за́мок* ("castle"). Russian is a fusional language, in which one inflectional ending marks several grammatical features, and it uses prefixes and suffixes for word formation more than most Slavic languages.

Church Slavonic, used as a literary language in the medieval period, gave Russian many borrowings and calques ultimately from Greek. Vocabulary was also influenced by Polish, German, French, and English. It became a standardized literary medium in the 18th century through reforms by figures such as [[Mikhail Lomonosov]].

=== KEY: Rutherford_scattering_experiments_60145a
HEADWORD: Rutherford scattering experiments
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alpha particle | Bohr model | Ernest Rutherford | J. J. Thomson
ENTRY:
Landmark experiments that showed atoms have a dense, positively charged nucleus. Performed from 1906 to 1913 at the University of Manchester by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden under [[Ernest Rutherford]], they fired [[Alpha particle|alpha particles]] at thin metal foil and measured how the beams scattered. The results showed that all of an atom's positive charge and most of its mass are concentrated in a central nucleus. [[J. J. Thomson|Thomson]]'s earlier model had spread positive charge through a sphere, but the data fit single scattering from a compact central charge. Rutherford explained the effect in a 1911 paper, which led to the widespread use of scattering to study subatomic matter. The paper also led to the planetary Rutherford model of the atom and, eventually, the [[Bohr model]].

=== KEY: Sadness_b9197e
HEADWORD: Sadness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 
ENTRY:
Emotional pain associated with feelings of loss, despair, grief, helplessness, disappointment, and sorrow.

A person experiencing sadness may become quiet or lethargic and withdraw from others; crying can be an indication. Severe sadness is exemplified by depression, a mood that can result from major depressive disorder or persistent depressive disorder, though normal sadness is temporary and differs from clinical depression. Sadness is one of the six basic emotions described by Paul Ekman, along with happiness, anger, surprise, fear, and disgust.

Functional theories hold that sadness serves two primary purposes in coping with loss. One is to promote cognitive changes that restructure beliefs and goals. The other is to signal a need for assistance and elicit support from others, and shared sadness may increase relationship building. Brain studies associate sadness with activity in the temporal cortex, midbrain, putamen, and caudate.

In childhood, sadness is a common experience and part of separating from the mother and becoming independent. Robin Skynner argued that treating sadness as "not allowed" can leave people shallow and manic, and T. Berry Brazelton argued that too much cheering up devalues the emotion.

Coping methods include seeking social support, spending time with a pet, and creating a list. Strategies such as ruminating or permanent isolation can prolong sadness. Cognitive behavioral therapy suggests challenging negative thoughts or scheduling positive events. Pupil size is also an indicator: sad faces with small pupils are judged more intensely sad, and viewers' own pupils shrink in response, a mirroring that predicts higher empathy scores.

=== KEY: Sahara_15fe3f
HEADWORD: Sahara
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Köppen climate classification | Mediterranean Sea | Monsoon | Nouakchott | Red Sea | Sahel | Timbuktu
ENTRY:
Largest hot desert in the world, spanning North Africa. With an area of 9,200,000 sq km, it is the third-largest desert overall, smaller only than the deserts of Antarctica and the northern Arctic. It extends from the [[Red Sea]] in the east and the [[Mediterranean Sea]] in the north to the [[Atlantic Ocean]] in the west, and is bounded on the south by the [[Sahel]], a belt of semi-arid tropical savanna. It covers large parts of Algeria, Chad, Egypt, Libya, Mali, Mauritania, Niger, Western Sahara, and Sudan.

The terrain includes rocky plateaus (hamada), sand seas (ergs) with dunes rising over 180 m, gravel plains (reg), dry valleys (wadi), dry lakes, and salt flats. Mountain massifs, many volcanic, include the Aïr, Ahaggar, and Tibesti ranges; the highest peak is Emi Koussi, a shield volcano in northern Chad. Important towns include [[Nouakchott]] and [[Timbuktu]].

The desert lies under the subtropical ridge, a belt of high pressure whose descending dry air prevents cloud formation and produces a hot desert climate ([[Köppen climate classification]] BWh). The Libyan Desert is the sunniest and driest part. For several hundred thousand years the region has alternated between desert and savanna in a 20,000-year cycle, driven by the precession of Earth's axis (about 26,000 years), which shifts the North African [[Monsoon|monsoon]].

Population is sparse, estimated at around 2.5 million, mostly nomadic pastoralists such as the Tuareg and Berbers. The name derives from the Arabic *ṣaḥrā'*, "desert."
