=== KEY: Meteor_shower_20f9e0
HEADWORD: Meteor shower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comet | Meteoroid | Outer space
ENTRY:
Celestial event of meteors seemingly radiating from one point in the night sky, caused by streams of cosmic debris. Meteor showers occur when [[Meteoroid|meteoroids]] enter Earth's atmosphere at high speed on parallel trajectories. Most meteors are smaller than a grain of sand and disintegrate before reaching the ground. The Meteor Data Centre lists over 900 suspected showers, about 100 of them well established. Very intense showers are called meteor storms, producing at least 1,000 meteors an hour, most notably the Leonids. In 1794, Ernst Chladni proposed that meteors originated in [[Outer space|outer space]]. In 1866, Giovanni Schiaparelli showed that the Leonids shared an orbit with Comet Tempel, linking meteor showers to [[Comet|comets]].

=== KEY: Meteorite_157585
HEADWORD: Meteorite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid belt | Mars | Moon | Tunguska event
ENTRY:
Rock from outer space, fallen to the surface of a planet or moon. Meteorites are traditionally divided into stony meteorites, mainly of silicate minerals; iron meteorites, largely of ferronickel; and stony-iron meteorites, which contain both metallic and rocky material. Meteorites recovered after being observed during their fall are called falls, and all others are finds. Most originate from a handful of asteroid breakup events in the [[Asteroid belt|asteroid belt]]. Iron meteoroids transit the atmosphere most easily and cause most of the hypervelocity impact craters on Earth. The [[Tunguska event]] probably resulted from a stony or icy body that disintegrated in the atmosphere without leaving a crater. Meteorites have also been found on the [[Moon]] and [[Mars]].

=== KEY: Meteoroid_49ab9d
HEADWORD: Meteoroid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid belt | Comet | Mars | Meteor shower | Moon
ENTRY:
Small body in outer space, ranging from grains up to one meter wide and far smaller than an asteroid. Most meteoroids come from the [[Asteroid belt|asteroid belt]], perturbed by planetary gravity, while others are particles from [[Comet|comets]] that give rise to [[Meteor shower|meteor showers]]. Some are impact debris ejected from the [[Moon]] or [[Mars]]. An estimated 25 million meteoroids, micrometeoroids, and other space debris enter Earth's atmosphere each day, which results in an estimated 15,000 metric tons (16,535 short tons) entering each year. Stony meteoroids that contain grain-like inclusions are called chondrites. In 2017 the International Astronomical Union revised the definition to objects between 30 μm and one meter in diameter.

=== KEY: Meteorology_e41faa
HEADWORD: Meteorology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Butterfly effect | Climatology
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the Earth's atmosphere and short-term weather, focusing on forecasting. Its applications include the military, aviation, energy production, transport, agriculture, construction, and disaster management. Together with [[Climatology|climatology]], atmospheric physics, and atmospheric chemistry, it forms the broader atmospheric sciences. Meteorologists use instruments such as thermometers, barometers, weather satellites, balloons, buoys, and radars to forecast weather at local, regional, and global scales. Ancient civilizations relied on folklore and astrology, and [[Aristotle]]'s treatise *Meteorology* summarized early observations. Numerical weather prediction in the 20th century, coupled with computers, transformed forecasting. Because the atmosphere is chaotic, uncertainty remains, a limit described by the [[Butterfly effect|butterfly effect]].

=== KEY: Methane_4e14f7
HEADWORD: Methane
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alkane | Combustion | Greenhouse gas | Mars | Natural gas | Syngas
ENTRY:
Simplest [[Alkane|alkane]] hydrocarbon, CH₄, and the main constituent of [[Natural gas]]. Methane is an odorless, colorless gas at standard temperature and pressure, with one carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms in a tetrahedral shape. It boils at −161.5 °C at one atmosphere and is flammable at concentrations of 5.4% to 17% in air. Methane is transparent to visible light but absorbs infrared radiation, which makes it a [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]]. Its largest reservoir is beneath the seafloor as methane clathrates. Its primary reactions are [[Combustion]], steam reforming to [[Syngas]], and halogenation, and methane has been detected on [[Mars]]. Its heat of combustion is 55.5 MJ/kg.

=== KEY: Methanol_1242e0
HEADWORD: Methanol
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acetic acid | Alcohol (chemistry) | Ancient Egypt | Carbon monoxide | Formaldehyde | Robert Boyle
ENTRY:
Simplest [[Alcohol (chemistry)|aliphatic alcohol]], CH₃OH, also called methyl alcohol or wood alcohol. It is a light, volatile, colorless, and flammable liquid with an odor similar to ethanol, but it is more acutely toxic. Methanol was once made by the destructive distillation of wood, but it is now mainly produced by hydrogenating [[Carbon monoxide]]. More than 20 million tons are produced annually as a precursor to [[Formaldehyde]], [[Acetic acid|acetic acid]], and other commodity chemicals. [[Ancient Egypt|Ancient Egyptians]] used a mixture containing methanol in embalming, and [[Robert Boyle]] first isolated it in 1661 by distilling boxwood. Methanol is also abundant in star-forming regions of space, where astronomers use it as a marker.

=== KEY: Methodism_2a5ad3

Protestant Christian tradition derived from the life and teachings of [[John Wesley]]. === KEY: Methodology_2ba0c1
HEADWORD: Methodology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hypothesis | Qualitative research | Quantitative research | Scientific method | Social science
ENTRY:
Study of research methods, and the philosophical assumptions behind them. A method is a structured procedure for reaching a goal, such as acquiring knowledge or verifying knowledge claims. Methodologies are traditionally divided into [[Quantitative research|quantitative]] and [[Qualitative research|qualitative]] research. Quantitative research is the main methodology of the natural sciences, using precise numerical measurements to find universal laws. The dominant approach there is the [[Scientific method]], which includes observation, formulating a [[Hypothesis|hypothesis]], testing it by experiment, and publishing the findings. Qualitative research, more characteristic of the [[Social science|social sciences]], seeks an in-depth understanding of meaning and relies on methods such as surveys and interviews. In recent decades, many social scientists have adopted mixed-methods research, which combines both approaches. A few theorists reject methodology as a discipline, while methodologists argue that a good methodology helps researchers reach reliable theories efficiently.

Figure of speech in which a concept is referred to by the name of something associated with it. === KEY: Metre_0b65c8
HEADWORD: Meter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomical unit | Equator | International System of Units | Light | Light-year | North Pole
ENTRY:
Base unit of length in the [[International System of Units]] (SI); symbol m. Since 2019 it has been defined as the distance traveled by [[Light|light]] in a vacuum during 1/299,792,458 of a second, with the second fixed by the hyperfine transition frequency of cesium. The meter was first defined in 1791 by the French National Assembly as one ten-millionth of the distance from the [[Equator]] to the [[North Pole]] along a great circle through Paris, setting 10,000 km as a quarter of Earth's polar circumference. In 1799 it was tied to a prototype bar, and in 1960 to a number of wavelengths of a krypton-86 emission line. The current definition was adopted in 1983 and modified slightly in 2002. Long distances are usually given in km, [[Astronomical unit|astronomical units]], or [[Light-year|light-years]] rather than in larger metric multiples.

=== KEY: Metric_space_2c753a
HEADWORD: Metric space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Distance | Euclidean space | Geometry | Mathematical analysis | Set (mathematics) | Sphere | Vector space
ENTRY:
Mathematical [[Set (mathematics)|set]] with a notion of [[Distance|distance]] between its points. The distance is given by a function called a metric, which gives zero distance from a point to itself, positive distance between distinct points, symmetry, and the triangle inequality. Metric spaces provide a general setting for studying concepts of [[Mathematical analysis|mathematical analysis]] and [[Geometry|geometry]], such as balls and completeness. The most familiar example is three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]], and a [[Sphere|sphere]] with angular distance is another. Metrics can also express nonphysical distance: the Hamming distance between 100-character Unicode strings counts the characters that must change. Riemannian manifolds, normed [[Vector space|vector spaces]], and graphs can all be viewed as metric spaces, and the p-adic numbers are the completion of the rational numbers under a certain metric.

=== KEY: Metric_system_ecc149
HEADWORD: Metric system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Celsius | Decimal | International System of Units | Kilogram | Metre | Newton (unit) | Second
ENTRY:
System of measurement built on standard base units and [[Decimal|decimal]] prefixes such as kilo and milli. The [[International System of Units]] (SI) is the main example, and other metric systems include the centimeter–gram–second system and the gravitational metric system. The SI has seven base units: the [[Metre|meter]], [[Kilogram|kilogram]], [[Second|second]], ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela. Derived units include the hertz and the [[Newton (unit)|newton]], and degrees [[Celsius]] are a shifted scale. Today all base units are defined by physical constants rather than physical prototypes. The SI is the official system of weights and measures in almost all countries, but the United States has resisted full adoption and continues to use other systems. Adopting the metric system is called metrication.

=== KEY: Metrology_cbda77
HEADWORD: Metrology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cubit | French Revolution | Granite | International System of Units | Measurement | Metric system
ENTRY:
Science of [[Measurement|measurement]] and its application. It establishes common units and is divided into three overlapping activities: defining units, realizing them in practice, and traceability, which links practical measurements to reference standards. Its sub-fields are scientific or fundamental metrology, which establishes units; applied or industrial metrology, which applies measurement to manufacturing and other processes; and legal metrology, which regulates measuring instruments and methods. Modern metrology grew out of the [[French Revolution]], when a length standard based on a natural source was proposed, leading to the decimal-based [[Metric system|metric system]] in 1795. The first permanent standard on record is the royal Egyptian [[Cubit|cubit]], carved from black [[Granite|granite]] in 2900 BC. The [[International System of Units]] (SI) evolved from the metric system after a 1960 resolution at the 11th General Conference on Weights and Measures.

=== KEY: Mexican_American_War_0e543e
HEADWORD: Mexican–American War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Civil War | James K. Polk | Mexico City | Rio Grande | Slavery in the United States | Texas
ENTRY:
1846–1848 invasion of Mexico by the United States. The war followed the 1845 American annexation of [[Texas]], which Mexico still considered its territory. The boundary dispute centered on the [[Rio Grande]], which the United States and Texas claimed, while Mexico claimed the more northerly Nueces River. President [[James K. Polk]] offered $25 million for the disputed territory and California, but Mexico refused. After Mexican forces repelled U.S. troops on April 25, 1846, Polk persuaded Congress to declare war. U.S. forces captured [[Mexico City]] in September 1847. The 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo ended the war: Mexico gave up present-day California, Nevada, and Utah and parts of Arizona, Colorado, New Mexico, and Wyoming, and relinquished its claims to Texas. The U.S. agreed to pay $15 million for war damages. The new acquisitions intensified the debate over [[Slavery in the United States|slavery]], and some scholars see the war as leading to the [[American Civil War]].

=== KEY: Mexican_Revolution_ce7e98
HEADWORD: Mexican Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Civil war | Emiliano Zapata | Mexico | Pancho Villa | Porfirio Díaz | Secularism
ENTRY:
Nationwide armed struggle in [[Mexico]], 1910–1920. Primarily a [[Civil war|civil war]], the Revolution drew in foreign powers with economic interests in Mexico, particularly the United States. It led to the deaths of around one million people, mostly noncombatants. The conflict began when Francisco I. Madero challenged [[Porfirio Díaz]] in the 1910 presidential election, was jailed, and called for an armed uprising. Díaz resigned in May 1911. [[Emiliano Zapata]] rebelled in Morelos, demanding agrarian reform. In February 1913 generals staged a coup, and Madero was forced to resign and then assassinated on the orders of Victoriano Huerta. By 1915 the Constitutionalist faction under Venustiano Carranza had defeated [[Pancho Villa]]. The Constitution of 1917 established universal male suffrage, workers' rights, land reform, and [[Secularism|secularism]]. From 1920 to 1940 revolutionary generals held the presidency, and the party they founded in 1929 ruled Mexico until the 2000 presidential election.

=== KEY: Mexican_War_of_Independence_166a09
HEADWORD: Mexican War of Independence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mexico City | Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla | Napoleon | New Spain
ENTRY:
Armed conflict, 1810–1821, that ended Spanish rule of [[New Spain]]. It began with the Cry of Dolores on September 16, 1810, when priest [[Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla]] rallied an initially disorganized revolt. Hidalgo was captured and executed in 1811, and José María Morelos led a second phase until his execution in 1815. The insurgency then became guerrilla warfare under Vicente Guerrero, and a military stalemate lasted until 1821. [[Napoleon]]'s 1808 invasion of Spain had undermined royal legitimacy, and in 1820 Spanish liberals overthrew Ferdinand VII, prompting Mexican conservatives to seek independence. Former royalist Agustín de Iturbide allied with Guerrero under the Plan of Iguala. The combined army entered [[Mexico City]] in September 1821, and the viceroy Juan O'Donojú signed the Treaty of Córdoba. The ephemeral First Mexican Empire was overthrown, and a federal republic was declared in 1823.

=== KEY: Mexican_cuisine_eda37d
HEADWORD: Mexican cuisine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztec Empire | Maize | Mexico | Slavery | Teotihuacan | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Culinary traditions of [[Mexico]], rooted in Mesoamerican food practices. Its ingredients and methods arise from early farming communities such as the Olmec and Maya, who domesticated [[Maize|maize]] and developed nixtamalization, the process of treating corn with lime. Successive groups, including the [[Teotihuacan|Teotihuacanos]], added their own cooking methods. The Mexica later formed the multi-ethnic Triple Alliance, the [[Aztec Empire]], and its foodways became infused into the cuisine. Native staples include corn, turkey, beans, squash, avocados, tomatoes, cacao, vanilla, agave, and chili peppers. After the Spanish conquest, Spaniards introduced beef, pork, chicken, cheese, rice, sugar, and olive oil. African influence came through [[Slavery|slavery]] in New Spain. Traditional Mexican cuisine was inscribed in 2010 on the [[UNESCO]] Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

=== KEY: Mexico_8dbb07
HEADWORD: Mexico
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztec Empire | Benito Juárez | Mesoamerica | Mexican Revolution | Mexican War of Independence | Mexico City | New Spain | Porfirio Díaz
ENTRY:
Country in North America (pop., 2025: c. 131 million), officially the United Mexican States, with its capital at [[Mexico City]]. It is the northernmost country in Latin America, bordering the United States to the north and Guatemala and Belize to the southeast. It covers 1,972,550 sq km and has the world's largest number of native Spanish speakers.

Human presence dates back to at least 11,500 BC. [[Mesoamerica]] was home to societies including the Olmecs, Maya, Zapotecs, and Purépecha. Spanish colonization began in 1521, when an indigenous alliance defeated the [[Aztec Empire]], establishing the colony of [[New Spain]]. The 1810–1821 [[Mexican War of Independence]] ended Spanish rule. In 1848 Mexico lost nearly half its territory in the Mexican–American War. The Second Mexican Empire under Maximilian I was overthrown by Republican forces led by [[Benito Juárez]]. The long dictatorship of [[Porfirio Díaz]] was ended by the 1910–1920 [[Mexican Revolution]], which produced the 1917 Constitution. The North American Free Trade Agreement took effect in 1994.

Mexico is a federal republic with a presidential system and a separation of powers into executive, legislative, and judicial branches. The bicameral Congress of the Union comprises the Chamber of Deputies and the Senate, and the country has 32 states.

It has the world's 13th-largest economy by nominal GDP and by purchasing power parity. It ranks first in the Americas and seventh in the world by number of UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and is one of 17 megadiverse countries. It received 42.2 million international arrivals in 2022. Poverty, corruption, and crime remain widespread; since 2006, approximately 127,000 deaths have been attributed to conflict between drug trafficking syndicates.

=== KEY: Mexico_City_0b4596
HEADWORD: Mexico City
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander von Humboldt | Mexico | New Spain | Same-sex marriage | Tenochtitlan
ENTRY:
Capital and largest city of [[Mexico]] (pop., 2020: 9,209,944), the most populous city in North America. It lies in the Valley of Mexico on the high Mexican Central Plateau at an altitude of 2,240 m and is divided into 16 boroughs, or *alcaldías*. The city proper covers 1,495 sq km and is the second-largest Spanish-speaking city proper in the world after Lima. Greater Mexico City, with 21,804,515 people, is the world's 15th-largest metropolitan area. In 2011 it had a GDP of $411 billion; the city accounted for 15.8% of Mexico's GDP and the metropolitan area for about 22%.

It is the oldest capital city in the Americas and one of only two founded by Indigenous peoples. It began as [[Tenochtitlan]], a Mexica city built around 1325 on islands in Lake Texcoco. After the 1521 siege it was nearly destroyed and rebuilt to Spanish urban standards, and during the colonial period it was a major political, administrative, and financial center, the capital of [[New Spain]]. The surrounding territory was organized in 1824 as the country's only federal district, the Distrito Federal.

Residents won the right to elect the head of government and the unicameral Legislative Assembly in 1997; since then left-wing parties, first the Party of the Democratic Revolution and later Morena, have controlled both offices. === KEY: Michael_Faraday_173c69
HEADWORD: Faraday, Michael
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Benzene | Charles Lyell | Electromagnetic field | Electromagnetic induction | Electromagnetism | Humphry Davy | James Clerk Maxwell | Surrey
ENTRY:
English chemist and physicist who contributed vastly to electrochemistry and [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]]. With little formal education, he became as an autodidact one of the most influential scientists in history. Born in Newington Butts, [[Surrey]], to a poor family, he apprenticed at 14 to a bookbinder and bookseller. In 1813 [[Humphry Davy]] appointed him Chemical Assistant at the Royal Institution, where he became its first Fullerian Professor of Chemistry, a lifetime position.

His research on the magnetic field around a conductor carrying a direct current established the concept of the [[Electromagnetic field|electromagnetic field]] in physics. He discovered the principles of [[Electromagnetic induction|electromagnetic induction]], diamagnetism, and the laws of electrolysis, and established that magnetism could affect rays of light. His inventions of electromagnetic rotary devices formed the foundation of electric motor technology. As a chemist he discovered [[Benzene|benzene]] and tetrachloroethylene, invented an early form of the Bunsen burner and the system of oxidation numbers, and popularized terms such as "anode," "cathode," "electrode," and "ion."

He conveyed his ideas in clear, simple language and thought in a visual manner; his mathematics did not extend beyond the simplest algebra. [[James Clerk Maxwell]] summarized his work and that of others in equations accepted as the basis of all modern theories of electromagnetic phenomena. He worked on lighthouses and protecting ships from corrosion, and with [[Charles Lyell]] investigated a colliery explosion at Haswell, showing for the first time that coal dust contributed to its severity. He refused to work on chemical weapons for the Crimean War, citing ethical reservations. He founded the Royal Institution's Friday Evening Discourses and popularized its Christmas Lecture Series. The SI unit of capacitance, the farad, is named after him.

=== KEY: Michelson_Morley_experiment_67a471
HEADWORD: Michelson–Morley experiment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert A. Michelson | Cleveland | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Experiment in 1887 to detect Earth's motion through the luminiferous aether, a medium thought to carry light waves. [[Albert A. Michelson]] and Edward W. Morley compared the speed of light in perpendicular directions at what is now Case Western Reserve University in [[Cleveland]], Ohio. They found no significant difference, a negative result generally considered the first strong evidence against some aether theories. The experiment initiated research that led to [[Special relativity]], which rules out motion against an aether. Later experiments with greater sensitivity found no aether wind; in 2009 optical resonator experiments confirmed its absence at the 10⁻¹⁷ level. Michelson–Morley type experiments were repeated many times with steadily increasing sensitivity and, together with the Ives–Stilwell and Kennedy–Thorndike experiments, form one of the fundamental tests of special relativity.

=== KEY: Microbial_cyst_21cbf0
HEADWORD: Microbial cyst
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Microorganism
ENTRY:
Resting or dormant stage of a [[Microorganism|microorganism]], marked by suspended metabolism. Encystment, the process of cyst formation, can help a microbe disperse or survive unfavorable conditions such as lack of nutrients or oxygen, extreme temperatures, desiccation, adverse pH and toxic chemicals. Many groups of single-celled microbes possess this ability. Cysts can also be sites for nuclear reorganization and cell division. In parasitic species the cyst is often the infectious stage between hosts. When conditions become favorable, the cyst wall breaks down in a process called excystation. The idea that microbes could enter such a state was first described in 1702 by [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]], who studied rotifers. In 1872 Wilhelm Preyer introduced the term *anabiosis*, meaning return to life.

=== KEY: Microbiology_674e10
HEADWORD: Microbiology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Louis Pasteur | Microorganism | Mycology | Protist | Robert Hooke | Robert Koch
ENTRY:
Scientific study of [[Microorganism|microorganisms]], which may be unicellular, multicellular or acellular. The field includes virology, bacteriology, [[Protist|protistology]], [[Mycology]], immunology and parasitology. Organisms are classified as prokaryotes or eukaryotes. Microbiologists traditionally relied on culture, staining and microscopy, but less than 1% of the microorganisms in common environments can be cultured in isolation with current means. === KEY: Microeconomics_1d219f
HEADWORD: Microeconomics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Goods | Inflation | Macroeconomics | Market failure | Supply and demand | Tax | Unemployment
ENTRY:
Branch of economics concerned with how individuals and firms make decisions about allocating scarce resources, and with the interactions among them. It focuses on individual markets, sectors, or industries, as opposed to the economy as a whole, which is studied in [[Macroeconomics]]. Macroeconomics addresses growth, [[Inflation]], and [[Unemployment]] and national policies related to them, whereas microeconomics also examines the effects of policies such as changes in [[Tax]] levels on individual behavior.

One goal of the field is to analyze the market mechanisms that establish relative prices among [[Goods]] and services and allocate limited resources among alternative uses. Historically it has been pursued through general equilibrium theory, developed by Léon Walras in *Elements of Pure Economics* (1874), and partial equilibrium theory, introduced by Alfred Marshall in *Principles of Economics* (1890). Its formal name as a distinct field emerged in the 1930s.

Theory typically begins with a single rational, utility-maximizing individual, whose rationality means stable preferences that are both complete and transitive. The utility maximization problem, a constrained optimization in which the individual maximizes utility subject to a budget constraint, is the heart of consumer theory; its solution is called a Walrasian demand function. An alternative approach, revealed preference theory, takes consumer choice as primitive.

The theory of [[Supply and demand]] usually assumes perfectly competitive markets, with many buyers and sellers, none able to influence prices significantly. Where the assumption fails, as under monopoly, more sophisticated analysis is needed. Microeconomics also analyzes [[Market failure]], in which markets produce inefficient outcomes, as with public goods. Critics question assumptions of perfect information and rationality, citing behavioral findings such as loss aversion. Much modern macroeconomic theory is built on microfoundations, especially after the Lucas critique.

Microscopic organism, either single-celled or a colony of cells; also called a microbe. Microorganisms are extremely diverse and represent most unicellular organisms in all three domains of life. === KEY: Microphone_2cc918

=== KEY: Microscope_97e0ff
HEADWORD: Microscope
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Electron microscope | Galileo Galilei | Lens | Light | Optical microscope | Red blood cell | Robert Hooke
ENTRY:
Laboratory instrument used to examine objects too small to be seen by the naked eye. The science of investigating small structures with one is called microscopy. Microscopes may be grouped by how they interact with a sample: by sending light or electrons through or onto it, by detecting photon emissions from it, or by scanning its surface with a probe. The most common, and first invented, is the [[Optical microscope]], which uses [[Lens|lenses]] to refract visible [[Light|light]] to form an observable image. Other major types are the fluorescence, [[Electron microscope|electron]], and scanning probe microscopes.

Simple microscopes date to the widespread use of lenses in eyeglasses in the 13th century. Compound microscopes, which combine an objective lens near the specimen with an eyepiece, appeared in Europe around 1620. The inventor is unknown, though Dutch spectacle makers and Cornelis Drebbel have been credited. [[Galileo Galilei]] built his own improved version after seeing Drebbel's instrument in Rome in 1624, and the name *microscopio* was coined by Giovanni Faber in 1625.

Microscopes were largely a novelty until the 1660s and 1670s, when naturalists began using them to study biology. [[Robert Hooke]]'s *Micrographia* (1665) had a huge impact. [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] reached up to 300 times magnification with a single ball lens, rediscovered [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]], and reported micro-organisms on October 9, 1676. In 1893 August Köhler developed Köhler illumination, central to reaching the resolution limits of the light microscope. Frits Zernike's phase contrast (1953) and Georges Nomarski's differential interference contrast (1955) allowed imaging of unstained, transparent samples.

Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll built the first prototype electron microscope, a transmission type, in 1931. It uses electrons instead of light and electromagnets in place of glass lenses, allowing much higher resolution.

=== KEY: Mid_Atlantic_Ridge_643af2
HEADWORD: Mid-Atlantic Ridge
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Mid-ocean ridge]] along the floor of the [[Atlantic Ocean]], a divergent tectonic plate boundary. The ridge is part of the longest mountain range in the world. It separates North America from Eurasia and Africa north of the [[Azores]], and separates Africa from South America to the south. It runs from near Greenland to [[Bouvet Island]]. Mostly underwater, it rises above sea level in [[Iceland]]. The ridge spreads at an average rate of about 2.5 cm (1 in) per year. Matthew Fontaine Maury first inferred it in 1853. Research on the global ridge system supported [[Alfred Wegener]]'s theory of continental drift, which led to seafloor spreading and [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. A deep rift valley runs along its axis, where magma erupts as lava to form new crust. Near the equator the Romanche Trench divides the ridge into North and South Atlantic ridges.

=== KEY: Mid_ocean_ridge_4ef824

ENTRY:
Underwater mountain system formed by [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonic]] spreading. A mid-ocean ridge is where seafloor spreading occurs at a divergent plate boundary. Mantle material rises as [[Magma|magma]] at the linear weakness between separating plates and emerges as [[Lava|lava]], creating new oceanic crust and [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]] on cooling. A ridge typically lies at a depth of about 2,600 meters (8,500 ft) and rises about 2,000 meters (6,600 ft) above the deepest part of an ocean basin. [[Hydrothermal vent|Hydrothermal vents]] along the ridges support ecosystems that do not depend on sunlight.

=== KEY: Middle_Ages_c7d665
HEADWORD: Middle Ages
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black Death | Classical antiquity | Crusades | Fall of the Western Roman Empire | Feudalism | Petrarch | Thomas Aquinas | Umayyad Caliphate
ENTRY:
Period of European history lasting approximately from the 5th to the late 15th centuries, also called the medieval period. It is the middle of the three traditional divisions of Western history, between [[Classical antiquity]] and the modern period, and is itself subdivided into the Early, High, and Late Middle Ages.

The era began with the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire]] and migrations of Germanic peoples, who formed new kingdoms. In the 7th century North Africa and the Middle East, once Byzantine, came under the [[Umayyad Caliphate]]. The Franks under the Carolingians briefly built an empire that succumbed to civil wars and invasions by Vikings, Magyars, and Saracens.

During the High Middle Ages, beginning after 1000, population grew as technological and agricultural innovations allowed trade to flourish. Society was organized through manorialism and [[Feudalism]]. The East–West Schism of 1054 divided the Christian church, and the [[Crusades]], first preached in 1095, were attempts by Western Christians to regain the Holy Land from Muslims. Achievements near the period's end include the theology of [[Thomas Aquinas]], the paintings of Giotto, the poetry of Dante and Chaucer, and Gothic cathedrals such as Chartres.

The Late Middle Ages brought famine, plague, and war; between 1347 and 1350 the [[Black Death]] killed about a third of Europeans.

The term first appears in Latin in 1469 as *media tempestas*. [[Petrarch]] regarded the post-Roman centuries as "dark" compared with classical antiquity, a view some historians challenge, citing continuity in legal traditions, technology, and monastic preservation of classical texts. Christoph Cellarius made the ancient–medieval–modern division standard in the 17th century.

=== KEY: Middle_East_158b06
HEADWORD: Middle East
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abrahamic religions | Anatolia | Arab world | Arabs | Fertile Crescent | Mesopotamia | Persian Gulf
ENTRY:
Geopolitical region generally defined as all of Egypt and all of West Asia except the Caucasus. It roughly corresponds to what Western Europe historically called the Near East. It follows political borders rather than continental ones, so it includes East Thrace as part of Turkey. The term has grown prominent since the early 20th century, but its definition is vague and changing; related concepts such as "Middle East and North Africa" and "Greater Middle East" extend it to include most Muslim-majority countries.

Thirteen of its 18 countries belong to the [[Arab world]]. The most populous are Egypt, Iran, and Turkey, and Saudi Arabia is the largest by area. [[Arabs]] are the most widespread ethnic group, followed by Turks, Persians, Kurds, Jews, and Assyrians. Major geographical features include the Arabian Peninsula, [[Anatolia]], the Levant and [[Mesopotamia]] (which together form the [[Fertile Crescent]]), and the Iranian plateau.

Located at the intersection of Africa, Europe, and Asia, the region was the first outside Africa settled by modern humans and the first in which writing systems were developed. All of the core [[Abrahamic religions]], Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, originated there and have their holiest sites there.

Most of the region is hot and arid, especially the Arabian and Egyptian areas. Major rivers, including the Nile, Tigris, Euphrates, and Jordan, support agriculture. The Levantine coast and most of Turkey have a temperate Mediterranean climate. The Gulf Arab states along the [[Persian Gulf]] hold vast reserves of petroleum, which has influenced regional politics. Because of its aridity and dependence on the fossil fuel industry, the region is among the most vulnerable to climate change and water scarcity.

Stage of the English language spoken in England from the [[Norman Conquest]] of 1066 until the late 15th century. Middle English evolved from [[Old English]] under the influence of Anglo-Norman French and Old Norse. Most grammatical case distinctions were reduced and eventually lost, and the language came to depend more on word order. The period's most studied work is *The Canterbury Tales* by [[Geoffrey Chaucer]]. In England, Middle English was succeeded by Early Modern English, which lasted until about 1650.

ENTRY:
Second subdivision of the [[Paleolithic]], or Old [[Stone Age]], as understood in Europe, Africa and Asia. It broadly spanned 300,000 to 50,000 years ago and was succeeded by the [[Upper Paleolithic]]. The earliest evidence of [[Behavioral modernity|behavioral modernity]] first appears in this period, although undisputed evidence becomes common only in the Upper Paleolithic. The earliest undisputed artistic expression includes bracelets, beads and [[Ochre|ochre]] used as body paint, at sites such as Blombos Cave. Burials at Krapina in Croatia and at the Qafzeh and Es Skhul caves in Israel have led some scholars to suggest religious beliefs, including an afterlife, though others attribute them to secular reasons. Long-distance trade in raw materials began as early as 120,000 years ago. According to the recent African origin theory, anatomically modern humans began migrating out of [[Africa]] around 125,000 years ago and replaced other Homo species, including [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]].

=== KEY: Middle_age_e1b9d4
HEADWORD: Middle age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Diabetes | Erik Erikson | Hypertension | Menopause
ENTRY:
Phase of adulthood between young adulthood and old age, commonly defined as ages 40 to 64. Definitions vary, and many people now place its start near age 55. Physical changes include graying hair and a decline in fertility, and women typically experience [[Menopause|menopause]] between ages 45 and 55. Mortality rates begin to rise, mainly because of heart problems, [[Cancer|cancer]], [[Hypertension|hypertension]] and [[Diabetes|diabetes]], although most middle-aged people in industrialized nations live into old age. [[Erik Erikson]] called this stage generativity versus stagnation, the seventh of eight stages of psychosocial development. Career satisfaction shifts toward inner contentment, and career changes remain common. The perception of a typical midlife crisis is described as largely false, though a subset of people may experience one.

=== KEY: Middle_class_8cc440
HEADWORD: Middle class
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: French Revolution | Friedrich Engels | Working class
ENTRY:
Class of people in the middle of a societal hierarchy, often defined by occupation, income, education or social status. Common definitions range from the middle fifth of people on a nation's income ladder to everyone except the poorest and wealthiest 20%. In the United States the term describes people who in other countries would be called [[Working class|working class]]. The term was first attested in a 1745 pamphlet by James Bradshaw. [[Friedrich Engels]] saw the category as lying between the nobility and the peasantry in late feudal society, and the middle classes helped drive the [[French Revolution]]. The modern usage dates to the 1913 U.K. Registrar-General's report, which placed the middle class between the upper and working classes. In 2009, *The Economist* asserted that over half the world's population belonged to the middle class.
