=== KEY: Principal_component_analysis_f50ddf
HEADWORD: Principal component analysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric science | Karl Pearson
ENTRY:
Linear technique for reducing the dimensionality of data. The data are transformed onto a new coordinate system in which the principal components, the directions capturing the largest variation, can be identified. Each component is the direction of a line that best fits the points while being orthogonal to the previous ones, and the components are uncorrelated. Many studies plot the first two components to identify clusters of related data points. PCA is applied in population genetics, microbiome studies and [[Atmospheric science|atmospheric science]]. The method originated with [[Karl Pearson]]'s 1901 work on lines and planes of closest fit to points in space.

=== KEY: Principle_of_relativity_664466
HEADWORD: Principle of relativity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Galileo Galilei | General relativity | Maxwell's equations | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Physics principle that the laws of physics take the same form in all inertial frames of reference. [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo Galilei]] articulated it for classical mechanics in the 17th century, using the example of a ship sailing smoothly on calm seas. In 1905 [[Albert Einstein]] extended the principle to all laws of physics, including electromagnetism, and combined it with the constancy of the speed of light for all observers, the basis of [[Special relativity|special relativity]]. In special relativity, [[Maxwell's equations|Maxwell's equations]] take the same form in all inertial frames. Einstein's 1915 [[General relativity|general relativity]] extended the principle to accelerated frames and gravity, which it interpreted as the curvature of spacetime. The principle implies that absolute motion does not appear in any law of physics.

=== KEY: Printed_circuit_board_75632e
HEADWORD: Printed circuit board
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Copper | Epoxy | Soldering
ENTRY:
Flat board that supports and connects electronic components. A printed circuit board (PCB) is a laminated structure of conductive and insulating layers, in which [[Copper|copper]] traces are etched from sheets bonded to a non-conductive substrate, often fiberglass-reinforced [[Epoxy|epoxy]]. Components are attached to conductive pads, generally by [[Soldering|soldering]], which both connects and fastens them. === KEY: Printing_b002a5
HEADWORD: Printing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Library | Diamond Sutra | Europe | Gutenberg Bible | Johannes Gutenberg | Renaissance | Scientific Revolution | Tang dynasty
ENTRY:
Process for mass reproducing text and images using a master form or template.

The earliest known form evolved from ink rubbings made on paper or cloth from texts on stone tablets in the sixth century, and printing by pressing an inked image onto paper (woodblock printing) appeared later that century. Mechanical woodblock printing on paper began in the 7th century in the [[Tang dynasty]] and spread through East Asia. The first completely surviving printed book is the *[[Diamond Sutra]]* of 868, found at Dunhuang and held in the [[British Library]].

From 900 to 1444 wooden and tin block printing (*ṭarsh*) was used across the Islamic world for amulets, tax receipts, and state decrees, and then vanished without explanation. In [[Europe]], block printing was common on cloth by 1300 and moved quickly to woodcut religious images and playing cards on paper once paper became easily available, around 1400. Block-books, cheaper than manuscripts, emerged in the mid-15th century.

Movable type allowed more flexible processes than hand copying or block printing. Bi Sheng created the first known system in China around 1040, using porcelain. Copper movable type originated in China at the beginning of the 12th century and was used to print Northern Song paper money, though woodblock remained the main method for Chinese, with its thousands of characters. Korea produced the *Jikji* (1377), the earliest extant book printed with metal movable type. [[Johannes Gutenberg]] invented the printing press in the 15th century in Europe, combining cast metal type with a screw press, and the [[Gutenberg Bible]] was printed around 1455.

Printing played a key role in the [[Renaissance]] and the [[Scientific Revolution]] and laid the material basis for the modern knowledge-based economy and the spread of learning to the masses. Methods today include offset lithography for commercial volumes and digital printing for on-demand production.

=== KEY: Printing_press_340ae5

Machine that applies pressure to transfer ink onto paper, cloth or a similar material. It was a major improvement on methods in which paper was rubbed by hand. The press contributed to the [[Reformation|Reformation]], through the spread of [[Martin Luther]]'s writings, and to the [[Scientific Revolution|Scientific Revolution]]. By 1500 it had enabled the printing of over 20 million books.

=== KEY: Printmaking_ea2dbb
HEADWORD: Printmaking
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Process of creating artworks by printing, normally on [[Paper|paper]] but also on fabric, [[Wood|wood]], [[Metal|metal]] and other surfaces. Common matrices include metal plates for [[Engraving|engraving]], etching and intaglio; stone or polymer for lithography; wood blocks for woodcuts; linoleum for linocuts; and silk or synthetic screens for screen printing. === KEY: Prion_14972b
HEADWORD: Prion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bacteria | Bovine spongiform encephalopathy | Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease | DNA | Nobel Prize | RNA | Virus
ENTRY:
Infectious, misfolded protein that causes fatal neurodegenerative diseases. Prions induce normal variants of the same protein to misfold, and the term derives from proteinaceous infectious particle. Unlike [[Virus|viruses]], [[Bacteria|bacteria]] and fungi, prions contain no nucleic acids, [[DNA|DNA]] or [[RNA|RNA]]. The prion protein (PrP) occurs naturally, and its twisted forms are prions. Prion diseases affect animals and humans, including scrapie in sheep, chronic wasting disease in deer, [[Bovine spongiform encephalopathy|bovine spongiform encephalopathy]] in cattle and [[Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease|Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease]] in humans. All known prion diseases in mammals affect the brain or neural tissue, are progressive and are invariably fatal. Prions resist heat, radiation and disinfectants, which complicates decontamination. Stanley Prusiner proposed the prion hypothesis and received the 1997 [[Nobel Prize|Nobel Prize]] in Physiology or Medicine.

=== KEY: Prism_optics_69967e
HEADWORD: Prism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binoculars | Fluorite | Polarization (waves) | Rainbow | Refraction
ENTRY:
Transparent optical element with flat, polished surfaces that [[Refraction|refract]] light. At least one surface must be angled, since elements with only two parallel surfaces are windows. The most familiar type is the triangular prism. Prisms are made from transparent materials such as glass, acrylic and [[Fluorite|fluorite]]. A dispersive prism splits white light into its spectral colors, the colors of the [[Rainbow|rainbow]], a phenomenon demonstrated by Isaac Newton. In spectrometers, prisms measure the minimum deviation angle of each wavelength to analyze the composition of light. Other prisms reflect light or split it into components with different [[Polarization (waves)|polarizations]], and they are used in [[Binoculars|binoculars]], periscopes and laser equipment.

ENTRY:
Thought experiment in [[Game theory]] in which two rational agents may cooperate for mutual benefit or defect for individual gain. === KEY: Privacy_c5f29b
HEADWORD: Privacy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Common law | Encryption | Human rights | International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights | Internet | Security | Surveillance | Universal Declaration of Human Rights
ENTRY:
Condition or claim of an individual or group to be free from unwanted access, observation, or disclosure, and to determine how far they and information about them are exposed to others.

The concept has no single accepted definition. Lawyers, philosophers, and social scientists have understood it as a right to be let alone, as limited access to the self, as secrecy, or as control over personal information.

Distinctions between public and private life appear in many ancient societies, but privacy emerged as a distinct legal claim only in the 19th century, when printing and photographic technologies and the popular press prompted calls for protection. In "The Right to Privacy" (1890), the Boston lawyers Samuel D. Warren and Louis Brandeis argued that the [[Common law|common law]] should recognize a general "right to be let alone". In the second half of the 20th century the spread of computers shifted attention to the collection and processing of personal data, producing data protection law that began in Western Europe in the 1970s and has since spread worldwide.

Privacy is protected as a [[Human rights|human right]] by Article 12 of the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]], Article 17 of the [[International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights]], and regional instruments such as Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. European law tends to treat privacy as an aspect of personal dignity and regulates personal data comprehensively, whereas U.S. law has traditionally emphasized liberty against the state and regulates the private sector by sector.

Privacy overlaps with but differs from [[Security|security]]: information security protects data against unauthorized access, while privacy also concerns whether data should be collected and used at all. The [[Internet]], mobile phones, and large-scale data analysis have intensified debate over [[Surveillance|surveillance]] by governments and corporations. Responses include legislation, litigation, and privacy-enhancing technologies such as [[Encryption|encryption]].

=== KEY: Probability_0d2765
HEADWORD: Probability
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andrey Kolmogorov | Blaise Pascal | Christiaan Huygens | Gerolamo Cardano | Pierre de Fermat | Probability theory | Statistics
ENTRY:
Numerical description of how likely an event is to occur, expressed as a number between 0 and 1.

The larger the probability, the more likely the event. It is often given as a percentage from 0% to 100%. For a fair coin, "heads" and "tails" are equally probable, so each has probability 1/2, or 0.5, or 50%. These ideas are formalized axiomatically in [[Probability theory]], used in [[Statistics|statistics]], mathematics, science, finance, gambling, artificial intelligence, machine learning, and game theory to draw inferences about the expected frequency of events and to describe regularities of complex systems.

Two main categories of interpretation compete. Objectivists describe an objective or physical state of affairs. The most popular version is frequentist probability, the relative frequency of an outcome when an experiment is repeated indefinitely. Subjectivists treat probability as a degree of belief. The most popular version is Bayesian probability, which combines expert knowledge, represented by a prior probability distribution, with experimental data to produce a posterior distribution.

The word derives from Latin *probabilitas*, which could also mean the authority of a witness in a legal case. In the 16th century the Italian polymath [[Gerolamo Cardano]] defined odds as the ratio of favorable to unfavorable outcomes. The doctrine of probabilities dates to the 1654 correspondence of [[Pierre de Fermat]] and [[Blaise Pascal]]. [[Christiaan Huygens]] (1657) gave the earliest known scientific treatment. Jakob Bernoulli's *Ars Conjectandi* (1713) and Abraham de Moivre's *Doctrine of Chances* (1718) treated the subject as a branch of mathematics. In 1933 [[Andrey Kolmogorov]] established its rigorous foundation, axiomatizing it using measure theory.

=== KEY: Probability_density_function_60281b
HEADWORD: Probability density function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Integral | Random variable
ENTRY:
Function describing the relative likelihood that a continuous [[Random variable|random variable]] takes a given value. Probability density is the probability per unit length. Because any single exact value of a continuous variable has probability zero, the density shows how much more likely one value is than another. The probability of falling within a range is the [[Integral|integral]] of the density over that range, and the density is nonnegative with total area equal to one. In one example, the probability that a bacterium dies between 5 and 5.01 hours is 0.02, giving a density of 2 hour⁻¹ at 5 hours. The term density function is also used for the probability mass function of discrete variables, which causes confusion.

=== KEY: Probability_distribution_78a226
HEADWORD: Probability distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binomial distribution | Normal distribution | Poisson distribution | Probability density function | Random variable
ENTRY:
Mathematical function assigning probabilities to the possible outcomes of a random phenomenon. Formally, it is a probability measure on events, which are sets of possible outcomes, satisfying the axioms of probability. Distributions are linked to [[Random variable|random variables]], which assign a value to each outcome. For a fair coin, a variable equal to 1 for heads and 0 for tails gives each value a probability of 1/2. Discrete distributions are described by a probability mass function, while many continuous distributions are described by a [[Probability density function|probability density function]]. Distributions of special importance have names, such as the [[Normal distribution|normal]], [[Binomial distribution|binomial]] and [[Poisson distribution|Poisson]] distributions.

=== KEY: Probability_theory_01ba09
HEADWORD: Probability theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andrey Kolmogorov | Blaise Pascal | Central limit theorem | Gerolamo Cardano | Law of large numbers | Pierre de Fermat | Random variable
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerned with probability. It treats probability rigorously through axioms that define a probability space, in which a measure between 0 and 1 is assigned to events, which are subsets of the sample space of outcomes. Its central subjects include [[Random variable|random variables]], probability distributions and stochastic processes. Although random events cannot be predicted perfectly, the [[Law of large numbers|law of large numbers]] and the [[Central limit theorem|central limit theorem]] describe their behavior. It is the mathematical foundation of statistics and is used in statistical mechanics. The roots of the theory lie in analyses of games of chance by [[Gerolamo Cardano]] in the sixteenth century and by [[Pierre de Fermat]] and [[Blaise Pascal]] in the seventeenth century. [[Andrey Kolmogorov]] presented the axiom system that became the basis of modern probability theory in 1933.

=== KEY: Productivity_73015e
HEADWORD: Productivity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gross domestic product | Leisure
ENTRY:
Efficiency of production of goods or services, usually measured as output per unit of input over a period of time. Measures are often ratios of aggregate output to a single input or to an aggregate of inputs, and the choice among them depends on the purpose and on data availability. The most common is labor productivity, such as [[Gross domestic product|GDP]] per worker, which relates output volume to hours worked or total employment. It can also make businesses more profitable.

Difference between revenue from outputs and the total costs of inputs, also called surplus value. Economic profit is smaller than accounting profit, which counts only the explicit costs that appear on financial statements. === KEY: Programmed_cell_death_ffab24

=== KEY: Property_5ad234
HEADWORD: Property
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Capitalism | Conservatism | Intellectual property | John Locke | Judiciary
ENTRY:
System of legal rights giving people control over valuable things, and the things themselves. Depending on its nature, an owner may have the right to consume, alter, share, rent, sell, transfer, or destroy it, or to exclude others from doing so. Property is usually defined by the code of the local sovereignty, and the [[Judiciary|judiciary]] can adjudicate and enforce property rights. Economics and political economy distinguish four broad forms: private, public, collective, and common property. Types include real property (land and improvements to it), personal property (physical possessions), and [[Intellectual property|intellectual property]], which covers exclusive rights over artistic creations and inventions but is not always widely recognized or enforced. In sociology and anthropology, property is often defined as a relationship between individuals and an object in which at least one holds a bundle of rights over it.

Philosophers disagree about its basis: some hold that property rights arise from social convention, others find justification in morality or natural law. [[John Locke]] wrote that every man has a property in his person, and classical liberalism holds that individuals own their lives and therefore the products of them. [[Adam Smith]] tied the expectation of profit from improving one's stock of capital to private-property rights, yet also wrote that civil government, so far as it is instituted for the security of property, is in reality instituted for the defense of the rich against the poor. [[Capitalism]] assumes that property rights encourage owners to develop property, generate wealth, and allocate resources efficiently through markets. [[Conservatism]] holds that the more widespread private possession is, the more stable and productive a state becomes.

=== KEY: Property_law_5ad508

ENTRY:
Body of law governing ownership and tenure in property. Property law regulates the acquisition, transfer and enforcement of proprietary rights, which usually involve relationships between private individuals. Its public-law aspects include compulsory land acquisition, environmental effects, antitrust, indigenous rights, and the human rights to property and housing. Legal systems generally distinguish real property, meaning land, from personal property, which may be tangible or intangible, such as [[Intellectual property]]. The most significant doctrinal influence between the two traditions is the reception of the [[Trust (law)|trust]] into civilian jurisdictions.

=== KEY: Proposition_704ff5
HEADWORD: Proposition
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Meaning of a declarative sentence, and bearer of truth value. Propositions are the meanings shared by sentences such as *Snow is white* and its German equivalent, and they are the objects of beliefs. === KEY: Propositional_logic_0080ea

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: First-order logic | Gottlob Frege
ENTRY:
Branch of classical logic dealing with propositions and their relations. Also called sentential calculus or statement logic, it studies compound propositions formed with connectives for conjunction, disjunction, implication, biconditional and negation. Propositions are represented by letters called propositional variables, and the subject deals with neither quantifiers nor non-logical objects. Ordinary form of language, following conversational speech without regular rhythm. Prose differs from poetry, which is organized by rhythmic meter, rhyme or other artistic structure. === KEY: Prosody_linguistics_9f2835

Study of suprasegmental features of speech, such as intonation, stress, rhythm and loudness. === KEY: Protein_7e667a
HEADWORD: Protein
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Enzyme | Gene | Insulin | Jöns Jacob Berzelius | Muscle | Peptide | Ribosome
ENTRY:
Large biomolecule consisting of one or more long chains of [[Amino acid|amino acid]] residues. Proteins catalyze metabolic reactions, replicate DNA, respond to stimuli, give structure to cells and organisms, and transport molecules. They differ mainly in their amino acid sequence, which is dictated by the nucleotide sequence of their [[Gene|genes]] and usually causes folding into a specific three-dimensional structure that determines activity. A linear chain of residues joined by peptide bonds is a polypeptide; chains shorter than 20–30 residues are rarely considered proteins and are commonly called [[Peptide|peptides]]. The genetic code specifies 20 standard amino acids, though some organisms add selenocysteine and, in certain archaea, pyrrolysine. During or shortly after synthesis, residues are often chemically modified, which alters folding, stability, activity, and function. Some proteins require a non-protein compound or ion, called a cofactor.

Synthesis, called translation, takes place on [[Ribosome|ribosomes]], where messenger RNA templates direct the assembly of amino acids. Many proteins are [[Enzyme|enzymes]] vital to metabolism. Others are structural, such as actin and myosin in [[Muscle|muscle]] and the scaffolding proteins of the cytoskeleton, or are important in cell signaling, immune responses, cell adhesion, and the cell cycle. Hemoglobin carries oxygen, and [[Insulin|insulin]] regulates metabolism. Proteins exist for minutes or years, averaging 1–2 days in mammalian cells; misfolded ones are degraded faster, often by the proteasome. Animals need dietary protein to obtain the essential amino acids they cannot make.

Proteins were first described by the Dutch chemist Gerardus Johannes Mulder and named by the Swedish chemist [[Jöns Jacob Berzelius]] in 1838, from the Greek *proteios*, "primary." Franz Hofmeister and Hermann Emil Fischer established in 1902 that proteins are chains of amino acids.

=== KEY: Protestantism_84ba5e
HEADWORD: Protestantism
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Major branch of Christianity that originated in the 16th-century [[Reformation|Protestant Reformation]]. It emphasizes justification of sinners through faith alone, salvation by unmerited divine grace, the priesthood of all believers, and the Bible as the sole infallible authority for Christian faith and practice; the five *solae* summarize mainstream Protestant belief. The Reformation began in the [[Holy Roman Empire]] in 1517, when [[Martin Luther]] published his [[Ninety-five Theses]] in reaction to abuses in the sale of indulgences by the [[Catholic Church]].

Lutheranism spread from Germany into Denmark–Norway, Sweden, Finland, Livonia, and Iceland. Protestants hold to the concept of an invisible church, whereas the Catholic and Orthodox churches each regard themselves as the one true church. Major families include Anglicans/Episcopalians, Baptists, Reformed, Lutherans, Methodists, and Moravians; Adventists, Pentecostals, Quakers, and nondenominational churches may also be counted. === KEY: Protist_3b9d98
HEADWORD: Protist
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algae | Eukaryote | Malaria | Protozoa
ENTRY:
Any [[Eukaryote|eukaryotic]] organism that is not an animal, land plant or fungus. Protists do not form a natural group but are a paraphyletic group encompassing the entire eukaryote tree of life, from which land plants, animals and fungi evolved. Most are single-celled, although some form multicellular colonies, giant slime molds or seaweeds with differentiated tissues. They were once classified as a kingdom, Protista, or lumped with plants and animals as [[Algae|algae]] and [[Protozoa|protozoa]]. Some are important parasites, causing diseases such as [[Malaria|malaria]] and toxoplasmosis, and free-living forms can cause harmful algal blooms. Protists make up the majority of eukaryotic diversity, and most remain undescribed.

=== KEY: Proton_4fbe48
HEADWORD: Proton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic number | Ernest Rutherford | J. J. Thomson | Large Hadron Collider | Neutron | Quark | Standard Model | Strong interaction
ENTRY:
Stable subatomic particle (symbol p, H⁺) with a positive electric charge of +1 e. Its mass is slightly less than that of a [[Neutron|neutron]] and approximately 1836 times that of an electron; protons and neutrons, each about one dalton, are jointly called nucleons. One or more protons are present in the nucleus of every atom. Their number, the [[Atomic number|atomic number]] (Z), defines the element and, with the matching number of electrons, determines its chemical characteristics. The nucleus of the most common hydrogen isotope is a lone proton; deuterium and tritium contain one proton bound to one and two neutrons, respectively.

Once thought elementary, protons are composite particles in the [[Standard Model]]: spin-1/2 fermions and baryons, a kind of hadron, made of three valence [[Quark|quarks]], two up quarks of charge +2/3 e and one down quark of charge −1/3 e, held together by gluons through the [[Strong interaction|strong force]]. Quark rest masses contribute only about 1% of a proton's mass; the remainder is quantum chromodynamics binding energy.

The idea of a hydrogen-like constituent of atoms goes back to William Prout's hypothesis of 1815, and [[J. J. Thomson]] later found canal rays consistent with hydrogen ions. In 1919 [[Ernest Rutherford]] used alpha particles from radium to strike air and observed what he called artificial disintegration of nitrogen atoms; by 1920 he concluded that hydrogen nuclei were a constituent of the nitrogen nucleus, and he named the particle from the Greek for "first." Free protons are used in accelerators for proton therapy and particle physics experiments, the most powerful being the [[Large Hadron Collider]].

=== KEY: Protostar_a5e828
HEADWORD: Protostar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angular momentum | Infrared | Molecular cloud | Star | Stellar evolution | Sun
ENTRY:
Very young [[Star|star]] still gathering mass from its parent [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]]. It is the earliest phase of [[Stellar evolution|stellar evolution]] and, for a low-mass star like the [[Sun]], lasts about 500,000 years. It begins when a molecular cloud fragment collapses under its own gravity to form an opaque core, and it ends when the infalling gas is depleted, leaving a pre-main-sequence star. Protostars are not powered by hydrogen fusion at their centers; their energy comes largely from shocks on their surfaces and on the surrounding disk. Surrounded by interstellar dust, they are not visible at optical wavelengths and radiate mainly in the [[Infrared|infrared]] and millimeter ranges. Infalling gas first builds a low-mass protostar and then a protoplanetary disk, and because of [[Angular momentum|angular momentum]] conservation, an increasing share of gas hits the disk rather than the star.

Modern hospitals emphasize treatment, usually combining psychiatric medication with [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]], rather than the containment of the older lunatic asylum. Many countries have banned physical restraint, though it is still periodically used in the United States, India and Japan. Bimaristans, Middle Eastern hospitals that often had wards for patients showing mania or other psychological distress, were built in the early 9th century; the first was built in [[Baghdad|Baghdad]] under [[Harun al-Rashid|Harun al-Rashid]]. The first mental hospital in Western Europe, the Hospital de los Inocentes, was organized in 1409 in [[Valencia|Valencia]], Spain.

=== KEY: Psychiatry_840ee5
HEADWORD: Psychiatry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cognition | Neurology | Psychotherapy | Social control | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Medical specialty devoted to the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of mental conditions, including disturbances of [[Cognition|cognition]], perception, mood, emotion and behavior. Initial assessment begins with a case history and a mental status examination, supplemented by laboratory tests, physical examination, psychological assessments and occasionally neuroimaging. Mental disorders are classified in manuals such as the International Classification of Diseases, edited by the [[World Health Organization|World Health Organization]], and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association. Treatment may include psychotropic medicines, [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] and substance-abuse treatment. The field has generally been considered a middle ground between [[Neurology|neurology]] and psychology. The term was first coined by the German physician Johann Christian Reil in 1808. Psychiatry has been controversial since its inception, with critics citing its medicalization of mental distress, reliance on pharmaceuticals and historical role in [[Social control|social control]].

=== KEY: Psycho_1960_film_01b39a

1960 American horror film produced and directed by [[Alfred Hitchcock|Alfred Hitchcock]], adapted by Joseph Stefano from Robert Bloch's 1959 novel. Filmed in black and white on a small budget, it was a departure from Hitchcock's previous [[North by Northwest|North by Northwest]]. Critics were initially divided by its subject matter, but audience interest and strong box-office returns led to a major critical reevaluation, and the film earned $50 million worldwide. It received four [[Academy Awards|Academy Award]] nominations, including Best Director for Hitchcock. Its shower scene is among the best-known in cinema, and the film is considered one of the earliest examples of the [[Slasher film|slasher film]] genre. In 1992 the [[Library of Congress|Library of Congress]] selected it for preservation in the United States National Film Registry.

=== KEY: Psychoanalysis_22c198
HEADWORD: Psychoanalysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Jung | Karl Popper | Literary criticism | Psychotherapy | Sigmund Freud
ENTRY:
Set of theories and therapeutic techniques for uncovering unconscious processes and their influence on thought, emotion and behavior. Established in the early 1890s by [[Sigmund Freud|Sigmund Freud]], it is a talk therapy for treating mental disorders. Freud held that unconscious drives largely determine cognition and behavior, and his structural model divides the psyche into the id, ego and superego. Two of his early colleagues, [[Carl Jung|Carl Jung]] and Alfred Adler, developed their own methods, which Freud said were not forms of psychoanalysis. The discipline has been controversial from the outset; the philosopher [[Karl Popper|Karl Popper]] criticized it as unfalsifiable, yet its influence on psychology and psychiatry is undisputed. Evidence suggests that long-term psychoanalytic [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] can be effective for certain disorders, though its overall efficacy remains contested. Its concepts are also widely used in [[Literary criticism|literary criticism]] and in the interpretation of myths and cultural phenomena.

=== KEY: Psychological_disorders_4b8faf
HEADWORD: Psychological disorders
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bipolar disorder | Dementia | Psychotherapy | Schizophrenia
ENTRY:
Behavioral or mental pattern that causes significant distress or impairment of personal functioning, also called a mental disorder or mental illness. It involves a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation or behavior, and its causes are often unclear. Diagnosis is usually made by a mental health professional, often relying on observation and questioning, and cultural and religious beliefs as well as social norms should be taken into account. Treatment may include [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] or psychiatric medication, along with lifestyle changes, social interventions and peer support; involuntary treatment occurs in a minority of cases. In 2019, major depression affected about 264 million people, [[Dementia|dementia]] about 50 million, [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]] about 45 million, and [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]] and other psychoses about 20 million. Stigma and discrimination add to the suffering and disability associated with these conditions.
