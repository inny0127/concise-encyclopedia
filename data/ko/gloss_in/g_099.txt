=== KEY: Olivine_463776
HEADWORD: Olivine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Igneous rock | Peridot | Red Sea
ENTRY:
Magnesium iron silicate mineral, (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄. Olivine forms a solid solution series between forsterite (Mg₂SiO₄) and fayalite (Fe₂SiO₄). Forsterite melts at almost 1,900 °C (3,450 °F), while fayalite melts at about 1,200 °C (2,190 °F). It is the primary component of the Earth's upper mantle but weathers quickly at the surface. Translucent olivine is used as the gemstone [[Peridot|peridot]], and some of the finest gem-quality olivine comes from Zabargad Island in the [[Red Sea|Red Sea]]. It occurs in [[Igneous rock|igneous rocks]] such as basalt and gabbro, and rocks with more than 40% olivine are called peridotites.

=== KEY: Olmec_colossal_heads_50af79
HEADWORD: Olmec colossal heads
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basalt | Mesoamerica | Olmecs
ENTRY:
Monumental [[Basalt|basalt]] sculptures of human heads made by the [[Mesoamerica|Mesoamerican]] Olmec civilization. Seventeen confirmed examples are known from four sites in the [[Olmecs|Olmec]] heartland on the Gulf Coast of Mexico, and a throne at Takalik Abaj in Guatemala may have been carved from a colossal head. They range in height from 1.17 to 3.4 m (3.8 to 11.2 ft). The boulders came from the Sierra de Los Tuxtlas, more than 150 km (93 mi) away. The largest, abandoned near the source of its stone, is estimated at 36 to 45 t (40 to 50 short tons). The heads are thought to portray powerful individual rulers, and their distinctive headdresses may be protective helmets. Most have been dated to the Early Preclassic period (1500–1000 BC). The first head was found at Tres Zapotes in 1862, and an excavation by Matthew Stirling in 1938 spurred the first archaeological investigations of Olmec culture.

=== KEY: Olmecs_5e03fc
HEADWORD: Olmecs
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mesoamerica | Natural rubber
ENTRY:
Earliest known major civilization of [[Mesoamerica|Mesoamerica]], centered in what are now the Mexican states of Veracruz and Tabasco. The Olmecs flourished from about 1200 to 400 BC. They were centered first at San Lorenzo Tenochtitlán and from the 10th century BC at La Venta. By about 400 BC their major centers had been abandoned, although elements of their tradition lived on in successor societies. The Olmecs appear to have practiced ritual bloodletting and played the Mesoamerican ballgame, practices shared by nearly all later Mesoamerican societies. They are known especially for their artwork, including colossal heads. The name Olmec derives from the Nahuatl term for [[Natural rubber|rubber]] people, a name applied in error to the ancient culture by early 20th-century archaeologists.

=== KEY: Olympic_Games_61ec4a

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Series of international multi-sport events, held as separate Summer and Winter Games. The Games are open to amateur and professional athletes, and each team represents a [[Sovereign state|sovereign state]] or territory. They were inspired by the [[Ancient Olympic Games|ancient Olympic Games]] held at Olympia, Greece, from the 8th century BC to the 4th century AD. The Games have been disrupted by world wars, Cold War boycotts and the COVID-19 pandemic: the 1916, 1940 and 1944 Games were canceled, and the 2020 Games were postponed to 2021.

=== KEY: Olympic_weightlifting_a66ace
HEADWORD: Olympic weightlifting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Powerlifting | Summer Olympic Games
ENTRY:
Competitive strength sport in which athletes lift a loaded barbell from the ground to overhead. Athletes compete in two lifts: the snatch, a wide-grip lift in which the bar is raised overhead in one motion, and the clean and jerk, in which it is lifted to the shoulders and then overhead. Each lifter has three attempts at each lift, and the score is the combined total, in kilograms, of the heaviest successful lifts. A third lift, the clean and press, was contested from 1924 to 1972. Weightlifting has been held at every [[Summer Olympic Games|Summer Olympic Games]] since 1920. Because the Olympic lifts are performed quickly and require mobility, they test explosive power, unlike the limit strength tested by [[Powerlifting|powerlifting]]. The International Weightlifting Federation governs the sport and runs the World Weightlifting Championships each year.

Sacred syllable, [[Mantra|mantra]] and invocation in [[Hinduism|Hinduism]], Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism. Its etymology has long been disputed.

ENTRY:
Globular cluster in the constellation [[Centaurus]], 17,090 light-years (5,240 parsecs) from [[Earth]]. It was first identified as a non-stellar object by [[Edmond Halley]] in 1677, having been cataloged by [[Ptolemy]] around 150 AD as a star. It is the largest known globular cluster in the [[Milky Way]], about 150 light-years across, with an estimated 10 million stars and a total mass of 4 million [[Solar mass|solar masses]]. Unlike most globular clusters, it is thought to be the core remnant of a disrupted [[Dwarf galaxy|dwarf galaxy]]. A 2008 study using the [[Hubble Space Telescope]] and the Gemini Observatory found evidence of an intermediate-mass black hole at its center, although this is disputed. It is one of the few globular clusters visible to the naked eye.

=== KEY: On_the_Origin_of_Species_9d3cbe
HEADWORD: On the Origin of Species
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Church of England | Evolution | Natural selection | Species
ENTRY:
Scientific work by the English naturalist [[Charles Darwin]], published November 24, 1859. Considered the foundation of evolutionary biology, it argued that populations evolve over generations through [[Natural selection|natural selection]] and that the diversity of life arose by [[Evolution|common descent]] through a branching pattern. Darwin drew on evidence collected on the Beagle expedition in the 1830s and on later research, correspondence and experiments. Written for non-specialist readers, it sparked widespread debate over its implications for the fixity of [[Species|species]] and for religion, at a time when the English scientific establishment was closely tied to the [[Church of England]]. Within two decades most scientists accepted that evolution had occurred, although natural selection was slow to gain prominence. After the modern evolutionary synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s, natural selection became the unifying concept of the life sciences.

=== KEY: Oncology_50b74a
HEADWORD: Oncology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biopsy | Cancer | Chemotherapy | Endoscopy | Medical imaging | Radiation therapy | Surgery
ENTRY:
Branch of medicine dealing with the study, diagnosis, treatment and prevention of [[Cancer|cancer]]. Practitioners are called oncologists, a term derived from the Greek *onkos*, meaning tumor, which the Greek physician Galen first used to describe tumors. Cancer was first documented in humans in Egypt around 3000 BC, when the Edwin Smith papyrus described a form of cancer as "a bulging tumor of the breast." Oncology covers diagnosis, therapy, monitoring after treatment, palliative care, screening and clinical research into cancer treatments. Diagnosis relies on a [[Biopsy|biopsy]] examined by a pathologist, with [[Endoscopy]] and [[Medical imaging|imaging]] such as X-rays, CT scanning, MRI and ultrasound used to locate suspicious tissue. Treatment may combine [[Surgery|surgery]], [[Chemotherapy|chemotherapy]] and [[Radiation therapy|radiation therapy]], depending on the cancer, and surgery is attempted when a tumor can be removed entirely. Oncologists often specialize by organ or modality, such as breast, brain or radiation oncology.

Its frame story concerns King Shahryar, who vowed to execute each new bride after his first wife's betrayal; the vizier's daughter Scheherazade volunteers to marry him and tells him tales each night, leaving each unfinished at dawn to postpone her death. === KEY: One_child_policy_b27b82
HEADWORD: One-child policy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abortion | China | Chinese Communist Party | Human rights | Sterilization (medicine)
ENTRY:
Population control policy in [[China]], in force from 1979 to 2015. A near-universal one-child limit was imposed in 1980 and written into the constitution in 1982. Exceptions included rural families whose first child was a daughter and some ethnic minorities; by 1984 only about 35.4% of the population was subject to the original restriction. Families who violated the limit faced large fines, and enforcement sometimes included forced [[Abortion|abortion]] and [[Sterilization (medicine)|sterilization]]. The policy skewed the sex ratio toward men and contributed to the abandonment of infant girls, creating what critics call a generation of "missing women." The limit was raised to two children in 2015 and to three in 2021, and all limits were removed in July 2021. The [[Chinese Communist Party]] credits the policy with preventing 400 million births, a figure some scholars dispute, while the policy has been widely criticized in the West for [[Human rights|human rights]] violations.

=== KEY: Onion_8eaa10

ENTRY:
[[Vegetable|Bulbous vegetable]] (*Allium cepa*), the most widely cultivated species of the [[Genus|genus]] *Allium*. It is a biennial usually grown as an annual, with hollow, bluish-green leaves and a bulb formed from shortened underground stems and fleshy scale leaves. The shallot, classed as a separate species until 2011, is a botanical variety of the onion. Close relatives include [[Garlic]], scallion, [[Leek]] and chives. The onion is known only from cultivation, though related wild species occur in [[Central Asia]] and [[Iran]]. Onions were placed in [[Ancient Egypt]] tombs as symbols of eternity, and they are now produced on every continent except Antarctica. Raw onions are about 89% water and contain 9.3 g of carbohydrates per 100 g. They can be eaten raw, cooked, pickled or dried, and when chopped they release chemicals that can irritate the eyes. The species was first formally described by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1753.

Origination and development of an organism, usually from fertilization of the egg to [[Adult|adulthood]]. === KEY: Ontology_0632b1
HEADWORD: Ontology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Database | George Berkeley | Materialism | Metaphysics | Socrates | Thought experiment
ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy that studies being, traditionally understood as the subdiscipline of [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]] concerned with the most general features of reality. Ontology examines the characteristics shared by all things and their classification into basic types, such as particulars and universals. Particulars are unique, non-repeatable entities, such as [[Socrates]], whereas universals are general, repeatable entities, such as the color green. Concrete objects exist in space and time; abstract objects, such as the number 5, exist outside them. Systems of categories employ notions such as substance, property, relation, state of affairs, and event.

Ontologists disagree about which entities exist at the most basic level. Platonic realism holds that universals exist objectively, conceptualism that they exist only in the mind, and nominalism denies their existence. [[Materialism]] posits that fundamentally only matter exists, whereas dualism holds that mind and matter are independent principles. [[George Berkeley]] proposed that all existence is mental, in the slogan "to be is to be perceived." Methods include the analysis of concepts and experience, the use of intuitions and [[Thought experiment|thought experiments]], and the integration of findings from natural science.

Formal ontology investigates the most abstract features of objects, while applied ontology studies entities in specific domains; social ontology, for example, examines basic concepts of the social sciences. In information and computer science, an ontology is a formal, explicit specification of a shared conceptualization of a domain, a usage popularized in the 1990s, and it allows the structured storage of information in a [[Database|database]].

The relation between ontology and metaphysics is disputed, and some philosophers use the terms as synonyms. The ancient Greeks did not use the word, which was coined by philosophers in the 17th century.

HEADWORD: Ontology

ENTRY:

=== KEY: Open_set_7601a1
HEADWORD: Open set
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclidean space | Manifold | Metric space | Real number | Topological space | Topology
ENTRY:
Basic subset of a [[Topological space|topological space]], generalizing an open interval of the [[Real number|real line]]. In a [[Metric space|metric space]], a set is open if every point in it has a surrounding ball of nearby points that also lie in the set. More generally, a [[Topology|topology]] is a collection of subsets that contains the empty set and the whole space and is closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections; its members are the open sets. Open sets allow continuity, connectedness and compactness to be defined without a distance. The discrete topology makes every subset open, while the indiscrete topology allows only the empty set and the whole space.

=== KEY: Optical_illusion_06ae27

Visually perceived image that arguably differs from reality, caused by the [[Visual system|visual system]]. Richard Gregory classified illusions as physical, physiological, or cognitive, with four kinds in each class: ambiguities, distortions, paradoxes, and fictions. A classic physical distortion is the apparent bending of a stick half immersed in water, discussed by [[Ptolemy]] (c. 150). An afterimage is a physiological fiction. === KEY: Optical_microscope_ed091b

Instrument that uses visible light and a system of [[Lens|lenses]] to produce magnified images of small objects, also called a light microscope. Microscopes of this kind are the oldest type, and the compound form appeared in the 17th century. [[Galileo Galilei]] developed an improved compound microscope in 1609. In a compound microscope, an objective lens forms a real image that an eyepiece magnifies, and most modern research microscopes are of this type. === KEY: Optical_telescope_e1282b

=== KEY: Optics_beea0f
HEADWORD: Optics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christiaan Huygens | Diffraction | Electromagnetic radiation | Euclid | Ibn al-Haytham | James Clerk Maxwell | Photon | Snell's law
ENTRY:
Branch of physics that studies the behavior, manipulation, and detection of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]], including its interactions with matter and with instruments that use or detect it. Optics usually describes visible, ultraviolet, and infrared light, and extends to radio waves, microwaves, and X-rays.

Geometric optics treats light as rays that travel in straight lines and bend when they pass through or reflect from surfaces. Physical optics adds wave effects such as [[Diffraction|diffraction]] and interference. The ray model was developed first, followed by the wave model; 19th-century progress in electromagnetic theory, notably by [[James Clerk Maxwell]], showed that light waves are electromagnetic radiation. Phenomena that depend on light's wave-like and particle-like properties require quantum mechanics, which models light as particles called [[Photon|photons]]. Optics is relevant to astronomy, engineering, photography, and medicine, and underlies mirrors, lenses, telescopes, microscopes, lasers, and fiber optics.

The earliest known lenses, of polished crystal, date from as early as 2000 BC from Crete. Greek philosophers split between the intromission theory of vision, held by Democritus, Epicurus, and Aristotle, and the emission theory articulated by Plato. [[Euclid]] linked vision to geometry in a treatise on optics. In 984 the Persian mathematician Ibn Sahl described a law of refraction equivalent to [[Snell's law]]. In the early 11th century [[Ibn al-Haytham]] wrote the *Book of Optics*, rejecting emission theories and proposing that light reflects in straight lines from every point of a viewed object into the eye. In the 17th century Isaac Newton proposed a particle model of light and [[Christiaan Huygens]] a wave explanation. Lasers were developed in 1960.

=== KEY: Oral_tradition_8ae0e9
HEADWORD: Oral tradition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Cameroon | Folklore | Hinduism | Jainism | Rwanda
ENTRY:
Form of human communication in which knowledge, art, beliefs, ideas, and culture are received, preserved, and transmitted orally from one generation to the next. Transmission is through speech or song and may include folktales, ballads, chants, prose, or poetry. The information is held by oral repositories, sometimes called "walking libraries," who are usually also performers, such as griots in West Africa. Societies use oral tradition to pass on oral history, oral literature, oral law, and other knowledge, either without a writing system or alongside one. It is the most widespread medium of human communication.

[[Folklore]] is one type of oral tradition. [[Buddhism]], [[Hinduism]], Catholicism, Yazidism, and [[Jainism]] have used it, in parallel to writing, to transmit canonical scriptures, rituals, hymns, and mythologies. The scholar John Foley calls it "the single most dominant communicative technology of our species."

African societies have broadly been labeled oral civilizations. In Africa the principal political, legal, social, and religious texts were transmitted orally, and when the Bamums in [[Cameroon]] invented a script, the first texts written down were the royal chronicle and the code of customary law. Most African courts had archivists who learned the royal genealogy by heart. Performance context shapes content: in Burundi traditions were short because they were told at informal gatherings where everyone took a turn, whereas in neighboring [[Rwanda]] a professional performer had to entertain a patron for a whole evening.

The fidelity of oral traditions varies. The Kyrgyz *Manas* cycle, exceeding 500,000 lines, has been recited across generations, yet traditions remain susceptible to telescoped timelines and conflations, so they serve as historical sources most reliably when corroborated by independent evidence.

Normally the term refers to a regularly repeating trajectory. === KEY: Ordinary_least_squares_7909c8
HEADWORD: Ordinary least squares
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Linear regression
ENTRY:
Method for estimating the unknown parameters in a [[Linear regression|linear regression]] model. OLS chooses parameters by minimizing the sum of the squared differences between observed values of the dependent variable and the values predicted by the linear function of the independent variables. Geometrically, this is the sum of the squared distances, parallel to the dependent variable's axis, between each data point and the regression surface. Under the Gauss–Markov theorem, OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator when errors are homoscedastic and serially uncorrelated. Crater Lake National Park, its only national park, protects the [[Caldera|caldera]] surrounding Crater Lake, the deepest lake in the U.S. Oregon is the top [[Lumber|lumber]] producer of the contiguous U.S. Technology has been important since the 1970s.

Trilogy of Greek tragedies by [[Aeschylus]]. Its three plays, *Agamemnon*, *The Libation Bearers* and *The Eumenides*, follow the murder of Agamemnon by Clytemnestra, the murder of Clytemnestra by Orestes, and Orestes' trial. The trilogy was written in the 5th century BC and is the only extant example of an ancient Greek theater trilogy. It shows how the gods shape the characters' decisions and ends the curse on the House of Atreus with the pacification of the Furies, also called the Erinyes or Eumenides. It won first prize at the Dionysia festival in 458 BC. Its principal themes are the contrast between [[Revenge|revenge]] and [[Justice|justice]] and the shift from personal vendetta to organized litigation. A satyr play, *Proteus*, originally followed the trilogy, but only a single line survives.

=== KEY: Organ_biology_9e371b
HEADWORD: Organ
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Human body | Organelle | Tissue (biology)
ENTRY:
Collection of [[Tissue (biology)|tissues]] joined in a structural unit to serve a common function. In multicellular organisms, tissues of different types combine to form an organ; the intestinal wall, for example, is made of epithelial tissue and smooth muscle tissue. Two or more organs working together form an organ system, also called a body system. An organ's tissues are classified as parenchyma, the functional tissue, and stroma, the structural tissue. Most multicellular organisms have organs. In single-celled eukaryotes the functional analogue is the [[Organelle|organelle]], and plants have three main organs. The number of organs in the [[Human body|human body]] depends on the definition used: approximately 79 organs, and the exact number remains debated.

=== KEY: Organelle_01e409
HEADWORD: Organelle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cell (biology) | Endoplasmic reticulum | Golgi apparatus | Mitochondria | Organ (biology) | Plastid | Prokaryote
ENTRY:
Specialized subunit within a [[Cell (biology)|cell]] that has a specific function. The name, from the diminutive suffix -elle, reflects the idea that organelles are parts of cells as [[Organ (biology)|organs]] are to the body. Membrane-bound organelles are enclosed by their own lipid bilayers, while non-membrane-bound ones are spatially distinct functional units. Eukaryotic cells contain many types, including the [[Endoplasmic reticulum|endoplasmic reticulum]], the [[Golgi apparatus|Golgi apparatus]], the nuclear envelope, [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and [[Plastid|plastids]]. [[Prokaryote|Prokaryotes]] lack eukaryotic organelles, although some have protein-shelled bacterial microcompartments that may be primitive organelles. Organelles are identified by microscopy and can be purified by cell fractionation.
