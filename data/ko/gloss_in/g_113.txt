Phylum of mostly marine [[Algae|algae]], among the oldest and largest groups of eukaryotic algae, with over 7,000 recognized species. === KEY: Red_blood_cell_53b6ec
HEADWORD: Red blood cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood transfusion | Cell nucleus | Hemoglobin | Oxygen
ENTRY:
Most common type of blood cell, which delivers [[Oxygen|oxygen]] to the body's tissues as it passes through the capillaries. Red blood cells, also called erythrocytes, are rich in [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]], an iron-containing protein that binds oxygen and gives blood its red color. Each human red blood cell contains about 270 million hemoglobin molecules. Mature human red blood cells are flexible biconcave disks without a [[Cell nucleus|nucleus]] or organelles, which leaves more space for hemoglobin. Adults produce about 2.4 million new cells per second in the bone marrow. Cells circulate for about 100–120 days before macrophages recycle their components. Red blood cells make up roughly 40% to 45% of blood volume. Donated red cells are stored for [[Blood transfusion|transfusion]], and pulse oximetry uses the color change of hemoglobin to measure blood oxygen saturation.

=== KEY: Red_giant_44c7d0
HEADWORD: Red giant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arcturus | Light-year | Main sequence | Planetary nebula | Sun | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Luminous giant star of low or intermediate mass, roughly 0.3–8 solar masses, in a late phase of stellar evolution. Its outer atmosphere is inflated and tenuous, so its radius is large and its surface temperature is around 5,000 K (4,700 °C; 8,500 °F) or lower, giving it a color from yellow-white to reddish-orange. Having exhausted the hydrogen in its core, it fuses hydrogen in a shell around an inert helium core. Red giants evolve from [[Main sequence|main-sequence]] stars. Their radii are tens to hundreds of times larger than that of the [[Sun]], and their luminosities can reach nearly three thousand times that of the Sun. Many well-known bright stars are red giants; [[Arcturus]], a K0 star on the red-giant branch, is 36 [[Light-year|light-years]] away. A red giant usually produces a [[Planetary nebula|planetary nebula]] and becomes a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]] at the end of its life.

=== KEY: Redshift_f52a9d
HEADWORD: Redshift
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andromeda Galaxy | Big Bang | Cosmic microwave background | Doppler effect | Expansion of the universe
ENTRY:
Increase in the wavelength, or decrease in the frequency, of electromagnetic radiation such as light. The opposite change is a blueshift. Astronomy and cosmology recognize three forms: [[Doppler effect|Doppler]] redshift from the relative motion of sources, gravitational redshift as radiation escapes a gravitational potential, and cosmological redshift caused by the [[Expansion of the universe|expansion of the universe]]. Strong redshifting can turn a gamma ray into an X-ray, and the initial 3,000 kelvin radiation of the [[Big Bang]] has redshifted to the 3 K [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]]. The effect is named after Christian Doppler, who offered the first known physical explanation in 1842. In 1912 Vesto Slipher found that the [[Andromeda Galaxy]] had a blueshift, indicating it was moving toward Earth. Redshift is also used in Doppler radar and radar guns.

=== KEY: Reductio_ad_absurdum_5eaea8
HEADWORD: Reductio ad absurdum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek philosophy | Aristotle | Contradiction | Euclid | Irrational number | Mathematical proof | Rational number
ENTRY:
Form of argument that establishes a claim by showing that its contrary leads to absurdity or [[Contradiction|contradiction]]. Known in Latin as *reductio ad absurdum*, it traces back to [[Ancient Greek philosophy]] and was formalized by [[Aristotle]]. In mathematics it is called proof by contradiction, and it is used in many [[Mathematical proof|proofs]], though not every school of mathematical thought accepts this nonconstructive method. [[Euclid]] used it to show the existence of [[Irrational number|irrational numbers]]. A classic example shows there is no smallest positive [[Rational number|rational number]]: if one existed, half of it would be a smaller positive rational.

=== KEY: Reductionism_07d6b3
HEADWORD: Reductionism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemistry | Determinism | Materialism | Physics
ENTRY:
Philosophical position that explains a complex system as the sum of its simpler, more fundamental parts. It is often contrasted with holism and is associated with [[Materialism|materialism]] and [[Determinism|determinism]]. The *Oxford Companion to Philosophy* calls it one of the most used and abused terms in the philosophical lexicon, and distinguishes ontological, methodological and theory reductionism. Methodological reductionism holds that the best scientific strategy is always to explain phenomena in terms of ever-smaller entities. In the sciences, the temperature of a gas is reduced to the average kinetic energy of its molecules, and some thinkers attempt to reduce biology to [[Physics|physics]] and [[Chemistry|chemistry]]. Reductionism does not preclude emergent phenomena, but implies they can be understood completely in terms of the processes from which they are composed.

=== KEY: Reflection_physics_5cdf02
HEADWORD: Reflection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acoustics | Fiber | Gamma ray | Mirror | Radar | Radio | Sonar | X-ray
ENTRY:
Change in direction of a wave at an interface between two media, so that the wave returns into the medium it came from. For specular reflection, as from a [[Mirror|mirror]], the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection. Specular reflection forms images, while diffuse reflection from rough surfaces scatters the wave in many directions. === KEY: Reflex_d37f7e
HEADWORD: Reflex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Breathing | Digestion | Infant | Medicine | Nervous system
ENTRY:
Involuntary, unplanned and nearly instantaneous response to a stimulus. A stimulus initiates a neural signal that travels along a reflex arc, and these signals do not always reach the brain. The simplest reflexes involve a single synapse. The startle reflex gives an automatic response to an unexpected stimulus, and the feline righting reflex reorients a falling cat for a safe landing. Reflexes are used in [[Medicine|medicine]] to assess the [[Nervous system|nervous system]]: doctors grade them from 0 to 4, with 2+ considered normal. Decreased reflexes generally indicate a peripheral problem, and lively or exaggerated reflexes a central one. Newborn [[Infant|infants]] have primitive reflexes, such as the rooting and Moro reflexes, that are not seen in adults. Processes such as [[Breathing|breathing]] and [[Digestion|digestion]] are regarded as reflex actions under some definitions.

=== KEY: Reformation_20cac4
HEADWORD: Reformation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anglicanism | Counter-Reformation | John Calvin | Martin Luther | Ninety-five Theses | Printing press | Protestantism
ENTRY:
16th-century theological movement in Western Christianity, also called the Protestant Reformation, that challenged the authority of the papacy and the Catholic Church hierarchy and began [[Protestantism]]. It is considered one of the events that signified the end of the Middle Ages and the beginning of the early modern period in Europe.

The Reformation is usually dated from [[Martin Luther]]'s publication of the [[Ninety-five Theses]] in 1517, which gave rise to Lutheranism; the most common starting date is October 31, 1517, when Luther allegedly nailed a copy to the door of the castle church in Wittenberg. Scholars dispute when the era ended, and many now stress that it was a set of parallel movements rather than one coherent phenomenon.

The Reformers argued that justification rests on faith in Jesus Christ alone, not on faith together with charitable acts, as in the Catholic view. Protestant groups disagree on doctrines such as the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist: Lutherans accept a corporeal presence, the Reformed a spiritual one.

The [[Printing press|printing press]] enabled rapid dissemination of religious material in local vernaculars. From Saxony the movement diversified: Zwingli and [[John Calvin]] developed the Continental Reformed tradition, and Thomas Cranmer and John Knox led the Reformation in England and Scotland, giving rise to [[Anglicanism]] and Presbyterianism. Henry VIII of England used the movement to assert national authority over religious affairs. Radical Reformers such as the Anabaptists sought to return to the practices of early Christianity. The Catholic response, the [[Counter-Reformation]], included the Council of Trent. The ensuing wars of religion, including the Schmalkaldic War and the French Wars of Religion, saw the deaths of between 7 and 17 million people.

=== KEY: Refraction_fb2aa8
HEADWORD: Refraction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Human eye | Lens | Light | Prism (optics) | Rainbow | Refractive index | Snell's law
ENTRY:
Redirection of a wave as it passes from one medium to another, caused by a change in its speed or in the medium. Refraction of [[Light|light]] is the most commonly observed type; sound and water waves also refract. The amount of bending depends on the change in wave speed and the initial direction of the wave. For light, refraction follows [[Snell's law]], which relates the sines of the angles of incidence and refraction to the [[Refractive index|refractive indices]] of the two media. Light slows in a medium such as air, glass or water, and when it enters at an angle, one side of the wavefront slows first, bending its path. Because the refractive index varies with wavelength, white light is split into colors, as in [[Prism (optics)|prisms]] and [[Rainbow|rainbows]]. The [[Human eye|human eye]] and [[Lens|lenses]] use refraction to focus light.

=== KEY: Refractive_index_cff15c
HEADWORD: Refractive index
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diamond | Prism (optics) | Rainbow | Snell's law | Thomas Young (scientist) | Water
ENTRY:
Ratio of the speed of light in a vacuum to its speed in a given optical medium, usually denoted n. === KEY: Relativistic_mechanics_47c058

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Branch of physics describing motion and forces at speeds comparable to that of light. Relativistic mechanics is compatible with [[Special relativity|special relativity]] and [[General relativity|general relativity]]. Unlike Galilean relativity, which permitted speeds above that of light, it extends classical mechanics to high velocities and energies and is consistent with electromagnetism. Like classical mechanics, it treats kinematics and dynamics, but it changes the concepts of space, time, mass, momentum and energy. Special relativity shows that mass, momentum and energy are aspects of the same physical quantity. Force remains the time derivative of momentum. Relativistic momentum is p = γmv, where γ is the Lorentz factor, and total energy is E = γmc², which reduces to E = mc² for an object at rest. === KEY: Religion_a1d603
HEADWORD: Religion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abrahamic religions | Buddhism | Christianity | Cicero | Hinduism | Islam | Philosophy of religion | Reformation
ENTRY:
Range of social-cultural systems of behaviors and practices, ethics, beliefs, worldviews, texts, sanctified places, prophecies, and organizations that generally relate humanity to supernatural, transcendental, and spiritual elements. There is no scholarly consensus on what precisely constitutes a religion, and the concept is described as essentially contested. Religions may contain the divine, sacredness, faith, or supernatural beings; those lacking such elements may be identified as secular religions.

The origin of religious belief is an open question; proposed explanations include awareness of individual death, a sense of community, and dreams. Religions have sacred histories, narratives, and mythologies, preserved in oral traditions, sacred texts, symbols, and holy places, that may attempt to explain the origin of life and the universe. Practice may include rituals, sermons, veneration of deities or saints, sacrifices, festivals, initiations, matrimonial and funerary services, meditation, prayer, music, art, dance, or public service.

There are an estimated 10,000 distinct religions worldwide, though nearly all have regionally based and relatively small followings. Four religions, [[Christianity]], [[Islam]], [[Hinduism]], and [[Buddhism]], account for over 73% of the world's population, and 97.6% of people either follow one of them or identify as nonreligious. The religiously unaffiliated include atheists, agnostics, and those who identify with no particular religion, though many of them hold religious beliefs. Many world religions are organized religions, including the [[Abrahamic religions]] (Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and the Baháʼí Faith), while folk religions, indigenous religions, and some Eastern religions are arguably less so. A portion of the world's population belongs to new religious movements.

The English word comes from Old French and Anglo-Norman and ultimately from Latin *religiō*. [[Cicero]] derived *religiō* from *relegere* ("to go over" or "consider carefully"); some modern scholars instead derive it from *religare* ("to bind"), an interpretation made prominent by St. Augustine following Lactantius. In antiquity *religiō* meant conscientiousness, a sense of right, or duty toward anything, including family, neighbors, and rulers, and often described emotions such as hesitation, caution, or anxiety rather than a relation to gods. When it entered English it meant a life bound by monastic vows. The compartmentalized concept, separating religious from worldly matters, was not used before the 1500s, when it came to distinguish the domain of the church from that of civil authorities.

Some scholars hold that religion is a modern concept, found in English texts from the 17th century, shaped by the splitting of Christendom in the [[Reformation]] and by contact with non-European cultures during the Age of Exploration. Ancient sacred texts, including the Bible and the Quran, had no word or concept of religion in their original languages. Hebrew has no precise equivalent, and Judaism does not clearly distinguish religious, national, racial, or ethnic identities. Some argue the term is inappropriate for non-Western cultures, and many cultures reject or lack a distinction between the natural and the supernatural. The study of religion spans theology, [[Philosophy of religion|philosophy of religion]], comparative religion, and the social sciences.

=== KEY: Religion_in_ancient_Rome_233a23

Polytheistic religion of ancient Rome, combining state cults, household worship and provincial practices. Roman religion was practical and contractual, based on the reciprocal principle of *do ut des*, and depended on correct ritual rather than faith or dogma. Romans attributed their success to their collective piety (*pietas*) toward the gods. Greek influence introduced the cult of [[Apollo|Apollo]], while augury derived from Etruscan religion. Ethical and moral codes taught by religious traditions. Examples of religiously derived legal codes include Christian canon law, Jewish [[Halakha]], Islamic [[Sharia|sharia]] and Hindu law. In some jurisdictions, religious law applies only to that religion's adherents; in others, civil authorities may enforce it for all residents. In Buddhism, the Patimokkha is a code of 227 rules and principles followed by monks and nuns. In [[Christianity]], the Mosaic Law, which Christians take from the [[Old Testament|Old Testament]], is also called divine or biblical law; the [[Ten Commandments]] are its most famous example. Some Christian denominations contrast law with grace, the contrast speaking to an attempt to gain [[Salvation|salvation]] by obedience to a code of laws rather than through faith.

=== KEY: Rembrandt_2302b9

Dutch Golden Age painter, printmaker, and draughtsman, in full Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn. === KEY: Ren_Descartes_20b6f7
HEADWORD: Descartes, René
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Analytic geometry | Cartesian coordinate system | Epistemology | Modern philosophy | Philosophy of mind | Rationalism | Stockholm
ENTRY:
French philosopher, mathematician, and scientist whose work was foundational to mathematics and [[Modern philosophy|modern philosophy]] and influenced early modern science. He joined geometry and algebra into [[Analytic geometry|analytic geometry]], introduced a systematic method of inquiry, and made major contributions to [[Epistemology|epistemology]], [[Philosophy of mind|philosophy of mind]], and metaphysics. The [[Cartesian coordinate system]] is named after him.

Born in La Haye en Touraine, he entered the Jesuit College of La Flèche in 1607, where he was introduced to mathematics and physics. In 1618 he joined the Protestant Dutch States Army at Breda and studied military engineering. According to Adrien Baillet, three dreams in November 1619 convinced him that a divine spirit had revealed a new philosophy. In 1623 he sold his property and invested in bonds, which gave him a comfortable income for life. Urged to publish his philosophy beyond the reach of the Inquisition, he returned to the Dutch Republic in 1628. His daughter Francine, born in 1635, died of scarlet fever aged 5; he wept at her death.

His best-known statement is *cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am"), offered as the foundation of certain knowledge amid radical doubt. In *La Géométrie* (1637) he showed how curves can be represented by equations, work credited with facilitating the discovery of infinitesimal calculus. His *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) argued for [[Rationalism|rationalism]] and for dualism between thinking substance and extended substance; critics have objected that he failed to explain how the two interact. He proposed a mechanistic view of nature, though specific hypotheses such as his vortex cosmology were later disproved. Summoned to tutor Queen Christina in [[Stockholm]], he died there of pneumonia.

=== KEY: Renaissance_bf1cc7
HEADWORD: Renaissance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Florence | Leonardo da Vinci | Michelangelo | Perspective (graphical) | Petrarch
ENTRY:
European period of history and cultural movement at the end of the Late Middle Ages and the beginning of the early modern era, characterized by the rediscovery and revival of the literary, philosophical, and artistic achievements of classical antiquity. It is variously defined as covering the 15th and 16th centuries or, more broadly, the 14th to the 17th.

The Renaissance was first centered in the Republic of [[Florence]], then spread to the rest of Italy and later throughout Europe. Proposed explanations include the patronage of the Medici family and the migration of Greek scholars to Italy after the fall of Constantinople in 1453. In Italy the period conventionally ends with the waning of humanism and the advent of the Reformation (1517), the Sack of Rome (1527), or the Counter-Reformation (1545).

Its intellectual basis was a humanism derived from the Roman concept of *humanitas* and the rediscovery of classical Greek philosophy. It brought a flowering of literary Latin and of vernacular literatures, beginning with the 14th-century resurgence of learning that contemporaries credited to [[Petrarch]]. In painting it developed linear [[Perspective (graphical)|perspective]] for rendering a more natural reality. Polymaths such as [[Leonardo da Vinci]] and [[Michelangelo]] inspired the term "Renaissance man". Metal movable type sped the spread of ideas from the later 15th century, and the period saw revolutions in navigation and the introduction of modern banking and accounting.

Historians debate the term. The traditional view treats the Renaissance as a break from the past, while many historians today emphasize its medieval aspects and regard it as an extension of the Middle Ages. Social and economic historians stress continuity between the two eras, linked, as Panofsky observed, "by a thousand ties".

=== KEY: Renewable_energy_9be0c5
HEADWORD: Renewable energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Fossil fuel | Geothermal power | Greenhouse gas | Hydropower | Nuclear power | Solar energy | Wind power
ENTRY:
Energy, also called green energy, made from natural resources that are replenished on a human timescale. The most widely used types are [[Solar energy|solar energy]], [[Wind power|wind power]], bioenergy or biomass, and [[Hydropower|hydropower]]; [[Geothermal power|geothermal power]] is also significant in some countries. [[Nuclear power]] is generally not considered renewable because it relies on non-renewable fuel; some consider it renewable, which is controversial.

The term is not synonymous with low-carbon energy: burning biomass can be very carbon-intensive if not offset by new plantings. Renewable systems have become more efficient and cheaper over the past 40 years. From 2011 to 2021, renewables grew from 20% to 28% of global electricity supply; solar and wind rose from a combined 2% to 10%, while fossil energy fell from 67% to 62%. In 2025 renewables supplied 34% of global electricity generation, up from 32% in 2024.

The main motivation is to reduce emissions of [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gases]] from [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]], which cause [[Climate change|climate change]]. Renewables also cause much less air pollution and are more evenly distributed around the world than fossil fuels. At the 2023 United Nations Climate Change Conference, around three-quarters of the world's countries set a goal of tripling renewable capacity by 2030. The International Energy Agency estimates that 90% of global electricity must come from renewables to reach net zero emissions by 2050, and the current pace of expansion remains far from that rate.

Obstacles include fossil fuel subsidies, lobbying by incumbent power providers, and local opposition to land use. Solar and wind are intermittent, with lower capacity factors than plants that can match grid demand precisely. Mining of minerals needed for renewable technologies also causes environmental damage.

=== KEY: Reproduction_8e2d9c
HEADWORD: Reproduction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asexual reproduction | Bryophyte | Cloning | Meiosis | Parthenogenesis | Sexual reproduction | Sperm
ENTRY:
Biological process by which new individual organisms, or offspring, are produced from their parent or parents. It takes two forms, asexual and sexual.

In [[Asexual reproduction|asexual reproduction]], an organism creates genetically similar or identical copies of itself without genetic material from another organism. It is not limited to single-celled organisms. Bacteria divide by binary fission, yeasts and hydras reproduce by budding, and viruses take control of host cells to produce more viruses. Most plants can reproduce vegetatively, without seeds or spores, and the ant *Mycocepurus smithii* is thought to reproduce entirely asexually. [[Cloning|Cloning]] is a form of asexual reproduction. Other forms include fragmentation, spore formation involving only mitosis, and [[Parthenogenesis|parthenogenesis]], the development of an embryo or seed without fertilization, which occurs naturally in some plants, invertebrates such as water fleas and aphids, some reptiles and fish, and very rarely domestic birds. Many species capable of asexual reproduction, including hydras, yeasts, and jellyfish, may also reproduce sexually. Asexual reproduction allows swift proliferation, but it limits adaptability because deleterious mutations accumulate over generations, a phenomenon known as Muller's ratchet.

[[Sexual reproduction|Sexual reproduction]] combines the genetic material of two organisms in a process that starts with [[Meiosis|meiosis]], a specialized cell division that creates haploid gametes. Typically a [[Sperm|sperm]] cell fertilizes an egg cell of the same species to form a zygote, so that each parent contributes half of the offspring's genetic makeup. In species with two kinds of gametes (anisogamy), the sexes are male, producing sperm or microspores, and female, producing ova or megaspores. In isogamous species the gametes look alike, as in the green alga *Chlamydomonas reinhardtii*, which has "plus" and "minus" gametes; many fungi and the ciliate *Paramecium aurelia* have more than two mating types. Most animals, including humans, and most plants reproduce sexually, and offspring inherit one allele for each trait from each parent. Over 99.99% of eukaryotes are reported to reproduce sexually at least periodically, which shuffles alleles and purges harmful mutations. The evolution of sexual reproduction is nonetheless a major puzzle for biologists because of its two-fold cost: only 50% of organisms reproduce, and organisms pass on only 50% of their genes.

In [[Bryophyte|bryophytes]] the larger, commonly seen organisms are haploid and produce gametes; the gametes fuse to form a zygote that develops into a sporangium producing haploid spores. Cross-fertilization of flowers by pollen from a different plant is called allogamy; self-fertilization, or autogamy, occurs in hermaphroditic organisms such as many vascular plants, some foraminiferans, and some ciliates. Mitosis occurs in somatic cells and yields daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent; meiosis yields four haploid cells with half that number. In animals, gametes arise by meiosis in the gonads: sperm by spermatogenesis and eggs by oogenesis.

=== KEY: Reptile_aa6451
HEADWORD: Reptile
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bird | Carboniferous | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Crocodile | Lizard | Snake | Tuatara | Turtle
ENTRY:
Class of tetrapod vertebrate animals with an ectothermic metabolism and amniotic development. Reptiles traditionally comprise four orders: Testudines ([[Turtle|turtles]]), Crocodilia ([[Crocodile|crocodiles]], alligators, and gharials), Squamata ([[Lizard|lizards]] and [[Snake|snakes]]), and Rhynchocephalia (the [[Tuatara|tuatara]]). About 12,000 extant species are listed in the Reptile Database, and Squamata makes up about 96% of species. The study of reptiles, customarily combined with that of amphibians, is called herpetology. Modern non-bird reptiles inhabit all continents except Antarctica.

Taxonomic definitions conflict. Classical evolutionary taxonomy treats reptiles as the class Reptilia, matching common usage. Modern cladistic taxonomy regards that group as paraphyletic, because [[Bird|birds]] are the only surviving group of Dinosauria and are nested among reptiles; many cladistic systems therefore redefine Reptilia as a clade that includes birds, though the definition varies between authors.

The earliest known members of the reptile lineage appeared in the late [[Carboniferous]] period. The two largest lineages, Archosauromorpha (crocodilians, birds, and kin) and Lepidosauromorpha (lizards and kin), diverged during the Permian. The [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]] wiped out the pterosaurs, plesiosaurs, and all non-avian dinosaurs.

Extant reptiles range from the Jaragua dwarf gecko (*Sphaerodactylus ariasae*), which grows only to 17 mm, to the saltwater crocodile (*Crocodylus porosus*), which can exceed 6 m in length and 1,000 kg in weight. Unlike amphibians, reptiles do not depend on water for reproduction and have no aquatic larval stage. Most lay shelled eggs, though several squamates are viviparous. Of reptile species, 21.1% were assessed as threatened with extinction in the 2022 global assessment.

=== KEY: Republic_0dd6c4
HEADWORD: Republic
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Form of government in which political power rests with the public, typically through representatives, in contrast to an [[Absolute monarchy|absolute monarchy]] or a [[Democracy|direct democracy]]. The word derives from the Latin *res publica*, meaning "public thing" or "people's thing." The term took its meaning from the [[Constitution|constitution]] of the [[Roman Republic|Roman Republic]], established after the overthrow of the kings in 509 BC. That constitution included a senate of wealthy aristocrats, popular assemblies and a series of magistracies. As of 2017, 159 of the world's 206 [[Sovereign state|sovereign states]] used "republic" in their official names, though not all of them have elected governments. [[Cicero|Cicero]] translated the Greek *politeia* into Latin as *res publica*, and [[Renaissance|Renaissance]] scholars rendered it as "republic." During the Protectorate of [[Oliver Cromwell|Oliver Cromwell]], "commonwealth" was the most common term for the new monarchless state.

Socratic dialogue by [[Plato|Plato]], written around 375 BC, concerning [[Justice|justice]], the just [[City-state|city-state]] and the just man. Known in Greek as *Politeia*, it is Plato's best-known work and one of the most influential works of [[Philosophy|philosophy]] and political theory. [[Socrates|Socrates]] examines the meaning of justice and whether the just man is happier than the unjust man. After considering existing regimes, he proposes a series of hypothetical cities, culminating in Kallipolis, a [[Utopia|utopian]] city-state ruled by a class of philosopher-kings. The dialogue also addresses aging, love, the [[Theory of forms|theory of forms]], the immortality of the [[Soul|soul]], and the role of poetry in society.

=== KEY: Republic_of_Venice_2c184c

Maritime republic centered on Venice, northeastern [[Italy]] (697–1797). Founded, according to tradition, in 697, it grew over about 1,100 years into one of Europe's major commercial and naval powers, annexing much of northeastern Italy, Istria, Dalmatia and numerous Adriatic and eastern Ionian islands. Between the 13th and 16th centuries it also governed [[Crete]], [[Cyprus]], the Peloponnese and several Greek islands. In 1509 a coalition of the [[Habsburg monarchy]], Spain and France defeated Venice at the Battle of Agnadello, though it kept most of its mainland possessions. Wars with the [[Ottoman Empire]] ended with the Treaty of Passarowitz in 1718, after which Venice had lost all its Aegean possessions. Occupied by [[Napoleon]]'s French troops in 1797, the republic was divided with the Habsburg monarchy under the Treaty of Campo Formio. Its head of state, the doge, was elective from the late 9th century, and its administration was directed by the Great Council, Senate and Council of Ten.

=== KEY: Research_f027f2
HEADWORD: Research
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Academic journal | Bias | Humanities | Hypothesis | Peer review
ENTRY:
Creative and systematic work undertaken to increase the stock of knowledge. It involves collecting, organizing, and analyzing evidence to increase understanding of a topic, with particular attention to controlling sources of [[Bias|bias]] and error. The word derives from Middle French *recherche*, "to go about seeking", and its earliest recorded use in English was in 1577.

Basic research, as opposed to applied research, aims at documentation, discovery, interpretation, and the development of methods and systems for advancing human knowledge; applied research targets solvable problems. Approaches depend on epistemologies, which vary considerably within and between the humanities and sciences. Forms include scientific, humanities, artistic, economic, social, business, and technological research. The scientific study of research practices is known as meta-research.

Original, or primary, research is not exclusively based on a summary or synthesis of earlier publications; its purpose is to produce new knowledge. Originality is a major criterion for publication in [[Academic journal|academic journals]], usually established by [[Peer review|peer review]]. Formal scientific research generally follows steps that vary by subject: choice of a topic, formation of a [[Hypothesis|hypothesis]], definitions of concepts and variables, data gathering with valid and reliable instruments, analysis, interpretation, testing and revision of the hypothesis, and conclusion. Research in the [[Humanities|humanities]] uses methods such as hermeneutics and semiotics; scholars there usually explore the issues and context surrounding a question rather than seek an ultimate answer.

Modern research methods are said to have evolved from the empirical traditions of the 17th century. Widespread failures to replicate findings, termed the replication crisis, particularly in psychology and biomedicine, have prompted calls for preregistration, larger samples, and transparent replication.

=== KEY: Reservoir_c79f7a
HEADWORD: Reservoir
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aqueduct (water supply) | Dam | Lake | Levee | Pipeline | River Thames | Siphon
ENTRY:
Enlarged [[Lake|lake]] behind a [[Dam|dam]], usually built to store fresh water and often doubling for hydroelectric power generation. Reservoirs can be created by controlling a watercourse that drains an existing body of water, by interrupting a watercourse to form an embayment, by excavation, or by building retaining walls or [[Levee|levees]] around an area. Dammed reservoirs flood a valley, with the dam built at its narrowest practical point. On-stream reservoirs lie on the original streambed and are filled by creeks, rivers or runoff, while off-stream reservoirs receive water diverted through an [[Aqueduct (water supply)|aqueduct]] or [[Pipeline|pipeline]]. Bank-side reservoirs store water [[Siphon|siphoned]] or pumped from rivers of variable quality, enclosed by embankments that may exceed 6 km (4 miles) in circumference. Water may stay in them for months, during which contaminants are reduced. London's water supply draws from the [[River Thames]] and River Lea into such storage.
