=== KEY: Volt_e991c9
HEADWORD: Volt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Ampere | Coulomb | International System of Units | Joule | Watt
ENTRY:
[[International System of Units|SI]] derived unit of electric potential, potential difference (voltage) and electromotive force, symbol V. It is named for [[Alessandro Volta]]. One volt is the potential difference between two points of a conducting wire when a current of one [[Ampere]] dissipates one [[Watt]] of power. Equivalently, it is one [[Joule]] of energy per [[Coulomb]] of charge, and in SI base units it equals kg·m²·s⁻³·A⁻¹. From 1990 to 2019 the volt was realized through the Josephson effect using a conventional constant, and since the 2019 revision of the SI the Josephson constant has had an exact value. Nominal voltages include about 1.5 V for an alkaline cell and 3 V for a lithium coin cell.

=== KEY: Voltage_4a92fb
HEADWORD: Voltage
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capacitor | Electric charge | Electric current | Electric field | Electromotive force | Volt
ENTRY:
Difference in electric potential between two points, measured in [[Volt|volts]] (V). Also called potential difference or electric tension, voltage is the work needed per unit of charge to move a positive test charge from one point to another in a static [[Electric field|electric field]]. It can arise from a build-up of [[Electric charge]], as in a [[Capacitor]], or from an [[Electromotive force]], as in electromagnetic induction in a generator. Electrochemical cells and batteries also produce voltage. A voltmeter measures the voltage between two points, often using a common reference such as the ground of a system. Conventional [[Electric current|current]] in a wire flows from higher voltage to lower voltage. Household wiring is typically 120 V in North America and 230 V in Europe, and high-voltage transmission lines can reach up to 1,100,000 V.

French Enlightenment writer, philosopher and satirist. Born François-Marie Arouet in Paris, he adopted the pen name Voltaire. He wrote in almost every literary form, including plays, poems, novels, essays and histories, and produced more than 20,000 letters and 2,000 books and pamphlets. An outspoken advocate of [[Freedom of speech|freedom of speech]], [[Freedom of religion|freedom of religion]] and the separation of church and state, he criticized the [[Catholic Church]] and slavery, and he was twice imprisoned and once briefly exiled to England. === KEY: Volume_bd7a97
HEADWORD: Volume
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Archimedes | Euclid's Elements | Gallon | Henry III of England | Isaac Newton | Litre | Mesopotamia
ENTRY:
Measure of the three-dimensional space occupied by a region or enclosed by a surface. Volume is quantified with SI derived units such as the cubic meter and the [[Litre|liter]], or with imperial and U.S. customary units such as the [[Gallon|gallon]], quart and cubic inch. The volume of a container is generally understood as its capacity, the amount of gas or liquid it could hold, rather than the space the container itself displaces. Simple shapes have volumes given by arithmetic formulas; more complicated shapes can be handled with integral calculus if a formula exists for their boundary.

The earliest evidence of volume calculation comes from [[Ancient Egypt]] and [[Mesopotamia]], as problems approximating the volume of cuboids, cylinders, frustums and cones, preserved in the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (c. 1820 BC). The last three books of [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]] (c. 300 BC) gave exact formulas for parallelepipeds, cones, pyramids, cylinders and spheres. [[Archimedes]] devised approximate formulas for several shapes by the method of exhaustion.

The many volume units of the Middle Ages led [[Henry III of England]] to standardize them in the Assize of Bread and Ale (1258), which introduced the gallon and bushel. In the early 17th century Bonaventura Cavalieri applied the ideas of integral calculus to the volume of any object; Pierre de Fermat, [[Isaac Newton]], Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and others extended this in the 17th and 18th centuries into modern calculus.

On April 7, 1795, French law defined the metric system, including the liter (1 dm³) for liquids. In 1824 the imperial gallon was defined as the volume of ten pounds of water at 17 °C; the Weights and Measures Act 1985 fixed it at precisely 4.54609 liters.

Group of individuals who agree to form a body, usually as volunteers, to pursue a common purpose. Also called a voluntary organization or common-interest association, it includes trade associations, [[Trade union|trade unions]], learned societies, professional associations, service organizations and environmental groups. Members may join or leave freely, although occupational closure can make membership effectively required in some fields. Voluntary groups existed in [[Ancient Greece]], from elite clubs of wealthy men to religious and professional associations, and in preindustrial Western Europe [[Guild|guilds]] often administered towns.

Spaceflight of the [[Vostok programme]] and the first human orbital flight in history. Launched from Baikonur Cosmodrome on April 12, 1961, the Vostok 3KA capsule carried Soviet cosmonaut [[Yuri Gagarin]] through a single orbit, making him the first human to reach orbital velocity and complete a full orbit of the Earth. ENTRY:
Soviet human spaceflight project that put the first cosmonauts into [[Low Earth orbit|low Earth orbit]] and returned them safely. Competing with the United States' Project Mercury, it carried out six crewed flights between 1961 and 1963. Its capsule was developed from the Zenit spy satellite, and its launch vehicle was adapted from the R-7 Semyorka [[Intercontinental ballistic missile|intercontinental ballistic missile]] design. The first flight, [[Vostok 1]], carried [[Yuri Gagarin]] into a single orbit on April 12, 1961. The longest flight lasted nearly five days, and the last four flights were launched in pairs, one day apart. The project was succeeded in 1964 and 1965 by two Voskhod flights, which used a larger launch rocket and modified capsules.

=== KEY: Voting_b6fb89

ENTRY:
Process of making collective decisions by submitting and adding up individual choices. Voting is most often used to elect representatives, but it can also decide policy, usually by majority, sometimes by a super-majority, or through a [[Referendum|referendum]]. Ballots may be formal, with specific rules, or informal, using raised hands or voice votes. === KEY: Vowel_7e9352
HEADWORD: Vowel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consonant | International Phonetic Alphabet | Prosody (linguistics) | Syllable
ENTRY:
Speech sound pronounced with minimal or no obstruction in the vocal tract. Vowels are one of the two principal classes of speech sounds, the other being the [[Consonant|consonant]]. They are usually voiced, vary in quality, loudness and length, and are closely involved in [[Prosody (linguistics)|prosodic]] variation such as tone, intonation and stress. The nucleus of a [[Syllable|syllable]] is typically a vowel, though some languages allow other sounds, such as syllabic l in the English word table, to form the nucleus. The word derives from Latin *vocalis*, meaning 'vocal'. Phonetically, a vowel is produced with an open vocal tract, whereas consonants involve a constriction or closure. Vowel quality is usually described by tongue height, tongue backness and lip rounding, a scheme shown on the [[International Phonetic Alphabet]] vowel diagram. In writing, the letters a, e, i, o, u and sometimes y represent vowel sounds.

American scientific program that employs two interstellar probes, [[Voyager 1]] and [[Voyager 2]]. [[Latin|Latin]] translation of the [[Bible]] from the late 4th century, largely the work of [[Jerome]]. Pope Damasus I commissioned Jerome in 382 to revise the Vetus Latina Gospels, and he extended the work to most of the books of the Bible, translating much of the [[Hebrew Bible]] directly from Hebrew. By the 13th century the text was known as *versio vulgata*, meaning the version commonly used, from which its name derives. The compound text also includes older Vetus Latina translations that Jerome did not revise.

ENTRY:
British evolutionary biologist. His theoretical work gave a rigorous genetic basis for altruism, an insight central to the gene-centered view of evolution, and he is considered a forerunner of sociobiology. His two 1964 papers, "The Genetical Evolution of Social Behaviour," introduced Hamilton's rule: a costly action should be performed when the benefit to the recipient, weighted by the genetic relatedness of actor and recipient, outweighs the cost to the actor. He studied at the [[University of Cambridge]] and from 1984 was a Royal Society Research Professor at Oxford University. [[Richard Dawkins]] has written that Hamilton was "the greatest Darwinian of my lifetime."

=== KEY: W_and_Z_bosons_cb2b02
HEADWORD: W and Z bosons
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beta decay | CERN | Neutrino | Photon | Standard Model | Weak interaction
ENTRY:
Vector bosons that mediate the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]]. The W+ and W− bosons carry an electric charge of +1 and −1 elementary charge and are each other's antiparticles, while the Z0 boson is electrically neutral and is its own antiparticle. All three have spin 1 and are very short-lived, with a half-life of about 3×10⁻²⁵ s. The W boson's mass is 80.3692±0.0133 GeV and the Z boson's is 91.1880±0.0020 GeV. Their high masses limit the range of the weak interaction, in contrast to the massless [[Photon|photon]]. The W bosons are best known for their role in [[Beta decay|beta decay]], in which a neutron is converted into a proton while emitting an electron. The Z boson transfers momentum, spin and energy when [[Neutrino|neutrinos]] scatter from matter, without changing electric charge. Their discovery at [[CERN]] in 1983 was pivotal in establishing the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics.

Tragicomedy in two acts by Irish playwright [[Samuel Beckett]]. === KEY: Walker_circulation_801606
HEADWORD: Walker circulation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric circulation | Famine | Monsoon | Pacific Ocean
ENTRY:
Conceptual model of air flow in the tropical lower [[Atmospheric circulation|atmosphere]], also called the Walker cell. In this model air parcels follow a closed circulation in the zonal and vertical directions, driven by differences in heat distribution between ocean and land. It is associated with a pressure gradient between a high-pressure system over the eastern [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific]] and a low-pressure system over Indonesia. From an oceanographic point of view, the equatorial cold tongue is caused by easterly winds. As originally proposed, the circulation weakens during El Niño events, when the temperature gradient across the Pacific and the easterlies are reduced, and strengthens during La Niña events. The name honors Gilbert Walker, who discovered the Southern Oscillation in the early 20th century while studying the Indian Ocean [[Monsoon|monsoon]], whose failed rains had caused severe [[Famine|famine]] in 1899. Norwegian-American meteorologist Jacob Bjerknes first used the term in 1969.

=== KEY: Walking_db6ea7
HEADWORD: Walking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hiking | Running
ENTRY:
Main gait of terrestrial locomotion among legged animals, typically slower than [[Running|running]]. Walking is defined as an "inverted pendulum" gait in which the body vaults over stiff limbs with each step, a pattern shared by arthropods with six, eight or more limbs. In humans, walking differs from running in that only one foot at a time leaves the ground, with a period of double support, while running begins when both feet leave the ground. === KEY: War_ab4b14
HEADWORD: War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biological warfare | Bronze Age | Chemical weapon | Civil war | Gunpowder | Insurgency | Nuclear weapon | Old English
ENTRY:
Armed conflict between the armed forces of states, or between governmental forces and armed groups organized under a command structure and able to sustain military operations, or between such organized groups. War is generally characterized by widespread violence, destruction and mortality, fought with regular or irregular forces, and its aims are typically political, economic or territorial. Warfare refers to the activities and characteristics of types of war, or of wars in general. Total war is warfare not restricted to legitimate military targets and can cause massive civilian suffering.

The English word derives from the 11th-century [[Old English]] *wyrre* and *werre*, from Old French *werre*, from Frankish *\*werra*, ultimately from Proto-Germanic *\*werzō* "mixture, confusion."

Anthropologists disagree about whether warfare was common throughout prehistory or arose later, after agriculture or organized states. It is hard to establish for the Paleolithic because few remains survive; evidence of violent conflict appears to increase in the Mesolithic, from about 10,000 years ago. In his 1996 book *War Before Civilization*, Lawrence H. Keeley estimates that approximately 90–95% of known societies engaged in at least occasional warfare. Since the rise of the state some 5,000 years ago, military activity has continued over much of the globe. The oldest known battlefield in Europe is thought to date to 1250 BC, and the [[Bronze Age]] has been described as a key period in the intensification of warfare. The use of [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]] brought major changes to war itself. Charles Tilly argued that "war made the state, and the state made war."

Since 1945, great power wars, territorial conquests and war declarations have declined in frequency, wars have been increasingly regulated by international humanitarian law, and battle deaths have declined, partly owing to military medicine. Scholars debate whether war is in decline: Joshua Goldstein, John Mueller and Steven Pinker argue that it is, while Bear F. Braumoeller challenges the claim. [[Civil war|Civil wars]] have increased in absolute terms since 1945, and combat has largely been a matter of civil wars and insurgencies. The major interstate exceptions include the Korean War, the Indo-Pakistani War of 1971, the Iran–Iraq War, the Gulf War, the Eritrean–Ethiopian War and the Russo-Ukrainian War.

Types of warfare include asymmetric warfare, between belligerents of drastically different capability; [[Biological warfare]], using infectious agents or toxins; [[Chemical weapon|chemical warfare]], in which poison gas in World War I caused over a million estimated casualties, including more than 100,000 civilians; cold warfare, an intense rivalry without direct military conflict; conventional warfare; cyberwarfare; [[Insurgency|insurgency]], a rebellion by irregular forces against authority; information warfare; [[Nuclear weapon|nuclear warfare]]; radiological warfare; and total war. One estimate puts deaths from the 20th century's major conflicts at 100–231 million when direct violence, starvation, disease and related political decisions are combined.

=== KEY: Waste_977fe3
HEADWORD: Waste
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Union | Groundwater | Invention | Landfill | OECD | Recycling
ENTRY:
Unwanted or unusable material, any substance discarded after primary use or regarded as worthless or defective. A by-product, by contrast, is a joint product of relatively minor economic value. A waste product may become a by-product, joint product or resource through an [[Invention|invention]] that raises its value above zero. Examples include municipal solid waste (household garbage), hazardous waste, wastewater, radioactive waste and others.

The Basel Convention (1989) defines wastes as substances or objects disposed of, intended to be disposed of, or required to be disposed of by national law. The [[European Union]]'s Waste Framework Directive (2008/98/EC) defines waste as "an object the holder discards, intends to discard or is required to discard."

The [[OECD]] defines municipal solid waste as "waste collected and treated by or for municipalities," typically household, commercial, and demolition or construction waste. In 2018 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) concluded that 292.4 million short tons of municipal waste were generated, about 4.9 pounds per person per day; approximately 69 million tons were recycled and 25 million tons composted. Most collected household waste ends up in [[Landfill|landfills]]. The EPA estimated that the U.S. generated about 600 million tons of construction and demolition waste in 2018. U.S. hazardous waste, defined as waste capable of harming health or the environment, is regulated under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act.

Globally, municipal solid waste reached 2.24 billion tonnes in 2020, or 0.79 kg per person per day, and is projected to rise to 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050. Only about 13.5% of waste is [[Recycling|recycled]] worldwide. Open dumping in developing areas contributes to methane emissions and [[Groundwater|groundwater]] contamination, and electronic waste exceeds 50 million tonnes yearly.

Processes that manage waste from its generation to final disposal, covering collection, transport, treatment, monitoring and regulation. In high-income countries waste is typically collected from the street and taken to sanitary [[Landfill|landfills]], incinerators or [[Recycling|recycling]] centers. In lower-income countries a large share is stored in open dumps or burned, and by one estimate about a quarter of municipal solid waste is not collected and another quarter is mismanaged. Poor practice pollutes air, water, soil and food. The waste hierarchy prioritizes prevention and reduction, then reuse, recycling and composting, energy recovery and, last, disposal. Landfills generate methane, a potent greenhouse gas, and recovery of plastics is under 10% worldwide. One projection forecasts a 70% rise in global waste by 2050 without policy changes.

Personal timepiece carried or worn by a person, designed to keep steady time despite the wearer's movements. Watches date from the 16th century, when German clockmakers in [[Nuremberg]] made the first wearable timepieces, although the credit given to Peter Henlein as inventor is uncertain. A balance spring, added to the balance wheel in 1657 in a disputed invention by [[Robert Hooke]] and [[Christiaan Huygens]], cut errors from several hours a day to about 10 minutes. In the 1960s the battery-powered [[Quartz|quartz]] watch was invented, and by the 1980s it dominated the market in the quartz revolution, which decimated the [[Switzerland|Swiss]] watch industry. In the 2010s [[Touchscreen|touchscreen]] smartwatches emerged, with functions far beyond timekeeping. Most timekeeping watches use quartz movements, while expensive collectible watches are valued for craftsmanship and mechanical movements.

=== KEY: Water_27634f
HEADWORD: Water
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Density | Hydrogen bond | Hydrothermal vent | Ocean | Thermohaline circulation | Triple point | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Inorganic compound with the chemical formula H₂O, a transparent, tasteless, odorless and nearly colorless liquid at room temperature. Water is the main constituent of Earth's streams, lakes and oceans and the fluid of all known living organisms, in which it acts as a solvent. It provides neither food energy nor organic micronutrients, yet it is vital to all known life. Because it dissolves more substances than any other liquid, it is often called the "universal solvent," though it dissolves nonpolar substances poorly. As found in nature it almost always contains dissolved substances, and special steps are needed to obtain chemically pure water.

The word comes from Old English *wæter*, from Proto-Germanic *\*watar*, from Proto-Indo-European *\*wod-or*.

Water is a polar molecule, and strong intermolecular [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonding]] largely determines its physical and chemical properties. Earth's surface temperature and pressure lie close to water's [[Triple point|triple point]], so water exists there as solid (ice), liquid and gas (water vapor or steam). Phase transitions are freezing, melting, vaporization, condensation, sublimation and deposition. At 1 atm, ice melts or water freezes at 0 °C and water boils at 100 °C; the boiling point falls by 1 °C every 274 m of altitude. Temperatures exceed 205 °C in the geyser Old Faithful and can exceed 400 °C in [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vents]], where water stays liquid under pressure.

Water is unusual in expanding as it cools within certain temperature ranges. At 1 atm its maximum [[Density|density]], 999.972 kg/m³, occurs at 3.98 °C. On freezing it expands by about 9%, reaching 917 kg/m³, which can burst pipes and crack rocks. Because ice floats and insulates the water below, most aquatic organisms survive winter in lakes. This anomalous behavior is also part of the [[Thermohaline circulation|thermohaline circulation]] that distributes heat around the oceans. Water is diamagnetic.

Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface. Seas and [[Ocean|oceans]] hold about 96.5% of the volume, groundwater 1.7%, glaciers and ice caps of Antarctica and Greenland 1.7%, and the air (vapor, clouds and precipitation) 0.001%. It moves continuously through the [[Water cycle|water cycle]] of evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and runoff, usually reaching the sea.

Water is central to the world economy. Approximately 70% of the fresh water humans use goes to agriculture, and fishing provides 6.5% of global protein. Much long-distance trade moves by boat on seas, rivers, lakes and canals. Water, ice and snow are also used for cooling, heating, industry, cooking and washing, and in sports such as swimming, surfing, diving, ice skating and skiing.

Series of about 250 oil paintings by [[Claude Monet]]. Monet painted the flower garden at his home in Giverny, France, and the series was the main focus of his output during the last 31 years of his life, many works made while he suffered from cataracts. The series grew from smaller canvases of the late 1890s to large, immersive panels. Chinese novel from the [[Ming dynasty]], one of the Four Great Classical Novels of Chinese literature. Attributed to Shi Nai'an, with possible contributions from Luo Guanzhong, it was among the first works of Chinese fiction written entirely in vernacular Chinese. Set during the [[Song dynasty|Northern Song]] period, it follows 108 outlaws who gather at Mount Liang to rebel against the government. Later they accept amnesty and fight the [[Liao dynasty]] and other rebels, though many meet tragic ends. The novel has been banned many times for its social critique. Its heroes, such as Song Jiang, Wu Song and Lin Chong, appear in later novels, operas and other media, and the critics Li Zhi and Jin Shengtan placed it among the greatest works of genius in Chinese literature.
