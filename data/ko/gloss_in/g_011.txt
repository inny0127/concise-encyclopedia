=== KEY: Atomic_nucleus_1b6d79
HEADWORD: Atomic nucleus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alpha particle | Ernest Rutherford | Nuclear physics | Werner Heisenberg
ENTRY:
Small, dense core of an atom, made of protons and neutrons. [[Ernest Rutherford]] discovered the nucleus in 1911 at the University of Manchester, based on the 1909 Geiger–Marsden gold foil experiment, in which many [[Alpha particle|alpha particles]] were deflected at large angles. The finding showed that an atom’s positive charge and mass are concentrated in a dense center. After the discovery of the neutron in 1932, Dmitri Ivanenko and [[Werner Heisenberg]] developed models of a nucleus of protons and neutrons. Almost all of an atom’s mass lies in its nucleus, which is held together by the nuclear force. Nuclear diameters range from about 1.70 fm for hydrogen to about 11.7 fm for uranium, roughly 27,000 to 60,000 times smaller than the atom. The branch of physics that studies it is [[Nuclear physics|nuclear physics]].

=== KEY: Atomic_number_663908
HEADWORD: Atomic number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ernest Rutherford | Hydrogen | Isotope | Periodic table | Uranium
ENTRY:
Number of protons in an atom’s nucleus, which uniquely identifies a chemical element. Its symbol Z comes from the German word *Zahl*, meaning number. In an uncharged atom it also equals the number of electrons, and for an atom with protons and neutrons, adding the neutron number gives the mass number. The natural elements run from 1 for [[Hydrogen]] to 92 for [[Uranium]]. Atoms with the same atomic number but different neutron numbers are [[Isotope|isotopes]]. In 1913 Antonius van den Broek proposed that nuclear charge equals an element’s position in the [[Periodic table]], and [[Ernest Rutherford]] used the term for that position. Henry Moseley’s X-ray spectroscopy in 1913–1914 showed that atomic number, rather than atomic weight, defines an element’s identity.

=== KEY: Atomic_orbital_7fa6b8
HEADWORD: Atomic orbital
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron configuration | Quantum mechanics | Spin (physics)
ENTRY:
Function describing the location and wave-like behavior of an electron in an atom. It can be used to calculate the probability of finding the electron in a region around the nucleus. Each orbital is characterized by three [[Quantum mechanics|quantum]] numbers, n, ℓ and mℓ, which give an electron’s energy, its orbital angular momentum and the projection of that momentum on an axis. An orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite [[Spin (physics)|spins]]. The names s, p, d and f refer to angular momentum values of 0, 1, 2 and 3, and they describe [[Electron configuration|electron configurations]]. Electrons exist as standing waves rather than orbiting like planets. The periodic table’s blocks of 2, 6, 10 and 14 elements arise from the number of electrons that fill complete s, p, d and f orbitals.

=== KEY: Atomic_physics_41b073
HEADWORD: Atomic physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Nuclear physics | Photon | Plasma (physics)
ENTRY:
Field of physics that studies atoms as systems of electrons and an atomic nucleus. It is primarily concerned with how electrons are arranged around the nucleus and how those arrangements change, including in ions. Unlike [[Nuclear physics|nuclear physics]], which studies nuclear reactions, it considers atoms in isolation, focusing on ionization and excitation by photons or collisions. Electrons absorb energy from light, magnetic fields or colliding particles, and an excited electron that drops to a lower state emits a [[Photon|photon]] of the energy difference. Because atomic and nuclear are synonymous in everyday English, the term is often associated with nuclear power and nuclear weapons. Atomic physics provides the underlying theory for [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] physics and atmospheric physics.

=== KEY: Attention_2aa97e
HEADWORD: Attention
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cognitive psychology | Consciousness | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Hermann von Helmholtz | Wilhelm Wundt
ENTRY:
Concentration of awareness directed at a task or phenomenon while mostly excluding others. In [[Cognitive psychology|cognitive psychology]], attention is often described as the allocation of limited processing resources to a subset of information, thoughts, or tasks. It is an umbrella term for several processes, including selective attention (prioritizing some stimuli over others), sustained attention (maintaining focus), divided attention (sharing resources across tasks), and orienting (shifting focus in space or time). Attention is closely linked to working memory, executive functions, and [[Consciousness|consciousness]]. [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] emphasized involuntary attention while also recognizing voluntary, directed attention. [[Wilhelm Wundt]] introduced its systematic study into psychology, and [[Hermann von Helmholtz]] showed that one can focus on one stimulus while still perceiving others.

=== KEY: Attention_deficit_hyperactivity_disorder_3f319d
HEADWORD: Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amphetamine | Mental disorder | Methylphenidate | Psychotherapy | Stimulant
ENTRY:
Neurodevelopmental disorder marked by inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation that are excessive, pervasive, and developmentally inappropriate. Its symptoms arise from executive dysfunction and can impair professional performance and relationships, predisposing to a diminished quality of life and reduced life expectancy. It is associated with other [[Mental disorder|mental disorders]]. Under the DSM-5, symptoms must be present for six months or more, with at least six symptoms of inattention or hyperactivity/impulsivity for those under 17 and at least five for those 17 or older. The disorder is primarily genetic, with a heritability rate of 70–80%. Treatment is most effective with medication, primarily [[Stimulant|stimulants]] such as [[Methylphenidate]] or [[Amphetamine|amphetamines]], often combined with [[Psychotherapy]]; exercise and dietary modifications have no proven benefit.

=== KEY: Auditory_system_b661b8
HEADWORD: Auditory system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amplitude | Brainstem | Cerebral cortex | Ear | Hearing | Sound
ENTRY:
Sensory system for [[Hearing|hearing]], comprising the [[Ear|ears]] and the central neural pathways that process [[Sound|sound]]. The outer ear funnels vibrations to the eardrum, and the middle-ear ossicles amplify the vibration pressure roughly 20 times. Outer hair cells in the cochlea amplify traveling wave [[Amplitude|amplitudes]] over 40-fold, and there are three to four times as many outer hair cells as inner hair cells. The basilar membrane is narrowest and stiffest at the cochlear base, where high frequencies are sensed, and widest and least stiff at the apex, where low frequencies are sensed. The cochlear nerve has 30,000+ fibers, each most sensitive to one frequency. The superior olivary complex, in the pons, is the first convergence of signals from both ears and helps determine the direction of sounds. Signals ascend to the [[Brainstem|brainstem]] and then to the [[Cerebral cortex|cerebral cortex]].

French civil engineer and optical physicist. === KEY: Augustus_d175b3
HEADWORD: Augustus
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Cleopatra | Julius Caesar | Mark Antony | Roman Empire | Roman Republic | Tiberius | Virgil
ENTRY:
Founder of the [[Roman Empire]] and its first emperor (r. 27 BC–AD 14), also known as Octavian. Born Gaius Octavius into an equestrian family, he was the great-nephew of [[Julius Caesar]], who named him primary heir in his will. After Caesar's assassination in 44 BC he inherited the estate and took the name. With [[Mark Antony]] and Marcus Lepidus he formed a triumvirate with legally sanctioned powers to outlaw and oppose Caesar's assassins. After victory at the Battle of Philippi in 42 BC they divided the [[Roman Republic]] among themselves. Octavian's naval commander Marcus Agrippa defeated Antony at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC, and Antony and [[Cleopatra]] killed themselves during the invasion of Egypt.

In 27 BC the Senate granted him the honorific Augustus, "the revered." He agreed to restore the façade of a free republic centered on the Senate, but control of the military and half of Rome's provinces preserved his autocratic power; critics have described the resulting principate as autocracy disguised in republican forms. He received the titles *princeps* ("first citizen") and *pater patriae* ("father of the country"), and the month of August was named after him.

Augustus annexed Egypt, Dalmatia, Pannonia, Noricum, and Raetia and completed the conquest of Hispania, though expansion suffered a major setback in Germania. He reformed taxation and currency, built roads with an official courier system, established a standing professional army and the Praetorian Guard, and created police and fire-fighting services for Rome. His reign began the Pax Romana, an era largely free of armed conflict. He was a patron of poets such as [[Virgil]]. The Senate deified him after his death, and his stepson and adoptive son [[Tiberius]] succeeded him.

Family (Alcidae) of seabirds in the order Charadriiformes, found throughout the [[Northern Hemisphere]]. === KEY: Authoritarianism_c7b1e3
HEADWORD: Authoritarianism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Civil liberties | Democracy | Separation of powers | Totalitarianism
ENTRY:
Political system characterized by the rejection of political plurality, the use of strong central power to preserve the political status quo, and reductions in [[Democracy|democracy]], [[Separation of powers|separation of powers]], [[Civil liberties|civil liberties]], and the rule of law. Authoritarian regimes may be autocratic or oligarchic and may rest on the rule of a party, the military, or a single person. States with a blurred boundary between democracy and authoritarianism are sometimes called hybrid regimes or competitive authoritarian states.

In his influential 1964 work *An Authoritarian Regime: Spain*, the political scientist Juan Linz defined authoritarianism by four qualities: limited political pluralism; legitimacy based on appeals to emotion and on presenting the regime as a necessary evil against problems such as underdevelopment or insurgency; minimal political mobilization, with suppression of anti-regime activity; and ill-defined executive powers used to extend the executive's reach. Minimally defined, an authoritarian government lacks free and competitive elections to legislatures, to executives, or both. Such states may keep nominally democratic institutions such as parties, legislatures, and elections that are managed to entrench the regime.

Power is highly concentrated and maintained by political repression. Adam Przeworski has theorized that "authoritarian equilibrium rests mainly on lies, fear and economic prosperity." Unlike [[Totalitarianism|totalitarian]] systems, which seek comprehensive ideological control, authoritarian regimes tend to tolerate some private economic activity and cultural expression that does not challenge their dominance. Constitutions in such states may serve as an "operating manual," a "billboard," a "blueprint," or "window dressing" for freedoms not honored in practice.

The share of authoritarian states in the international system rose from 1946 until the mid-1970s and declined until 2000. Before 2000, dictatorships typically began with a coup; since then they most often arise through democratic backsliding, in which an elected leader establishes an authoritarian regime.

The term necropsy is generally used for non-human animals. Autopsies are usually carried out in cases of sudden or unnatural death, under legal authority and without the consent of relatives. [[Judaism]] and [[Islam]] usually discourage them. In most Western countries the number performed in hospitals has declined every year since 1955. A systematic review found a major diagnostic error in about 25% of autopsies, a rate that has decreased over time.

=== KEY: Avalanche_e0f977
HEADWORD: Avalanche
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Rapid flow of [[Snow|snow]] down a slope, such as a hill or mountain. Avalanches may be triggered spontaneously by increased precipitation or weakening of the snowpack, or by people, animals or [[Earthquake|earthquakes]]. Large avalanches can capture and move ice, rocks and [[Tree|trees]]. Slab avalanches, in which tightly packed snow breaks along a weak layer, are the most hazardous type, while loose-snow avalanches start at a point and gather more snow as they descend. === KEY: Avicenna_fbf081

Persian polymath, physician, and philosopher of the Islamic world, known in Arabic as Ibn Sina. A seminal figure of the [[Islamic Golden Age]], he served in the courts of various Iranian rulers and influenced medieval European medical and Scholastic thought. His best-known works are *[[The Book of Healing]]*, a philosophical and scientific encyclopedia, and *[[The Canon of Medicine]]*, which became a standard text at many medieval European universities and remained in use as late as 1650.

Avicenna was born in the village of Afshana, near the Samanid capital of Bukhara, to a Persian family; his father, a Samanid official, came from Balkh in [[Bactria]]. === KEY: Axiom_2c5d94

ENTRY:
Statement accepted as true to serve as a premise for further reasoning. The word comes from the Greek *axíōma*, meaning “that which is thought worthy or fit.” In classical philosophy an axiom is self-evident, while in modern logic it is simply a starting point. A non-logical axiom in mathematics makes a substantive assertion about a domain, such as a + 0 = a in [[Integer|integer]] arithmetic, and is also called a postulate or assumption. The ancient Greeks developed the logico-deductive method, in which all other assertions, called [[Theorem|theorems]], must be proved from axioms. === KEY: Axiom_of_choice_44faa8
HEADWORD: Axiom of choice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bertrand Russell | Real number | Set theory | Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory
ENTRY:
Axiom of [[Set theory|set theory]] stating that one element can be chosen from each set in any collection of nonempty sets. Formulated in 1904 by Ernst Zermelo to formalize his proof of the well-ordering theorem, it establishes the existence of such a choice rather than constructing it. No axiom is needed when a canonical rule exists, as with the smallest number in each set of natural numbers, but it is needed for nonempty subsets of the [[Real number|real numbers]], where no such rule is known. [[Bertrand Russell]] illustrated the difference with shoes, which can be told apart as left and right, and socks, which cannot. Although initially controversial, the axiom is used by most mathematicians and is part of [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory]] with the axiom of choice (ZFC). Many results, such as Tychonoff’s theorem, depend on it.
