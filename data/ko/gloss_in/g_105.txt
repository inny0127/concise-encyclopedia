=== KEY: Phoenicia_2f7104
HEADWORD: Phoenicia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Canaan | Carthage | Greek alphabet | Late Bronze Age collapse | Tyrian purple
ENTRY:
Ancient Semitic-speaking maritime civilization of city-states along the Levantine coast of the eastern Mediterranean, primarily in present-day Lebanon.

The Phoenicians emerged directly from the Bronze Age [[Canaan|Canaanites]], and their cultural traditions survived the [[Late Bronze Age collapse]]. They called themselves Canaanites and their land Canaan. *Phoenicia* is a Greek exonym, from *phoînix*, which meant variously "Phoenician person," "Tyrian purple, crimson," or "date palm"; it did not correspond to a unified native identity. Modern scholarship generally views the distinction after c. 1200 BC as artificial.

Phoenician society was organized into independent city-states, notably Byblos, Sidon, and Tyre, each politically autonomous, with no evidence of a shared national identity. The Phoenicians established one of antiquity's most extensive maritime networks, active for over a millennium and linking Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Greece. They founded colonies and trading posts from Cyprus to the Maghreb and the Iberian Peninsula; [[Carthage]] became a major power by the 7th century BC. Exports included cedar wood, linen, and the dye [[Tyrian purple]].

The cities flourished most in the 9th century BC and subsequently declined under the expansion of empires such as the Neo-Assyrian and Achaemenid. [[Alexander the Great]] besieged Tyre in 332 BC. Phoenician influence nevertheless endured in the western Mediterranean until the Roman destruction of Carthage in the mid-2nd century BC.

Because little native literature survives, the Phoenicians were long regarded as a "lost" civilization and became better understood only after the discovery of inscriptions in the 17th and 18th centuries. Their most enduring legacy is the earliest verified alphabet, a 22-consonant script derived from the Proto-Sinaitic script. It spread across the Mediterranean and gave rise to the [[Greek alphabet]], ancestor of the Latin and Cyrillic scripts.

=== KEY: Phoenician_alphabet_3f06cb
HEADWORD: Phoenician alphabet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carthage | Cuneiform | Egyptian hieroglyphs | Greek alphabet | Phoenicia
ENTRY:
Consonantal writing system (an abjad) used in [[Phoenicia]] and across the Mediterranean from c. 1050 to 146 BC. It was one of the first alphabets and the first to have a fixed writing direction, written horizontally from right to left. It developed from the Proto-Sinaitic script of the Late Bronze Age, which derived in turn from [[Egyptian hieroglyphs]]. Its 22 consonant letters left vowels implicit, though late varieties sometimes used matres lectionis to mark some vowels. Phoenician merchants spread it across the Mediterranean, and its descendants include the Aramaic and Samaritan alphabets and the [[Greek alphabet|Archaic Greek alphabets]]. Because each sound had its own symbol, it needed only a few dozen signs, far fewer than [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] or hieroglyphic writing, which required long training. The alphabet proper was used in [[Carthage]] until the 2nd century BC.

=== KEY: Phoneme_5c28aa
HEADWORD: Phoneme
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consonant | International Phonetic Alphabet | Phonetics | Phonology | Vowel
ENTRY:
Basic unit of [[Phonology|phonology]], a set of speech sounds that speakers regard as a single sound distinguishing one word from another. All languages contain phonemes, and all spoken languages include both [[Consonant|consonant]] and [[Vowel|vowel]] phonemes. Written between slashes, as in /p/ and /b/, phonemes separate English words such as *pat* and *bat*. Phonemes are abstract, and the varying pronunciations of one phoneme are called allophones: the English /p/ is aspirated in *pit* but not in *spit*. Phonemes differ across languages, so [n] and [ŋ] are distinct in English, distinguishing *sin* and *sing*, but form one phoneme in Spanish. Linguists transcribe phonemes between slashes using the [[International Phonetic Alphabet]], and write precise pronunciations in square brackets, a distinction known as the difference between [[Phonetics|phonetic]] and phonemic transcription.

=== KEY: Phonetics_6d87bc

ENTRY:
Branch of [[Linguistics|linguistics]] concerned with how humans produce and perceive speech sounds. It is traditionally divided into articulatory, acoustic and auditory phonetics, which study the articulation, sound-wave properties and perception of speech. Its minimal unit is the phone, an individual speech sound, whereas the [[Phoneme|phoneme]] is the abstract, language-specific unit that affects meaning. The term phonetics was first used in its present sense in 1841. The field also addresses [[Sign language|sign languages]], which are produced manually and perceived visually.

=== KEY: Photochemistry_61e3f1
HEADWORD: Photochemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Chemistry | DNA | Fluorescence | Photosynthesis | Ultraviolet | Vitamin D
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Chemistry|chemistry]] concerned with the chemical effects of light. It usually describes reactions caused by absorption of [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] (wavelength 100 to 400 nm), visible (400–750 nm) or infrared (750–2500 nm) radiation. Photochemistry underlies [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], vision and the formation of [[Vitamin D|vitamin D]] in sunlight, but it also causes [[DNA|DNA]] mutations that lead to skin cancers. Photochemical paths reach high-energy intermediates that cannot be generated thermally. Its first law, the Grotthuss–Draper law, holds that light must be absorbed for a reaction to occur. The Stark–Einstein law, to which [[Albert Einstein]] contributed, holds that each absorbed photon activates no more than one molecule. Excited molecules can relax through [[Fluorescence|fluorescence]] or phosphorescence, and photodegradation can destroy plastics.

=== KEY: Photoelectric_effect_ac0c53
HEADWORD: Photoelectric effect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Electromagnetic radiation | Electron | Gilbert N. Lewis | Photon | Robert Millikan
ENTRY:
Emission of [[Electron|electrons]] from a material when [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] such as light strikes it. The ejected electrons are called photoelectrons. Classical electromagnetism predicted that dim light would eventually transfer enough energy to release electrons, but experiments showed that emission occurs only above a material-specific threshold frequency, regardless of intensity or exposure time. Increasing intensity raises the number of electrons emitted but not their energy. In 1905, [[Albert Einstein]] proposed that light consists of discrete energy packets, later named [[Photon|photons]] by [[Gilbert N. Lewis]]. [[Robert Millikan]] verified the relationship experimentally in 1916, and Einstein received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics. The effect underpins solar cells, photodetectors and photomultiplier tubes.

=== KEY: Photographic_film_30e07f
HEADWORD: Photographic film
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dye | Gelatin | Infrared | Visible spectrum
ENTRY:
Strip or sheet of transparent base coated with a [[Gelatin|gelatin]] emulsion of light-sensitive silver halide crystals, used to take photographs. Exposure to light creates an invisible latent image, which chemical development converts into a visible picture. Unmodified crystals respond only to blue light until sensitizing [[Dye|dyes]] were adsorbed onto them, producing orthochromatic and then panchromatic films, which are sensitive to all [[Visible spectrum|visible]] colors. Special films can also be sensitive to [[Infrared|infrared]] light. Black-and-white film has one layer of crystals, which develop into metallic silver. === KEY: Photography_a2a5c3
HEADWORD: Photography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camera obscura | Charge-coupled device | Digital photography | George Eastman | Ibn al-Haytham | Louis Daguerre | Nicéphore Niépce | Photographic film
ENTRY:
Art, application, and practice of creating images by recording light, either electronically by an image sensor or chemically on a light-sensitive material such as [[Photographic film]].

A lens focuses light reflected or emitted from objects into a real image on a light-sensitive surface inside a camera during a timed exposure. With an electronic sensor, this produces an electrical charge at each pixel. With photographic emulsion, it produces an invisible latent image that is chemically developed into a negative or positive; a negative is traditionally used to make a positive print on paper by enlarger or contact printing.

The word derives from Greek roots meaning "light" and "drawing." Its earliest known appearance in print was in a German newspaper article of February 25, 1839; the astronomer John Herschel is also credited with coining it that year.

The [[Camera obscura]] dates back to ancient China; Aristotle and Euclid described it in the 5th and 4th centuries BCE. [[Ibn al-Haytham]] gave the first correct analysis of the phenomenon and invented the first true pinhole camera. In June 1802 Thomas Wedgwood made the first known attempt to capture a camera image with a light-sensitive substance. [[Nicéphore Niépce]] made the first known permanent photograph, *View from the Window at Le Gras*, around 1826 on a bitumen-coated pewter plate. In 1839 [[Louis Daguerre]] announced the daguerreotype while William Henry Fox Talbot independently developed the calotype negative-positive system, which allowed multiple prints.

[[George Eastman]] introduced roll film in 1888. Digital capture began with the [[Charge-coupled device]] in the 1960s, followed by consumer [[Digital photography]] in the 1990s. Digital manipulation and AI generation have raised debates over the authenticity of images.

=== KEY: Photometer_674d3d
HEADWORD: Photometer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Light | Photography | Photon | Spectroscopy
ENTRY:
Instrument for measuring quantities such as luminous flux, illuminance or luminance. Early photometry relied on visual estimation, comparing the luminous flux of a source with a standard source. By 1861, three types were in common use: Rumford's photometer, which compared the depths of shadows cast by two lights; Ritchie's photometer, which compared equal illumination of surfaces; and devices based on the extinction of shadows, considered the most precise. === KEY: Photometry_astronomy_1effc3
HEADWORD: Photometry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apparent magnitude | Astronomical object | Flux | Nebula | Photometer | Telescope | Variable star
ENTRY:
Astronomical technique for measuring the [[Flux|flux]] or intensity of light from [[Astronomical object|celestial objects]]. Electronic [[Photometer|photometers]] used with a [[Telescope|telescope]] are calibrated against standard stars of known intensity and color to measure the [[Apparent magnitude|apparent magnitude]] of objects. Observations use standard filters that define a photometric system, such as the UBV system. === KEY: Photon_fd22b6
HEADWORD: Photon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Boson | C. V. Raman | Gilbert N. Lewis | Laser | Max Planck | Wave–particle duality
ENTRY:
Elementary particle that is a quantum of the electromagnetic field, including light and radio waves, and the force carrier of the electromagnetic force.

Photons are massless and can move at only one speed in a vacuum, the speed of light. The photon belongs to the class of [[Boson|bosons]]. Photons exhibit [[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]], behaving as both waves and particles.

The modern concept originated in the first two decades of the 20th century with the work of [[Albert Einstein]], who built on the research of [[Max Planck]]. Planck proposed that the energy stored in a material object is composed of an integer number of discrete, equal-sized parts. To explain the photoelectric effect, Einstein introduced the idea that light itself is made of discrete units of energy. In 1926 [[Gilbert N. Lewis]] popularized the term *photon* for these units. The concept has led to advances including [[Laser|lasers]], Bose–Einstein condensation, quantum field theory, and quantum cryptography.

The photon has no electric charge, is generally considered to have zero rest mass, and is stable. The experimental upper limit on its mass is on the order of 10⁻⁵³ g, and its lifetime would be more than 10¹⁸ years. Photons obey Bose–Einstein statistics, so more than one can occupy the same quantum state.

Photon energy is proportional to frequency (*E* = *h*ν, where *h* is the Planck constant), and its energy and momentum are related by *E* = *pc*. A photon carries spin angular momentum of either +ħ or −ħ, corresponding to the two pure states of circular polarization; its independence from frequency was verified experimentally by [[C. V. Raman]] and Suri Bhagavantam in 1931. Collision of a particle with its antiparticle creates at least two photons.

=== KEY: Photosynthesis_3d0794
HEADWORD: Photosynthesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Chlorophyll | Chloroplast | Cyanobacteria
ENTRY:
Biological process by which photopigment-bearing organisms, such as most plants, algae, and [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]], convert light energy, typically from sunlight, into chemical energy.

The term usually refers to oxygenic photosynthesis, which releases oxygen as a byproduct of water splitting. The converted energy is stored in the bonds of organic compounds, typically carbohydrates such as glucose, starches, and cellulose. It maintains the oxygen content of Earth's atmosphere and supplies most of the biological energy necessary for complex life.

The process begins when light energy is absorbed by reaction centers, proteins containing photosynthetic pigments. In plants these pigments are [[Chlorophyll|chlorophylls]], which absorb the red and blue spectra and so reflect green, held inside [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] in leaf cells. In the light-dependent reactions, energy is used to strip electrons from water, producing oxygen gas, and to create NADPH and ATP. In the light-independent reactions, called the Calvin cycle, atmospheric [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] is incorporated into existing organic compounds such as ribulose bisphosphate and reduced to carbohydrates.

Some organisms perform anoxygenic photosynthesis, which produces no oxygen. Purple bacteria split hydrogen sulfide, releasing sulfur instead. Archaea such as *Halobacterium* use retinal to absorb green light and power ATP synthesis; this form might have been the earliest to evolve.

The first photosynthetic organisms probably used hydrogen or hydrogen sulfide rather than water. Cyanobacteria appeared later, and the excess oxygen they produced contributed directly to the oxygenation of the Earth. Global photosynthesis captures energy at an average rate of approximately 130 terawatts, about eight times the total power consumption of human civilization. Jan Ingenhousz discovered photosynthesis in 1779, showing that plants need light, not just soil and water.

=== KEY: Phylogenetics_a3c624

ENTRY:
Study of the evolutionary relationships among organisms, inferred from heritable traits such as [[DNA]] sequences and morphology. === KEY: Physical_chemistry_23eba5
HEADWORD: Physical chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical equilibrium | Chemical kinetics | Colloid | Mikhail Lomonosov | Quantum chemistry | Spectroscopy | Statistical mechanics | Thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Branch of chemistry that studies macroscopic and microscopic phenomena in chemical systems in terms of the principles of physics, such as motion, energy, force, time, [[Thermodynamics]], [[Quantum chemistry]], [[Statistical mechanics]], and [[Chemical equilibrium|chemical equilibria]].

In contrast to chemical physics, it is predominantly, but not always, a supra-molecular science, since most of its founding principles concern bulk matter rather than molecular or atomic structure alone, as in [[Colloid|colloids]]. It studies, for example, how intermolecular forces affect plasticity, tensile strength, and surface tension.

A key concept is that chemical compounds can be described as groups of atoms bonded together and reactions as the making and breaking of those bonds. Predicting the properties of compounds from their atoms and bonds is a major goal. Quantum chemistry applies quantum mechanics to determine how strong and what shape bonds are, how nuclei move, and how light is absorbed or emitted by a compound. [[Spectroscopy]] is the related subdiscipline concerned with the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter. Chemical thermodynamics sets limits on how far a reaction can proceed.

[[Chemical kinetics]] concerns which reactions occur and how fast. Most species must pass through transition states higher in energy than reactants or products; the higher the barrier, the slower the reaction. Statistical mechanics explains why a mixture of a very large number of particles (of the order of the Avogadro constant, 6 × 10²³) can often be described by a few variables such as pressure, temperature, and concentration.

The term was coined by [[Mikhail Lomonosov]] in 1752, when he presented a lecture course titled *A Course in True Physical Chemistry* to students at Petersburg University. Modern physical chemistry originated in the 1860s to 1880s.

=== KEY: Physical_cosmology_9cceb9
HEADWORD: Physical cosmology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Big Bang | Cosmic microwave background | Dark energy | Dark matter | Edwin Hubble | General relativity | Georges Lemaître
ENTRY:
Branch of physics concerned with modeling the universe on the basis of the laws of physics. A cosmological model gives a mathematical description of the universe's largest-scale structures and dynamics.

The modern field began with [[Albert Einstein|Einstein's]] [[General relativity|general theory of relativity]], published in 1916. Believing in a static universe, Einstein added a cosmological constant to his field equations in 1917, but the resulting model proved unstable. In the early 1920s Alexander Friedmann found solutions in which the universe may expand or contract. In 1927 the Belgian Roman Catholic priest [[Georges Lemaître]] independently derived the same equations and proposed that the universe began with the "explosion" of a "primeval atom", later called the [[Big Bang]]. In 1929 [[Edwin Hubble]] used Cepheid variable stars to show that the spiral nebulae were galaxies and found that their redshift relates to their distance, a relationship now known as Hubble's law. Fred Hoyle's steady state model offered a rival explanation of the expansion, but the discovery of the [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]] in 1965 lent strong support to the Big Bang model.

Observational advances since the 1990s, including distant supernovae and galaxy redshift surveys, led to a standard model of cosmology. It requires the universe to contain large amounts of [[Dark matter|dark matter]] and [[Dark energy|dark energy]], whose nature is not well understood, yet it gives detailed predictions in excellent agreement with many observations. Most cosmologists agree that the Big Bang theory best explains the observations, although a few researchers advocate alternative cosmologies. The field draws on particle physics, astrophysics, general relativity, quantum mechanics, and plasma physics.

=== KEY: Physical_disability_2b109a
HEADWORD: Physical disability
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epilepsy | Hearing loss | Injury | Pregnancy | Visual impairment | Wheelchair | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Limitation on a person's physical functioning or on the ability to carry out activities such as walking or eating. The term is very broad. Examples include blindness, deafness, respiratory disorders, [[Epilepsy|epilepsy]], and some genetic disorders, and the severity of impairment and the restriction of daily activities vary widely between types. An inclusive and accessible environment may mitigate many restrictions. The [[World Health Organization]] does not differentiate between physical and mental disabilities. Estimates of significant functional limitations of all kinds put the global total at about 1.3 billion people, or 16% of the world's population.

Causes are commonly divided by timing. Prenatal disabilities arise before birth from diseases or substances to which the mother was exposed during [[Pregnancy|pregnancy]], from developmental accidents in the embryo or fetus, or from genetic disorders. Perinatal disabilities arise from some weeks before birth to four weeks after it, for example through prolonged lack of oxygen, brain damage during birth, or premature birth. Post-natal disabilities may be gained at any time from accidents, [[Injury|injuries]], obesity, infection, or other illnesses; research suggests road traffic injuries are the leading cause.

Mobility impairment includes limb loss or impairment, poor manual dexterity, paraplegia, and damage to one or more organs. Sensory impairment is partial or complete loss of one or more senses, most commonly [[Visual impairment|visual impairment]] and [[Hearing loss|hearing loss]]. Management depends on the condition, and pain is a significant problem for many. Assistive devices, including [[Wheelchair|wheelchairs]], walkers, canes, prosthetic limbs, hearing aids, guide dogs, screen readers, and communication devices, can counter many barriers. Debate over definition contrasts a medical model, which emphasizes physiological impairment, with accounts that stress societal barriers.

=== KEY: Physics_50ae99
HEADWORD: Physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Democritus | Ibn al-Haytham | Industrial Revolution | Isaac Newton | Johannes Kepler | Natural philosophy | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
Scientific study of matter, its fundamental constituents, its motion and behavior through space and time, and the related entities of energy and force. Physics is one of the oldest academic disciplines and one of the most fundamental sciences. Its branches include mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and optics, as well as quantum physics, relativity, nuclear physics, astrophysics, and applied areas such as medical physics. The boundaries of the field are not rigidly defined, and it intersects with biophysics and quantum chemistry. New ideas in physics often explain the fundamental mechanisms studied by other sciences.

Advances in physics often enable new technologies. Understanding of electromagnetism, solid-state physics, and nuclear physics led directly to television, computers, domestic appliances, and nuclear weapons; thermodynamics led to industrialization; and mechanics inspired the development of calculus.

The word comes from the Latin *physica* ('study of nature'), borrowed from the Greek *phusikē* ('natural science'). Early civilizations before 3000 BC, such as the Sumerians, ancient Egyptians, and the Indus Valley Civilization, had predictive knowledge of the motions of the Sun, Moon, and stars. [[Natural philosophy]] began in Greece in the Archaic period (650–480 BC), when pre-Socratic philosophers such as Thales rejected non-naturalistic explanations and proclaimed that every event had a natural cause. Atomism, proposed by Leucippus and his pupil [[Democritus]], was found to be correct about 2,000 years later. [[Aristotle]], a student of Plato, wrote a substantial treatise, *Physics*, in the 4th century BC; its framework, built on four elements and a "prime mover," was influential for about two millennia but did not rely on experimental verification and is entirely superseded today.

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century, intellectual pursuits declined in western Europe, while the Byzantine Empire continued to advance learning. In the sixth century John Philoponus challenged the Aristotelian approach, and Isidore of Miletus compiled works of Archimedes. During the Islamic Golden Age, scholars including Ibn Sahl, Al-Kindi, [[Ibn al-Haytham]], and Avicenna made notable advances in optics. In his *Book of Optics*, Ibn al-Haytham presented light rays as an alternative to the Greek idea of visual rays and used controlled experiments to verify the laws of refraction and reflection.

Physics became a separate science during the [[Scientific Revolution]], when early modern Europeans used experimental and quantitative methods. Major developments included the heliocentric Copernican model, the laws of planetary motion determined by [[Johannes Kepler]] between 1609 and 1619, Galileo's work with telescopes, and [[Isaac Newton]]'s unification of the laws of motion and universal gravitation. Newton and, separately, Leibniz developed calculus. Research during the [[Industrial Revolution]], as energy needs grew, produced the laws of thermodynamics, chemistry, and electromagnetics, and by the end of the 19th century these theories matched a wide variety of observations.

=== KEY: Physics_beyond_the_Standard_Model_69e32b
HEADWORD: Physics beyond the Standard Model
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dark energy | Dark matter | General relativity | Large Hadron Collider | Standard Model | String theory | Supersymmetry
ENTRY:
Theories extending the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics to explain its deficiencies. The Standard Model cannot explain the strong CP problem, neutrino oscillations, matter–antimatter asymmetry, or the nature of [[Dark matter|dark matter]] and [[Dark energy|dark energy]]. It is also inconsistent with [[General relativity]], so one or both theories break down under conditions such as the Big Bang and black hole event horizons. Cosmological observations indicate that the Standard Model accounts for about 5% of the universe's mass-energy. Proposals include extensions through [[Supersymmetry]], such as the Minimal Supersymmetric Standard Model, and novel explanations such as [[String theory]], M-theory and extra dimensions. Because many of these theories reproduce current phenomena, the choice among them can only be settled by experiment, such as searches at the [[Large Hadron Collider]].

=== KEY: Physiology_3e1c7e
HEADWORD: Physiology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anatomy | Biochemistry | Biology | Claude Bernard | Disease | Genetics | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the functions and mechanisms of living systems. The name comes from the Ancient Greek *phusis* ('nature, origin') and *-logia* ('study of'). As a subdiscipline of [[Biology|biology]], physiology focuses on how organisms, organ systems, individual organs, cells, and biomolecules carry out chemical and physical functions. By the class of organism studied, it divides into medical, animal, plant, cell, and comparative physiology.

Central to physiological functioning are biophysical and biochemical processes, homeostatic control mechanisms, and communication between cells. Physiological state is the condition of normal function, in contrast to a pathological state, which includes human [[Disease|diseases]]. Homeostasis, the self-regulating preservation of internal stability, traces to [[Claude Bernard]]'s formulation of the *milieu intérieur*, the internal environment whose constancy ensures survival.

The field rests on several disciplines. [[Anatomy]] supplies knowledge of structure, which often dictates function; [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]] explains cellular and molecular processes; biophysics explains how organisms sense and respond to stimuli such as light, sound, and temperature; [[Genetics|genetics]] shows the genetic basis of physiological processes; and evolutionary biology explains their adaptive significance.

Plant physiology, a subdiscipline of botany, covers the functioning of plants, including [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], respiration, plant nutrition, tropisms, circadian rhythms, seed germination, dormancy, and stomata function and transpiration. Human physiology studies how the body's systems, among them the nervous, endocrine, cardiovascular, respiratory, digestive, and urinary systems, work together to maintain a stable internal environment. Understanding of the field underpins the diagnosis and treatment of health conditions and informs exercise science, ecology, and biotechnology. The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognizes exceptional achievements in physiology related to medicine.

=== KEY: Pi_127125
HEADWORD: Pi
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Calculus | Circle | Irrational number | Karl Weierstrass | Leonhard Euler | Trigonometry
ENTRY:
Mathematical constant, approximately 3.14159, equal to the ratio of a [[Circle|circle's]] circumference to its diameter. It is written with the lowercase Greek letter π, a symbol introduced by the Welsh mathematician William Jones in 1706 and popularized by [[Leonhard Euler]]. The constant appears in many formulas across mathematics and physics, and some of these are used to define it, for example [[Karl Weierstrass]]'s 1841 definition as an integral.

Pi is an [[Irrational number|irrational number]], so it cannot be written exactly as a ratio of two integers, although fractions such as 22/7 and 355/113 are commonly used to approximate it. Johann Heinrich Lambert proved its irrationality in 1761. Pi is also transcendental, meaning it is not a solution of an algebraic equation involving only finite sums, products, powers, and integers, as Ferdinand von Lindemann showed in 1882. This implies that the ancient problem of squaring the circle with a compass and straightedge cannot be solved.

Around 250 BC [[Archimedes]] devised an algorithm that approximates pi with arbitrary accuracy. In the 5th century AD, Chinese mathematicians approximated it to seven digits and Indian mathematicians to five. The first computational formula based on infinite series was discovered about a millennium later. The development of [[Calculus|calculus]] in the 17th century soon led to the calculation of hundreds of digits, enough for all practical scientific computations, and computers have since extended the decimal representation to hundreds of trillions of digits.

Because it relates to the circle, pi appears in [[Trigonometry|trigonometry]] and geometry, especially in formulas for circles, ellipses, and spheres. It is also found in cosmology, fractals, thermodynamics, mechanics, electromagnetism, number theory, and statistics.

=== KEY: Piano_060e8e

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Keyboard instrument]] in which felt-covered hammers strike strings when keys are pressed. Modern pianos have 88 keys tuned to equal temperament. The two main types are the grand piano, which offers better sound and key control, and the upright piano, which is smaller, cheaper and more commonly used. === KEY: Pig_32e9ed

LINK TARGETS: Bologna | China | Neolithic | Pork | Tigris | Wild boar
ENTRY:
Omnivorous, domesticated, even-toed, hoofed mammal (*Sus domesticus*), also called swine or hog. Some authorities consider it a subspecies of *Sus scrofa*, the [[Wild boar|wild boar]]; the American Society of Mammalogists treats it as a separate species.

The pig has a large head with a long snout, strengthened by a prenasal bone and a disk of cartilage at the tip, which it uses to dig for food. There are four hoofed toes on each foot. Adults generally weigh between 140 and 300 kg, though a pig called Big Bill weighed 1,157 kg. They have small lungs for their body size and are more susceptible than other domesticated animals to fatal bronchitis and pneumonia.

Pigs were domesticated independently twice during the [[Neolithic]], in the Near East around the [[Tigris]] Basin and in [[China]], starting some 8,000 years ago. Pigs were present in Cyprus more than 11,400 years ago, introduced from the mainland. Pigs reached Europe from the Near East at least 8,500 years ago and over the next 3,000 years interbred with European wild boar until their genome showed less than 5% Near Eastern ancestry, yet they retained domesticated features.

Pigs are farmed primarily for their meat, called [[Pork|pork]], often cured as bacon, and their hide is used for leather. China is the largest producer, followed by the European Union and the United States. Around 1.5 billion pigs are raised each year, producing some 120 million metric tons of meat. Some are kept as pets. Pigs have featured in human culture since Neolithic times, and cities such as [[Bologna]] celebrate their meat products.

ENTRY:
Clade of semiaquatic, fin-footed carnivorous mammals, commonly called seals. Its 34 living species belong to three families: Phocidae (the earless, or true, seals), Otariidae (the eared seals, such as sea lions and fur seals) and Odobenidae, whose only living member is the [[Walrus]]. Pinnipeds belong to the order Carnivora, and their closest relatives are musteloids such as weasels, skunks and [[Red panda|red pandas]], from which they diverged about 50 million years ago. Most species prefer colder waters, and they come ashore to mate, give birth and molt, or to avoid predators such as [[Shark|sharks]] and [[Orca|orcas]]. A layer of blubber keeps them warm, and all species except the walrus are covered in fur. The Mediterranean and Hawaiian monk seals are endangered, and the Japanese sea lion and Caribbean monk seal became extinct in the past century.

ENTRY:
Unit of volume or capacity in the imperial and U.S. customary systems, equal to one-eighth of a [[Gallon|gallon]]. The imperial pint, about 568 mL, is used in the United Kingdom, Ireland and other Commonwealth countries. The U.S. liquid pint is about 473 mL, and the less common dry pint about 551 mL. The imperial pint is 20.095% larger than the U.S. pint because the British redefined their system in 1824, whereas the U.S. system derives from earlier English units. Australia, South Africa and New Zealand converted to the [[Metric system|metric system]] in the 1960s and 1970s.

=== KEY: Pinwheel_Galaxy_8818d6
HEADWORD: Pinwheel Galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Space Agency | H II region | Hubble Space Telescope | Milky Way | NASA | Ursa Major
ENTRY:
Face-on intermediate spiral galaxy in the constellation [[Ursa Major]]. Also known as Messier 101 (M101) or NGC 5457, it was discovered by Pierre Méchain in 1781, and Charles Messier verified its position for his catalog. On February 28, 2006, [[NASA]] and the [[European Space Agency]] released an image composed of 51 exposures, at the time the largest and most detailed image of a galaxy taken by the [[Hubble Space Telescope]]. The galaxy has around a trillion stars and a diameter of 252,000 light-years, compared with 87,400 light-years for the [[Milky Way]]. Its spiral arms host many bright [[H II region|H II regions]], 1,264 of which were cataloged in a 1990 study. Gravitational interaction with its companion galaxies may have produced its grand design pattern.

=== KEY: Pinyin_935074
HEADWORD: Pinyin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Chinese characters | Singapore | Taiwan | United Nations
ENTRY:
Most common romanization system for Standard Chinese, official in [[China]], [[Singapore]] and the [[United Nations]]. Pinyin means "spelled sounds." Each syllable is spelled with an optional initial and a final, and diacritics mark the four tones, though these are often omitted in non-Chinese texts. It was developed in the 1950s by linguists including Zhou Youguang, called the "father of pinyin," and promulgated in 1958. The International Organization for Standardization adopted it as ISO 7098 in 1982. Pinyin is used to teach Standard Chinese, which is normally written with [[Chinese characters]]. [[Taiwan]] adopted it as its official system in 2009, replacing Tongyong Pinyin.

=== KEY: Pion_ef6fcb
HEADWORD: Pion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Meson | Quark | Strong interaction
ENTRY:
Lightest of the [[Meson|mesons]], a subatomic particle made of a [[Quark|quark]] and an antiquark. There are three pions: π⁰, π⁺ and π⁻. Charged pions decay after a mean lifetime of 26.033 nanoseconds, mostly into muons and neutrinos. The neutral pion decays in 8.5×10⁻¹⁷ seconds, usually into gamma rays. Hideki Yukawa predicted in 1935 the mesons that carry the strong nuclear force; pions were confirmed in 1947 (charged) and 1950 (neutral). Virtual pion exchange explains the residual [[Strong interaction|strong interaction]] between nucleons.

=== KEY: Pipe_fluid_conveyance_241cfb
HEADWORD: Pipe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Pipeline | Plumbing
ENTRY:
Tubular section or hollow cylinder, usually of circular cross-section, used mainly to convey fluids, slurries and powders. A hollow pipe is far stiffer per unit weight than a solid member, so pipes are also used structurally, as in handrails and scaffolding. Pipe is usually specified by nominal diameter and a schedule that sets wall thickness, while tube is typically specified by outside diameter and wall thickness. Pipes imply rigidity and permanence, unlike flexible hoses, and are usually assembled with fittings such as elbows and tees. Uses include [[Plumbing|plumbing]], irrigation and [[Pipeline|pipelines]] carrying gas or liquid over long distances. The first known use of pipes was in [[Ancient Egypt]], where the Pyramid of Sahure, completed around the 25th century BC, included more than 380 m (1,247 ft) of copper piping. Early steel pipes were riveted, and by the early 1930s welding had replaced riveting.

=== KEY: Pipe_organ_626641
HEADWORD: Pipe organ
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aachen | Ancient Greece | Charlemagne | Franks
ENTRY:
Musical instrument that produces sound by driving pressurized air, or wind, through pipes. Each pipe produces one pitch, and pipes of the same tonal quality are grouped into ranks, which the player selects with controls called stops. Most organs have one or more keyboards, called manuals, and a pedalboard for the feet. The largest can have over 33,000 pipes and seven manuals. Its ancestor, the hydraulis of [[Ancient Greece]] in the 3rd century BC, used displaced water to supply wind. In 757 the Byzantine emperor Constantine V sent an organ to Pepin the Short, King of the [[Franks]], and in 812 Pepin's son [[Charlemagne]] requested one for his chapel in [[Aachen]]. By the 17th century, most sounds of the modern classical organ had been developed.

=== KEY: Pipeline_aeaeae
HEADWORD: Pipeline
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Corrosion | Natural gas | Petroleum
ENTRY:
System of pipes for long-distance transport of liquids or gases, usually to a market area. Pipelines carry crude and refined [[Petroleum|petroleum]], [[Natural gas|natural gas]], hydrogen, water, sewage and other fluids. Oil pipelines are usually buried steel or plastic tubes, with pump stations along their length. Pipelines are generally the most economical way to move large quantities of oil or gas over land; in 2014, pipeline transport of crude oil cost about $5 per barrel, while rail cost about $10 to $15. In the United States, 70% of crude oil and petroleum products are shipped by pipeline. The first crude oil pipeline was built in Pennsylvania in the 1860s. Pipelines are among the safest ways to transport materials, though in war they are often military targets. [[Corrosion|Corrosion]] and leakage are the main sources of pollution from pipelines.

=== KEY: Piracy_827427
HEADWORD: Piracy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: English Channel | Gibraltar | Gulf of Guinea | Sea Peoples | Strait of Malacca
ENTRY:
Robbery or criminal violence at sea by attackers from ships or boats, usually aimed at stealing cargo or taking hostages. The earliest documented piracy dates to the 14th century BC, when the [[Sea Peoples]] raided ships in the Aegean and Mediterranean. Narrow channels such as [[Gibraltar]], the [[English Channel]] and the [[Strait of Malacca]] have long offered opportunities for attack. Romanticized images of pirates owe much to *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724) and to novels such as *Treasure Island* (1883). Modern piracy is mainly armed robbery off the Somali coast, in the [[Gulf of Guinea]] and in the Strait of Malacca, often carried out from small motorboats. The International Maritime Bureau recorded 116 incidents in 2024, the lowest since 1994.
