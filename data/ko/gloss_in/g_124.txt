=== KEY: Singing_eec550
HEADWORD: Singing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Opera | Rapping
ENTRY:
Art of creating music with the human voice. Often called the oldest form of musical expression, it is performed with or without instrumental accompaniment, by soloists or in ensembles such as a choir, and ranges from a single accompanying instrument to a symphony orchestra or big band. It may be formal or informal, arranged or improvised, and serves religious devotion, ritual, pleasure, comfort, education, or profession. Many styles exist throughout the world.

Physically, singing depends on the lungs, which supply air; the larynx, which acts as a vibrator; the chest, head cavities, and skeleton, which amplify the sound; and the tongue, palate, teeth, and lips, which shape consonants and vowels. Pitch is altered with the vocal cords. Each person's voice is unique, shaped by the vocal cords and by the size and shape of the whole body. Singers can learn to project sound so that it resonates better in the vocal tract, and different functions of the larynx produce different vocal registers, such as chest, head, and falsetto. Vocal folds vibrate at frequencies from 60 to 2000 Hz.

In European classical music and [[Opera|opera]], voices are treated like instruments and classified by range, weight, tessitura, and timbre into types such as soprano, mezzo-soprano, contralto, tenor, baritone, and bass. Professional singers usually specialize in one genre, such as classical or rock, and typically train throughout their careers with vocal coaches or teachers.

Singing is distinguished from [[Rapping|rapping]], which does not require pitch accuracy and uses rhythmic speech rather than sung pitches, although some rap artists alternate between the two. Group singing predominates in most cultures, in religious rituals, dances, and community bonding.

ENTRY:
Depression or hole in the ground caused by collapse of the surface layer. Most are caused by [[Karst|karst]] processes, the chemical dissolution of [[Carbonate|carbonate]] rocks such as [[Limestone|limestone]], or by collapse or suffosion processes. They are usually circular, from tens to hundreds of meters in diameter and depth, and may form gradually or suddenly. They are found worldwide. Maritime disaster in the [[Atlantic Ocean|North Atlantic Ocean]] on April 15, 1912. === KEY: Sino_Burmese_War_f0936a
HEADWORD: Sino-Burmese War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Konbaung dynasty | Qianlong Emperor | Qing dynasty
ENTRY:
War of 1765–1769 between the [[Qing dynasty]] of China and the [[Konbaung dynasty]] of Burma, also called the Qing invasions of Burma. The [[Qianlong Emperor]] launched four invasions, considered one of his Ten Great Campaigns. Burmese forces defeated the first two, in 1765–1766 and 1766–1767. The third, led by elite Manchu bannermen in 1767–1768, penetrated within a few days' march of the capital, Ava, but the bannermen could not cope with unfamiliar tropical terrain and lethal endemic diseases. Burma's defense laid the foundation for the present-day boundary between the two countries.

=== KEY: Sino_Tibetan_languages_761828
HEADWORD: Sino-Tibetan languages
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chinese language | Himalayas | Tibetan Plateau
ENTRY:
Language family of more than 400 languages, native to Asia. It is second only to Indo-European in number of native speakers: about 1.4 billion people speak a Sino-Tibetan language, most of them among the 1.3 billion speakers of [[Chinese language|Chinese]] (Sinitic) languages. Other large members are Burmese (33 million) and the Tibetic languages (6 million). Most others are spoken in small, poorly documented communities in the [[Himalayas]], the Southeast Asian Massif and the eastern edge of the [[Tibetan Plateau]]. The family is traditionally divided into Sinitic and Tibeto-Burman branches, but its higher-level structure remains unclear because a common origin of the non-Sinitic languages has never been demonstrated. Early classifications included Kra–Dai and Hmong–Mien, a grouping still supported by Chinese linguists but rejected internationally since the 1940s.

=== KEY: Siphon_892780
HEADWORD: Siphon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric pressure | Barometer | Hero of Alexandria | Reservoir | Vacuum
ENTRY:
Device, usually a tube bent into an inverted U shape, that lifts liquid above the surface of a [[Reservoir|reservoir]] and discharges it lower, powered by gravity with no pump. The traditional explanation holds that [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]] pushes the liquid up into the reduced pressure at the top, as in a [[Barometer|barometer]]. Gravity siphons also work in a [[Vacuum|vacuum]], so a cohesion tension theory has been advanced, and both explanations may hold under different pressures. Egyptian reliefs from 1500 BC depict siphons drawing liquid from storage jars, and [[Hero of Alexandria]] wrote about them in *Pneumatica*. In a Venturi siphon, a second fluid is drawn into a fast-moving stream, the principle behind the carburetor of a gasoline engine.

ENTRY:
Order of fully aquatic, herbivorous mammals, commonly called sea cows or sirenians. Sirenians belong to the clade Paenungulata with [[Elephant|elephants]] and hyraxes, and evolved in the [[Eocene]] about 50 million years ago. The two living families are Dugongidae, which contains the dugong and the extinct Steller's sea cow, and Trichechidae, which contains three [[Manatee|manatee]] species. Sirius is a [[Binary star|binary]] system of Sirius A, a main-sequence star about twice the mass of the [[Sun]] and 25 times as luminous, and Sirius B, a faint [[White dwarf|white dwarf]] companion. === KEY: Skeletal_muscle_c02f43
HEADWORD: Skeletal muscle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Mitochondria | Sarcomere | Skeleton | Tendon | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
One of three types of [[Vertebrate|vertebrate]] muscle tissue, the others being cardiac and smooth muscle. Skeletal muscles are usually attached by [[Tendon|tendons]] to the bones of the [[Skeleton]] and appear striated because of their [[Sarcomere|sarcomeres]]. Each muscle fiber is a long, multinucleated cell formed by the fusion of myoblasts, and it contains many [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] to meet its energy needs. === KEY: Skeleton_6ab48f
HEADWORD: Skeleton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Bone | Cartilage | Chitin | Human skeleton | Sponge | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Structural frame that supports the body of most animals. The main types are the exoskeleton, a rigid outer shell; the endoskeleton, a rigid internal frame to which organs and soft tissues attach; and the hydroskeleton, a flexible structure supported by the hydrostatic pressure of body fluids. The word comes from Ancient Greek *skeletós*, "dried up." Skeletons may also be classed as pliant or rigid, depending on whether they deform under stress and regain their shape.

Exoskeletons cover the body, protect against predators, and provide surfaces for muscle attachment. [[Arthropod|Arthropods]] must periodically molt as they grow, and the shells of mollusks are another form. Because an external skeleton is heavy relative to body mass, land animals with one are mostly small, while larger forms live in water. Rigid skeletons are built from materials such as [[Chitin|chitin]] in arthropods, calcium carbonate in stony corals and mollusks, and silicate in diatoms and radiolarians.

Endoskeletons are composed of mineralized tissues and occur in chordates, echinoderms, and sponges. They range from purely supportive structures, as in sponges, to attachment sites that transmit muscular forces. [[Vertebrate|Vertebrate]] skeletons are endoskeletons centered on an axial vertebral column and made mainly of [[Bone|bone]] and [[Cartilage|cartilage]]. The [[Human skeleton|human skeleton]] has 206 bones in adults, which fuse from about 270 at birth, and also serves as a reservoir for minerals and a site of blood cell production.

Hydrostatic skeletons are muscle- and connective-tissue-walled cavities of fluid found in soft-bodied animals such as jellyfish, flatworms, nematodes, and earthworms; alternating muscle contractions let the animal move. Invertebrate skeletons vary widely, including plated internal shells, ossicles, and the spicules of [[Sponge|sponges]]. The cytoskeleton serves a similar function at the cellular level.

=== KEY: Skin_931356
HEADWORD: Skin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fur | Integumentary system | Leather | Mammal | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Soft, flexible outer tissue covering the body of a [[Vertebrate|vertebrate]] animal, with three main functions: protection, regulation, and sensation. In [[Mammal|mammals]] it is an organ of the [[Integumentary system|integumentary system]] made of multiple layers of ectodermal tissue, guarding the underlying muscles, bones, ligaments, and internal organs.

The skin is the first line of defense against the environment, protecting against pathogens and excessive water loss. It also provides insulation, regulates temperature, and produces vitamin D. Mammalian skin has two primary layers. The epidermis provides waterproofing and a barrier to infection. It is a stratified squamous epithelium in which keratinocytes, which make up 95% of the epidermis, proliferate in the basal layer by mitosis, move upward, and are eventually shed from the surface. It contains no blood vessels and is nourished by diffusion from capillaries in the dermis. The dermis lies beneath it, consists of connective tissue, cushions the body from stress and strain, and gives the skin tensile strength and elasticity through collagen and elastic fibers.

Skin differs among groups. All mammals have some hair, and [[Fur|fur]] augments insulation. Reptiles and most fish have scales, and birds have feathers, all made of beta-keratins. Amphibian skin is a weak barrier and permits the passage of chemicals. Thick animal skin can be processed into [[Leather|leather]]. The oldest known skin, possibly from an ancient reptile, was fossilized about 289 million years ago.

=== KEY: Slavery_ba04fe
HEADWORD: Slavery

ENTRY:
Ownership of a person as property, especially in regard to their labor. Slavery typically involves compulsory work, with the location of work and residence dictated by the party holding the person in bondage. Enslavement occurred through law-breaking, debt, military defeat, or exploitation for cheaper labor, and some forms were instituted along lines of race or sex. Slaves were held for life or for a fixed period. In chattel slavery, the slave is legally the personal property of the owner and can be bought and sold.

Slavery existed in most societies and was a common feature of civilization. After the [[French Revolution]] France abolished slavery in 1794, Napoleon reintroduced it in 1802, and permanent abolition came in 1848. Slavery was abolished in much of the [[British Empire]] in 1833, throughout the United States in 1865, in [[Cuba]] in 1886, and in [[Brazil]], the last country in the Americas to do so, in 1888.

Slavery is now outlawed in every country except as punishment for a crime, but persists in some form everywhere. In 2019 approximately 40 million people, 26% of them children, were enslaved, most often in forced labor, debt bondage, forced marriage, or [[Human trafficking|human trafficking]].

Naturally recurring state of reduced mental and physical activity in which [[Consciousness|consciousness]] is altered and certain sensory activity is inhibited. Muscle activity and interaction with the surroundings decrease markedly, yet sleep involves active brain patterns and is more reactive than a coma. Sleeping persons perceive fewer stimuli but can generally still respond to loud noises.

Sleep occurs in repeating cycles that alternate between non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep. NREM, divided by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine into stages N1, N2, and N3, comes first; N3 is also called slow-wave or deep sleep, during which body temperature and heart rate fall and growth hormone is secreted in bursts. REM sleep is the main occasion for [[Dream|dreams]] and is marked by fast brain waves, eye movements, and loss of muscle tone. A cycle averages 90 minutes and occurs 4–6 times in a good night's sleep. The internal [[Circadian rhythm|circadian]] clock promotes sleep at night.

The brain uses significantly less energy during sleep; in quiet waking it accounts for 20% of the body's energy use. Most body systems are in an anabolic state, restoring the immune, nervous, skeletal, and muscular systems and supporting mood, memory, and cognitive function. The purposes of sleep remain under active research. It is highly conserved across animal evolution, and researchers have found that cleansing the brain of waste, including amyloid, may be a core purpose.

Sleep disorders include [[Insomnia|insomnia]], hypersomnia, [[Narcolepsy|narcolepsy]], [[Sleep apnea|sleep apnea]], sleepwalking, and REM sleep behavior disorder. Artificial light, including the blue light of digital screens, has altered human sleep patterns by disrupting release of the hormone melatonin.

=== KEY: Smallpox_79c5e6
HEADWORD: Smallpox
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Columbian exchange | Edward Jenner | Smallpox vaccine | Syphilis | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Infectious disease caused by the variola virus, genus *Orthopoxvirus*, and the only human disease to have been eradicated. The last naturally occurring case was diagnosed in October 1977, and the [[World Health Organization]] certified global eradication in 1980.

Initial symptoms were fever and vomiting, followed by mouth ulcers and a skin rash. Over days the rash turned into fluid-filled blisters with a central dent, which scabbed over and fell off, leaving scars. Transmission was primarily through prolonged face-to-face contact with an infected person, rarely through contaminated objects. Variola major, the severe and more common form, carried a death risk of about 30%, higher among babies; variola minor was milder, with historical death rates of 1% or less. Survivors often had extensive scarring, and some were left blind.

The earliest evidence dates to about 1500 BC in Egyptian mummies. The disease was among those introduced to the New World by the [[Columbian exchange]], killing large numbers of Native Americans. In 18th-century Europe an estimated 400,000 people died of it each year, and one-third of all blindness was due to smallpox. It is estimated to have killed up to 300 million people in the 20th century and around 500 million in its last 100 years.

Inoculation appears to have begun in China around the 1500s, and Europe adopted it from Asia in the first half of the 18th century. In 1796 [[Edward Jenner]] introduced the modern [[Smallpox vaccine|smallpox vaccine]]. In 1967 the WHO intensified its eradication campaign, using ring vaccination of contacts of cases. The term "smallpox" was first used in England in the 16th century to distinguish it from [[Syphilis|syphilis]], then called the "great pox."

=== KEY: Smoking_759f43

Combustion of a substance, usually plant material, and inhalation of the resulting fumes. It is practiced mainly as a route of administration for psychoactive chemicals, which vaporize and are rapidly absorbed through the lungs into the bloodstream, reaching the central nervous system. Tobacco smoking, the most popular form, is practiced by over one billion people, most of them in developing countries. In tobacco the active substance is [[Nicotine|nicotine]], an alkaloid carried in aerosol particles. Other drugs smoked include [[Cannabis|cannabis]], [[Opium|opium]], morphine, cocaine, and methamphetamine.

Tobacco is smoked mostly as [[Cigarette|cigarettes]], which are industrially manufactured or hand-rolled, but also in pipes, cigars, bidis, hookahs, and bongs. Most cigarette smokers inhale; most pipe and cigar smokers do not.

Smoking dates to as early as 5000 BC and appears in many cultures, where it was associated with religious ceremonies, offerings to deities, cleansing rituals, and shamanic divination. After European contact with the Americas, tobacco smoking spread quickly worldwide. In India and [[Sub-Saharan Africa]] it merged with existing smoking practices, mostly of cannabis. Attitudes have varied between places and eras, from holy to sinful and from panacea to health hazard; by the late 20th century smoking was viewed negatively, especially in Western countries.

Smoking is among the leading causes of [[Lung cancer|lung cancer]], heart attack, [[Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]], erectile dysfunction, and birth defects. Related diseases are shown to kill approximately half of long-term smokers. It is estimated to kill more than seven million people worldwide each year, including 1.6 million non-smokers exposed to passive smoke. In the United States about 480,000 die annually. Many countries respond by taxing tobacco heavily, publishing anti-smoking advertising, limiting or banning tobacco advertising, and helping smokers quit.

HEADWORD: Snell's law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexandria | Baghdad | Light | Pierre de Fermat | Ptolemy | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Formula relating the angles of incidence and refraction of [[Light|light]] passing between two media. The ratio of the sines of these angles equals the ratio of the refractive indices of the two media. The law was first discovered by the Persian scientist Ibn Sahl at [[Baghdad]] in 984. [[Ptolemy]] had earlier found an inaccurate relation in [[Alexandria]]. Willebrord Snellius derived an equivalent form in 1621 without publishing it, and [[René Descartes|René Descartes]] published a derivation in 1637. [[Pierre de Fermat|Fermat]] derived it from his principle of least time.

=== KEY: Sniper_007be3
HEADWORD: Sniper
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artillery | Camouflage | Military | Officer (armed forces) | World War I
ENTRY:
Highly trained [[Military|military]] or paramilitary marksman. Snipers engage targets from concealment or at distances beyond the target's ability to detect them, using high-precision rifles with high-magnification optics. They often also serve as scouts or observers, feeding information back to their units. Military snipers are trained in stalking, [[Camouflage|camouflage]], tracking and target range estimation. Typical missions include shooting high-value targets, especially [[Officer (armed forces)|officers]], and providing feedback for [[Artillery|artillery]] and air strikes. The term became common in the [[World War I|First World War]]. It derives from the verb *to snipe*, meaning to shoot the game bird snipe, which arose in the 1770s among soldiers in British India.
