=== KEY: Long_jump_416961
HEADWORD: Long jump
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Olympic Games | Tokyo | Track and field
ENTRY:
[[Track and field|Track and field]] event in which athletes combine speed, strength and agility to leap as far as possible from a takeoff point. The event dates to the [[Ancient Olympic Games]] and has been a men's Olympic event since 1896 and a women's since 1948. Jumps are measured perpendicular from the foul line to the nearest break in the sand, so competitors take off as close to the line as possible. In major competitions, the best eight after three trials receive three more attempts, and the longest valid jump wins. Tailwinds above 2 m/s (4.5 mph) do not count for records. In 2025 World Athletics began testing a wider 40-centimeter takeoff zone instead of the traditional board, to reduce invalid attempts, which had reached around 30%. The men's world record of 8.95 meters was set by Mike Powell in 1991 at the World Championships in [[Tokyo]].

=== KEY: Longevity_90e8eb
HEADWORD: Longevity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Genetics | Health care | Jeanne Calment | Life expectancy | Supercentenarian
ENTRY:
Length of an organism's life, especially exceptional longevity in humans. [[Life expectancy]], the average number of years remaining at a given age, is a statistical measure distinct from longevity, which refers to especially long-lived members of a population. In developed countries life expectancy ranges from 77–90 years; in developing countries, from 32–80 years. Canada's was 81.29 years (2010 est.) and Mozambique's 41.37 years. Significant factors in life expectancy include gender, [[Genetics|genetics]], access to [[Health care|health care]], hygiene, diet and nutrition, exercise, and crime rates. Twin studies estimate that 20–30% of the variation in human lifespan relates to genetics. The oldest person whose age has been verified by modern documentation was [[Jeanne Calment]], who lived 122 years and 164 days. Jiroemon Kimura, who lived 116 years and 54 days, is the oldest man so verified. The Gerontology Research Group validates records and maintains a list of [[Supercentenarian|supercentenarians]].

=== KEY: Longitude_9b4dab
HEADWORD: Longitude
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's rotation | Hipparchus | Latitude | Lunar eclipse | Prime meridian
ENTRY:
East-west geographic coordinate of a point on the surface of Earth or another celestial body. Longitude is an angular measurement, usually expressed in degrees and denoted by the Greek letter lambda (λ). The [[Prime meridian]] defines 0° longitude; by convention it passes near the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, England, and positive longitudes lie east of it. Because of [[Earth's rotation]], a difference of 15° of longitude corresponds to a one-hour difference in local time, so longitude can be found by comparing local time with an absolute time. [[Hipparchus]], in the 2nd century BC, divided the spherical Earth into 360° and proposed comparing the local time of a [[Lunar eclipse|lunar eclipse]] observed at two places. Determining longitude at sea was a central problem until clockmaker John Harrison's marine chronometers; his H4 model, tested in 1761–1762, lost only seconds over months-long voyages. Unlike [[Latitude|latitude]] lines, meridians converge at the poles.

=== KEY: Loom_bc723f
HEADWORD: Loom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Old English | Tapestry | Weaving
ENTRY:
Device for [[Weaving|weaving]] cloth and [[Tapestry|tapestry]]. The basic purpose of any loom is to hold warp threads under tension so that weft threads can be interlaced with them. The earliest looms, dating from the 5th millennium BC, were bars or beams fixed in place to hold parallel threads in tension. Weaving requires three principal motions. Shedding separates the warp threads to form a shed through which the shuttle carries the weft; picking passes the weft through the shed; and battening compresses each new weft thread against the fell. Conventional shuttle looms run at about 150 to 160 picks per minute. Take-up and let-off motions wind the finished cloth onto the cloth beam and release warp from the warp beam. An automatic loom requires 0.125 hp to 0.5 hp (100W to 400W). The word derives from the [[Old English]] *geloma*, a term for a utensil, tool or machine of any kind.

=== KEY: Loon_22df62
HEADWORD: Loon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Duck
ENTRY:
Aquatic diving bird of the family Gaviidae, found in much of North America and northern Eurasia, known as a diver in British and Irish English. All living loons belong to the genus *Gavia* and order Gaviiformes. Loons are the size of large [[Duck|ducks]] or small geese, with webbed feet, a spear-shaped bill and largely black-and-white summer plumage. Their feet are set far back on the body, making them strong swimmers but awkward on land. Most species must run across the water to take off; only the red-throated loon (*G. stellata*) can take off from land. Satellite-tracked loons have flown up to 1,078 km (670 mi) in 24 hours. Loons can live as long as 30 years and hold their breath for as long as 90 seconds underwater. In winter they move from northern freshwater lakes to southern marine coastlines, where salt glands above the eyes filter salts from the blood.

=== KEY: Looting_d6edda
HEADWORD: Looting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hurricane Katrina | Napoleonic Wars | Natural disaster | Riot | War crime | World War II
ENTRY:
Indiscriminate taking of goods by force, typically during war, [[Natural disaster|natural disasters]] or [[Riot|riots]]. Its proceeds are called booty, loot, plunder, spoils or pillage, and looting occurs when law and civil enforcement are temporarily ineffective. Looting by victorious armies was long considered a customary right until norms against wartime plunder became widely accepted after the [[Napoleonic Wars]] and especially after [[World War II]]. Modern armed conflicts prohibit it under international law, and it constitutes a [[War crime|war crime]]; the Hague Conventions of 1899 and 1907 explicitly prohibited pillage and looting. Disaster researchers have found that post-disaster looting is often cooperative rather than criminal, and that reports of mass looting after [[Hurricane Katrina]] were often inaccurate or overstated. Scholars argue that such narratives can reinforce stereotypes; media coverage after Katrina more often labeled Black residents as "looters" than white residents, who were described as "finding" supplies.

British mathematician, mathematical physicist and engineer, 1st Baron Kelvin. Born in [[Belfast]], he was professor of Natural Philosophy at the University of [[Glasgow]] for 53 years, researching the mathematical analysis of electricity and helping formulate the first and second laws of [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]]. He determined the correct value of [[Absolute zero]] as approximately −273.15 degrees Celsius or −459.67 degrees Fahrenheit, and absolute temperatures are stated in [[Kelvin|kelvin]] in his honor. === KEY: Lorentz_force_c684f4
HEADWORD: Lorentz force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric motor | Electromagnetism | Hendrik Lorentz | James Clerk Maxwell | Maxwell's equations | Particle accelerator | Plasma (physics) | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Force exerted on a charged particle by electric and magnetic fields. The electric component acts along the electric field, accelerating positive charges in its direction and negative charges opposite to it. The magnetic component is perpendicular to both the particle's velocity and the magnetic field, so it curves the particle's path, often into circles or helices. Together with [[Maxwell's equations]], the force law forms the foundation of classical [[Electromagnetism|electrodynamics]]. It underlies the operation of [[Electric motor|electric motors]], [[Particle accelerator|particle accelerators]] and the behavior of [[Plasma (physics)|plasmas]]. The law remains valid in [[Special relativity|special relativity]] but breaks down at small scales, where the intrinsic spin of particles gives rise to interactions it does not account for. [[Hendrik Lorentz]] completed the derivation in 1895, identifying the electric force a few years after Oliver Heaviside identified the magnetic force. Historians suggest the law is implicit in a paper by [[James Clerk Maxwell|James Clerk Maxwell]] published in 1865.

=== KEY: Lorentz_transformation_debc91
HEADWORD: Lorentz transformation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Hendrik Lorentz | Special relativity | Speed of light | Time dilation
ENTRY:
Linear transformation between the space and time coordinates of two inertial frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other. Named after the Dutch physicist [[Hendrik Lorentz]], the transformations use the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²), where v is relative velocity and c is the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. For motion along the x-axis, t′ = γ(t − vx/c²) and x′ = γ(x − vt), while y and z are unchanged. They supersede the Galilean transformation of Newtonian physics, which is a good approximation only at speeds much less than the speed of light. The transformations predict effects such as [[Time dilation|time dilation]] and length contraction; observers in different frames may measure different distances and elapsed times, yet the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames. [[Albert Einstein]] made them a cornerstone of [[Special relativity|special relativity]].

=== KEY: Lottery_879112

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gambling | Great Wall of China | Han dynasty | United States
ENTRY:
Form of [[Gambling|gambling]] based on random drawing of numbers for prizes. Some governments outlaw lotteries, while others endorse them and run national or state lotteries. The most common regulations ban sales to minors and license ticket vendors. Keno slips from China's [[Han dynasty]], dated between 205 and 187 BC, are the first recorded signs of a lottery, and such lotteries may have helped finance the [[Great Wall of China]]. The first recorded lotteries selling tickets for cash prizes were held in the Low Countries in the 15th century, when towns used them to raise money for fortifications and the poor. The Dutch state-owned Staatsloterij, dating from 1726, is the oldest running lottery. By the beginning of the 20th century, most forms of gambling, including lotteries, were illegal in the [[United States]] and most of Europe, a ban that lasted until after World War II.

=== KEY: Loudspeaker_1a33d6

ENTRY:
Electroacoustic transducer converting an electrical audio signal into sound. A loudspeaker system combines one or more drivers, an enclosure, and electrical connections. The dynamic driver, the most common type, shares the basic configuration of a [[Microphone|microphone]] but operates in reverse. Current from the signal passes through a voice coil in a magnetic field, and the resulting movement, due to [[Faraday's law of induction]], drives a diaphragm that moves air. === KEY: Louis_Pasteur_18abdf

French chemist, pharmacist, and microbiologist, renowned for his discoveries of the principles of [[Vaccination|vaccination]], microbial [[Fermentation|fermentation]], and [[Pasteurization|pasteurization]], which was named after him. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern bacteriology and has been called the "father of bacteriology" and the "father of microbiology," the latter title shared with [[Robert Koch]] and also attributed to [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]]. His work is credited with saving millions of lives through vaccines for [[Rabies|rabies]] and [[Anthrax|anthrax]].

His experiments supported the germ theory of disease by showing that diseases could be prevented by killing or stopping germs, and he developed the treatment of milk and wine to stop bacterial contamination. He developed vaccines for anthrax in cattle and sheep (1881) and for rabies (1885), the latter first given to Joseph Meister, a boy bitten by a rabid dog.

He directed the Pasteur Institute, established in 1887, until his death and was interred in a vault beneath it. His reputation became associated with controversies; a historical reassessment of his notebook revealed that he practiced deception to overcome his rivals.

=== KEY: Love_8bd7a1

ENTRY:
Range of strong, positive emotional and mental states, from the most sublime virtue to the deepest interpersonal affection to the simplest pleasure. A mother's love differs from a spouse's, which differs from love of food; most commonly the word refers to a feeling of strong attraction and emotional attachment. Many languages use several words for what English calls love, and cultural differences make a universal definition difficult.

Love is considered both positive and negative. Its virtue represents human kindness, compassion, and affection ("the unselfish, loyal and benevolent concern for the good of another"), while its vice is a moral flaw akin to vanity, selfishness, and egotism, potentially leading to [[Mania|mania]], obsessiveness, or codependency. It is commonly contrasted with hate and with [[Lust|lust]], facilitates interpersonal relationships, and is one of the most common themes in the creative arts.

Ancient Greek philosophers identified six forms: familial love (*storge*), friendly or platonic love (*philia*), romantic love (*eros*), self-love (*philautia*), guest love (*xenia*), and divine or unconditional love (*agape*). The triangular theory holds that intimacy, passion, and commitment are love's core components. Some historians date modern conceptions of romantic love to courtly Europe during or after the [[Middle Ages]], though ancient love poetry attests earlier romantic attachments.

Philosophers have offered competing definitions. [[Thomas Aquinas]], following [[Aristotle]], defined love as "to will the good of another," and [[Bertrand Russell]] described it as a condition of "absolute value." Neurobiological accounts instead describe romantic love as driven by hormones such as [[Oxytocin|oxytocin]], vasopressin, and [[Dopamine|dopamine]] in phases of lust, attraction, and attachment, and as an adaptation promoting pair-bonding, parental care, and cooperation.

=== KEY: Low_Earth_orbit_c6aeb7
HEADWORD: Low Earth orbit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollo program | Outer space | Space station | Van Allen radiation belt
ENTRY:
Orbit around Earth with a period of 128 minutes or less and low eccentricity. Most artificial objects in [[Outer space|outer space]] are in low Earth orbit, peaking in number at an altitude of about 800 km (500 mi). The farthest orbits in this range reach about 2,000 km (1,200 mi), near the inner [[Van Allen radiation belt]]. Only the nine [[Apollo program|Apollo]] lunar missions from 1968 to 1972, and the Artemis II lunar flyby in 2026, have carried people beyond it. All [[Space station|space stations]] to date have operated in low Earth orbit. Circular orbits need a speed of about 7.8 km/s (28,000 km/h), and satellites below 300 km (190 mi) decay quickly from atmospheric drag. Objects in these orbits can collide with satellites, so they are carefully tracked.

=== KEY: Low_pressure_area_19b5c5
HEADWORD: Low-pressure area
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coriolis force | Cyclone | High-pressure area | Rocky Mountains | Tibetan Plateau | Tropical cyclone
ENTRY:
Region of the atmosphere where pressure is lower than in surrounding areas. Low-pressure areas are usually associated with cloudy, windy weather and possible rain or storms, while [[High-pressure area|high-pressure areas]] bring lighter winds and clear skies. Winds circle counterclockwise around lows in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, because of the [[Coriolis force]]. Large rotating lows are called [[Cyclone|cyclones]]. German composer and pianist, a seminal figure in the [[History of music|history of music]] whose personal style was a key driver of the transition from Classical to Romantic music. Brazilian politician and former trade unionist; president of Brazil from 2003 and again since 2023. Born in [[Pernambuco]], he worked as a child and did not learn to read until he was 10. As a [[Trade union|trade unionist]] he led the ABC workers' strikes against Brazil's military government in 1978–80, and in 1980 he helped found the Workers' Party. He was a leader of the 1984 *Diretas Já* movement for direct elections. After unsuccessful presidential bids in 1989, 1994, and 1998, he won in 2002 and was reelected in 2006. His first presidency expanded social programs such as *Bolsa Família*, but was marked by the Mensalão vote-buying scandal. He was sentenced to prison in connection with [[Operation Car Wash]] in a controversial trial, and the Supreme Federal Court later annulled his convictions. He defeated the incumbent [[Jair Bolsonaro]] in 2022 and won a record-breaking fourth term in 2026.
