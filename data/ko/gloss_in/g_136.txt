=== KEY: Thermobaric_weapon_298c47
HEADWORD: Thermobaric weapon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Explosive | Oxygen
ENTRY:
Type of [[Explosive|explosive]] munition that disperses an aerosol cloud of fuel and ignites it using atmospheric [[Oxygen|oxygen]]. Also called an aerosol bomb or vacuum bomb, it needs no oxidizer of its own, so it is significantly more energetic than conventional explosives of equal weight. Its blast wave lasts significantly longer than that of a conventional explosive, and it is considerably more effective in enclosed spaces such as tunnels, buildings and fortifications. It is unsuitable underwater, at high altitude or in adverse weather. Many types can be fitted to hand-held launchers. The term derives from Greek words for "heat" and "pressure."

=== KEY: Thermodynamic_cycle_44bb7f
HEADWORD: Thermodynamic cycle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Entropy | Heat | Heat pump | Internal combustion engine | Steam turbine
ENTRY:
Linked sequence of thermodynamic processes that returns a system to its initial state. The working fluid may convert heat from a warm source into useful work and reject the remainder to a cold sink, which makes the cycle a [[Heat|heat]] engine. Run in reverse, the cycle becomes a [[Heat pump|heat pump]] that uses work to move heat from a cold source to a warm sink. Because the system returns to its initial state, its internal energy does not change over the cycle, and the net work equals the net heat transferred. Power cycles include the Rankine cycle of [[Steam turbine|steam turbines]] and the Otto and Diesel cycles of [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engines]]. The net [[Entropy|entropy]] change over a cycle is zero, because entropy is a state function. On a pressure–volume diagram, a clockwise loop indicates a power cycle.

=== KEY: Thermodynamic_free_energy_325058
HEADWORD: Thermodynamic free energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Enthalpy | Gibbs free energy | Hermann von Helmholtz | Potential energy | Statistical mechanics
ENTRY:
State function whose change is the maximum work a system can perform at constant temperature. Its sign shows whether a process is thermodynamically favorable or forbidden. Because free energy usually contains [[Potential energy|potential energy]], only changes in it are physically meaningful. The [[Gibbs free energy|Gibbs free energy]], G = H − TS, where H is [[Enthalpy|enthalpy]], is most useful for processes at constant pressure and temperature and is the usual choice in chemistry. The Helmholtz free energy, A = U − TS, measures the reversible work obtainable at constant temperature and is the usual choice in physics. [[Hermann von Helmholtz|Hermann von Helmholtz]] introduced the concept in 1882. The Helmholtz function is proportional to the logarithm of the partition function in [[Statistical mechanics|statistical mechanics]].

=== KEY: Thermodynamics_f1ed49
HEADWORD: Thermodynamics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Energy | Entropy | Heat | Laws of thermodynamics | Lord Kelvin | Physics | Statistical mechanics | Steam engine
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Physics|physics]] that deals with [[Heat|heat]], work, and temperature, and their relation to [[Energy|energy]], [[Entropy|entropy]], and the physical properties of matter and radiation.

Its behavior is governed by four [[Laws of thermodynamics|laws]] that give a quantitative description using measurable macroscopic quantities and can be explained in terms of microscopic constituents by [[Statistical mechanics|statistical mechanics]]. The zeroth law establishes the concept of temperature. The first law states that energy can be transferred between systems as heat, as work, and with the transfer of matter. The second law defines entropy, which describes the direction in which a system can evolve. The third law holds that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature nears absolute zero. The field applies to engines, phase transitions, chemical reactions, and even black holes, and is essential to physical chemistry, biochemistry, chemical and mechanical engineering, meteorology, materials science, and economics.

The history of the discipline generally begins with Otto von Guericke, who built the first vacuum pump in 1650. In 1656 Robert Boyle and Robert Hooke built an air pump and noted a correlation between pressure, temperature, and volume, leading to Boyle's law. Thomas Savery built the first engine in 1697, followed by Thomas Newcomen in 1712. James Watt's external condenser greatly raised [[Steam engine|steam engine]] efficiency.

Thermodynamics developed from the desire to improve steam engine efficiency, particularly through Sadi Carnot's work in 1824. Rudolf Clausius first stated the second law in his 1850 paper "On the Moving Force of Heat" and named entropy in 1865. [[Lord Kelvin]] formulated a concise definition of the subject in 1854. In 1909 Constantin Carathéodory presented an axiomatic formulation.

=== KEY: Thermohaline_circulation_c6162c
HEADWORD: Thermohaline circulation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Climate | Climate change | Flux | Gulf Stream | Ocean | Southern Ocean
ENTRY:
Part of the large-scale [[Ocean|ocean]] circulation driven by global density gradients from surface heat and freshwater [[Flux|fluxes]]. The name combines "thermo-" (temperature) and "haline" (salt content), which together determine seawater density. Wind-driven surface currents such as the [[Gulf Stream]] carry water poleward, where it cools, sinks and eventually flows into the ocean basins. The bulk upwells in the [[Southern Ocean]], while the oldest waters, with a transit time of approximately 1000 years, upwell in the North Pacific. The circulation transports heat and dissolved matter around the globe, strongly affecting [[Climate|climate]]. Its two major limbs are the Atlantic circulation centered in the North Atlantic and the Southern Ocean circulation near [[Antarctica]]. Evidence suggests both are slowing as [[Climate change|climate change]] adds meltwater from ice sheets, and outright collapse of either would increase droughts in its hemisphere while making the other wetter.

=== KEY: Thermometer_d9cb7b
HEADWORD: Thermometer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Absolute zero | Galen | Galileo Galilei | Hero of Alexandria | Meteorology
ENTRY:
Device that measures temperature using a sensor whose change is converted into a numerical value. The sensor may be the bulb of a mercury-in-glass thermometer or an infrared detector, and the change is shown on a scale or digital readout. Thermometers are used in technology, industry, [[Meteorology|meteorology]], medicine and cooking. Early devices included Philo of Byzantium's air-filled tube and the pneumatic fountain of [[Hero of Alexandria]]. In the late 16th and early 17th centuries, [[Galileo Galilei]] and others developed air-filled thermoscopes, which indicated temperature changes without numerical scales. [[Galen]] proposed a fixed reference point of equal parts ice and boiling water. Gabriel Fahrenheit's mercury thermometer, invented in 1714, led to the Fahrenheit scale. The Kelvin scale places [[Absolute zero|absolute zero]] at 0 K.

=== KEY: Thermonuclear_weapon_121bff
HEADWORD: Thermonuclear weapon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edward Teller | John von Neumann | Nuclear arms race | Nuclear fusion | Nuclear weapon | Tsar Bomba
ENTRY:
Second-generation [[Nuclear weapon|nuclear weapon]] using [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]], also called a hydrogen bomb or H-bomb. X-rays from a fission primary compress a separate fusion stage in the Teller–Ulam configuration, believed to underlie all such weapons. Named for [[Edward Teller]] and Stanisław Ulam, the design was outlined in 1951 with contributions from [[John von Neumann]]. The first full-scale test, Ivy Mike, was carried out by the United States in 1952. The Soviet Union (1955), United Kingdom (1957), China (1966) and France (1968) later developed multi-stage devices. Thermonuclear weapons are the only artificial source of explosions above one megaton of TNT. The [[Tsar Bomba]], the most powerful bomb ever detonated, yielded 50 megatons of TNT. Most current designs derive much of their energy from fast fission of a uranium tamper, which causes high fallout. Their development dominated the [[Nuclear arms race|nuclear arms race]] of the Cold War.

=== KEY: Thermoregulation_089838
HEADWORD: Thermoregulation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bat | Bear | Hypothermia | Organism | Thermometer
ENTRY:
Ability of an [[Organism|organism]] to keep its body temperature within certain boundaries despite very different surrounding temperatures. Endotherms, often called warm-blooded, generate most of their heat through metabolism and keep their temperature relatively constant. Ectotherms, often called cold-blooded, rely mainly on external sources of heat and on behavior to regulate temperature. Thermoconforming organisms simply adopt the surrounding temperature. Hyperthermia occurs when body temperature rises well above normal; humans may experience lethal hyperthermia when the wet bulb temperature is sustained above 35 °C (95 °F) for six hours. [[Hypothermia|Hypothermia]] occurs when core body temperature falls below 35 °C (95 °F). Some animals, such as hibernating [[Bear|bears]] and [[Bat|bats]] in torpor, allow their temperature to drop to conserve energy. Exact data on animal temperature became possible only with the introduction of [[Thermometer|thermometers]].

=== KEY: Theropoda_197836
HEADWORD: Theropoda
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clade | Cretaceous | Giganotosaurus | Herbivore | Jurassic | Ornithischia | Triassic | Tyrannosaurus
ENTRY:
One of three major [[Clade|clades]] of dinosaurs, alongside [[Ornithischia]] and Sauropodomorpha. Theropods are characterized by hollow bones and three-toed feet with claws. They were ancestrally carnivorous, though some groups evolved to be [[Herbivore|herbivores]] and omnivores. Many coelurosaurs were covered with feathers. Birds evolved from small coelurosaurian theropods in the [[Jurassic]], and around 11,000 living bird species make theropods the only known living group of dinosaurs. Theropods first appeared in the Late [[Triassic]], about 231.4 million years ago, and included most large land carnivores until the end of the [[Cretaceous]], about 66 million years ago. These included [[Tyrannosaurus]] and [[Giganotosaurus]], among the largest land carnivores ever.

=== KEY: Thiol_351ecd
HEADWORD: Thiol
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcohol (chemistry) | Boiling point | Garlic | Hydrogen bond | Mercury (element) | Natural gas | Redox
ENTRY:
Organosulfur compound of the form R–SH, the sulfur analog of an [[Alcohol (chemistry)|alcohol]]. The name blends "thio-" with "alcohol," and the prefix comes from Ancient Greek *theion*, "sulfur." Many thiols smell like [[Garlic|garlic]], cabbage or rotten eggs, and they are added as odorants to [[Natural gas|natural gas]], which is naturally odorless. Skunk spray consists mainly of low-molecular-weight thiols, which the human nose can detect at 10 parts per billion. Thiols have lower [[Boiling point|boiling points]] than alcohols because of weaker [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonding]]. They oxidize to disulfides under mild conditions, a reversible process central to [[Redox|redox]] reactions in biology. Thiols are also called mercaptans, a term derived from Latin for "capturing [[Mercury (element)|mercury]]."

Destructive war fought mainly in [[Central Europe]], 1618–1648. === KEY: Thomas_Edison_875813
HEADWORD: Edison, Thomas
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Direct current | Electric power distribution | General Electric | George Westinghouse | Incandescent light bulb | Michigan | Phonograph | Telegraphy
ENTRY:
American inventor and businessman, holder of 1,093 U.S. patents. Edison is known for his work on the [[Incandescent light bulb|incandescent light bulb]], the [[Phonograph|phonograph]], [[Electric power distribution|electric power distribution]], and early motion pictures. Born in Milan, Ohio, he grew up in Port Huron, [[Michigan]], the seventh and last child of Samuel Ogden Edison Jr. and Nancy Matthews Elliott. He attended school for only a few months, learning from his mother, a former school teacher, and from reading on his own. His hearing declined from age 12, and he later believed the loss helped him concentrate. At 13 he sold newspapers, candy, and vegetables on trains and founded the *Grand Trunk Herald*. From 1863 to 1869 he held night-shift [[Telegraphy|telegraphy]] jobs in several states, and by 22 he had sold a few early inventions and moved to New York City. By 29 he owned a telegraph recorder factory in Newark with over one hundred employees.

His Menlo Park laboratory, established in 1876, is considered the first industrial research laboratory, and the phonograph made him internationally famous. In 1878 he began work on an electrical lighting system meant to replace gas lamps. In the "War of the Currents," he championed [[Direct current|direct current]] and was criticized for publicizing the dangers of alternating current through animal electrocutions aimed at rivals such as [[George Westinghouse]]. Competition from Westinghouse's system forced him to yield, and the merger of the Edison General Electric Company with Thomson-Houston produced [[General Electric]]. Later he founded a cement company, built a motion picture camera, and ran firms making batteries, films, records, and phenol. His last project was a search for domestic rubber production. He had troubled relationships with his children, and he lived with diabetes.

=== KEY: Thomas_Robert_Malthus_e41f54

PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Charles Darwin | Famine | Industrial Revolution
ENTRY:
English economist and Anglican cleric. In *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798), Malthus argued that gains in food production were temporary because they led to population growth, which restored the earlier level of production per person. Populations tended to grow until the poor suffered hardship, [[Famine]], disease and war, a pattern later called the "Malthusian trap." He criticized the Poor Laws for causing inflation rather than improving the well-being of the poor, and he supported taxes on grain imports, the Corn Laws. Pioneers of evolutionary biology, including [[Charles Darwin]] and [[Alfred Russel Wallace]], read him. His failure to predict the [[Industrial Revolution]] was a frequent criticism, yet his work is described as the theoretical foundation of conventional thinking on global hunger and famine for almost two centuries.

=== KEY: Thought_036823
HEADWORD: Thought
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Behaviorism | Cognitive psychology | Mind | Perception | Phenomenology (philosophy) | Psychoanalysis
ENTRY:
Cognitive process in which the [[Mind|mind]] considers, creates, or manipulates ideas, representations, or information. Core forms include judging, reasoning, concept formation, problem solving, and deliberation. Memory and imagination are also frequently counted as types of thought. Thought may arise in response to [[Perception|perception]] but can also occur without immediate sensory input. In a broad sense, any mental event, including unconscious processes, may be called thought, and the term can also denote the resulting mental states or systems of ideas.

Theories of thinking differ. Platonism holds that thought discerns eternal forms and their interrelations, distinguishing them from imperfect sensory imitations. Aristotelianism interprets it as instantiating the universal essence of an object within the mind, derived from sense experience. Inner speech theories describe thought as silent verbal expression, in a natural language or in a "mental language," or Mentalese, as proposed by the language of thought hypothesis. Associationism views it as a succession of ideas governed by laws of association, while [[Behaviorism|behaviorism]] reduces it to behavioral dispositions. Computationalism compares it to information processing in computers. These theories are not necessarily mutually exclusive.

Recognized types include judgment, which affirms or denies a proposition; reasoning, which draws conclusions from premises or evidence; problem solving, which aims at specific goals; and deliberation, which weighs possible courses of action. Thinking is often contrasted with feeling, seen as rational and dispassionate. Many disciplines study thought. [[Phenomenology (philosophy)|Phenomenology]] examines its subjective experience, [[Cognitive psychology|cognitive psychology]] treats it as information processing, and [[Psychoanalysis|psychoanalysis]] stresses unconscious processes. Neuroscience, linguistics, and artificial intelligence also investigate it. The word derives from Old English *þoht*.

Imaginary scenario meant to elucidate or test an argument or theory. === KEY: Three_dimensional_space_2697af
HEADWORD: Three-dimensional space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Cartesian coordinate system | Euclid's Elements | Euclidean space | General relativity | Leonhard Euler | René Descartes | William Rowan Hamilton
ENTRY:
Geometric model in which three values, called coordinates, are required to determine the position of a point. Most commonly the term means three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]], which models physical space; more general three-dimensional spaces are called 3-manifolds. In classical physics it serves as a model of the universe in which all known matter exists, and in [[General relativity|relativity theory]] it is a local subspace of space-time. Any three directions not lying in one plane may serve as coordinate directions; when pairwise perpendicular, the values are often labeled width, height or depth, and length.

In the [[Cartesian coordinate system]], three axes, usually labeled x, y, and z, are perpendicular to one another at the origin, and each point is given by an ordered triple of real numbers measuring its distance from the planes determined by the other two axes. Cylindrical and spherical coordinates are other common methods. The set of such triples is denoted ℝ³.

[[Aristotle]] held that three dimensions are all there are, since what is divisible in three directions is divisible in all. Books XI to XIII of [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]] treat solid geometry, including perpendicularity, parallelism, and the five regular Platonic solids. In the 17th century [[René Descartes]] described space with coordinates in *La Géométrie*, and Pierre de Fermat independently developed similar ideas. In the 18th century [[Leonhard Euler]] derived the first analytical geodesic equation and, with Gaspard Monge, laid foundations for differential geometry. In the 19th century [[William Rowan Hamilton]] developed quaternions and coined the terms scalar and vector. Josiah Willard Gibbs later identified the dot and cross products in their own right, and Arthur Cayley applied matrix mathematics to n-dimensional geometry.

=== KEY: Thrust_fault_223322
HEADWORD: Thrust fault
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's crust | Fault (geology)
ENTRY:
Type of reverse [[Fault (geology)|fault]] with a dip of 45 degrees or less. A thrust fault is a break in the [[Earth's crust]] across which older rocks are pushed above younger rocks. If the fault plane lies at a shallow angle, often less than 15 degrees from horizontal, and the overlying block is displaced far, the fault is called an overthrust. === KEY: Tidal_locking_4e7fac
HEADWORD: Tidal locking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charon (moon) | Earth | Mercury (planet) | Moon | Pluto | Rotation | Sun
ENTRY:
Gravitational state in which a body's rotation period matches its orbital period around a larger partner. Tidal locking occurs when gravitational interaction slows a body's [[Rotation|rotation]] until its rotation rate no longer changes over a complete orbit. As a result, the [[Moon]] always shows the same face to [[Earth]]. Usually only the satellite is locked to the larger body, but where both masses and distances are relatively small, each can be locked to the other, as with [[Pluto]] and [[Charon (moon)|Charon]]. Friction from tidal bulges dissipates rotational energy as heat, and the process that ends in locking is called tidal despinning. Some sources use the term only for 1:1 synchronous rotation, while others include resonances such as that of [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]], which completes three rotations for every two orbits of the [[Sun]].

=== KEY: Tide_5a36fb
HEADWORD: Tide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coast | Earth's rotation | Full moon | Isaac Newton | Moon | Sun | Wind
ENTRY:
Periodic rise and fall of sea level, caused mainly by the gravity of the [[Moon]] and the [[Sun]]. Tides result from differential gravitational forces combined with the orbital motion of the Earth–Moon system and the [[Earth's rotation|Earth's rotation]]. [[Isaac Newton]] described the gravitational cause mathematically in 1687. Most coasts have ocean tides that rise and fall about twice a day, though the shape of ocean basins, [[Coast|coastlines]] and friction modify their height and timing. Tidal range varies over a two-week cycle: near [[Full moon|full]] and new moons, when the Sun and Moon align, spring tides produce higher high waters and lower low waters, while at quarter moons neap tides are milder. Storm surges caused by [[Wind|wind]] and barometric pressure can also change sea level, especially in shallow seas and near coasts.

Agricultural preparation of [[Soil|soil]] by mechanical agitation, such as digging, stirring and overturning. Tillage can be done by hand with tools such as [[Hoe (tool)|hoes]] and shovels, or by draft animals and machines pulling [[Plough|plows]] and [[Harrow (tool)|harrows]]. Primary tillage, such as plowing, loosens the soil and mixes in fertilizer or plant material, leaving a rough surface. Conservation tillage, which disturbs less of the soil surface and keeps crop residue, covered about 72% of U.S. cropland in 2017, helping to reduce [[Erosion|erosion]].

=== KEY: Timbuktu_396fbe
HEADWORD: Timbuktu
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mali Empire | Mansa Musa | Songhai Empire | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
City in Mali, capital of the Tombouctou Region, about 20 km north of the Niger River (pop., 2023: about 84,000). Timbuktu became a permanent settlement early in the 12th century and flourished on trans-Saharan trade in salt, gold and ivory after a visit by [[Mansa Musa]] around 1325. It became part of the [[Mali Empire]] in the early 14th century and was absorbed by the [[Songhai Empire]] in 1468. A Moroccan army defeated the Songhai in 1591 and made the city its capital. Timbuktu's Islamic scholars and the Sankoré Madrasah, an Islamic university, made it a scholarly center in Africa, supported by an important book trade. After its golden age came decline, and France took over Mali in 1893. Timbuktu was designated a [[World Heritage Site]] in 1988 for its mud-brick mosques. Extremist groups later destroyed cultural sites, but local efforts preserved much of its manuscript collection.
