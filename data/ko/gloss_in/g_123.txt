Natural submerged ridge or bank of sand or other loose material that rises from the bed of a body of water to near the surface. Shoals are typically long and narrow, forming where [[River|rivers]], streams or ocean currents deposit [[Sediment|sediment]]. Their shallowness is a danger to [[Navigation|navigation]]. === KEY: Shock_circulatory_7e1ce5
HEADWORD: Shock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bleeding | Cardiac arrest | Circulatory system | Myocardial infarction | Physical examination | Sepsis
ENTRY:
Medical condition of insufficient blood flow to the body's tissues, caused by problems with the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]]. Early symptoms include weakness, elevated heart rate, fast breathing, sweating, anxiety and increased thirst, and complications can progress to confusion, unconsciousness or [[Cardiac arrest|cardiac arrest]]. Shock has four main types: hypovolemic, from [[Bleeding|bleeding]], diarrhea or vomiting; cardiogenic, which may follow a [[Myocardial infarction|heart attack]]; obstructive, from cardiac tamponade or a tension pneumothorax; and distributive, from [[Sepsis|sepsis]], anaphylaxis, upper spinal cord injury or certain overdoses. Diagnosis combines symptoms, [[Physical examination|physical examination]] and laboratory tests. Shock is a medical emergency; emergency help should be called at once, and while waiting the person should be kept warm, with legs raised if possible.

=== KEY: Short_story_023426
HEADWORD: Short story
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anton Chekhov | Edgar Allan Poe | Geoffrey Chaucer | Giovanni Boccaccio | Novel | Prose | Roman Empire | William Faulkner
ENTRY:
Brief work of narrative fiction in [[Prose|prose]] that can typically be read in a single sitting.

A short story focuses on a self-contained incident or a series of linked incidents, intended to evoke a single effect or mood. It uses the techniques of the [[Novel|novel]], but typically to a lesser degree. Defining it is problematic. [[Edgar Allan Poe]]'s essay "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) made the classic point that it should be readable in one sitting, and H. G. Wells said it should take from fifteen to fifty minutes to read aloud. Somerset Maugham held that a story must have a plot, while [[Anton Chekhov]] thought it should be a suggestive "slice of life" with neither beginning nor end. [[William Faulkner]] called it character-driven. Typical length is 1,000 to 4,000 words, with some up to 15,000; under 1,000 is flash fiction.

The form descends from oral storytelling traditions that produced epics such as the *Ramayana*, the *Mahabharata*, and Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, and it existed as legends, myths, and fables in ancient communities. The anecdote, a brief realistic narrative embodying a point, was popular during the [[Roman Empire]]; many were collected in the 13th or 14th century as the *Gesta Romanorum*. In Europe, written form developed in the early 14th century, most notably with [[Giovanni Boccaccio]]'s *Decameron* and [[Geoffrey Chaucer]]'s *Canterbury Tales*.

The modern short story developed in the early 19th century, and the term acquired its modern meaning in the 1880s, having initially referred to children's tales. Washington Irving, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Nikolai Gogol, and Guy de Maupassant shaped it, and the 20th century brought expansive experimentation. Its form has been closely tied to the publishing industry.

Smoothbore [[Firearm|firearm]] that fires a shotshell of numerous small pellets, or a single solid slug. === KEY: Shrimp_2f2e18
HEADWORD: Shrimp
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crab | Crustacean | Lobster | Mangrove | Whale
ENTRY:
Decapod [[Crustacean|crustacean]] with an elongated body that moves mainly by swimming. Shrimp usually belong to the decapod groups Caridea or Dendrobranchiata. They swim by paddling swimmerets under the abdomen and escape by flicking the tail, which drives them backward in a movement called "lobstering". Unlike [[Crab|crabs]] and [[Lobster|lobsters]], shrimp have thin, fragile legs used mainly for perching. Thousands of species live in freshwater and marine habitats, and they are an important food for fish and [[Whale|whales]]. Most live from one to seven years. Their muscular tails are widely eaten, with about 440 billion farmed and about 25 trillion wild shrimp killed annually. Excessive bycatch and overfishing are significant concerns, and intensive farming has raised concerns over [[Mangrove|mangrove]] deforestation and pollution.

=== KEY: Shyness_a99f09
HEADWORD: Shyness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Self-esteem
ENTRY:
Psychological trait marked by apprehension and inhibition in new social settings or when others may judge. Shyness, also called diffidence, is frequently associated with social avoidance and is considered a normal personality variant. It is distinct from social anxiety disorder, which impairs daily functioning. Although shyness can be an attribute of people with [[Self-esteem|low self-esteem]], it is often defined by concern about the negative evaluation of others, such as mocking or rejection. Genetic data suggest that shyness is heritable, though environmental factors such as child abuse or ridicule contribute to its development. Research indicates a positive correlation between childhood fearfulness and shyness, and higher cortisol levels in shy children are associated with increased susceptibility to illness. Shyness is distinct from introversion, which involves a preference for low-stimulation environments rather than fear of social judgment.

Geographical region comprising all of North Asia, part of [[Russia]] since its conquest began in 1582. Inland province in southwestern [[China]], lying in the Sichuan Basin and the Tibetan Plateau. Its capital is [[Chengdu]], and its population is about 83 million. In antiquity the area was home to the kingdoms of Ba and Shu until their incorporation by the Qin around 316 BC. Austrian neurologist, founder of [[Psychoanalysis]].

=== KEY: Signal_modulation_958224

ENTRY:
Process of varying one or more properties of a periodic waveform to transmit information. In electronics and telecommunication, a message signal, such as sound from a [[Microphone]], video from a camera or a stream of binary digits, is encoded onto a carrier wave. === KEY: Signal_processing_fc9e76

[[Electrical engineering]] subfield that analyzes, modifies and synthesizes signals such as sound, images and scientific measurements. === KEY: Signal_to_noise_ratio_4e6907

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Measure of the level of a desired signal relative to background [[Noise|noise]], usually expressed in [[Decibel|decibels]]. It is defined as the ratio of signal power to noise power, and a ratio above 1:1 (greater than 0 dB) indicates more signal than noise. SNR is a key parameter for communication systems, audio equipment, [[Radar|radar]], imaging and data acquisition systems. A high SNR means a signal is clear and easy to detect, while a low SNR means the signal is obscured or corrupted. It can be improved by increasing signal strength, reducing noise, filtering or using error-correction techniques. The Shannon–Hartley theorem, a fundamental law of information theory, shows that the maximum data rate of a channel depends on its bandwidth and SNR. In decibels, SNR (dB) = 10 log₁₀(signal power ÷ noise power).

=== KEY: Sikhism_5fec3a

ENTRY:
Monotheistic Indian religion and philosophy that originated in the [[Punjab]] region around the end of the 15th century.

Followed by 25–30 million adherents, known as Sikhs, it is one of the most recently founded major religions. It developed from the teachings of [[Guru Nanak]], the first guru, and nine successors. The tenth, [[Guru Gobind Singh]], named the [[Guru Granth Sahib]] as his successor, closing the line of human gurus; Sikhs regard it as the 11th and eternally living guru.

Core beliefs, articulated in the Guru Granth Sahib, include faith in one creator (Ik Onkar), called Waheguru; the divine unity and equality of all humankind; selfless service (sevā); striving for justice for all (sarbat da bhala); and honest conduct and livelihood. [[Meditation]] and remembrance of God (simran) are expressed through devotional music (kirtan) or inwardly through naam japna. Ego, anger, greed, attachment, and lust, the "Five Thieves", are held to distract from devotion.

The religion developed in times of religious persecution and gained converts from both [[Hinduism]] and [[Islam]]. The Mughal emperors executed two gurus, Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur, after they refused to convert to Islam. In response, Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa in 1699, an order to protect freedom of conscience and religion whose members embody the ideal of the sant-sipāhī ("saint-soldier"). Baptised Sikhs wear the five Ks, articles of faith that include kesh (uncut hair), which is why most religious Sikh men wear a turban. Some Sikhs prefer the term Sikhi to Sikhism.

=== KEY: Silicon_ec4738
HEADWORD: Silicon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical element | Concrete | Integrated circuit | Jöns Jacob Berzelius | Oxygen | Semiconductor | Silicate | Silicone
ENTRY:
[[Chemical element]] (symbol Si, atomic number 14), a hard, brittle crystalline solid with a blue-gray metallic luster.

A tetravalent non-metal (sometimes considered a metalloid) and [[Semiconductor|semiconductor]], silicon belongs to group 14 of the periodic table, below carbon, and is relatively unreactive. It melts at 1414 °C and boils at 3265 °C, the second highest values among the metalloids and nonmetals after boron. Its density is 2.329 g/cm³, and its main stable isotope is ²⁸Si (92.2%). It is the eighth most common element in the universe by mass and, at about 28% by mass, the second most abundant in the Earth's crust after [[Oxygen|oxygen]]. It rarely occurs pure; more than 90% of the crust is composed of [[Silicate|silicate]] minerals.

Its high affinity for oxygen long delayed isolation. Antoine Lavoisier suspected in 1787 that silica was an oxide of an unknown element. Humphry Davy proposed the name "silicium", from Latin *silex*, "flint"; in 1817 Thomas Thomson gave it its present name. [[Jöns Jacob Berzelius]] is usually credited with the discovery for preparing and purifying amorphous silicon.

Most silicon is used commercially without being separated, as clay, silica sand, and stone in construction, in Portland cement and [[Concrete|concrete]], and in porcelain and glass. Silicon carbide serves as an abrasive and in high-strength ceramics, and silicon is the basis of the synthetic polymers called [[Silicone|silicones]]. Less than 15% of silicon is highly purified for semiconductor electronics, where it is essential to transistors, solar cells, and [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuit]] chips, so the late 20th and early 21st centuries have been called the Silicon Age. Sea sponges, diatoms, and radiolaria build skeletons of silica.

=== KEY: Silk_Road_388f7b
HEADWORD: Silk Road
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asia | Belt and Road Initiative | Black Death | Buddhism | Han dynasty | Ottoman Empire | Parthian Empire | Zhang Qian
ENTRY:
Network of [[Asia|Asian]] trade routes, active from the second century BC until the mid-15th century, linking East and West.

Spanning over 6,400 km on land, it facilitated economic, cultural, political, and religious exchange. It took its name from the trade in silk textiles produced mainly in China; the term was coined in the late 19th century and popularized in 1877 by Ferdinand von Richthofen. The network began with the expansion of the [[Han dynasty]] into Central Asia around 114 BC, through the missions of the imperial envoy [[Zhang Qian]]. The [[Parthian Empire]] bridged the network to the Mediterranean, and by the first century AD Chinese silk was widely sought in Rome, Egypt, and Greece. Eastern goods included tea, dyes, perfumes, and porcelain; Western exports included horses, camels, honey, wine, and gold.

The network was decentralized and security sparse; few travelers covered its full length, relying instead on middlemen such as the Sogdians. It also carried religious and scientific ideas, especially those of [[Buddhism]], and plague, possibly contributing to the [[Black Death]]. From 1453 the [[Ottoman Empire]] competed for control of the overland routes, which prompted European polities to seek alternatives and began the Age of Discovery. "New Silk Road" now describes infrastructure projects such as the Chinese [[Belt and Road Initiative]]. UNESCO designated the Chang'an-Tianshan corridor a World Heritage Site in 2014.

The concept is contested. Some historians prefer "Silk Routes" because it was never a single route. Warwick Ball calls it a "myth" of modern academia, arguing that the maritime spice trade mattered more to the Roman economy and that no free overland movement of goods existed before the Mongol Empire. Critics add that the term sidelines steppe nomads, India, and Iran.

=== KEY: Silver_f96e34

ENTRY:
[[Chemical element]] (symbol Ag, from Latin *argentum*; atomic number 47), a soft, white, lustrous transition metal.

Silver has the highest electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and reflectivity of any metal. It is extremely ductile and malleable, with a density of 10.503 g/cm³, a melting point of 961.8 °C, and a boiling point of 2,162 °C. It resists oxidation in air but tarnishes on exposure to sulfur compounds, forming black silver sulfide. It resembles its group 11 neighbors, [[Copper|copper]] and [[Gold|gold]]. Natural silver consists of two stable isotopes, ¹⁰⁷Ag (51.839%) and ¹⁰⁹Ag.

One of the seven metals of antiquity, silver has long been valued as a precious metal and a coinage metal, with an enduring role in monetary systems, jewelry, tableware, and decorative arts. Its purity is measured per mille; a 94%-pure alloy is "0.940 fine". Because pure silver is soft, [[Coin|coinage]] and jewelry silver is usually alloyed with copper.

Industrial uses accounted for about 59% of demand in 2024. They include photovoltaics, electrical contacts, brazing alloys, catalysis, mirrors, and antimicrobial materials. Silver halides are important in photographic film and paper, though digital imaging has reduced demand. Silver is rarely used for its conductivity because of its cost, but during World War II in the U.S., 13,540 tons were used for the electromagnets of calutrons that enriched uranium, because of a wartime shortage of copper.

=== KEY: Simple_machine_34f4a2
HEADWORD: Simple machine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Galileo Galilei | Inclined plane | Lever | Pulley | Screw | Wedge | Wheel and axle
ENTRY:
Mechanical device that changes the direction or magnitude of a force. The term usually refers to the six classical simple machines defined by Renaissance scientists: the [[Lever|lever]], [[Wheel and axle]], [[Pulley|pulley]], [[Inclined plane|inclined plane]], [[Wedge|wedge]], and [[Screw|screw]]. A simple machine uses a single applied force to do work against a single load force. Ignoring friction losses, the work done on the load equals the work done by the applied force, so a machine can increase output force only at the cost of a proportional decrease in the distance the load moves. The ratio of output force to applied force is the mechanical advantage.

Simple machines can be regarded as elementary building blocks of compound machines; a bicycle, for example, uses wheels, levers, and pulleys. The mechanical advantage of a compound machine is the product of the advantages of its component simple machines.

The idea originated with the Greek philosopher [[Archimedes]] around the 3rd century BC, who studied the lever, pulley, and screw and discovered the principle of mechanical advantage in the lever. Greek understanding was limited to statics, without the tradeoff between force and distance or the concept of work. In 1586 the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin derived the mechanical advantage of the inclined plane. The Italian scientist [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] worked out the complete dynamic theory in 1600 in *Le Mecaniche*, showing that simple machines do not create energy, only transform it.

Modern mechanics has moved beyond treating the six machines as the ultimate building blocks. By the late 1800s Franz Reuleaux had identified hundreds of machine elements, and modern machine theory analyzes machines as kinematic chains of elementary linkages called kinematic pairs.

Under Ismael Zambada García and Joaquín Guzmán Loera, it expanded into the global trade of [[Cocaine|cocaine]], [[Fentanyl|fentanyl]], [[Heroin|heroin]] and [[Methamphetamine|methamphetamine]]. Since 2024 an internal conflict has split it between followers of Guzmán's sons and of Zambada's son. The United States Intelligence Community considers it the largest and most powerful drug trafficking organization in the world.

Southeastern province of Pakistan, third-largest by land area and second-largest by population. Its capital, [[Karachi]], is the country's largest city and main financial hub. Sindh is bounded by the [[Arabian Sea]] to the south and borders Gujarat and Rajasthan in India to the east. Its landscape includes alluvial plains of the [[Indus River]], the Thar Desert and the Kirthar Mountains. Sindh is prominent for its Bronze Age history under the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]], and [[Mohenjo-daro]] is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Arabs conquered Sindh in 712 CE under the [[Umayyad Caliphate]], laying foundations for the Muslim period in South Asia. The [[Mughal Empire]] captured it in the late 16th century. The East India Company captured Sindh in 1846, and it became a separate province in 1936. It joined the Dominion of Pakistan in 1947. Its culture is strongly influenced by [[Sufism]].

=== KEY: Sindhi_language_fc294b
HEADWORD: Sindhi language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Indo-Aryan languages | Indus River | Sanskrit | Sindh
ENTRY:
[[Indo-Aryan languages|Indo-Aryan]] language and official language of Pakistan's [[Sindh]] province. It is written in a Perso-Arabic script in Pakistan and in Perso-Arabic and Devanagari scripts in India, where it is an official scheduled language. Sindhi is inflected, with five noun cases and two genders. Its name derives from *síndhu*, the Sanskrit name of the [[Indus River]], and it descends from [[Sanskrit]] through Middle Indo-Aryan. After the Umayyad conquest of 712 CE, Sindh was among the first Indian regions to encounter Persian and Arabic influence. Its literature includes the mystic poetry of Shah Abdul Latif Bhittai, from the 18th century. More than 34 million people speak it in Pakistan and 2.7 million in India.

=== KEY: Sine_and_cosine_2fce97
HEADWORD: Sine and cosine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angle | Light | Sound | Unit circle
ENTRY:
Fundamental trigonometric functions. In a right triangle, the sine of an acute [[Angle|angle]] is the ratio of the opposite side to the hypotenuse, and the cosine is the ratio of the adjacent side to the hypotenuse. Their definitions extend to any real value through the [[Unit circle|unit circle]], and modern definitions use infinite series or differential equations. They also extend to complex numbers. They are commonly used to model periodic phenomena such as [[Sound|sound]] and [[Light|light]] waves, harmonic oscillators and day length.

=== KEY: Singapore_458e4c
HEADWORD: Singapore

Island country and city-state in [[Southeast Asia]] (pop., 2025: 6.11 million), off the southern tip of the [[Malay Peninsula]]. Its territory comprises a main island, over 60 satellite islands and islets, and one outlying islet, about one degree of latitude (137 km) north of the equator.

In early history the island was a maritime emporium known as Temasek. Its contemporary era began in 1819, when Stamford Raffles established it as a trading post of the [[British Empire|British Empire]]; it came under direct British control in 1867 as part of the Straits Settlements. Japan occupied it in 1942, and it returned to Britain as a Crown colony in 1945. Singapore gained self-governance in 1959 and in 1963 joined the new federation of [[Malaysia]]. Ideological differences led to its expulsion two years later, and it became independent on August 9, 1965.

Singapore is a parliamentary republic with a legal system based on [[Common law|common law]]. The People's Action Party has held power continuously since 1959; supporters credit its policies of meritocracy and rule of law, including the leadership of [[Lee Kuan Yew]], while critics note limited political pluralism and laws restricting free speech and assembly. It has four official languages: English, Malay, Mandarin, and Tamil.

Despite lacking natural resources, the country became one of the Four Asian Tigers and a major financial, aviation, and shipping hub. It has the highest PPP-adjusted GDP per capita in the world and the second highest population density of any country. It is a founding member of [[ASEAN]].

Flag carrier of Singapore, with its hub at [[Changi Airport]]. It flies about 160 Airbus and Boeing passenger and freighter aircraft and was the launch customer for the [[Airbus A380]]. The airline has been a member of Star Alliance since April 2000. Its group includes Scoot, a low-cost carrier, and SIA Engineering Company, which handles maintenance across nine countries. === KEY: Singin_in_the_Rain_d46100
HEADWORD: Singin' in the Rain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Debbie Reynolds | Gene Kelly | Library of Congress | Silent film
ENTRY:
1952 American musical film by Gene Kelly and Stanley Donen. Starring [[Gene Kelly|Gene Kelly]], Donald O'Connor and [[Debbie Reynolds]], it depicts Hollywood's transition from [[Silent film|silent films]] to talkies in the late 1920s. Arthur Freed conceived it from songs written by himself and Nacio Herb Brown, and writers Betty Comden and Adolph Green set the story in the transition era. It was released nationwide on April 11, 1952, and was only a modest hit, but today is frequently cited as the greatest musical film and one of the greatest films ever made. It topped the AFI's Greatest Movie Musicals list, and Kelly's rendition of its title song ranked third among the greatest film songs. In 1989 it was among the first 25 films selected by the [[Library of Congress]] for the National Film Registry.
