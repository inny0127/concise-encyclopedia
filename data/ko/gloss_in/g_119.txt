ENTRY:
Indigenous [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherer]] cultures of southern Africa, also called Saan or Bushmen, and the oldest surviving cultures of the region. Genetic analysis suggests divergence from other humans as early as 100,000 to 200,000 years ago. Their ancestral territories span [[Botswana]], [[Namibia]], Angola, Zambia, [[Zimbabwe]], [[Lesotho]] and [[South Africa]]. Their languages belong to the Khoe, Tuu and Kxʼa families. In 2017 Botswana was home to approximately 63,500 San, the highest proportion of any country at 2.8%; Namibia enumerated 71,201 in 2023, or 2.4%. "San" is a Khoekhoe term adopted by Western anthropologists, and in 2003 representatives stated their preference for their individual nation names. "Bushmen" is now considered pejorative by many South Africans.

=== KEY: Sand_cec7ca
HEADWORD: Sand
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caribbean | Concrete | Quartz
ENTRY:
Granular material of finely divided rock and mineral particles, usually defined by grain size. Sand grains range from 0.0625 to 2 mm in diameter, smaller than gravel and coarser than silt. Sand can also refer to a soil texture class containing more than 85 percent sand-sized particles by mass. In inland and non-tropical coastal settings its most common constituent is silica (SiO₂), usually as [[Quartz]], which resists weathering. Calcium carbonate dominates in reef areas such as the [[Caribbean]], where it derives from coral and shellfish. Sand is a non-renewable resource over human timescales, and desert sand is unsuitable for [[Concrete|concrete]]; 50 billion tons of beach and fossil sand are used each year for construction.

=== KEY: Sandpiper_4a452a
HEADWORD: Sandpiper
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Invertebrate
ENTRY:
Shorebird of the family *Scolopacidae*, which includes about 98 extant or recently extinct species, among them sandpipers, woodcocks, curlews and snipes. Sandpipers have long bodies, legs and narrow wings, and most species have sensitive bills that probe mud and sand for small [[Invertebrate|invertebrates]]. They measure 12 to 66 cm (4.7–26.0 in) long and usually have dull brown, gray or streaked plumage, although some species display brighter colors during the breeding season. Most species nest in open areas, laying three or four eggs in a simple scrape on the ground. The family was introduced by Constantine Samuel Rafinesque in 1815, and its fossil record extends back into the Paleogene.

=== KEY: Sandstone_3f2554
HEADWORD: Sandstone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aquifer | Feldspar | Hematite | Quartz
ENTRY:
Clastic sedimentary rock composed mainly of sand-sized grains (0.0625 to 2 mm) cemented together by another mineral. Sandstones make up about 20–25% of all sedimentary rocks. Their grains are weathered from bedrock and carried by rivers or wind to basins where sediments accumulate. Most are composed of [[Quartz|quartz]] or [[Feldspar|feldspar]], the minerals most resistant to surface weathering. Impurities give sandstone its color, most commonly tan, brown, yellow, red, gray, pink, white or black, and red varieties owe their color to [[Hematite|hematite]]. Porous sandstone can store large quantities of water and petroleum, making it a valuable [[Aquifer|aquifer]] and reservoir. Quartz-bearing sandstone can be changed into quartzite through metamorphism, usually related to tectonic compression.

=== KEY: Sanitary_sewer_1e1f90
HEADWORD: Sanitary sewer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Groundwater | Sewage treatment
ENTRY:
Underground pipe or tunnel system that carries sewage from buildings, but not stormwater, to a [[Sewage treatment|treatment plant]]. Most sanitary sewers are gravity sewers, typically much smaller in diameter than combined sewers, which also carry urban runoff. Separating stormwater avoids the combined sewer overflows that occur when runoff from heavy rain exceeds the capacity of treatment plants. Manholes provide access for inspection and maintenance. Where land lies lower than the treatment plant, pumped force mains or lift stations raise sewage to a higher elevation. Backups of raw sewage can occur when stormwater or [[Groundwater|groundwater]] enters through leaking joints or defective pipes in aging infrastructure. London's sewer network, completed in the 1860s under engineer Joseph Bazalgette, is a pioneering model that separated sewage from stormwater.

=== KEY: Sanitation_7601b0
HEADWORD: Sanitation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cholera | Diarrhea | United Nations | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Public health conditions related to clean drinking water and the treatment and disposal of human excreta and sewage. Sanitation systems aim to protect health by keeping people from contact with feces and stopping disease transmission, especially by the fecal–oral route. Adequate sanitation reduces [[Diarrhea|diarrhea]], a main cause of malnutrition and stunted growth in children, as well as [[Cholera|cholera]], hepatitis, polio, and trachoma.

Definitions vary. The [[World Health Organization]] defines it as the provision of facilities and services for the safe disposal of human urine and feces, together with the maintenance of hygienic conditions through services such as garbage collection and wastewater disposal. Many in the water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) sector include only excreta management. A sanitation system covers the capture, storage, transportation, treatment, and disposal or reuse of excreta and wastewater; those who clean, maintain, or empty its technologies are called sanitation workers.

The purposes are a healthy living environment, protection of natural resources such as surface water, groundwater, and soil, and safety and dignity for people when they defecate or urinate. The human right to water and sanitation was recognized by the [[United Nations]] General Assembly in 2010, and sanitation is the subject of Sustainable Development Goal 6.

The Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) sanitation ladder, defined in 2016, begins at open defecation and rises through "unimproved," "limited," and "basic" to "safely managed." A 2017 JMP estimate found 4.5 billion people without safely managed sanitation. Later figures indicate that 1.2 billion people gained safely managed services between 2015 and 2024, raising coverage from 48% to 58%, while 3.4 billion still lack them and 432,000 deaths a year are attributed to inadequate sanitation.

=== KEY: Sanskrit_4037f7
HEADWORD: Sanskrit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brahmic scripts | Buddhism | Devanagari | Dravidian languages | Hinduism | Indo-European languages | Pāṇini | Southeast Asia
ENTRY:
Classical language of the Indo-Aryan branch of the [[Indo-European languages]], arising in South Asia in the late Bronze Age. It is the sacred language of [[Hinduism]], the language of classical Hindu philosophy, and of historical texts of [[Buddhism]] and Jainism. As Hindu and Buddhist culture spread, it became a language of religion, high culture, and political elites in [[Southeast Asia]], East Asia, and Central Asia, leaving a lasting mark on their learned vocabularies.

The most archaic form is Vedic Sanskrit, found in the Rigveda, a collection of 1,028 hymns composed between 1500 and 1200 BC by Indo-Aryan tribes migrating east from what is now northern Afghanistan. The [[Dravidian languages]] influenced its phonology and syntax. Classical Sanskrit, a standardized form that emerged in the mid-1st millennium BC, was codified in the *Aṣṭādhyāyī* ('Eight chapters') of [[Pāṇini]]. The foundations of modern arithmetic were first described in it. The epics *Mahābhārata* and *Rāmāyaṇa* were composed in Epic Sanskrit, used in northern India between 400 BC and AD 300. Afterward Sanskrit stopped being learned as a first language and ceased developing as a living language.

The Rigveda was transmitted orally with exceptional fidelity, so its preserved syntax and morphology are vital to reconstructing Proto-Indo-European. Sanskrit has no attested native script; from around the turn of the 1st millennium AD it has been written in various [[Brahmic scripts]], most commonly in [[Devanagari]] today.

The name comes from *sáṃskṛta-*, 'well prepared, pure and perfect, polished, sacred.' Although several thousand citizens have reported it as their mother tongue in recent censuses, there are no first-language speakers. The oldest Sanskrit college, the Benares Sanskrit College, was founded in 1791.

Myofibrils appear under the microscope as alternating dark and light bands. Sarcomeres are made of long, fibrous proteins that slide past each other as a [[Muscle contraction|muscle contracts]] or relaxes. Myosin forms the thick filament and actin the thin filament. The myosin head binds to actin and uses ATP as its energy source, but it can bind only when calcium ions expose the binding sites on actin. The giant protein titin, described as the biggest single highly elasticated protein in nature, extends from the Z-line to the M-band and acts as a sarcomeric ruler. Sarcomeres give skeletal and cardiac muscle its striated appearance, but the myofibrils of smooth muscle are not arranged into sarcomeres.

=== KEY: Satellite_c2b5e7
HEADWORD: Satellite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthur C. Clarke | Geostationary orbit | Isaac Newton | Low Earth orbit | Orbit | Spacecraft | Sputnik 1
ENTRY:
Object, typically a [[Spacecraft|spacecraft]], placed into [[Orbit|orbit]] around a celestial body. Satellites serve for communication relay, weather forecasting, navigation, broadcasting, scientific research, and Earth observation; military uses include reconnaissance, early warning, and signals intelligence.

Except for passive satellites, most generate electricity with solar panels or radioisotope thermoelectric generators, and most carry transponders for communication with ground stations. Many use a standardized bus, the most popular being small CubeSats, and similar satellites can work together as constellations. A spacecraft becomes a satellite by reaching orbital velocity in an orbit high enough to avoid decay from atmospheric drag; propulsion, usually chemical or ion thrusters, can then change or maintain the orbit. As of 2018, about 90% of satellites orbiting Earth were in [[Low Earth orbit]] or [[Geostationary orbit|geostationary orbit]], where a satellite stays fixed relative to a point on the ground.

The first published mathematical study of an artificial satellite was Newton's cannonball, a thought experiment by [[Isaac Newton]] in 1687. In 1903 Konstantin Tsiolkovsky published the first academic treatise on using rocketry to launch spacecraft. In 1945 [[Arthur C. Clarke]] described how three geostationary satellites could cover the entire planet with communications, and in 1946 Lyman Spitzer proposed an orbiting space telescope.

The first artificial satellite was the Soviet Union's [[Sputnik 1]], launched October 4, 1957, followed by the U.S. *Explorer 1* in 1958. As of June 28, 2025, 12,952 satellites orbited Earth: 8,530 belonging to the United States, 1,559 to Russia, and 908 to China. Growing numbers of satellites and debris raise the threat of collision, including a debris cascade known as Kessler syndrome.

=== KEY: Satellite_navigation_b141bf
HEADWORD: Satellite navigation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Union | Global Positioning System | Radio | Russia | Satellite
ENTRY:
Use of [[Satellite|satellites]] for navigation or geopositioning. A global navigation satellite system (GNSS) provides coverage for any user on Earth, including air, land, and sea. Four operational GNSS systems exist: the United States' [[Global Positioning System|Global Positioning System (GPS)]], [[Russia]]'s GLONASS, China's BeiDou, and the [[European Union]]'s Galileo. Japan's Quasi-Zenith Satellite System and India's NavIC are regional systems. Satellite-based augmentation systems, such as WAAS and EGNOS, are designed to enhance the accuracy of the global systems. Receivers determine their location to within a few centimeters to meters using time signals transmitted by [[Radio|radio]] from satellites. The same signals allow receivers to calculate local time to high precision. Global coverage is generally achieved by a constellation of 18–30 medium Earth orbit satellites, at an altitude of about 20,000 kilometers (12,000 miles). Satellite navigation operates independently of telephone or internet reception.

=== KEY: Satellite_television_2e956d
HEADWORD: Satellite television
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cable television | Communications satellite | Encryption | Equator | Geostationary orbit
ENTRY:
Service that delivers television programming by relay from an orbiting [[Communications satellite|communications satellite]]. Viewers receive the signal with an outdoor parabolic dish and a low-noise block downconverter, and a receiver decodes the program for the television set. It is often the only television service available in remote areas without terrestrial or [[Cable television|cable]] service. Some channels are free-to-air, others are [[Encryption|encrypted]], and pay television requires a monthly subscription. Modern systems use digital signals on the X band (8–12 GHz) or Ku band (12–18 GHz), requiring a dish less than a meter in diameter. The first systems, called television receive-only, used weaker analog C-band (4–8 GHz) signals and needed dishes 2–3 meters across, making them expensive and unpopular. Satellites carrying television are usually in [[Geostationary orbit|geostationary orbit]], about 36,000 kilometers (22,000 miles) above the [[Equator]], so dishes can stay aimed at one point. Digital broadcasting also made high-definition television practical.

=== KEY: Saturn_233ea1
HEADWORD: Saturn
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Enceladus | Gas giant | Metallic hydrogen | Ring system | Titan (moon) | Voyager 1
ENTRY:
Sixth planet from the Sun and second largest in the Solar System, after Jupiter. A [[Gas giant|gas giant]] with an average radius about 9 times that of Earth, it has an eighth of Earth's average density but is over 95 times more massive. It orbits the Sun at 9.59 AU (1,434 million km) in 29.45 years, with a day of about 10.7 hours.

Saturn is composed predominantly of hydrogen and helium and lacks a definite surface. Its interior is thought to include a diffuse rocky and icy core, a deep layer of [[Metallic hydrogen|metallic hydrogen]], a layer of liquid hydrogen and helium, and an outer gas layer. Its average density, 0.69 g/cm³, makes it the only planet less dense than water. It radiates 2.5 times more energy than it receives from the Sun. Ammonia crystals in the upper atmosphere give it a pale yellow hue, and winds reach 1,800 km/h. A persistent hexagonal pattern surrounds the north pole.

The [[Ring system|rings]] are bright and extensive, composed mainly of water ice with a smaller amount of rocky debris and dust. The main system spans about 282,000 km, with divisions such as the 4,700-km-wide Cassini Division. At least 293 moons orbit Saturn, 63 of them officially named. [[Titan (moon)|Titan]], the largest, is the second largest moon in the Solar System, larger than Mercury, and the only moon with a substantial atmosphere. [[Enceladus]] ejects water plumes that indicate a subsurface ocean.

NASA's Pioneer 11 flew past in 1979, followed by [[Voyager 1]] and Voyager 2 in the early 1980s and the Cassini–Huygens mission from 2004 to 2017, which landed the Huygens probe on Titan.

Medium-grained [[Metamorphic rock|metamorphic rock]] with pronounced schistosity, meaning it splits easily into thin flakes or plates. Schist is generally derived from fine-grained sedimentary rock such as [[Shale|shale]], and it can also form from igneous [[Tuff|tuffs]]. Its texture reflects a high content of platy minerals such as [[Mica|mica]], talc, chlorite or graphite, often interleaved with granular minerals such as [[Feldspar|feldspar]] or [[Quartz|quartz]]. It typically forms during regional metamorphism accompanying mountain building. Schist bedrock can pose a challenge for [[Civil engineering|civil engineering]] because of its pronounced planes of weakness.

=== KEY: Schizoid_personality_disorder_cf02a8
HEADWORD: Schizoid personality disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Autism | Schizophrenia
ENTRY:
Condition marked by extreme asociality, emotional coldness and detachment, alongside a rich internal fantasy world. Symptoms typically begin in late childhood or adolescence, and the cause is uncertain, though a neglectful childhood, genetics and neurological factors are implicated. Shared genetic risks link it to [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]]. It is diagnosed by clinical observation and can be hard to distinguish from [[Autism|autism]]. Because people rarely seek treatment, the effectiveness of therapy and medication has not been well studied. Studies generally report a prevalence of less than 1%.

=== KEY: Schizophrenia_79ce9b
HEADWORD: Schizophrenia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cannabis (drug) | Delusion | Hallucination
ENTRY:
Mental disorder characterized by [[Hallucination|hallucinations]] (typically hearing voices), [[Delusion|delusions]], disorganized thinking or behavior, and flat or inappropriate affect. Symptoms usually begin in young adulthood, and there is no objective diagnostic test. Males are more often affected and have an earlier average onset. Causes include genetic and environmental factors, such as [[Cannabis (drug)|cannabis use]] during adolescence. About half of those diagnosed improve over the long term without further relapses, while the rest have lifelong impairment. Average life expectancy is reduced by 20 to 28 years, and the suicide rate is about 5% overall. Treatment centers on antipsychotic medication, with counseling, job training and social rehabilitation. Clozapine is offered when initial antipsychotics fail.

=== KEY: Schizotypal_personality_disorder_d6eb93
HEADWORD: Schizotypal personality disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anxiety | Personality disorder | Psychosis
ENTRY:
Mental disorder characterized by thought disorder, paranoia, [[Anxiety|social anxiety]], derealization, transient [[Psychosis|psychosis]] and unconventional beliefs. Affected people often feel strong discomfort in forming social connections, believing that others hold negative views of them, and paranormal or superstitious beliefs are common. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) classifies it as a [[Personality disorder|personality disorder]] in cluster A, while the ICD-11 places it among psychotic disorders. Those affected usually disagree that their thoughts are a disorder. It occurs in about 3% of the general population and is more often diagnosed in males.
