=== KEY: Inductive_reasoning_59b655
HEADWORD: Inductive reasoning
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Bayesian inference | David Hume | Deductive reasoning | Francis Bacon | John Stuart Mill
ENTRY:
Method of logical reasoning in which the conclusion of an argument is supported with some degree of probability but not deductive certainty. Unlike [[Deductive reasoning|deductive reasoning]], where the conclusion is certain if the premises are correct, inductive conclusions are at best probable. Types include generalization, prediction, statistical syllogism, argument from analogy and causal inference. A statistical generalization infers a conclusion about a population from a random sample, and is reliable within a margin of error when the sample is genuinely random and large. Hasty generalization and the biased sample are fallacies of generalization. Probabilities can be estimated with techniques such as [[Bayesian inference]]. [[Aristotle]] distinguished induction as a move from particulars to universals, [[Francis Bacon]] advocated empirical induction for science, and [[David Hume]] questioned why past patterns justify future expectations. [[John Stuart Mill]] later refined inductive methods such as agreement and difference.

=== KEY: Inductor_38cfc0
HEADWORD: Inductor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Capacitor | Inductance | Radio | Resistor | Wire
ENTRY:
Passive two-terminal electrical component that stores energy in a magnetic field, also called a coil, choke or reactor. It typically consists of an insulated [[Wire|wire]] wound into a coil. When the current through it changes, the changing magnetic field induces a voltage that opposes the change, so inductors resist changes in current. Its [[Inductance|inductance]] is measured in henries (H), named after American scientist Joseph Henry. Many inductors have a core of iron or ferrite, which increases the magnetic field and inductance. Along with [[Capacitor|capacitors]] and [[Resistor|resistors]], inductors are one of the three passive linear circuit elements. They are widely used in [[Alternating current|alternating-current]] equipment, particularly [[Radio|radio]] equipment, where chokes block alternating current while allowing direct current to pass. Values typically range from 1 μH (10⁻⁶ H) to 20 H.

=== KEY: Indus_Valley_Civilisation_4e9d33
HEADWORD: Indus Valley Civilisation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Indus River | Metallurgy | Mohenjo-daro | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Bronze Age urban civilization of northwestern South Asia, c. 3300–1300 BC.

The civilization, also called the Indus Civilisation or Harappan civilization, lasted from 3300 BC to 1300 BC, and in its mature form from 2600 BC to 1900 BC. With ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and China, it was one of the four centers of the ancient world, and it was geographically larger than ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia combined. It spanned much of Pakistan, northwestern India, and northeast Afghanistan on the alluvial plain of the [[Indus River]] and along monsoon-fed rivers. The name Harappan comes from Harappa, the type site, first excavated early in the 20th century.

Its cities were noted for urban planning, baked brick houses, elaborate drainage and water supply systems, clusters of large non-residential buildings, and techniques of handicraft and [[Metallurgy|metallurgy]]. [[Mohenjo-daro]] and Harappa very likely held between 30,000 and 60,000 people, and the civilization may have had between one and five million. Over a thousand Mature Harappan sites have been reported, but only five are major urban centers: Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, Ganeriwala, Dholavira, and Rakhigarhi. Trade in goods such as carnelian beads and lapis lazuli reached Mesopotamia,. The Indus script remains undeciphered, and the language's affiliations are uncertain; some scholars favor a relationship with the Dravidian or Elamo-Dravidian family.

A gradual drying of the region during the 3rd millennium BC may have stimulated urbanization, and further drying reduced the water supply enough to cause the civilization's decline and disperse its population to the east. Mohenjo-daro was declared a [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Site in 1980 and Dholavira in 2021.

=== KEY: Industrial_Revolution_d5a252
HEADWORD: Industrial Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: James Watt | Second Industrial Revolution | Steam engine
ENTRY:
Transition of the global economy, beginning in Great Britain around 1760, from hand production to machine manufacturing and the factory system.

Sometimes called the First Industrial Revolution to distinguish it from the later [[Second Industrial Revolution]], it had spread to continental Europe and the United States by about 1840. It involved the move from hand methods to machines, new chemical and iron production processes, the increasing use of water and steam power, the development of machine tools, and the rise of the mechanised factory system. Textiles were the first industry to use modern production methods; by the 1830s mechanised cotton spinning had increased output per worker by a factor of around 500.

Several factors enabled industrialization: high agricultural productivity, managerial and entrepreneurial skills, ports, rivers, canals, and roads, natural resources such as coal and iron, political stability, and available financial capital. Britain was the leading commercial nation by the mid-18th century. The improved [[Steam engine|steam engine]] of [[James Watt]] was patented in 1769.

Historians debate the precise start and end of the revolution and the pace of its changes; some, including John Clapham and Nicholas Crafts, argue that change was gradual and that "revolution" is a misnomer. Average income and population showed unprecedented sustained growth, and the standard of living improved for most people in the Western world, although others say it did not improve meaningfully until the 20th century. Before the revolution most manufacturing occurred in China and India; afterward it took place mainly in North America and Western Europe.

=== KEY: Industrialisation_bceae5
HEADWORD: Industrialization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Child labour | East Asia | Fossil fuel | Industrial Revolution | Second Industrial Revolution | Steam engine | Talcott Parsons
ENTRY:
Period of social and economic change that transforms an agrarian and feudal society into an industrial one. It involves an extensive reorganization of an economy for manufacturing, and is associated with polluting industries dependent on [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]]. With the focus on sustainable development, it increasingly includes technological leapfrogging. The first phase, the [[Industrial Revolution]], began in Great Britain in the mid-18th century and spread to Belgium, Switzerland, Germany and France. The [[Second Industrial Revolution]] followed the refinement of the [[Steam engine|steam engine]], the internal combustion engine and electricity, and the assembly line gave it a boost. Industrialization changed family structures as extended families stopped living together, though historians widely contest the theory of [[Talcott Parsons]] that it created the nuclear family. Workers moved to cities, increasing urbanization and the spread of disease, and industrialization contributed to [[Child labour|child labor]]. By the end of the 20th century, [[East Asia]] had become one of the most recently industrialized regions.

Mathematical relation making a non-equal comparison between two numbers or other expressions. Inequalities most often compare two numbers by their size on the number line. === KEY: Inertia_b19a24
HEADWORD: Inertia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Friction | Galileo Galilei | Gravity | Isaac Newton | Lucretius | Mass
ENTRY:
Fundamental principle of classical physics, the tendency of an object to stay at rest or in uniform motion unless a force changes its velocity. Inertia is one of the primary manifestations of [[Mass|mass]], and [[Isaac Newton]] described it as his first law of motion. In his 1687 work *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, Newton defined inertia as the innate power of matter to resist changes in its state. On Earth, [[Friction|friction]] and [[Gravity|gravity]] often mask inertia, which led [[Aristotle]] to conclude that moving objects come to rest unless a force keeps them moving. [[Lucretius]] argued that motion, not stasis, was the default state of matter. The principle was first articulated by [[Galileo Galilei]] in the early 17th century through thought experiments on objects that continue indefinitely without friction.

=== KEY: Infant_50d7fd
HEADWORD: Infant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fetus
ENTRY:
Very young human being, typically a child under one year of age.

The word comes from Latin *īnfāns*, "unspeaking." Baby is an alternative term, and definitions of infant vary, sometimes extending to children up to two years of age. A child who has learned to walk is called a toddler. A newborn is, in mainstream use, an infant only hours, days, or weeks old; in medical contexts a newborn or neonate is an infant in the first 28 days after birth. Infants born before 37 weeks of gestation are called premature, those born between 39 and 40 weeks full term, those born through 41 weeks late term, and those beyond 42 weeks post term. Before birth the offspring is a [[Fetus|fetus]].

A newborn's head is large in proportion to the body; the skull is about one quarter of total body length, against about one seventh in adults. Soft spots called fontanels remain where the skull bones have not yet fused. In developed countries the average length of a newborn is 35.6–50.8 cm and the average birth weight of a full-term newborn is about 3.4 kg, typically within 2.7–4.6 kg. Body weight falls by 3–7% over the first 5–7 days. Some newborns have fine body hair called lanugo, which disappears within a few weeks.

Infants are altricial and fully dependent on their mothers or other caretakers. They cry as basic communication, expressing hunger, discomfort, overstimulation, boredom, or loneliness. Breastfeeding is the method recommended by all major infant health organizations.

=== KEY: Infection_f0ddc0
HEADWORD: Infection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bacteria | Malaria
ENTRY:
Invasion of an organism's tissues by pathogens, their multiplication, and the host's reaction to them and the toxins they produce.

An infectious disease is an illness resulting from an infection, and some are also communicable. Not every infection causes illness: many are asymptomatic, and an infection is not synonymous with an infectious disease. Pathogens include [[Bacteria|bacteria]], viruses and subviral agents such as viroids and prions, fungi, and parasites. The yeast *Candida* causes the most common fungal infection. Parasites include unicellular organisms such as the cause of [[Malaria|malaria]], and macroparasites such as nematodes, tapeworms, and flukes.

Infection begins when an organism enters the body, grows, and multiplies. Entry generally occurs through the mucosa of orifices such as the mouth, nose, eyes, and genitalia, or through open wounds. Many pathogens migrate to cause systemic infection in different organs. People with weakened immune systems are more susceptible to chronic or persistent infections and to opportunistic infections. Colonization by microorganisms is distinct from infection, and the difference is often a matter of circumstance, since non-pathogenic organisms can become pathogenic under specific conditions.

Hosts fight infection with the immune system. Mammals respond with an innate response, often involving inflammation, followed by an adaptive response. Bacterial and viral infections can cause the same symptoms, which makes the cause hard to distinguish; antibiotics cure bacterial but not viral infections. Treatment depends on the pathogen: antibiotics, antivirals, antifungals, antiprotozoals, or antihelminthics. Communicable, maternal, neonatal, and nutritional diseases together accounted for an estimated 18.6% of global deaths in 2017.

=== KEY: Infinity_eb2ac5
HEADWORD: Infinity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Augustin-Louis Cauchy | Bertrand Russell | Georg Cantor | Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory
ENTRY:
Boundless, limitless, or endless quantity, denoted by the symbol ∞.

Infinity has been debated philosophically since the ancient Greeks. The earliest recorded Greek idea may be that of Anaximander, who used the word *apeiron*, "unbounded." [[Aristotle]] distinguished potential infinity from actual infinity, which he regarded as impossible because of the paradoxes it seemed to produce. The paradoxes of Zeno of Elea, especially Achilles and the Tortoise, exposed the inadequacy of popular conceptions; [[Bertrand Russell]] described them as "immeasurably subtle and profound." In 1821 [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] gave a satisfactory definition of a limit and a proof for the sum of the geometric series, which showed that Achilles does overtake the tortoise.

In the 17th century the infinity symbol and infinitesimal calculus were introduced, and mathematicians began to work with infinite series, though infinity remained associated with endless processes and its status as a number stayed unclear. At the end of the 19th century [[Georg Cantor]] studied infinite sets and infinite numbers, showing that they can be of various sizes. For example, the infinite number of points on a line, its cardinality, is larger than the number of integers.

The axiom of infinity, among the axioms of [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory]], on which most of modern mathematics can be developed, guarantees the existence of infinite sets. In physics and cosmology, whether the universe is spatially infinite is an open question.

=== KEY: Inflammation_fd4d5d
HEADWORD: Inflammation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atherosclerosis | Immune system | Osteoarthritis | Pathogen
ENTRY:
Biological defense response of body tissues to harmful stimuli. Triggers include physical trauma, [[Pathogen|pathogens]], irritants, toxins, overuse, autoimmunity, allergens and foreign bodies. The five cardinal signs of acute inflammation are heat, pain, redness, swelling and loss of function (Latin *calor*, *dolor*, *rubor*, *tumor* and *functio laesa*). It is a generic response and is considered a mechanism of [[Immune system|innate immunity]]. Its function is to eliminate the initial cause of cell injury, clear damaged cells and tissues, and initiate repair. Acute inflammation is a short-term process that usually appears within minutes or hours and ceases when the injurious stimulus is removed. Chronic inflammation is prolonged and involves simultaneous destruction and healing of tissue. Too much chronic inflammation is associated with diseases such as hay fever, periodontal disease, [[Atherosclerosis]] and [[Osteoarthritis]].

=== KEY: Inflation_d68d75
HEADWORD: Inflation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Civil War | Deflation | Unemployment
ENTRY:
General increase in the average price of goods and services in terms of money. Inflation reduces the purchasing power of money and is usually measured as the annualized percentage change in a price index, typically the consumer price index (CPI). Changes are attributed to increases in the money supply, demand shocks, supply shocks such as energy crises, and falls in interest rates set by the central bank. === KEY: Influenza_5dc103
HEADWORD: Influenza
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Pneumonia | Spanish flu
ENTRY:
Infectious disease of the respiratory tract caused by influenza viruses, commonly called the flu.

Symptoms range from mild to severe and often include fever, runny nose, sore throat, muscle pain, headache, coughing, and fatigue. They begin one to four days after exposure and last about two to eight days. The disease may progress to [[Pneumonia|pneumonia]] from the virus or a later bacterial infection, and other complications include meningitis and encephalitis.

There are four types of influenza virus: A, B, C, and D. Aquatic birds are the primary source of influenza A, which also infects mammals including humans and pigs. Types A and B circulate in humans and cause seasonal epidemics; type C causes mild infection, mainly in children. Transmission is primarily through respiratory droplets from coughing and sneezing, and also through aerosols and contaminated surfaces. Influenza viruses, particularly type A, evolve quickly, so vaccines are updated regularly; they protect against influenza A subtypes H1N1 and H3N2 and one or two influenza B subtypes. Treatment is supportive and, in severe cases, includes antivirals such as oseltamivir.

In a typical year five to 15 percent of the population contracts influenza, with 3 to 5 million severe cases and up to 650,000 respiratory-related deaths globally. Cases peak in winter in temperate regions and occur year-round in the tropics. Since the late 1800s, pandemics of novel strains have occurred every 10 to 50 years. Five have occurred since 1900: the [[Spanish flu]] of 1918–20, the most severe, and those of 1957, 1968, 1977, and 2009.

=== KEY: Information_Age_bcb1cf
HEADWORD: Information Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bell Labs | Claude Shannon | Konrad Zuse | Transistor
ENTRY:
Historical period, beginning in the mid-20th century, marked by a shift from traditional industry to an economy centered on information technology.

The onset of the Information Age has been linked to the development of the [[Transistor|transistor]] in 1947. According to the United Nations Public Administration Network, it was formed by advances in computer miniaturization that led to modernized information systems and internet communications. It is also called the Digital Age or Third Industrial Revolution. The digital revolution converted technology from analog to digital format, which made identical copies possible and let information move easily between media.

Charles Babbage proposed a mechanical general-purpose computer, the Analytical Engine, which was never successfully built. In 1941 [[Konrad Zuse]] completed the Z3, the world's first working programmable, fully automatic digital computer.

In 1947 John Bardeen and Walter Houser Brattain, working under William Shockley at [[Bell Labs]], invented the first working transistor, a germanium-based point-contact device. [[Claude Shannon]], a Bell Labs mathematician, is generally credited with laying the foundations of digitalization in his 1948 article *A Mathematical Theory of Communication*. Jack Kilby achieved the first integrated circuit milestone in 1958. The personal computer arrived in the 1970s and made digital communication economical for widespread adoption.

Historians and commentators debate whether the Third Industrial Revolution has ended and whether breakthroughs in artificial intelligence and biotechnology have begun a Fourth Industrial Revolution, theorized to bring the Imagination Age, the Internet of things, and rapid advances in machine learning.

=== KEY: Information_a82be0
HEADWORD: Information
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bit | Claude Shannon
ENTRY:
Abstract concept referring to something that has the power to inform.

At the most fundamental level, information pertains to the interpretation of that which may be sensed, or its abstractions. Any natural process that is not completely random, and any observable pattern in any medium, can be said to convey some information. Information is not knowledge itself but the meaning that may be derived from a representation through interpretation. It can be transmitted in time through data storage and in space through communication, and it can be encoded or encrypted.

The English word comes from Middle French *informacion*, "a criminal investigation," and Latin *informatiō*, "conception, teaching, creation." References to the formation of the mind or character, training, and instruction date from the 14th century.

In information theory, founded by [[Claude Shannon]] in 1948, the uncertainty of an event is measured by its probability of occurrence and is proportional to the negative logarithm of that probability. More uncertain events require more information to resolve. The [[Bit|bit]] is the standard unit of information, described as that which reduces uncertainty by half; one fair coin flip encodes 1 bit and two encode 2 bits. In physics, erasing a bit requires a minimum energy, according to Landauer's principle, and DNA sequences store biological information. Whether information is fundamental, like mass and energy, or emergent remains debated.

=== KEY: Information_technology_3dd7c4

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Study or use of computers, telecommunication systems and other devices to create, process, store, retrieve and transmit information. The term is commonly used for computers and [[Computer network|computer networks]], but it also covers technologies such as [[Television]] and [[Telephone|telephones]]. An IT system includes all hardware, software and peripheral equipment operated by a limited group of users. === KEY: Information_theory_32eb51

Mathematical study of the quantification, storage and communication of information. The field was established and formalized by [[Claude Shannon]] in the 1940s, although Harry Nyquist and Ralph Hartley made early contributions in the 1920s. Physiological damage to an organism caused by mechanical trauma, toxins, interactions with other organisms, or abiotic environmental factors.

In animals, injury is sometimes defined as mechanical damage to an anatomical structure, but the wider sense covers damage from any cause, including drowning, [[Burn|burns]], and poisoning. === KEY: Inorganic_chemistry_22110c
HEADWORD: Inorganic chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fritz Haber | Haber process | Hemoglobin | Organic chemistry | Organometallic chemistry | Pyrite | Sodium chloride | Sulfuric acid
ENTRY:
Branch of chemistry that studies the preparation, properties, and behavior of inorganic and organometallic compounds, mainly those not based on carbon.

Unlike [[Organic chemistry]], it deals chiefly with non-carbon compounds, though the fields overlap, especially in [[Organometallic chemistry|organometallic chemistry]]. Its products are used in catalysts, materials, paints and pigments, coatings, medicines, fuels, and agriculture. Many inorganic compounds occur as minerals, such as iron sulfide as [[Pyrite|pyrite]]; others serve as biomolecules, such as the electrolyte [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]], ATP, and the polyphosphate backbone of DNA.

Compounds may be ionic, as in magnesium chloride (MgCl₂), which contains Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions; covalent; or polar covalent, common in oxides, carbonates, and halides. Many have high melting points, and some ionic compounds, such as NaCl, dissolve easily in water. Subdivisions include organometallic, cluster, bioinorganic, materials, and solid-state chemistry.

Classical coordination compounds feature metals bound to lone pairs on ligands such as H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻, and CN⁻. Structures range from tetrahedral for titanium to square planar for some nickel complexes and octahedral for cobalt complexes. Iron in [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]] is one biologically important example. Alfred Werner's coordination theory earned him the 1913 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The field is highly practical. Traditionally, a nation's economic scale could be gauged by its output of [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]]. Ammonium nitrate fertilizer depends on ammonia from the [[Haber process]], whose practical synthesis with iron catalysts was achieved by Carl Bosch and [[Fritz Haber]] in the early 1900s. Portland cement is another large-scale inorganic material, and vanadium(V) oxide and titanium(III) chloride serve as industrial catalysts.

=== KEY: Insect_960c08

Invertebrate class (Insecta) of the phylum Arthropoda, the most diverse group of animals.

Insects have an exoskeleton, a body of head, thorax, and abdomen, three pairs of jointed legs, compound eyes, and a pair of antennae. More than a million species have been described, over half of all animal species; about 5.5 million may exist. The orders Hemiptera, Lepidoptera, Diptera, Hymenoptera, and Coleoptera each have more than 100,000 described species. The name comes from Latin *insectum*, "cut up", introduced by [[Pliny the Elder]] as a translation of Aristotle's Greek term.

Many insects are crop pests, parasites, or disease vectors, but pollinators are essential to many plants' reproduction, and many insects prey on pests. Silkworms and the [[Honey bee|honey bee]] were domesticated for silk and honey. Insects are eaten in 80% of the world's nations, by roughly 3,000 ethnic groups. Human activities are seriously affecting insect biodiversity.

=== KEY: Insurance_eaff1b
HEADWORD: Insurance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belgium | Genoa | Great Fire of London | Justinian I | Law of large numbers | London | Rhodes
ENTRY:
Means of protection from financial loss in which, for a fee, one party agrees to compensate another for a certain loss, damage, or injury.

A form of risk management, it protects against contingent or uncertain loss. The insurer issues a policy detailing when it will compensate the insured or a designated beneficiary. The policyholder pays a premium, accepting a small known loss for the promise of compensation, usually for something in which the insured has an insurable interest. After a loss, the insured submits a claim, processed by a claims adjuster. Insurers rely on the [[Law of large numbers|law of large numbers]] to forecast aggregate losses.

Property insurance can be traced to the [[Great Fire of London]] in 1666, which destroyed more than 13,000 houses. In 1681 Nicholas Barbon and eleven associates established the first fire insurance company, the "Insurance Office for Houses". Insurance faces moral hazard, in which insured parties may take more risks, and insurer insolvency during correlated losses; regulation enforces solvency, transparency, and fair practices.

=== KEY: Integer_a0faef
HEADWORD: Integer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abelian group | David Hilbert | Field (mathematics) | Georg Cantor | Leonhard Euler | Natural number | Rational number | Ring (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Number that is zero, a positive natural number, or the negation of a positive natural number.

The negations of the positive natural numbers (−1, −2, −3, ...) are the negative integers. The set of integers is denoted Z or ℤ, from the German *Zahlen*, "numbers", a usage attributed to [[David Hilbert]]. It contains the [[Natural number|natural numbers]] and lies within the [[Rational number|rational numbers]]. Like the natural numbers, it is countably infinite. The numbers 21, 4, 0, and −2048 are integers; 9.75, 5/4, and the square root of 2 are not.

The word is Latin for "whole" or "untouched". Historically only positive integers were considered, and the definition widened as negative numbers proved useful. Negative numbers first appear in Chinese mathematics between 200 BC and 200 AD, and Indian texts of about the 7th century AD set rules for operating with them. [[Leonhard Euler]] defined integers to include negatives in his 1765 *Elements of Algebra*. The phrase "the set of the integers" was not used before the end of the 19th century, when [[Georg Cantor]] introduced set theory. The earliest known textbook use of Z is in Nicolas Bourbaki's *Algèbre* (1947). Whether "whole numbers" includes negatives remains ambiguous.

The integers are closed under addition, multiplication, and subtraction, but not under division or exponentiation. Under addition they form an [[Abelian group]], the only infinite cyclic group up to isomorphism. With multiplication they form a commutative [[Ring (mathematics)|ring]] with unity, the smallest ring containing the natural numbers, and an integral domain because they lack zero divisors. They are not a [[Field (mathematics)|field]], since not every integer has a multiplicative inverse; the smallest field containing them is the rational numbers.

=== KEY: Integrated_circuit_305ad8

Compact assembly of electronic circuits, also called a microchip or chip, fabricated on a thin, flat piece of [[Semiconductor|semiconductor]] material, usually [[Silicon|silicon]]. Integrated circuits perform functions such as data processing, control, memory and power management in [[Computer|computers]], [[Smartphone|smartphones]] and televisions. === KEY: Intellectual_disability_c8e450
HEADWORD: Intellectual disability
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alzheimer's disease | Autism | Down syndrome | Intelligence quotient
ENTRY:
Neurodevelopmental disorder marked by significant impairment in intellectual and adaptive functioning.

Also called disorder of intellectual development, and known as learning difficulty in the United Kingdom, it usually becomes apparent in infancy, early childhood, or school age. Affected children typically have an [[Intelligence quotient|intelligence quotient]] (IQ) below 70 and deficits in at least two adaptive behaviors affecting everyday living. === KEY: Intelligence_8f7ba5
HEADWORD: Intelligence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hume | Francis Bacon | Intellectual disability | Intelligence quotient | John Locke | Middle Ages | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Capacity to perceive or infer information and retain it as knowledge to be applied to adaptive behavior within an environment or context.

It has been defined in many ways, including capacity for abstraction, logic, learning, reasoning, planning, creativity, and problem-solving. A 1995 American Psychological Association report noted that two dozen prominent theorists asked to define it gave two dozen somewhat different definitions. The term rose to prominence in the early 1900s, and most psychologists believe intelligence divides into various domains. It has also been observed in non-human animals.

The word derives from Latin *intelligentia* or *intellectus*. In the [[Middle Ages]], *intellectus* became the scholarly term for understanding and a translation of the Greek *nous*. [[Francis Bacon]], [[Thomas Hobbes]], [[John Locke]], and [[David Hume]] rejected the scholastic theories attached to it and preferred "understanding".

Human intelligence includes abilities to learn, form concepts, reason, plan, solve problems, and use language, organized in frameworks such as fluid versus crystallized intelligence. It is distinct from learning, the retention of facts and abilities.

[[Intelligence quotient|IQ]] tests were first developed in the early 20th century to screen children for [[Intellectual disability|intellectual disability]] and later used to screen immigrants, military recruits, and applicants. The *g* factor summarizes correlations among scores on cognitive tests; Charles Spearman identified it in 1904. Most psychologists agree IQ measures some aspects of intelligence, particularly academic performance, while many question its validity as a whole measure. Twin and adoption studies estimate heritability at about 20–40% in childhood and 50–80% in adulthood, though the balance of genetic and environmental influences is debated. Researchers also disagree about average group differences in scores: one view holds that genetics does not explain average differences between racial groups, while others argue they are partly heritable.

HEADWORD: Intelligence quotient
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Total score from standardized tests designed to assess human intelligence. Modern IQ tests transform raw scores to a [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]] with a mean of 100 and a [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] of 15, so about two-thirds of people score between 85 and 115. They have been associated with factors such as nutrition and parental socioeconomic status, and they are used for educational placement and for evaluating job applicants. Raw scores have risen by an average of three points per decade since the early 20th century, a phenomenon called the Flynn effect.

=== KEY: Internal_combustion_engine_93bfe9
HEADWORD: Internal combustion engine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Combustion | Napoleon | Nicolaus Otto | Rudolf Diesel | Steam engine | Stirling engine | Wankel engine
ENTRY:
Heat engine in which fuel burns with an oxidizer, usually air, in a combustion chamber that is part of the working fluid flow circuit.

The expansion of high-temperature, high-pressure gases from [[Combustion|combustion]] applies force to pistons, turbine blades, a rotor, or a nozzle, converting chemical energy into kinetic energy. The term usually means engines with intermittent combustion, such as two-stroke and four-stroke piston engines and the [[Wankel engine]]. A second class, with continuous combustion, includes gas turbines, jet engines, and most rocket engines. In external combustion engines, such as the [[Steam engine|steam]] and [[Stirling engine|Stirling]] engines, energy goes to a working fluid not mixed with combustion products.

Most are used in mobile applications, as the primary power for cars, aircraft, and boats. They run on hydrocarbon fuels such as natural gas, gasoline, diesel fuel, or ethanol; biodiesel and bioethanol are renewable alternatives.

In 1807 French engineers Nicéphore Niépce and Claude Niépce ran the Pyréolophore, patented by [[Napoleon]], on a boat on the Saône, and François Isaac de Rivaz built a hydrogen-fueled engine, fitted to a primitive vehicle in 1808. In 1876 [[Nicolaus Otto]] patented the compressed-charge, four-cycle engine, the first modern internal combustion engine. Karl Benz patented a two-stroke gasoline engine in 1879 and began commercial production of motor vehicles in 1886. In 1892 [[Rudolf Diesel]] developed the first compression-ignition engine. Robert Goddard launched the first liquid-fueled rocket in 1926, and the Heinkel He 178 became the first jet aircraft in 1939.

Exhaust includes carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, and unburnt hydrocarbons, contributing to air pollution. Catalytic converters, fuel standards, and engine controls have sharply reduced some pollutants, while carbon dioxide emissions have prompted a shift toward electrification.

=== KEY: Internal_energy_a79947
HEADWORD: Internal energy
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Energy that defines the state of a thermodynamic system, excluding the kinetic energy of its overall motion and the effects of external fields. It is a state variable: it depends only on the internal state of the system, not on the process by which energy enters or leaves. The total internal energy cannot practically be measured, so thermodynamics concerns changes in it. These changes arise from energy transferred as [[Heat|heat]] or thermodynamic work, or from transfers of matter. === KEY: Internal_structure_of_Earth_8434ca
HEADWORD: Internal structure of Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basalt | Convection | Crust (geology) | Earth's magnetic field | Earth's mantle | Lithosphere
ENTRY:
Layered variation of chemical and physical properties within the solid Earth.

Mechanically, it consists of a rigid lithosphere, a semi-fluid asthenosphere, a semi-fluid mesosphere, a liquid outer core, and a solid inner core. Chemically, it consists of a silicate crust, a ferromagnesian mantle, and an iron-nickel core whose flowing upper portion generates the [[Earth's magnetic field]]. Knowledge rests on topography, rock outcrops, samples brought up by volcanoes, seismic waves, gravitational and magnetic measurements, and high-pressure experiments; the layered model was deduced in the early 20th century chiefly from seismic waves. Earth's mass is about 6×10²⁴ kg and its average density 5.515 g/cm³.

The [[Crust (geology)|crust]] ranges from 5 to 70 km thick. Oceanic crust, 5–10 km thick, is dense and mafic; continental crust is thicker, less dense, and felsic. The [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]] comprises the crust and uppermost mantle. At the Mohorovičić discontinuity, primary seismic waves speed up from 6.7–7.2 km/s, consistent with [[Basalt|basalt]], to 7.6–8.6 km/s. The oldest known mineral grains are about 4.4 billion years old.

The [[Earth's mantle|mantle]] extends to a depth of 2,890 km, the thickest layer, at 83.7% of Earth's volume. A transition zone separates upper and lower mantle, and pressure at the base is about 140 GPa. Though solid, its hot silicate rock flows over long timescales, and [[Convection|convection]] drives the motion of tectonic plates. Viscosity rises with depth from 10²¹ to 10²⁴ pascal-second.

The outer core, liquid iron and nickel alloy roughly 2,200–2,300 km thick, generates the magnetic field through convective motion. The solid inner core has a radius of about 1,220–1,250 km and temperatures up to 5,400°C.

Continuous human occupancy began on November 2, 2000, the longest continuous human presence in space. As of August 2025, 290 individuals from 26 countries had visited it. Alongside China's Tiangong, it is one of two operational space stations.

=== KEY: International_System_of_Units_78d662
HEADWORD: International System of Units
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ampere | Candela | Kelvin | Kilogram | Metre | Mole (unit) | Second
ENTRY:
Modern form of the metric system and the world's most widely used system of measurement, abbreviated SI (from French *Système international d'unités*). It is the only system with official status in nearly every country and is used in science, technology, industry, and commerce. The International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) coordinates it.

The SI comprises seven base units: the [[Second]] (s, time), [[Metre]] (m, length), [[Kilogram]] (kg, mass), [[Ampere]] (A, electric current), [[Kelvin]] (K, thermodynamic temperature), [[Mole (unit)|mole]] (mol, amount of substance), and [[Candela]] (cd, luminous intensity). Twenty-two coherent derived units, always expressible as products of powers of the base units, have special names and symbols, and 24 prefixes, such as kilo- for 10³ and nano- for 10⁻⁹, produce decimal multiples and sub-multiples.

The base units are defined through seven defining constants with exact values: the speed of light, the hyperfine transition frequency of caesium, the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the Boltzmann constant, the Avogadro constant, and the luminous efficacy Kcd. The kilogram, for example, rests on the Planck constant h being 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J⋅s. A revision adopted by the 26th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) in 2018, effective May 20, 2019, redefined the kilogram, ampere, kelvin, and mole this way, completing a move away from physical artifacts, which can be lost, damaged, or changed.

The system arose from the diversity of units in the centimeter–gram–second (CGS) systems and a lack of coordination among disciplines. The CGPM, established by the Metre Convention of 1875, set the definitions and rules. An initiative begun in 1948 led to publication of the system in 1960.

=== KEY: International_law_374cfa
HEADWORD: International law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consent | Diplomacy | Geneva Conventions | Jeremy Bentham | Sovereign state | Treaty | War
ENTRY:
Set of rules, norms and standards governing relations among states and other international actors. Also called public international law or the law of nations, it operates largely through [[Consent|consent]], because no universally accepted authority enforces it against [[Sovereign state|sovereign states]]. Its sources are international custom (general state practice accepted as law), [[Treaty|treaties]], and general principles of law recognized by most national legal systems. Violations, particularly of peremptory norms, can draw disapproval or coercive responses, including [[Diplomacy|diplomacy]], economic sanctions and [[War|war]]. The term was coined by [[Jeremy Bentham]] in his 1789 book *Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, replacing the older law of nations. Lassa Oppenheim defined it as "a law between sovereign and equal states based on the common consent of these states." Treaties such as the [[Geneva Conventions]] require national law to conform to treaty provisions.

=== KEY: International_relations_b72c71
HEADWORD: International relations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | Globalization | Political science | Soviet Union | United Kingdom | Western Europe
ENTRY:
Academic discipline that studies interactions among states and other international actors. Also called international studies or international politics, it is generally classified as a major multidiscipline of [[Political science|political science]] and draws heavily on anthropology, economics, geography, history, law, philosophy and sociology. The most prominent schools of thought within it are realism, liberalism and constructivism. Analysis of international politics dates to antiquity, but the field became a distinct area of study in 1919, when Aberystwyth University in the [[United Kingdom]] first offered it as an undergraduate major. Interest grew after the Second World War, particularly in North America and [[Western Europe]], where the [[Cold War]] shaped the field. The collapse of the [[Soviet Union]] and the rise of [[Globalization]] in the late 20th century prompted new theories and evaluations. Its broader scope includes war, diplomacy, trade, and relations with intergovernmental organizations and multinational corporations.

=== KEY: International_trade_bc0d2d
HEADWORD: International trade
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capital (economics) | Goods | Natural resource | Tourism | World Trade Organization
ENTRY:
Exchange of [[Capital (economics)|capital]], goods and services across international borders. Trade occurs because of demand for [[Goods|goods]] or services, and it links local, regional and national economies with the global economy. It moves commodities such as [[Natural resource|natural resources]] and manufactured goods, along with services such as banking, [[Tourism|tourism]], consulting and transportation. A product sold from one country to a party in another is an export from the first and an import to the second. Cross-border trade is typically more costly than domestic trade because of tariffs, non-tariff barriers such as border delays, and differences in language, product safety and legal systems. In 2024, global trade in goods and commercial services reached $32.2 trillion, a 4% expansion from the previous year. Post-World War II institutions, including the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and its successor, the [[World Trade Organization]] (1995), have significantly reduced average tariffs.

=== KEY: Internet_Protocol_6e0543
HEADWORD: Internet Protocol
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: IP address | IPv4 | IPv6 | Internet | Internet protocol suite
ENTRY:
Network-layer communications protocol in the [[Internet protocol suite]] for relaying datagrams across network boundaries. Its routing function enables internetworking and essentially establishes the [[Internet]]. IP delivers packets from source host to destination host based solely on the [[IP address|IP addresses]] in their headers. === KEY: Internet_c8205c
HEADWORD: Internet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ARPANET | Email | IP address | Internet protocol suite | United States Department of Defense | University of California, Los Angeles | World Wide Web
ENTRY:
Global system of interconnected computer networks using the [[Internet protocol suite]] (TCP/IP) to communicate between networks and devices. A network of networks, it links private, public, academic, business, and government networks by electronic, wireless, and optical technologies.

The internet carries services such as the hypertext documents of the [[World Wide Web]], [[Email|electronic mail]], internet telephony, streaming media, and file sharing. It has transformed telephone, radio, television, paper mail, and newspapers, and has enabled instant messaging, social networking, and online shopping. The web is only one of many internet services. About 5.5 billion people, 68% of the world's population, used the internet by 2024, with use correlating strongly with economic development.

Its origins lie in time-sharing of computer resources, packet switching, and computer networks. Paul Baran at RAND in the early 1960s and, independently, Donald Davies at the United Kingdom's National Physical Laboratory in 1965 pioneered packet switching. The [[ARPANET]], proposed by the Advanced Research Projects Agency of the [[United States Department of Defense]], first linked two nodes, at the [[University of California, Los Angeles]] and the Stanford Research Institute, on October 29, 1969. By the end of 1971, 15 sites were connected. Protocols for internetworking came from research commissioned in the 1970s by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) with universities and researchers in the United States, the United Kingdom, and France. The NSFNET backbone in the 1980s and privatization in the 1990s turned a research network into commercial infrastructure.

The internet has no single centralized governance, and each constituent network sets its own policies. The Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN) directs the [[IP address]] space and the Domain Name System, while the non-profit Internet Engineering Task Force standardizes the core protocols.

=== KEY: Internet_meme_08a9ff
HEADWORD: Internet meme
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Cultural item, such as an idea, behavior or style, that spreads across the internet, now mainly through [[Social media|social media]] platforms. The term "meme" was introduced by [[Richard Dawkins]] in his 1976 book *The Selfish Gene* for a conceptual unit of cultural transmission, analogous to a gene. Mike Godwin coined "internet meme" in 1993 for the way memes proliferated through early online communities, including message boards, [[Usenet]] groups and email. The emergence of social media platforms such as [[YouTube]] further diversified memes and accelerated their spread. Internet memes often represent fleeting trends, and their defining traits are creative reproduction, through mimicry or remix, and intertextuality. For example, the phrase "binders full of women" from the 2012 US presidential debate of US politician [[Mitt Romney]] was superimposed on a scene from the Korean pop song "[[Gangnam Style]]". Memes are also the subject of academic research and can raise [[Copyright|copyright]] disputes.

=== KEY: Internet_protocol_suite_2ee65d
HEADWORD: Internet protocol suite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ARPANET | HTTP | Internet | Internet Protocol | Transmission Control Protocol | United States Department of Defense
ENTRY:
Framework organizing the communication protocols used in the [[Internet]] and similar computer networks. Commonly known as TCP/IP, the suite is built on the [[Transmission Control Protocol]] (TCP), the User Datagram Protocol (UDP) and the [[Internet Protocol]] (IP). Its four layers run from lowest to highest: the link layer, for data within a single network segment; the internet layer, for internetworking between independent networks; the transport layer, for host-to-host communication; and the application layer, for process-to-process exchange, which supports protocols such as [[HTTP]]. Its roots lie in research sponsored by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) of the [[United States Department of Defense]] in the late 1960s, which included the [[ARPANET]]. Early versions were called the Department of Defense Internet Architecture Model. The Internet Engineering Task Force maintains its standards, and the suite predates the OSI model.

=== KEY: Internment_of_Japanese_Americans_bd936a
HEADWORD: Internment of Japanese Americans
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Attack on Pearl Harbor | Franklin D. Roosevelt | Jimmy Carter | Ronald Reagan | World War II
ENTRY:
Wartime policy under which the U.S. government forcibly relocated and incarcerated about 120,000 people of Japanese descent during [[World War II]]. Executive Order 9066, issued by President [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] on February 19, 1942, followed the [[Attack on Pearl Harbor|attack on Pearl Harbor]]. In 1944 the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion orders in Korematsu v. United States on grounds of military necessity, a decision later repudiated. A commission appointed under President [[Jimmy Carter]] reported in 1983 that internment reflected racism rather than Japanese disloyalty. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988, signed by President [[Ronald Reagan]], officially apologized and authorized a payment of $20,000 to each surviving detainee.

=== KEY: Interpersonal_relationship_cc4c54

ENTRY:
Social association, connection or affiliation between two or more people. Relations vary in intimacy, self-disclosure, duration, reciprocity and power distribution, and they form the basis of [[Social group|social groups]] and societies. Major themes include family, [[Kinship|kinship]], [[Friendship|friendship]], [[Love|love]], [[Marriage|marriage]], business, [[Employment|employment]], clubs, neighborhoods, ethical values, support and [[Solidarity|solidarity]]. === KEY: Interplanetary_medium_9b0d9d
HEADWORD: Interplanetary medium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cosmic ray | Heliosphere | Interstellar medium | Plasma (physics) | Solar System | Solar wind | Vacuum
ENTRY:
Mass and energy that fill the [[Solar System]], through which planets, asteroids and comets move. It ends at the [[Heliosphere|heliopause]], beyond which the [[Interstellar medium]] begins. Before 1950 it was widely considered either an empty [[Vacuum|vacuum]] or filled with "aether." It includes interplanetary dust and gas, [[Cosmic ray|cosmic rays]] and hot [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] from the [[Solar wind|solar wind]], whose density falls in inverse proportion to the square of distance from the Sun. Near Earth the medium contains about 5 particles/cm³, though values as high as 100 particles/cm³ have been observed, compared with about 2.9 × 10¹⁹ particles per cm³ for air at sea level. Its plasma makes the Sun's magnetic field at Earth's orbit over 100 times greater than originally anticipated, at around 7 nT.
