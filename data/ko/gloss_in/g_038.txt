=== KEY: Dot_product_4f1c38
HEADWORD: Dot product
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cartesian coordinate system | Cross product | Euclidean geometry | Three-dimensional space
ENTRY:
Algebraic operation on two equal-length sequences of numbers, usually coordinate vectors. The dot product is the sum of the products of corresponding entries; for the vectors [1, 3, −5] and [4, −2, −1] it equals 3. Geometrically, it is the product of the two vectors' lengths and the cosine of the angle between them, and the two definitions agree in [[Cartesian coordinate system|Cartesian coordinates]]. In modern [[Euclidean geometry]], lengths and angles are defined through it: a vector's length is the square root of its dot product with itself. The name derives from the dot operator (⋅) used to write it. The alternative name scalar product stresses that the result is a scalar rather than a vector, as with the [[Cross product|vector product]] in [[Three-dimensional space]].

=== KEY: Double_slit_experiment_794ecf

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Physics experiment demonstrating [[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]] in light and matter. First described by [[Thomas Young (scientist)|Thomas Young]] in 1801 to support the wave theory of light, it sends light through two parallel slits, forming an interference pattern of bright and dark bands. === KEY: Drag_physics_70a15a
HEADWORD: Drag
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Concorde | Force | Lift (force) | Reynolds number | Velocity | Viscosity | Wing
ENTRY:
Retarding [[Force|force]] exerted by a fluid on an object moving through it, opposite to its direction of motion. Drag is proportional to [[Velocity|velocity]] at low speeds and to velocity squared at high speeds, a distinction measured by the [[Reynolds number]]. Form drag comes from pressure around a body and depends on its cross-sectional shape, while skin friction drag comes from [[Viscosity|viscous]] friction along the surface. Flying insect of the suborder Anisoptera in the order [[Odonata]]. About 3,000 living species are known, most of them tropical. Adults have a pair of large, multifaceted [[Compound eye|compound eyes]], two pairs of strong transparent wings and an elongated body, and many have iridescent or metallic colors. === KEY: Drainage_19f08c

=== KEY: Drainage_basin_c10fed
HEADWORD: Drainage basin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Pacific Ocean | Río de la Plata
ENTRY:
Land area in which all flowing surface water converges to a single point or body of water. It is bounded by the drainage divide, a perimeter of ridges and hills that separates adjacent basins. Smaller basins may merge at river confluences, forming a hierarchical pattern. In North America a drainage basin is commonly called a watershed. In an endorheic basin, water converges on an interior sink, such as a permanent lake, a dry lake or a point where surface water is lost underground. About 48.71% of the world's land drains into the [[Atlantic Ocean]], and just over 13% into the [[Pacific Ocean]]. The five largest river basins by area are those of the Amazon (7 million sq km), the Congo (4 million sq km), the Nile (3.4 million sq km), the Mississippi (3.22 million sq km) and the [[Río de la Plata]] (3.17 million sq km).

=== KEY: Drake_Passage_1cad6d
HEADWORD: Drake Passage
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Cape Horn | Chile | Francis Drake | Plate tectonics | South America
ENTRY:
Body of water between [[Cape Horn]] in [[Chile]] and the South Shetland Islands of [[Antarctica]]. It connects the southwestern Atlantic (Scotia Sea) with the southeastern Pacific and is the narrowest passage around Antarctica, so it strongly influences Antarctic and global ocean circulation and climate. Named after the 16th-century English explorer and privateer [[Francis Drake|Sir Francis Drake]], it is considered one of the most treacherous crossings for ships, since the Antarctic Circumpolar Current meets no resistance from any landmass and waves top 40 feet (12 m). The Dutch navigator Willem Schouten was the first to sail through it, in 1616. In 2019 a crew of six became the first to row across it. The passage opened as Antarctica separated from [[South America]] through [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], though estimates of when range from 49 to 17 million years ago.

=== KEY: Drake_equation_9c360d
HEADWORD: Drake equation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Milky Way | Search for extraterrestrial intelligence
ENTRY:
Probabilistic formula estimating the number of active, communicative extraterrestrial civilizations in the [[Milky Way]]. Frank Drake formulated it in 1961 as a way to stimulate scientific dialogue at the first meeting on the [[Search for extraterrestrial intelligence|search for extraterrestrial intelligence]] (SETI). It is more an approximation than a precise count, and its factors, such as the fraction of stars with planets and the average number of life-supporting planets per star, are highly conjectural. === KEY: Drama_205b8c
HEADWORD: Drama
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeschylus | Aristotle | Dionysus
ENTRY:
Specific mode of fiction represented in performance, such as a play, opera, mime or ballet. Considered as a genre of poetry, the dramatic mode has been contrasted with the epic and lyric modes since [[Aristotle]]'s *Poetics* (c. 335 BC), the earliest work of dramatic theory. The word comes from a Greek term meaning deed or act, and its two masks represent the traditional division between comedy and tragedy. Western drama originated in classical Athens, which produced tragedy, comedy and the satyr play for competitions held in honor of [[Dionysus]] from the 5th century BC. The oldest surviving drama is [[Aeschylus]]' *The Persians*. In the modern era, drama came to mean a play that is neither comedy nor tragedy, and the term became a genre in film and television. Mime, in which the story is told through movement alone, and musicals, which combine speech and song, are also forms of drama.

A quick freehand drawing not intended as a finished work is called a sketch; other categories include figure drawing, [[Cartoon|cartoon]], caricature, and doodle, and methods include line drawing, stippling, shading, and tracing. Outside art, plans of buildings, machinery, and circuitry are also called drawings, and drafters work in [[Technical drawing|technical drawing]].

Drawing is one of the oldest forms of human expression and is believed to have preceded written communication; [[Cave painting|cave paintings]] of quadrupeds in southern France and Spain date to around 30,000 years ago. Before paper was widely available in Europe, monks used drawings as underdrawings for illuminated manuscripts on vellum or parchment, or as final images. In 1609 [[Galileo Galilei]] explained the phases of Venus and sunspots through his telescopic drawings, and in 1924 [[Alfred Wegener]] used illustrations to show the origin of the continents. After paper became widely available in the 14th century, drawing increased in the arts as a tool for study and preparation. The [[Renaissance]] brought greater sophistication and more realistic representation. In the 17th century the "Quarrel of Color" pitted proponents of color against those of drawing, a distinction between contour lines and colored surface that Leonardo da Vinci had made.

=== KEY: Dream_2a2542
HEADWORD: Dream
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Anxiety | Babylonia | COVID-19 pandemic | Mesopotamia | Psychotherapy | Sleep | Sumer
ENTRY:
Succession of images, scenes, ideas, emotions, and sensations that usually occur involuntarily in the mind during certain stages of [[Sleep|sleep]]. Humans generally spend more than two hours dreaming per night, and each dream lasts around 5–20 minutes. Dreams occur mainly in the rapid-eye movement (REM) stage, when brain activity is high and resembles that of waking, but humans also dream during non-REM sleep, and not all REM awakenings produce dream reports. Because a dream must be reduced to a verbal report to be studied, dreaming by non-humans, human fetuses, and pre-verbal infants is currently unprovable. It is not known where in the brain dreams originate or what their purpose is. The scientific study of dreams is called oneirology; most modern study focuses on neurophysiology and on testing hypotheses about function.

Dream interpretation was practiced by the [[Babylonia|Babylonians]] in the third millennium BC and earlier by the [[Sumer|Sumerians]], and it figures in religious texts of several traditions and in [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]]. Writings from [[Mesopotamia]] and [[Ancient Egypt]] stress visitation dreams in which a deity or forebear commands the dreamer to act; Gudea of Lagash rebuilt a temple as the result of such a dream. Between the 1940s and 1985, Calvin S. Hall collected more than 50,000 dream reports; the most common emotion was [[Anxiety|anxiety]], negative emotions were much more common than positive ones, and sexual content appeared in no more than 10% of dreams. A study of more than 15,000 dream reports found fear, illness, and death themes two to four times more prevalent after the onset of the [[COVID-19 pandemic]]. People blind from birth do not have visual dreams. In Old English, *drēam* meant "noise", "joy", or "music"; the sense of a sleep experience dates from the 13th century.

=== KEY: Drink_40491d
HEADWORD: Drink
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beer | Dionysus | Drinking water | Ethanol | Milk | Tea | Wine | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Liquid intended for human consumption, with water as its common basis. Besides satisfying thirst, drinks play important roles in human culture. Common types include plain [[Drinking water|drinking water]], [[Milk|milk]], juice, smoothies, and soft drinks; traditionally warm beverages include coffee, tea, and hot chocolate. Caffeinated drinks have been consumed for centuries, and alcoholic drinks such as [[Wine|wine]], [[Beer|beer]], and liquor, which contain [[Ethanol|ethanol]], have been part of human culture for more than 8,000 years. Thirst is regulated by the hypothalamus, and complete deprivation of drinks results in death faster than removal of any other substance besides oxygen. Water has also been the carrier of many diseases: the [[World Health Organization]] points out that 94% of deaths from diarrhea, the third biggest cause of infectious death worldwide at 1.8 million annually, could be prevented by improving the victim's environment, particularly safe water.

The earliest archaeological evidence of wine production comes from Georgia (c. 6000 BC) and Iran (c. 5000 BC). Beer may have been known in Neolithic Europe as far back as 3000 BC. [[Tea]] likely originated in Yunnan, China, during the Shang dynasty (1500–1046 BC) as a medicinal drink. In ancient Greece, a social gathering for drinking was known as a symposium, where watered-down wine was drunk. Many early societies considered alcohol a gift from the gods, leading to gods such as [[Dionysus]]. Coffee was banned in Ottoman Turkey during the 17th century for political reasons. Preparation of drinks can include water purification by filtration and chlorination; pasteurization, used primarily on milk; juicing; infusion; percolation; carbonation; and fermentation, used since the Neolithic age.

=== KEY: Drinking_26bab4
HEADWORD: Drinking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amphibian | Cat | Elephant | Esophagus | Mouth | Stomach
ENTRY:
Ingestion of water or other liquids into the body, usually through the [[Mouth|mouth]]. Humans drink by swallowing, with peristalsis carrying the liquid through the [[Esophagus|esophagus]] to the [[Stomach|stomach]]. Most animals drink to maintain hydration, though many survive on water gained from their food. [[Cat|Cats]] lap with the tip of the tongue, ruminants draw in water by a plunging action, and [[Elephant|elephants]] draw water into their trunks and squirt it into their mouths. Most birds scoop water into their bills, while the common pigeon can suck it in directly. [[Amphibian|Amphibians]] and freshwater animals absorb water through their skin, while saltwater fish drink and purge excess salt through their gills. Lack of hydration causes thirst, which is regulated by the hypothalamus, and both inadequate and excessive water intake are linked to health problems.

=== KEY: Drinking_water_76f7c9
HEADWORD: Drinking water
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aquifer | Disease | Famine | Glacier | Infrastructure | Spring (hydrology) | Water pollution
ENTRY:
Water safe for ingestion, either drunk directly or consumed through food preparation; also called potable water. For most terrestrial animals, including humans, it is fresh water, surface or underground, whose quality does not cause immediate poisoning or significant short-term harm. In modern societies it is typically supplied to households through a distribution system ending in taps. Sources include [[Spring (hydrology)|springs]], [[Aquifer|aquifers]], rainwater harvesting, surface water from rivers, streams, and [[Glacier|glaciers]], and desalinated seawater; all must receive adequate treatment and meet quality standards. Most bottled water is packaged tap water.

Requirements vary with activity, age, health, and environment; those working in a hot climate may need up to 16 liters a day. In the United States the Adequate Intake for total water is 4.0 liters per day for males older than 18 and 3.0 liters for females over 18, assuming about 80% from drink and 20% from food. The European Food Safety Authority recommends 2.0 liters for women and 2.5 liters for men. The common advice to drink 8 glasses of plain water per day is not scientific; thirst is a better guide. Water intoxication can cause death in minutes or hours.

As many as two billion people lack safe drinking water, with developing countries most affected. Unsafe water, typically due to [[Water pollution|water pollution]] and the lack of water treatment, can carry [[Disease|disease]] and is a major cause of preventable death and illness worldwide. Water scarcity is also a cause of [[Famine|famine]], mass migration, and violent conflict over control of water supplies. Leakage rates of 50% are not uncommon in urban systems, and replacing deteriorating water and sanitation [[Infrastructure|infrastructure]] in industrialized countries may cost as much as $200 billion a year.

ENTRY:
Meteorological phenomenon in which strong wind lifts loose sand and dirt from dry surfaces, common in arid and semi-arid regions.
