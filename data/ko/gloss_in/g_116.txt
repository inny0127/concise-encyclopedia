ENTRY:
[[Supercontinent]] of the [[Proterozoic|Mesoproterozoic and Neoproterozoic]] eras. It assembled 1.26–0.90 billion years ago and broke up 750–633 million years ago. Reconstructions of its layout differ in many details, and paleomagnetic data constrain latitude far better than longitude. The name, from the Russian for "motherland," was given in 1990. === KEY: Rogue_wave_9e2287

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Tsunami
ENTRY:
Unusually large, unpredictable ocean surface wave. Also called a freak wave or monster wave, it is more than twice the significant wave height, which is defined as the mean height of the largest third of waves in a record. Rogue waves endanger ships and isolated structures such as lighthouses. They have no single distinct cause, but can occur when high winds and strong currents merge waves into one large wave. === KEY: Rolling_metalworking_427c1c

ENTRY:
Metal-forming process in which metal stock is passed through one or more pairs of rolls to reduce its thickness, make its thickness uniform, or impart a desired mechanical property. === KEY: Roman_Empire_98f216
HEADWORD: Roman Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustus | Byzantine Empire | Constantine the Great | Constantinople | Diocletian | Trajan | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
State that dominated the Mediterranean and much of Europe, Western Asia and North Africa during the classical period. Its rule became permanently single-person after [[Augustus|Octavian]] was granted the title Augustus in 27 BC. The first two centuries of the Empire were known as the Pax Romana, and its territory reached its greatest extent under [[Trajan]] (r. 98–117 AD). ENTRY:
Earliest period of Roman history, when the city and its territory were ruled by kings, c. 753–c. 509 BC. === KEY: Roman_Persian_wars_11f84e
HEADWORD: Roman–Persian wars
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anatolia | Byzantine Empire | Parthian Empire | Rashidun Caliphate | Sasanian Empire
ENTRY:
Series of conflicts between Roman states and Iranian empires, from 54 BC to AD 628, beginning with the Roman Republic and the [[Parthian Empire]]. Much of the fighting involved vassal kingdoms and allied nomadic tribes, which acted as buffer states or proxies. Despite nearly seven centuries of hostility, the wars ended inconclusively. The border remained largely stable, although towns, fortifications and provinces were repeatedly sacked, captured and traded, and neither side could sustain lengthy campaigns far from its borders. By the 6th century the [[Byzantine Empire|Byzantines]] and [[Sasanian Empire|Sasanians]] were militarily similar and evenly matched. Their exhausted rivalry left both vulnerable when the [[Rashidun Caliphate]] launched the early Muslim conquests, which destroyed the Sasanian Empire and confined the Byzantines largely to [[Anatolia]] and southeastern Europe.

=== KEY: Roman_Republic_faf063
HEADWORD: Roman Republic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustus | Carthage | Cleopatra | Julius Caesar | Mark Antony | Pompey | Punic Wars | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Period of classical Roman history, c. 509–27 BC, between the Roman Kingdom and the [[Roman Empire]]. Its government consisted of annually elected magistrates overseen by the Senate. Although there were elections, it was an elective oligarchy rather than a true democracy, with powerful families monopolizing senior offices. The Republic was in near-constant war. It defeated [[Carthage]] in the [[Punic Wars]], culminating at Zama in 202 BC, and became the dominant power in the Mediterranean world. Civil wars in the 1st century BC then broke the Republic. [[Julius Caesar]] and [[Pompey]] fought in 49 BC, and Caesar was assassinated in 44 BC. After [[Mark Antony]] and [[Cleopatra]] were defeated at Actium in 31 BC, the Senate granted [[Augustus|Octavian]] the title Augustus in 27 BC, which is often seen as the end of the Republic.

=== KEY: Roman_art_410e26

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mosaic
ENTRY:
Art made in Ancient Rome and the territories of its Republic and Empire, including architecture, painting, sculpture and [[Mosaic|mosaic]] work. Sculpture was perhaps considered the highest form of art by Romans, and figure painting was also highly regarded. A very large body of sculpture survives from about the 1st century BC onward, but very little painting remains. === KEY: Roman_mythology_3215b0
HEADWORD: Roman mythology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeneid | Metamorphoses | Ovid | Romulus and Remus | Virgil
ENTRY:
Body of myths of ancient Rome, as represented in the literature and visual arts of the Romans. The Romans usually treated their traditional narratives as historical, even when they contained supernatural elements, and the stories often concern politics, morality and heroism. Ritual rather than myth was central to Roman religion, so some scholars have doubted that Rome had a native mythology, a view shaped by Romanticism and 19th-century classical scholarship. Roman myth drew on Italic traditions and, through the Hellenistic period and the conquest of Greece, on Greek mythology; the Romans identified their gods with Greek deities. Founding myths trace the city to the Trojan prince Aeneas and to the twins [[Romulus and Remus]]. Major sources include [[Virgil]]'s [[Aeneid]] and [[Ovid]]'s [[Metamorphoses]].

=== KEY: Roman_numerals_ffd761
HEADWORD: Roman numerals
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Ben | Hindu–Arabic numeral system | Statue of Liberty
ENTRY:
Additive numeral system originating in ancient Rome, written with seven letters of the Latin alphabet: I (1), V (5), X (10), L (50), C (100), D (500) and M (1000). A smaller symbol before a larger one is subtracted, as in IV (4) and IX (9). The system has no zero. From the 14th century it was gradually replaced by the positional [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], but Roman numerals persist on clock faces, such as that of [[Big Ben]] (designed in 1852), and in year numbers and copyright dates. For example, 1776 is written MDCCLXXVI, as on the book held by the [[Statue of Liberty]].

=== KEY: Romanesque_architecture_13f8e7
HEADWORD: Romanesque architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gothic architecture
ENTRY:
Medieval European architectural style predominant in the 11th and 12th centuries, distinguished from the later [[Gothic architecture|Gothic]] style by its semicircular arches. It was the first pan-European architectural style since Imperial Roman architecture, and it combined features of ancient Roman and Byzantine buildings with local traditions. Its buildings are known for massive walls, round arches, sturdy pillars, barrel vaults, large towers and decorative arcading. The most significant are the great abbey churches, many still standing. Many were rebuilt in the Gothic style in prosperous areas such as England and Portugal, so the largest surviving groups are in less prosperous regions, including southern France, rural Spain, rural Portugal and rural Italy.

=== KEY: Romanesque_art_114ee1
HEADWORD: Romanesque art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Isles
ENTRY:
European art style from about AD 1000 until the rise of the Gothic in the 12th century, or later in some regions. The term was coined by 19th-century art historians, especially for architecture that retained Roman features such as round-headed arches, barrel vaults and apses. Romanesque art was the first style to spread across all of Catholic Europe, from Sicily to Scandinavia, and it drew heavily on Byzantine painting and the Insular art of the [[British Isles]]. High relief was the dominant sculptural mode, and tympanums were carved with monumental scenes such as Christ in Majesty and the Last Judgment. Stained glass became widely used. Portraiture hardly existed, and compositions usually had little depth.

=== KEY: Romani_people_a6a7c1

ENTRY:
Indo-Aryan ethnic group, traditionally nomadic and itinerant. Linguistic and genetic evidence suggests the Romani originated in northwestern regions of the Indian subcontinent, and their westward migration is believed to have occurred around AD 1000. They arrived in Europe around the 13th to 14th century, via the [[Balkans]], and their most concentrated populations are now in Central, Eastern and Southern Europe and in West Asia, mainly [[Turkey]] and Iran. === KEY: Romantic_music_0cb48a
HEADWORD: Romantic music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Classical music | Industrial Revolution | Ludwig van Beethoven | Orchestra | Romanticism | Symphony
ENTRY:
Stylistic movement in Western [[Classical music|classical music]] associated with the Romantic era of the 19th century. It is closely related to [[Romanticism]], the intellectual, artistic and literary movement prominent in Western culture from about 1798 until 1837. Romantic composers sought music that was individualistic, emotional and dramatic, and often programmatic. Much of it was ostensibly inspired by nature, literature, poetry or other non-musical stimuli. E. T. A. Hoffmann established the principles of musical Romanticism in a review of [[Ludwig van Beethoven|Beethoven]]'s Fifth [[Symphony]] published in 1810. Compared with the Classical period, its themes tended to be longer and more emotionally evocative, chromaticism increased, and large [[Orchestra|orchestras]] became common, with a wider range of dynamics from ppp to fff. The broader movement strengthened in reaction to the [[Industrial Revolution]].

ENTRY:
Artistic and intellectual movement originating in Europe toward the end of the 18th century.

Romanticism advocated subjectivity, imagination, and appreciation of nature in response to the [[Age of Enlightenment]] and the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Its adherents rejected social conventions in favor of individualism, argued that passion and intuition were crucial to understanding the world, and held that beauty evokes a strong emotional response rather than residing in form alone. Recurring themes included reverence for nature and the supernatural, idealization of the past, fascination with the exotic and mysterious, and celebration of the heroic and the sublime. Romantics were particularly fond of the [[Middle Ages]], an era they associated with chivalry; critics have countered that such portrayals overlooked the downsides of medieval life.

The first Romantic ideas arose from the German Counter-Enlightenment movement *Sturm und Drang* ("Storm and Stress"), which questioned the claim that reason alone can explain the world. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's *The Sorrows of Young Werther* (1774) helped shape the movement, and many early Romantics sympathized with the aims of the [[French Revolution]]. The movement is generally considered to have peaked from 1800 to 1850. In literature it is associated with William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, whose *Lyrical Ballads* appeared in 1798; in painting with [[Caspar David Friedrich]]; and in music with [[Ludwig van Beethoven]].

Its decline in the mid-19th century followed the rise of Realism and Naturalism, Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, a more conservative political climate, and growing attention to the effects of urbanization on the working class. By World War I it had dispersed into later movements, though Late Romantic and Neoromantic currents persisted. Romanticism influenced conservatism, liberalism, radicalism, nationalism, and modern conservation efforts.

=== KEY: Rome_1f49f7

City (pop. 2.7 million), capital of Italy and of the Lazio region.

Rome is a special municipality, Roma Capitale, covering 1,287.36 sq km. It is the third most populous city in the [[European Union]] within city limits, and its metropolitan city, with 4.2 million people, is the most populous in Italy. It lies in the central-western Italian peninsula along the Tiber Valley. Vatican City, the smallest country in the world and seat of the [[Catholic Church]] under the Holy See, is an independent state within its boundaries. It is called the "Eternal City" (Latin *Urbs Aeterna*).

Roman mythology dates the founding of the city to around 753 BC, but archaeological evidence of human occupation goes back about 14,000 years. Rome became successively the capital of the Roman Kingdom, the Roman Republic, and the [[Roman Empire]]. After the fall of the western Empire, Rome fell under the political control of the papacy and in the 8th century became capital of the [[Papal States]], which lasted until 1870. From the [[Renaissance]] onward, popes pursued an architectural and urban program over four hundred years, making Rome an artistic and cultural center and the birthplace of the Baroque style and Neoclassicism. It became capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871 and of the Italian Republic in 1946.

In 2019 Rome received 8.6 million tourists, ranking 14th among the world's most visited cities. Its historic center is a [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Site. The city hosts UN agencies, including the Food and Agriculture Organization.

=== KEY: Rotation_f1a42b
HEADWORD: Rotation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's orbit | Group (mathematics) | Orbit | Sun
ENTRY:
Movement of an object that leaves at least one point unchanged, also called rotational motion. In two dimensions, a plane figure rotates clockwise or counterclockwise around a center of rotation. In three dimensions, a solid rotates around an axis of rotation. A rotation about an internal axis through the body's center of mass is called a spin, and Earth's rotation defines the geographical poles. A rotation around an external axis is a [[Orbit|revolution]], such as [[Earth's orbit]] around the [[Sun]]. Mathematically, a rotation is a rigid body movement that, unlike a translation, keeps at least one point fixed. Rotations around a point or axis form a [[Group (mathematics)|group]], and any spatial rotation can be decomposed into rotations around the x, y and z axes.

=== KEY: Rotavirus_1496d9
HEADWORD: Rotavirus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diarrhea | Gastroenteritis | RNA | Small intestine | Species | Vaccination
ENTRY:
Genus of double-stranded [[RNA]] viruses in the family Reoviridae, and the most common cause of [[Diarrhea|diarrheal]] disease among infants and young children. Nearly every child is infected at least once by age five, and immunity develops with each infection. The virus is transmitted by the fecal–oral route. It infects the cells lining the [[Small intestine|small intestine]], causing [[Gastroenteritis|gastroenteritis]]. Rotavirus was discovered in 1973 by Ruth Bishop and her colleagues, and it accounts for about one-third of hospitalizations for severe diarrhea in infants and children. Rotavirus caused an estimated 151,714 deaths from diarrhea among children under 5 worldwide in 2019. The genus includes 11 [[Species|species]], and RVA causes more than 90% of human infections. In countries that added rotavirus [[Vaccination|vaccine]] to routine childhood immunization, incidence and severity have declined significantly. Public health campaigns emphasize oral rehydration therapy.

=== KEY: Roulette_3cf644
HEADWORD: Roulette
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blaise Pascal | Casino | Monte Carlo
ENTRY:
[[Casino|Casino]] game of chance, named after the French word for "little wheel." A player may bet on a single number, groups of numbers, red or black, odd or even, or high or low. The croupier spins the wheel one way and a ball the other way around a track, and the ball falls into a numbered and colored pocket. Winning bets are then paid. The game was likely developed from the Italian game Biribi. Some accounts credit [[Blaise Pascal]] with a primitive form in the 17th century, but his role is disputed. The game has been played in its present form as early as 1796 in Paris. In 1843, François and Louis Blanc introduced the single-zero wheel at Bad Homburg, and it became the premier game at [[Monte Carlo]]. In the United States, the double-zero wheel remained dominant. House edges are about 2.70% for European roulette and 5.26% for American roulette.

=== KEY: Router_computing_2ee8e1
HEADWORD: Router
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer network | Internet | Optical fiber
ENTRY:
Computer networking device that forwards data packets between [[Computer network|computer networks]], including the global [[Internet]]. A router reads the network address in each packet header and uses its routing table or policy to send the packet toward its destination. Home and small-office routers forward IP packets between home computers and the Internet, while enterprise routers connect large networks to core routers on the [[Optical fiber|optical fiber]] backbone. Routers are mostly purpose-built embedded systems, though early models ran software on a general-purpose CPU. Advanced models use application-specific integrated circuits (ASICs) to improve performance and add filtering and firewall functions. The first true IP router was developed by Ginny Travers at BBN from 1975 to 1976 as part of a DARPA-initiated effort that created the TCP/IP architecture.

=== KEY: Rubella_d08b89

ENTRY:
Viral infection, also known as German measles or three-day measles, caused by the rubella virus. Symptoms are often mild. A rash, usually starting on the face, lasts three days, and fever, swollen lymph nodes and joint pain may also occur. The virus spreads through the air in coughs, and people are infectious for about a week before and after the rash. Infection in early [[Pregnancy|pregnancy]] may cause miscarriage or congenital rubella syndrome, which can affect the eyes, hearing, [[Heart|heart]] and brain. A single dose of the [[Vaccine|vaccine]] is more than 95% effective, and it is often given as the combined [[Measles|measles]], mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine. There is no specific treatment. The name comes from the Latin for "little red." In April 2015 the [[World Health Organization]] declared the [[Americas]] free of rubella transmission.

[[Chemical element]] with symbol Rb and atomic number 37. It is a very soft, whitish-gray solid [[Alkali metal|alkali metal]] similar to [[Potassium]] and [[Caesium|cesium]]. It is the first alkali metal in its group with a density higher than water, and it reacts explosively with water to produce rubidium hydroxide and hydrogen gas. German chemists Robert Bunsen and [[Gustav Kirchhoff]] discovered it in 1861 by flame spectroscopy. The name comes from the Latin *rubidus*, meaning deep red, the color of its emission spectrum. Natural rubidium is 72% stable [[Isotope|isotope]] ⁸⁵Rb and 28% slightly radioactive ⁸⁷Rb, with a [[Half-life|half-life]] of 48.8 billion years. Rubidium chloride is used to induce living cells to take up [[DNA]].
