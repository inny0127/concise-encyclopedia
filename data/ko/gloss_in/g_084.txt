=== KEY: Man_627661
HEADWORD: Man
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gender equality | Puberty
ENTRY:
Adult male human. Before adulthood, a male child or adolescent is called a boy. A man's genome usually inherits an X chromosome from the mother and a Y chromosome from the father, and sex differentiation of the male fetus is governed by the *SRY* gene on the Y chromosome. During [[Puberty|puberty]], hormones that stimulate androgen production produce secondary sexual characteristics, including greater muscle mass, greater height, facial hair growth, and lower body fat composition. Male anatomy is distinguished by the male reproductive system, which includes the testicles, sperm ducts, prostate gland, epididymides, and penis. Men tend to be taller and heavier than women and, adjusted for height, to have greater lean and bone mass and lower fat mass.

The English word derives from Old English *mann*, which primarily meant "person" or "human being" and referred to men, women, and children alike; the distinct word for an adult male was *wer*, which survives today only in compounds such as "werewolf."

Throughout history, traditional gender roles have often defined masculinity and men's activities and opportunities, and many societies have been patriarchal, giving men considerable power, particularly over women. Men are over-represented as both perpetrators and victims of violence, are often directed into professions with high mortality rates, and are most often subject to conscription into military service. Many religious doctrines set rules for men, such as religious circumcision. Critiques of the impact of these roles have prompted movement toward [[Gender equality|gender equality]] and the diverse men's movement. Trans men have a gender identity that does not align with the female sex they were assigned at birth, while intersex men may have sex characteristics that do not fit typical notions of male biology.

American-born, French-naturalized visual artist who spent most of his career in [[Paris]]. Born Emmanuel Radnitzky in South [[Philadelphia]] to Russian Jewish immigrants, he adopted the name Man Ray. He contributed to the [[Dada]] and [[Surrealism|Surrealist]] movements, although his ties to each were informal, and considered himself a painter above all. Items linked to his father's tailoring trade, such as [[Sewing machine|sewing machines]] and mannequins, appear throughout his work. In Paris he was associated with [[Tristan Tzara]].

=== KEY: Management_fe4dbc
HEADWORD: Management
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin
ENTRY:
Administration of organizations, whether businesses, nonprofit organizations, or government bodies, through business administration, nonprofit management, or public administration respectively. It is the process of managing the resources of businesses, governments, and other organizations.

Larger organizations generally have three hierarchical levels of managers. Senior management, including the board of directors and a chief executive officer (CEO) or president, sets strategic goals and policies. Middle management, including branch, regional, department, and section managers, directs front-line managers and communicates senior management's goals to them. Line management, made up of supervisors and frontline managers or team leaders, oversees regular employees or volunteers and performs the functions traditionally considered the core of management.

The English verb derives from the fifteenth-century French *mesnager*, which referred in equestrian language to holding a horse's reins, and ultimately from the [[Latin]] *manus* (hand) and *agere* (to act). Definitions vary. Henri Fayol stated: "To manage is to forecast and to plan, to organize, to command, to co-ordinate and to control." Peter Drucker saw the basic task of management as twofold: marketing and innovation. Frederick Winslow Taylor's scientific management applied empirical analysis of workflows through time-motion studies to improve efficiency, and it also prompted debate over worker autonomy.

Management is taught as both theory and practice at colleges and universities, with prominent degree programs in management, business administration, and public administration. Social scientists study it as an academic discipline, investigating social organization, organizational adaptation, and organizational leadership. In recent decades there has been a movement for evidence-based management.

Small, rounded [[Citrus]] fruit, *Citrus reticulata*, usually eaten fresh or in fruit salads. The mandarin is oblate, unlike the roughly spherical sweet orange, which is a mandarin-pomelo hybrid. === KEY: Mandate_of_Heaven_4a59cb
HEADWORD: Mandate of Heaven
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Mencius | Qing dynasty | Shang dynasty | Zhou dynasty
ENTRY:
Chinese political doctrine of divine legitimacy for rulers, used in ancient and imperial [[China]]. Heaven was held to bestow its mandate on a virtuous ruler, the Son of Heaven, and to withdraw it if the ruler proved unworthy. Famine and flood were read as signs of Heaven's displeasure, and revolts often followed major disasters. The mandate did not require noble birth; dynasties such as the Han and Ming were founded by men of common origin but were seen as having succeeded because they gained it. The doctrine implied a right of rebellion against an unjust ruler. The classical statement of that right, in [[Mencius]], was often edited out of the text. The [[Zhou dynasty]] first used the concept to justify overthrowing the [[Shang dynasty]], and later dynasties, including the [[Qing dynasty]], invoked it.

[[Fractal]] in the complex plane, named after mathematician [[Benoit Mandelbrot]]. It is the set of complex numbers *c* for which the iteration *z* → *z*² + *c*, starting at *z* = 0, remains bounded. Robert W. Brooks and Peter Matelski first defined and drew it in 1978, and in 1980 Mandelbrot produced high-quality images at [[IBM]]'s research center in Yorktown Heights, New York. [[Transition metal]] with atomic number 25 (symbol Mn). Dualistic [[Religion|religion]] founded in the third century AD by the Iranian prophet Mani in the [[Sasanian Empire]]. Most forms of the faith regarded Mani as the final prophet after [[Zoroaster]], [[The Buddha|Buddha]] and [[Jesus]]. === KEY: Manifold_76dd9d

ENTRY:
[[Topological space]] locally resembling [[Euclidean space]] near each point. An *n*-dimensional manifold has a neighborhood around each point that is homeomorphic to an open subset of *n*-dimensional Euclidean space. One-dimensional manifolds include lines and circles, and two-dimensional manifolds, called surfaces, include the [[Sphere|sphere]], the [[Torus|torus]] and the Klein bottle. Manifolds are central to geometry and modern physics: differentiable manifolds allow calculus, a Riemannian metric allows distances and angles to be measured, and four-dimensional Lorentzian manifolds model spacetime in [[General relativity|general relativity]]. === KEY: Manufacturing_e86883
HEADWORD: Manufacturing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Assembly line | Bronze Age | Handicraft | Henry Ford | Industrial Revolution | Iron Age | Mesopotamia | Smelting
ENTRY:
Production of goods with the help of equipment, labor, machines, tools, and chemical or biological processing. Manufacturing is the essence of the secondary sector of the economy. The term covers activity from [[Handicraft|handicraft]] to high technology but is most commonly applied to industrial production, in which raw materials from the primary sector are transformed into finished goods on a large scale. Such goods may be sold to other manufacturers for making more complex products, such as aircraft, household appliances, furniture, sports equipment, or automobiles, or distributed through the tertiary sector to consumers, usually via wholesalers and retailers. Manufacturing engineering designs and optimizes the steps by which raw materials become a final product, beginning with product design and materials specification. Some industries, such as semiconductor and steel manufacturing, use the term fabrication instead.

The English word probably derives from Middle French *manufacture* ("process of making"), from Latin *manū* ("hand") and French *facture* ("making"). Its earliest recorded English use, in the mid-16th century, referred to making products by hand.

Human ancestors made stone tools long before *Homo sapiens* appeared about 200,000 years ago. The earliest method, the Oldowan industry, dates to at least 2.3 million years ago, and the earliest direct evidence of tool use, from Ethiopia, dates to 2.5 million years ago. Stone toolmaking advanced through the prepared-core technique (about 300,000 years ago) and pressure flaking (beginning about 40,000 years ago) to the polished stone tools of the Neolithic. [[Smelting|Smelting]] of copper is believed to have begun when pottery kilns reached sufficiently high temperatures. In the [[Bronze Age]], bronze improved on stone because of its strength and ductility and because it could be cast in molds; the [[Iron Age]] is defined by the widespread making of weapons and tools from iron and steel. Several of the six classic simple machines were invented in [[Mesopotamia]], where the potter's wheel, the first wheel and axle mechanism, appeared in the 5th millennium BC. Egyptian papyrus paper and pottery were mass-produced and exported across the Mediterranean basin.

Papermaking, a 2nd-century Chinese technology, reached the Middle East in the 8th century and Europe through Spain; a paper mill was established in Sicily in the 12th century. The blast furnace came into widespread use in France in the mid 15th century because of cannon casting. The stocking frame, invented in 1598, raised a knitter's output from 100 to 1000 knots per minute.

The [[Industrial Revolution]], from 1760 to the 1830s in Europe and the United States, shifted production from hand methods to machines, with steam and water power, machine tools, and the mechanized factory system. Textiles dominated in employment, output value, and capital invested, with mechanized spinning beginning in Britain in the 1780s. Mass production on [[Assembly line|assembly lines]] was pioneered by [[Henry Ford]] in 1913, followed by automation and digital integration.

Manufacturing accounts for about 15% of world GDP. China produced 32% of global manufacturing value added in 2024 and the United States 15%. Concerns include environmental costs, job losses in high-wage nations, and vulnerable concentrated supply chains, as shown by disruptions during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Interpretation of [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] holding that all possible measurement outcomes occur in different worlds. Chinese revolutionary and political theorist, founder of the People's Republic of China (PRC) and its paramount leader from 1949 to 1976. === KEY: Map_46f3ea
HEADWORD: Map
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Akkadian Empire | Cartography | Geographic information system | Map projection | Mercator projection
ENTRY:
Representation or abstraction of geographic reality, the product of [[Cartography|cartography]]. Maps serve navigation, scientific research, governance, and warfare. Scientists use maps to organize spatial data in geology, meteorology, seismology, and demographics; governments use them for census work, electoral districts, taxation, and disaster relief; militaries use them for mission planning, logistics, and intelligence analysis.

One of the earliest preserved maps, from the [[Akkadian Empire]] in modern Iraq, is inscribed on a clay tablet and dated to 2300 BCE. Ptolemy's *Geographia* offered systematic projections in the second century. Maps were important tools in the [[Age of Discovery]], when mariners used them to explore new lands. In the late 17th century thematic maps appeared, portraying data such as rainfall or population density rather than rivers, mountains, and cities. Computer cartography and [[Geographic information system|geographic information systems]], developed in the mid-20th century, allowed vast amounts of geographic data to be stored and dynamically displayed.

Designing a map involves selecting and arranging graphical elements so that it is informative, useful, and uncluttered. Because available data often exceeds what can be shown, designers apply generalization to decide what to display and what to omit. Detail depends on map scale, the ratio between a distance on the map and the corresponding ground distance. Every [[Map projection|map projection]] introduces characteristic distortions; the [[Mercator projection]], developed in 1569 for sailing along lines of constant bearing, preserves direction but inflates high-latitude landmasses.

Definitions of a map have widened over time. The cartographer Erwin Raisz in 1938 defined it as "a conventionalized picture of the Earth's pattern as seen from above, to which lettering is added for identification"; later definitions include digital and tactile media and depictions of things other than the Earth.

=== KEY: Map_projection_c9fa64
HEADWORD: Map projection
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Globe | Mercator projection | United Nations | United States
ENTRY:
Systematic method of representing the curved surface of a [[Globe|globe]] on a flat plane. Every projection of a sphere onto a plane distorts the surface in some way, so each one preserves some properties, such as area, shape, direction or distance, at the expense of others. The purpose of the map determines which projection suits it, and the study of projections centers on characterizing their distortions. The best-known is the [[Mercator projection]], which preserves angles but enlarges regions far from the equator; it was criticized through the 20th century and revived in the 21st for online mapping. Equal-area projections, such as the Gall–Peters projection, show the correct sizes of countries relative to one another but distort angles. In September 2026 the [[United Nations]] resolved to promote equal-area projections, notably the Equal Earth projection; 164 countries voted in favor, the [[United States]] voted against, and six abstained.

=== KEY: Marie_Curie_8ee140
HEADWORD: Curie, Marie
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Henri Becquerel | Nobel Prize | Pierre Curie | Polonium | Radium | Warsaw | World War I | X-ray
ENTRY:
Polish-born French physicist and chemist who pioneered research on radioactivity, a term she coined. Born Maria Salomea Skłodowska in [[Warsaw]], then in the Russian Empire, she studied at the clandestine Flying University, which admitted women, and in 1891 followed her elder sister Bronisława to Paris, where she earned her higher degrees. In 1895 she married [[Pierre Curie]], with whom she conducted her early research. She shared the 1903 [[Nobel Prize]] in Physics with Pierre and [[Henri Becquerel]] for their joint researches on the radioactivity phenomena Becquerel had discovered. Pierre died in a Paris street accident in 1906, and she became the first woman to hold a professorship at the University of Paris that year.

In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of the elements [[Radium|radium]] and [[Polonium|polonium]], by the isolation of radium and the study of its nature and compounds. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person to win twice, and the only person to win in two different scientific fields; she and Pierre were the first married couple to win. She named polonium after her native Poland and never lost her sense of Polish identity, teaching her daughters Polish and taking them on visits there.

Under her direction the first studies of treating neoplasms with radioactive isotopes were conducted. She founded the Curie Institute in Paris in 1920 and another in Warsaw in 1932; both remain major medical research centers. During [[World War I]] she developed mobile radiography units, known as *petites Curies*, to provide [[X-ray]] services to field hospitals.

She died in 1934 of aplastic anemia, likely caused by radiation exposure in her research and radiological work. In 1995 she became the first woman entombed in the Paris Panthéon on her own merits. The synthetic element curium is named in her honor.

=== KEY: Marilyn_Monroe_60b701
HEADWORD: Monroe, Marilyn
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
American actress and model, one of the most popular sex symbols of the 1950s and early 1960s and an emblem of the era's [[Sexual revolution|sexual revolution]]. Known for comic "blonde bombshell" roles, she starred in 1953 in *Niagara* and the comedies *Gentlemen Prefer Blondes* and *How to Marry a Millionaire*, which established her star image as a "dumb blonde." Her nude images appeared on the first issue of [[Playboy]] that year. Feeling typecast and underpaid by the studio, she founded her own production company, and her later films included *The Seven Year Itch* (1955) and *Some Like It Hot* (1959), for which she won a Golden Globe. Her marriages to [[Joe DiMaggio]] and playwright [[Arthur Miller]] ended in divorce. Her death from a barbiturate overdose was ruled a probable suicide.

=== KEY: Marina_Abramovi_ba7c2a
HEADWORD: Abramović, Marina
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Belgrade | Great Wall of China | Museum of Modern Art
ENTRY:
Serbian conceptual and performance artist, who calls herself the "grandmother of performance art." Her work explores body art, endurance, the relationship between performer and audience, and the limits of the body. Born in [[Belgrade]], she worked with the German artist Ulay on relational pieces, including walking the length of the [[Great Wall of China]] before their separation. Her 2010 retrospective *The Artist Is Present* at the [[Museum of Modern Art]] had her sit silently opposite visitors for up to 736 hours over three months. In 2007 she founded the Marina Abramović Institute, a nonprofit foundation for performance art.
