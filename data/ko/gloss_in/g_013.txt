=== KEY: Bangladesh_Liberation_War_ee6942
HEADWORD: Bangladesh Liberation War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bangladesh | Cold War | Dhaka | Genocide
ENTRY:
Armed conflict from 25 March to 16 December 1971 between the Pakistani military and Bengali nationalist forces, which led to the independence of [[Bangladesh]]. It began with Operation Searchlight, a Pakistani army crackdown on Bengali intellectuals, students and military personnel in [[Dhaka]], which initiated the [[Genocide|genocide]]. The Mukti Bahini, a guerrilla force of Bengali defectors, paramilitary units and civilians, fought back. India joined the war on 3 December 1971, and Pakistan surrendered in Dhaka on 16 December, in what remains the largest surrender of armed personnel since the Second World War. An estimated 10 million Bengali refugees fled to India, and 30 million were internally displaced. Estimates of civilian deaths range from 300,000 to 3 million; Bangladesh's official figure is 3 million, while independent assessments often place the toll lower. The war was a major episode in [[Cold War]] tensions, and most UN member states recognized Bangladesh as a sovereign nation in 1972.

=== KEY: Bangladesh_f78a77
HEADWORD: Bangladesh
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bangladesh Liberation War | Climate change | Dhaka | Gupta Empire | Maurya Empire | Mughal Empire | Partition of India | Sheikh Mujibur Rahman
ENTRY:
Country in South Asia, on the Bay of Bengal. Officially the People's Republic of Bangladesh, it has a population of almost 180 million in 148,461 sq km, making it the eighth most populous country and one of the most densely populated. [[Dhaka]] is the capital and largest city; Chittagong is the second-largest city and busiest port.

The territory was ruled by successive Hindu and Buddhist dynasties, including the [[Maurya Empire]] and [[Gupta Empire]], before the Muslim conquest of 1204. It later fell under the Bengal Sultanate and the [[Mughal Empire]], whose largest subdivision, Bengal, became a commercial center known for textiles. The Battle of Plassey in 1757 began nearly two centuries of British rule. After the [[Partition of India]] in 1947, the region became East Pakistan, the eastern wing of Pakistan.

After the Pakistani military launched Operation Searchlight, the [[Bangladesh Liberation War]] began; Bangladesh became sovereign on December 16, 1971, after Pakistani forces had carried out what is known as the Bangladesh genocide, with estimates of deaths up to three million. [[Sheikh Mujibur Rahman]] led the country until his assassination in 1975. Hussain Muhammad Ershad ruled as dictator through the 1980s. Since parliamentary democracy was restored in 1991, politics has been dominated by the Awami League under Sheikh Hasina and the Bangladesh Nationalist Party under Khaleda Zia. Hasina was ousted by a mass uprising in 2024, and an interim administration governed until the February 2026 election, which the Nationalist Party won.

Bangladesh is a unitary parliamentary republic with eight divisions and 64 districts. It hosts the world's largest mangrove forest and is exposed to floods, cyclones, and [[Climate change]].

=== KEY: Bank_eb45bd
HEADWORD: Bank
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basel | Bond (finance) | Interest (economics) | Money | Renaissance
ENTRY:
Financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and makes loans. Banks create demand deposits while lending, either directly or through capital markets and acting as payment agents through checking accounts and transfers. Banks issue new [[Money]] when they make loans.

Banks play an important role in financial stability and national economies, so most countries regulate them heavily. Most countries use fractional-reserve banking, under which banks hold liquid assets equal to only a portion of their current liabilities. Banks are also generally subject to minimum capital requirements under an international set of standards, the [[Basel]] Accords.

Banks borrow by accepting deposits and by issuing debt securities such as banknotes and [[Bond (finance)|bonds]]. They lend through advances on current accounts, installment loans, and investment in marketable debt securities. Activities range from personal, corporate, and private banking to investment banking, trade finance, and consumer finance, and customers reach them through branches, ATMs, and online and mobile banking. Revenue comes mainly from the spread between the [[Interest (economics)|interest]] paid on deposits and charged on loans, with fees and financial advice providing a more stable income stream.

Early practices of credit and lending date to the ancient world; one account places temple-based safekeeping and lending in Mesopotamia around 2000 BC. Banking in its modern sense evolved in the 14th century in the cities of [[Renaissance]] Italy, where banking dynasties such as the Medicis and Fuggers played a central role. The Bank of Amsterdam was founded in 1609 and the Bank of England in 1694. The oldest existing retail bank is Banca Monte dei Paschi di Siena (founded in 1472), and the oldest existing merchant bank is Berenberg Bank (founded in 1590).

=== KEY: Bankruptcy_90e728

ENTRY:
Legal process by which people or other entities unable to repay creditors may obtain relief from some or all of their debts. In most jurisdictions it is imposed by court order, often initiated by the debtor. Bankruptcy is not a synonym for insolvency, since an insolvent person may hold other legal statuses. === KEY: Bantu_expansion_3d17ba
HEADWORD: Bantu expansion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bantu languages | Cameroon | Central Africa | Lake Victoria | Nigeria | Southern Africa
ENTRY:
Series of prehistoric migrations of Proto-Bantu-speaking peoples from an original nucleus in West-Central Africa across [[Central Africa]], Eastern Africa and [[Southern Africa]]. The settlers absorbed, displaced and in some cases replaced the hunter-gatherer and pastoralist groups they met, and genetic evidence indicates admixture with local populations. Linguistic evidence supports the expansion; the linguistic core of the [[Bantu languages]] lies from north-western [[Cameroon]] to southern [[Nigeria]]. The expansion is believed to have taken place between about 6,000 and 1,500 years ago (approximately 4,000 BC to 500 AD). One branch reached central Angola around 500 BC, while the Eastern Stream reached west of [[Lake Victoria]] around 500 BC. The expansion spread farming, iron technology and pottery, and reached South Africa, probably as early as 300 AD.

=== KEY: Bantu_languages_39e32d

ENTRY:
Language family, or branch of the proposed Niger-Congo family, spoken across Central, Southern, Eastern and Southeast Africa by the [[Bantu peoples]]. The total number of distinct Bantu languages is estimated at between 440 and 680, depending on whether a variety is classed as a language or a dialect, and many borrow words from one another. Many Bantu languages are tonal and share a noun class system in which prefixes mark singular and plural forms.

Protestant tradition of Christianity that baptizes only believers, by total immersion. American cytogeneticist and Nobel laureate whose research on [[Maize|maize]] chromosomes led to the discovery of transposons. === KEY: Barcode_b93eae
HEADWORD: Barcode
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Morse code | QR code | Smartphone | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Optical, machine-readable representation of data.

Linear barcodes encode data in parallel lines whose widths, spacings and sizes vary, and are read by optical scanners. Later two-dimensional (2D) matrix codes use rectangles, dots or hexagons, and can be read by a digital camera running software, such as on a [[Smartphone|smartphone]]. The barcode was based on [[Morse code]] extended to thin and thick bars, and was patented in the U.S. in 1952 by Norman Joseph Woodland and Bernard Silver. Their first working system used [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] ink, which faded.

Barcodes became commercially successful after they were adopted to automate supermarket checkout. In 1973 the Uniform Grocery Product Code Council chose a vertical-bar design by George Laurer. The U.K. supermarket group Sainsbury's used one of the first successful systems in 1972, and in June 1974 a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio, scanned a Universal Product Code on a pack of Wrigley's chewing gum. [[QR code|QR codes]], a 2D type, rose in popularity in the second decade of the 2000s with growing smartphone ownership.

=== KEY: Bark_botany_c97e29
HEADWORD: Bark
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Resin | Rope | Tannin | Vascular cambium
ENTRY:
Outermost layers of the stems and roots of woody plants.

Bark refers to all tissues outside the [[Vascular cambium|vascular cambium]] and consists of inner bark and outer bark. In older stems the inner bark is living tissue, while the outer bark includes dead surface tissue and is also called the rhytidome. Bark is found only on woody plants; herbaceous plants and young stems lack it. Cork, derived from the cork cambium, is the outermost layer and protects the plant from parasites, herbivores, disease, dehydration and fire.

=== KEY: Base_chemistry_4e323f
HEADWORD: Base
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acid | Amine | Ammonia | Proton | Salt (chemistry) | Sodium hydroxide | Svante Arrhenius
ENTRY:
Chemical substance that reacts with acids. Three definitions are in common use, all agreeing that bases react with [[Acid|acids]].

In 1884 [[Svante Arrhenius]] proposed that a base dissociates in aqueous solution to form hydroxide ions (OH⁻), which combine with hydrogen ions from acids to form water. A base was therefore a metal hydroxide such as NaOH or Ca(OH)₂. In the more general Brønsted–Lowry theory (1923), a base is a substance that accepts hydrogen cations (H⁺), that is, [[Proton|protons]]. This includes aqueous hydroxides and other bases such as [[Ammonia]] (NH₃) and its organic derivatives, the [[Amine|amines]]. In Lewis theory, a base is an electron-pair donor; this is more general still, since the Lewis acid need not be a proton and can be a molecule such as boron trifluoride (BF₃).

In water, bases yield solutions with a pH above 7.0 at standard conditions. A soluble base that releases OH⁻ ions quantitatively is called an alkali. Basic solutions feel slippery, taste bitter, turn red litmus paper blue, and conduct electricity. Concentrated or strong bases are caustic to organic matter and react violently with acids. Strong bases such as [[Sodium hydroxide]] ionize completely in water, while weak bases ionize only partially. A reaction between aqueous solutions of an acid and a base is called neutralization and produces water and a [[Salt (chemistry)|salt]].

The term base was first used in 1717 by Louis Lémery as a synonym for the Paracelsian term matrix. Its modern meaning is usually attributed to G.-F. Rouelle, who in 1754 defined a neutral salt as the product of an acid with any substance capable of serving as a base for it.

Species of [[Shark|shark]] (*Cetorhinus maximus*), the second-largest living fish after the [[Whale shark]]. Basking sharks typically reach 7.9 m (26 ft) in length, and some grow more than 10 m (33 ft). A slow-moving filter feeder, it feeds at the surface, which gives the species its common name. === KEY: Basque_language_90273d

ENTRY:
Language of the [[Basques]], Europe's only known language isolate. Basque has 806,000 speakers, of whom 93.7% (756,000) are in the Spanish Basque Country and 6.3% (51,000) in France. Under [[Francisco Franco|Franco]]'s regime, Basque was suppressed in education and publishing, and newborns could not be registered under Basque names. Basque grammar is agglutinative with ergative-absolutive alignment, markedly different from that of [[Indo-European languages]].

=== KEY: Basques_f2feec

ENTRY:
Southwestern European ethnic group native to the Basque Country, a region around the western end of the [[Pyrenees]] on the coast of the [[Bay of Biscay]]. Basques are characterized by the [[Basque language]], a common culture and shared genetic ancestry with the ancient Vascones and Aquitanians, and are considered among the last remaining direct descendants of [[Neolithic]] European populations. In Basque, people call themselves *euskaldunak*; the neologism *euskotar*, coined in the 19th century, denotes a Basque person whether or not that person speaks the language. Although genetically distinctive in some respects because of isolation, Basques are very typically European in their Y-DNA and mtDNA sequences, which are widespread across the western half of Europe.

Strait separating [[Tasmania]] from the Australian mainland, specifically the coast of [[Victoria (state)|Victoria]]. === KEY: Bass_guitar_c35c39

Lowest-pitched member of the [[Guitar|guitar]] family, also called the electric bass. It resembles an [[Electric guitar|electric guitar]] but has a longer neck and scale length. Most models have four strings tuned E, A, D and G, an octave below the four lowest strings of a guitar, though five-, six- and other multi-string models exist. Double-reed [[Woodwind instrument|woodwind]] instrument in the tenor and bass ranges. It is usually made of wood, composed of six pieces, and is non-transposing. === KEY: Bat_2d5b3d

ENTRY:
Winged mammal of the order Chiroptera, the only mammals capable of true and sustained flight. Bats are more agile in flight than most birds, using long, spread-out digits covered by a thin membrane. The smallest bat, Kitti's hog-nosed bat, has a mass of 2 g, while the largest, the giant golden-crowned flying fox, weighs 1.5 kg (3.3 lb) and has a wingspan of 1.6 m. Traditionally divided into [[Megabat|megabats]] and [[Microbat|microbats]], most bats are insectivores, while others eat fruit or nectar and a few, such as [[Vampire bat|vampire bats]], feed on blood. Bats are important [[Pollination|pollinators]] and seed dispersers, and they control insect populations. As natural reservoirs of pathogens such as [[Rabies]], they can pose risks to humans.

=== KEY: Battering_ram_d0f768
HEADWORD: Battering ram
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Assyria | Cannon | Fortification | Middle Ages
ENTRY:
Siege engine designed to break open the masonry walls or wooden gates of [[Fortification|fortifications]]. In its simplest form it was a large, heavy log carried by several people and driven against an obstacle. Later rams were encased in an arrow-proof, fire-resistant canopy on wheels, with the log swung from chains or ropes. Rams were effective because stone and brick were weak in tension and cracked under repeated blows until a breach appeared. An [[Assyria|Assyrian]] ram from the 9th century BC shows defenders trying to set one alight. The introduction of siege [[Cannon|cannons]] in the later [[Middle Ages]] ended the use of traditional rams, although smaller hand-held versions are still used by law enforcement and military personnel to break open locked doors. A capped ram, with an iron or steel head, did more damage than an uncapped one but was heavier to carry.

=== KEY: Battery_crime_dd5f45

Criminal offense involving the unlawful intentional infliction of harmful or offensive physical contact with another person without consent. It may be simple or aggravated, and is distinct from [[Assault|assault]], which is the act of creating reasonable fear or apprehension of such contact. Battery is a specific [[Common law|common law]] offense, and under American common law it is any unlawful or unwanted touching of another person. === KEY: Battle_747d99
HEADWORD: Battle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl von Clausewitz | Trench warfare | War | World War I
ENTRY:
Combat in warfare between opposing military units, and a series of military engagements well defined in duration, area and force commitment. A [[War|war]] usually consists of multiple battles, and an engagement with limited commitment and no decisive result is called a skirmish. The German strategist [[Carl von Clausewitz]] stated that the employment of battles to achieve the object of war was the essence of strategy. A battle ends in victory when one side forces the other to abandon its mission and surrender, routs it or annihilates it; a Pyrrhic victory ultimately favors the defeated party. Until the 19th century most battles lasted part of a day, but [[Trench warfare|trench warfare]] in [[World War I]] lengthened battles to days and weeks. The word is a loanword from Old French bataille, first attested in 1297.

=== KEY: Battle_axe_fb66bc
HEADWORD: Battle axe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axe
ENTRY:
[[Axe|Axe]] designed specifically for combat, with a blade more akin to a cleaver than to a wood axe. Battle axes were designed to cut limbs rather than wood, so their slightly narrow, slicing blades facilitated deep wounds, and their lighter weight made them quick to bring to bear. Axes designed for warfare ranged from just over 0.5 to 3 kg (1 to 7 lb) in weight and from just over 30 cm (1 ft) to upwards of 150 cm (5 ft) in length. Many were used one-handed, while larger examples were two-handed. Scandinavian warriors used them as a stock weapon from the 8th to the 11th century, including the bearded axe, whose trailing lower blade could catch an opponent's shield. Hardwood handles were reinforced with metal bands called langets.
