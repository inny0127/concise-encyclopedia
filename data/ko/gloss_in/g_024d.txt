=== KEY: Charlemagne_d481cb
HEADWORD: Charlemagne
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Aachen | Constantinople | Franks
ENTRY:
King of the Franks (r. 768–814), King of the Lombards from 774, and first emperor of the Carolingian Empire from 800. He united most of Western and Central Europe and was the first recognized emperor to rule from the west since the fall of the Western Roman Empire about three centuries earlier.

The eldest son of Pepin the Short and Bertrada of Laon, he became king of the [[Franks]] in 768 with his brother Carloman I and sole ruler three years later. Continuing his father's policy, he became the chief defender of the papacy and ended Lombard rule in northern Italy in 774. His campaigns brought Bavaria, Saxony, and northern Spain under his rule. He spread Christianity into conquered lands, sometimes by force: the Saxon Wars involved mass executions, including the Massacre of Verden, and forced baptisms.

On Christmas Day 800 he was crowned emperor in Rome by Pope Leo III. Historians debate the coronation's significance, but it brought him into conflict with the Eastern Roman Empire in [[Constantinople]], and he is considered the forerunner of the Holy Roman Emperors. He carried out reforms in administration, law, education, military organization, and religion. His reign began a period of cultural activity known as the Carolingian Renaissance.

He died in 814 and was buried in the Palatine Chapel, now part of Aachen Cathedral, in [[Aachen]], his imperial capital. His empire fragmented after his death among his heirs. Many historians call him the 'Father of Europe,' and several European states and royal houses claim him as a founding figure.

=== KEY: Charles_A_Beard_937a99
HEADWORD: Beard, Charles A.
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | Columbia University | Constitution of the United States | World War I
ENTRY:
American historian and political scientist, known for the economic interpretation of history. Beard taught at [[Columbia University]] and wrote mainly in the first half of the 20th century. In *An Economic Interpretation of the [[Constitution of the United States|U.S. Constitution]]* (1913), he argued that the framers were motivated more by economics than by philosophical principles. The book has been criticized for its methodology and conclusions, but it prompted a wide-ranging reinterpretation of early American history. Beard resigned from Columbia in 1917 amid disputes over academic freedom during [[World War I]]. With his wife, Mary Ritter Beard, he wrote multivolume histories such as *The Rise of American Civilization* (1927). His reputation declined during the [[Cold War]], when most U.S. historians dropped analyses of economic class conflict, but scholarly interest revived after it ended.

French and Armenian singer-songwriter, actor and diplomat. === KEY: Charles_Babbage_2a5ec9
HEADWORD: Babbage, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Ada Lovelace | Cambridge | Polymath | Punched card
ENTRY:
English mathematician, philosopher, inventor and mechanical engineer. Babbage is credited with inventing the first mechanical computer, the difference engine, and is often called the "father of the computer". His analytical engine, programmed with [[Punched card|punched cards]] using a principle borrowed from the Jacquard loom, contained the essential ideas of the modern computer, though he died before many of his designs were completed. [[Ada Lovelace]] wrote the first algorithms for it. He also designed the first computer printers. In 1991 a functioning difference engine was built from the original plans, indicating that his machine would have worked. Babbage was Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at [[Cambridge]] from 1828 and is described as pre-eminent among the [[Polymath|polymaths]] of his century.

=== KEY: Charles_Baudelaire_81fa5f

ENTRY:
French poet, essayist, translator and art critic. His lyric collection *Les Fleurs du mal* (*The Flowers of Evil*) expresses the changing nature of beauty in Paris during Haussmann's renovation of the mid-19th century. Its publication in 1857 led to an obscenity trial, a fine and the censorship of six poems. He coined the term modernity to designate the fleeting experience of urban life and the responsibility of art to capture it. His prose poetry influenced [[Arthur Rimbaud]], Paul Verlaine and [[Stéphane Mallarmé]]. His translations of [[Edgar Allan Poe]] introduced psychological depth to French audiences, and his work profoundly shaped [[Symbolism (movement)|Symbolism]]. Baudelaire experimented with [[Opium]] in bohemian circles, and he was educated in [[Lyon]].

=== KEY: Charles_Darwin_09a208
HEADWORD: Darwin, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Charles Lyell | Evolution | Natural selection | On the Origin of Species | University of Cambridge | Westminster Abbey
ENTRY:
English naturalist, geologist, and biologist, known for establishing the theory of [[Evolution|evolution]] by [[Natural selection|natural selection]]. He was honored by burial in [[Westminster Abbey]].

Born in Shrewsbury, Shropshire, he neglected his medical studies at the University of Edinburgh. At the [[University of Cambridge]]'s Christ's College (1828–31) his passion for natural science grew. His five-year voyage on HMS *Beagle* (1831–36) established him as an eminent geologist; his observations supported [[Charles Lyell]]'s concept of gradual geological change. His travel journal, *The Voyage of the Beagle* (1839), made him a popular author, and *The Structure and Distribution of Coral Reefs* (1842) was his first scientific work. Together with his work on barnacles, it won him the Royal Medal in 1853.

Puzzled by the distribution of the wildlife and fossils he had collected, he devised his theory of natural selection in 1838. In 1858, while he was writing it up, [[Alfred Russel Wallace]] sent him an essay outlining the same idea, prompting a joint presentation to the Linnean Society of London. Darwin set out his evidence in *[[On the Origin of Species]]* (1859). Later books included *Fertilisation of Orchids* (1862), *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex* (1871), and *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872).

By the 1870s the scientific community and a majority of the educated public had accepted evolution, though many favored explanations giving natural selection only a minor role. Broad consensus on natural selection as the basic mechanism came with the modern evolutionary synthesis of the 1930s to the 1950s.

=== KEY: Charles_Dickens_1aa60e
HEADWORD: Dickens, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: A Christmas Carol | Great Expectations | Victorian era
ENTRY:
English writer and journalist. He created some of literature's best-known fictional characters and is regarded by many as the greatest novelist of the [[Victorian era]]. His literary success began with the 1836 serial publication of *The Pickwick Papers*, and within a few years he was an international literary celebrity known for his humor, satire and observation of character and society. His novels, most published in monthly or weekly instalments, pioneered the serial publication of narrative fiction. *[[A Christmas Carol|A Christmas Carol]]* (1843) remains especially popular, and *[[Great Expectations|Great Expectations]]* and *Oliver Twist* are frequently adapted. *Bleak House* (1853), a satire on the judicial system, helped support a reformist movement that culminated in 1870s legal reform in England. He also campaigned for children's rights and education. The adjective *Dickensian* describes things reminiscent of his writings, such as poor social or working conditions and comically repulsive characters.

=== KEY: Charles_Hermite_ebe3fc
HEADWORD: Hermite, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Arthur Cayley | Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi | E (mathematical constant) | Niels Henrik Abel
ENTRY:
French mathematician. He is known for proving the transcendence of the number [[E (mathematical constant)|*e*]]. In 1842 he published a simple proof of [[Niels Henrik Abel]]'s proposition that equations of the fifth degree have no algebraic solution. In papers published in 1858, 1865 and 1866, he showed that roots of the quintic polynomial could be obtained using elliptic integrals. He corresponded with [[Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi]] on Abelian and elliptic functions. With [[Arthur Cayley]] and James Joseph Sylvester he developed the theory of invariants, and within that work he discovered the law of reciprocity. Elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1848, he became professor of mathematics at the University of Paris in 1869 and remained there until his death.

King of England, Scotland, and Ireland (r. 1660–85). ENTRY:
King of Spain (r. 1665–1700). The last member of the [[House of Habsburg]] to rule Spain, he experienced lengthy periods of ill health, which made the question of his succession central to European diplomacy. His death without children led to the [[War of the Spanish Succession|War of the Spanish Succession]] (1701–14). Either leading candidate acquiring an undivided Spanish Empire threatened the European balance of power, and war followed.

=== KEY: Charles_IV_Holy_Roman_Emperor_92a389
HEADWORD: Charles IV
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bohemia | Holy Roman Empire | Prague
ENTRY:
Holy Roman Emperor (r. 1355–78). Born Wenceslaus in [[Prague]], he was the eldest son of John of Bohemia and inherited the County of Luxembourg from his father. He was elected King of the Romans in 1346 and became King of [[Bohemia]] that year. He was crowned King of Italy and Holy Roman Emperor in 1355 and, after his coronation as King of Burgundy at Arles in 1365, became the crowned ruler of all the kingdoms of the [[Holy Roman Empire]]. He founded Charles University in 1348 and made Prague a major European cultural and political hub. The Golden Bull of 1356 marked a structural change in imperial politics. Several aspects of his legacy remain contentious. The image of him as a wise, pious, peace-loving king, partly constructed by Charles himself, has proved influential.

=== KEY: Charles_I_of_England_698ddb
HEADWORD: Charles I
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Constitutional monarchy | English Civil War | Puritans
ENTRY:
King of England, Scotland, and Ireland (r. 1625–49). He believed in the divine right of kings and quarrelled with Parliament over taxation levied without its consent. His religious policies and his marriage to a Catholic, Henrietta Maria of France, generated mistrust from [[Puritans]] and Scottish Covenanters. His attempt to force the Church of Scotland to adopt high Anglican practices led to the Bishops' Wars, which strengthened the English and Scottish parliaments. From 1642 he fought Parliament's armies in the [[English Civil War]]. After his defeat in 1645 he surrendered to a Scottish force. He refused demands for a [[Constitutional monarchy|constitutional monarchy]] and was tried, convicted and executed for high treason. The monarchy was abolished and a republic established until its restoration in 1660 under his son Charles II.

[[Hong Kong]] electrical engineer. Born in Shanghai, he studied electrical engineering in London. === KEY: Charles_Lindbergh_5761e1
HEADWORD: Lindbergh, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Kidnapping | National park | Paris | Pulitzer Prize | Time (magazine) | Transplantation (medicine) | World War II
ENTRY:
American aviator, military officer, and author. In 1927 he made the first solo nonstop flight from New York to [[Paris]] in the *Spirit of St. Louis*, covering about 3,600 miles (5,800 km) in over 33 hours. The feat brought him worldwide fame, and [[Time (magazine)|*Time*]] named him its first Man of the Year. In 1932 his infant son was kidnapped and murdered, prompting the U.S. to make [[Kidnapping|kidnapping]] across state lines a federal crime. Before the United States entered [[World War II]], he opposed American intervention. His statements about Jews and race led many to believe he was a Nazi sympathizer, though he never publicly stated support for the Nazis and condemned them several times. He associated with them on numerous occasions in the 1930s and also endorsed eugenic selective breeding. He later became a [[Pulitzer Prize|Pulitzer Prize]]-winning author and helped establish [[National park|national parks]] in the U.S.

=== KEY: Charles_Lyell_d8e3cc
HEADWORD: Lyell, Charles
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Anthropocene | Charles Darwin | Eocene | Georges Cuvier | James Hutton | Miocene | Natural selection | Pliocene
ENTRY:
Scottish geologist. His *Principles of Geology* (1830–33) presented to a wide audience the idea that the Earth was shaped by natural processes still operating today at similar intensities. The philosopher William Whewell called this gradualistic view "uniformitarianism" and contrasted it with the catastrophism championed by [[Georges Cuvier]]. Building on the work of [[James Hutton]], Lyell favored an indefinitely long age for the Earth. His division of the Tertiary period into the [[Pliocene]], [[Miocene]] and [[Eocene]] was highly influential. His separate period for human history, the "Recent," is widely cited as providing the foundations for the modern discussion of the [[Anthropocene]]. He was a close friend of [[Charles Darwin]] and contributed significantly to Darwin's thinking on the processes of evolution. In 1858 he helped arrange the simultaneous publication of papers by Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace on [[Natural selection|natural selection]], despite his personal religious qualms about the theory.

=== KEY: Charles_Martel_22cb7b
HEADWORD: Charles Martel
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Charlemagne | Feudalism | Francia | Franks | Gaul | Umayyad Caliphate | Western Europe
ENTRY:
Frankish military and political leader. Known by the sobriquet "Martel," meaning "the Hammer," he was Duke and Prince of the [[Franks]] and Mayor of the Palace, and de facto ruler of the Franks from 718 until his death. He restored centralized government in [[Francia]] and campaigned until the Franks were the undisputed masters of all of [[Gaul]]. He gained a victory against an invasion of Aquitaine by the [[Umayyad Caliphate]] at the Battle of Tours, which curbed the spread of Islam in [[Western Europe]]. He is also traditionally credited with a role in developing the Frankish system of [[Feudalism|feudalism]]. At the end of his reign he divided Francia between his sons, Carloman and Pepin, and Pepin became the first king of the Carolingian dynasty. Pepin's son [[Charlemagne]] extended the Frankish realms. Older historiography calls Charles illegitimate, but the line between wives and concubines was not clear-cut in 8th-century Francia.
