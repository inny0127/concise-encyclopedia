HEADWORD: Chalk
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Soft, white, porous sedimentary carbonate rock, a form of [[Limestone]]. It is composed mainly of [[Calcite|calcite]] from the shells of [[Plankton|plankton]] that settled on the sea floor. In Western Europe it formed during the Late [[Cretaceous]] and early Paleocene. Steep cliffs at Dover, on the [[Kent]] coast, mark where it meets the [[English Channel]]. Mined for quicklime, bricks and putty, chalk is also used to raise the pH of acidic soils.

=== KEY: Challenger_Deep_74ad25

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mariana Trench | Pacific Ocean | Seabed
ENTRY:
Deepest known point of Earth's [[Seabed|seabed]], at the southern end of the [[Mariana Trench]] in the western [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific]]. A 2021 study put its depth at 10,935 ± 6 meters (35,876 ± 20 feet). It was named after the British Royal Navy survey ship *HMS Challenger*, whose 1872–1876 expedition first located it. The first descent, made by the U.S. Navy's bathyscaphe *Trieste*, took place in January 1960. As of July 2022, 27 people had descended to the Challenger Deep.

=== KEY: Chamber_music_b041f6
HEADWORD: Chamber music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baroque | Classical music | Johann Sebastian Bach | Johann Wolfgang von Goethe
ENTRY:
Form of [[Classical music|classical music]] written for a small group of instruments, with one performer to each part. Traditionally it was written for a group that could fit in a palace chamber or a large room, and it usually excludes solo performance. For more than 100 years it was played mainly by amateurs in their homes, and it requires musical and social skills different from those of solo or orchestral playing. [[Johann Wolfgang von Goethe|Goethe]] described a string quartet as "four rational people conversing," an analogy for the way one instrument introduces a melody that others answer. In the [[Baroque]] era, trio sonatas gave equal importance to their three instruments, and works such as *The Art of Fugue* by [[Johann Sebastian Bach]] could be played on keyboard or by a string quartet. The string quartet, with two violins, viola and cello, became the most prominent form.

=== KEY: Chameleon_1a30c2
HEADWORD: Chameleon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camouflage | Florida | Hawaii | Insect | Lizard | Madagascar
ENTRY:
Family of Old World [[Lizard|lizards]], best known for color-changing [[Camouflage|camouflage]]. Chameleons have zygodactylous feet, a prehensile tail and independently mobile eyes, and they catch [[Insect|insects]] with projectile tongues. Typically arboreal, they live in habitats from rainforest to desert. The vast majority occur in Africa, and about half of the species are restricted to [[Madagascar]]; a few live across southern Asia as far east as India and Sri Lanka. Their color change arises from layers of skin cells, including iridophores containing guanine nanocrystals. Although the change is often described as camouflage, studies also link it to social communication. Chameleons have been introduced to [[Hawaii]] and [[Florida]].

=== KEY: Champa_23c852
HEADWORD: Champa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hanoi | Hinduism | Indian Ocean | Islam | Nguyễn dynasty | Vietnam
ENTRY:
Collection of independent Cham states along the coast of central and southern [[Vietnam]], from the 2nd century AD until 1832. Early Champa evolved from the seafaring Sa Huỳnh culture. [[Hinduism]], adopted through conflict and conquest of territory from neighboring Funan in the 4th century AD, shaped its art and culture, including red brick temples. Champa reached its height in the 9th and 10th centuries CE. Its trade networks linked the [[Indian Ocean]] with East Asia until the 17th century, when pressure from the Vietnamese polity centered at [[Hanoi]] caused a gradual decline. In 1832 Emperor Minh Mạng of the [[Nguyễn dynasty]] annexed the remaining territories. Many Cham people now adhere to [[Islam]], a conversion that began in the 10th century.

=== KEY: Chanakya_79de27
HEADWORD: Chanakya
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Chandragupta Maurya | Maurya Empire
ENTRY:
Semi-legendary Brahmin adviser of [[Chandragupta Maurya|Chandragupta]], the first Mauryan emperor. Legendary narratives from traditions dating from the 4th to 11th century CE say that he helped Chandragupta rise to power and helped establish the [[Maurya Empire]], serving as chief adviser to Chandragupta and to his son Bindusara. He is conventionally identified with Kauṭilya, also called Vishnugupta, the author of the *Arthashastra*. That treatise is now thought, with high probability, to have been composed by several authors in the early centuries CE, and its attribution to Chanakya may have been meant to add prestige. There is no documented historical information about him.

=== KEY: Chandigarh_33d760
HEADWORD: Chandigarh
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Haryana | India | Le Corbusier | Pakistan | Punjab | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
Union territory and city in northwestern [[India]], serving as the shared capital of [[Punjab|Punjab]] and [[Haryana]]. It was established after the 1947 partition, when Lahore was ceded to [[Pakistan]] and East Punjab needed a new capital. A masterplan for the modernist city was prepared by Swiss-French architect [[Le Corbusier]], and its initial phases were built in the 1950s and 1960s. A 1966 reorganization created Haryana and Punjab, with Chandigarh as a union territory serving as their joint capital, and its status has remained disputed. The Capitol Complex, the culmination of Le Corbusier's career, was designated a [[World Heritage Site]] by UNESCO in 2016 as part of his buildings. The tri-city area has a combined population of over 1,611,770.

=== KEY: Chandragupta_II_c69375
HEADWORD: Chandragupta II
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bengal | Gupta Empire | Himalayas | Kalidasa | Samudragupta
ENTRY:
Gupta emperor (r. c. 375–415), also known as Vikramaditya. He continued the expansionist policy of his father [[Samudragupta]] through military conquests and marital alliances. Under his rule the [[Gupta Empire]] reached its zenith, directly controlling territory from the Oxus River in the west to the [[Bengal]] region in the east, and from the foothills of the [[Himalayas]] in the north to the Narmada River in the south. Historical evidence attests to victories over the Sassanids and the Western Kshatrapas and to the vassalization of the Hunas. Modern scholars generally identify him with King Chandra of the Delhi iron pillar inscription. A devout Vaishnava who tolerated other faiths, he was a patron of learning, and his court is said to have hosted nine scholars known as the Navaratnas. The poet [[Kalidasa]] may have been his court poet. The Chinese pilgrim Faxian, who visited India during his reign, suggests that he ruled a peaceful and prosperous kingdom.

=== KEY: Chandragupta_Maurya_a70b58
HEADWORD: Chandragupta Maurya
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Ashoka | Bihar | Chanakya | Magadha | Maurya Empire | Seleucus I Nicator
ENTRY:
Founder and first emperor of the [[Maurya Empire]] (r. c. 320–c. 298 BCE), based in [[Magadha]] (present-day [[Bihar]]). After Alexander the Great's Indian campaign, he defeated the unpopular Nanda dynasty and established his own dynasty. He also raised an army against the Greeks and took control of the eastern Indus Valley. According to the play *Mudrarakshasa*, his mentor [[Chanakya]] later became his minister. Through a dynastic marriage alliance with [[Seleucus I Nicator]] c. 305–303 BCE, he expanded into the western Indus Valley and possibly eastern Afghanistan. The Maurya Empire reached its peak under his grandson [[Ashoka]]. There are no historical facts about his origins and early life; the narrative of his reign rests on fragments of Greek and Roman sources and on Indian religious texts written centuries after his death.

ENTRY:
City (pop., 2020: 10,047,914), capital of [[Hunan]] Province, central China. Located on the lower reaches of the Xiang River, it has more than 2,400 years of urban history; its name first appeared in the pre-Qin *Yi Zhou Shu*. ENTRY:
Archipelago in the [[English Channel]], off the coast of [[Normandy]], France. The two major islands, [[Jersey]] and [[Guernsey]], make up 99% of the population and 92% of the area. === KEY: Channel_Tunnel_78bfd5
HEADWORD: Channel Tunnel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: English Channel | Eurostar | Great Britain | High-speed rail | William Ewart Gladstone
ENTRY:
Undersea rail tunnel linking England and France beneath the [[English Channel]]. Opened in 1994, the 50.46 km tunnel is the only fixed link between [[Great Britain]] and the European mainland. Its undersea section, 37.9 km long, is the longest underwater section of any tunnel in the world. Trains are limited to 160 km/h, although the tunnel was designed for 200 km/h. It is operated by Getlink (formerly Eurotunnel) and used by [[Eurostar]] trains, LeShuttle road services and freight trains, and it connects to [[High-speed rail|high-speed]] lines in France and the United Kingdom. Proposals date back to 1802, but national security concerns delayed development. In 1865 a deputation proposed the idea to Chancellor of the Exchequer [[William Ewart Gladstone]]. The modern project, begun by Eurotunnel in 1988, cost £4.65 billion. Service has been disrupted by technical faults, fires, severe weather and unauthorized access by migrants near Calais.

=== KEY: Chanterelle_e07060
HEADWORD: Chanterelle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Eurasia | French cuisine | North America
ENTRY:
Common name for several species of fungi in the genera *Cantharellus*, *Craterellus*, *Gomphus* and *Polyozellus*. The fruiting bodies are orange, yellow or white, meaty and funnel-shaped, with rounded, forked folds on the underside that run down the stipe. Many species emit a fruity aroma and have a mildly peppery taste. Chanterelles grow in forested areas of [[Eurasia]], [[North America]] and [[Africa]]. They gained popularity as an edible mushroom in the 18th century through their inclusion in [[French cuisine]]. The name derives from the Greek *kantharos*, meaning "tankard" or "cup", a reference to their shape. DNA analysis showed that the yellow chanterelles of western North America, once classified as *C. cibarius*, are a group of related species. The false chanterelle (*Hygrophoropsis aurantiaca*) has true gills and is edible but, according to *Edible Wild Mushrooms of North America*, not especially tasty.

=== KEY: Chaos_theory_380a98
HEADWORD: Chaos theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benoit Mandelbrot | Brazil | Butterfly effect | Fractal | Meteorology | Texas | Tornado
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics concerned with deterministic systems that are highly sensitive to initial conditions. Chaotic systems follow fixed laws, so their future is fully determined by their initial conditions, yet small differences in those conditions, such as measurement or rounding errors, can yield widely diverging outcomes, making long-term prediction impossible in general. The [[Butterfly effect]] describes this sensitivity: a butterfly flapping its wings in [[Brazil]] can cause or prevent a [[Tornado|tornado]] in [[Texas]]. Edward Lorenz summarized the theory as: "When the present determines the future but the approximate present does not approximately determine the future." Chaotic behavior occurs in fluid flow, heartbeat irregularities, weather and climate. The theory has applications in [[Meteorology]], economics, ecology and computer science. Work by [[Benoit Mandelbrot]] linked chaos to [[Fractal|fractal]], self-similar patterns.

=== KEY: Chaparral_1b241a
HEADWORD: Chaparral
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baja California | California | Chile | Mediterranean climate
ENTRY:
Shrubland plant community found primarily in [[California]], southern Oregon and northern [[Baja California]]. It is shaped by a [[Mediterranean climate]] of mild wet winters and hot dry summers, and by infrequent, high-intensity crown fires. Many shrubs have hard, evergreen leaves. Mature stands form dense thickets that are flammable in late summer and autumn. Conservation International considers chaparral a biodiversity hotspot. According to the California Academy of Sciences, Mediterranean shrubland contains more than 20% of the world's plant diversity. Similar communities occur in central [[Chile]], the South African Cape Region and Western Australia.

=== KEY: Characiformes_7261bd
HEADWORD: Characiformes
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central America | Piranha | South America
ENTRY:
Order of ray-finned fishes comprising the characins and their allies. It includes more than 2,000 described species, among them the [[Piranha|piranha]] and the tetras popular in aquaria. Most characins are small shoaling forage fish, but a few, such as the goliath tigerfish, golden dourado and traíra, exceed 1 m in length. They are most diverse in the Neotropics, where they live in lakes and rivers throughout most of [[South America]] and [[Central America]], while at least 209 species occur in Africa. Characins possess a Weberian apparatus, a series of bony parts connecting the swim bladder and inner ear, and their bodies are almost always covered in well-defined scales.

=== KEY: Character_arts_60bec9
HEADWORD: Character
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: August Strindberg | Carl Jung | Film | Henrik Ibsen | Novel | Play (theatre) | William Shakespeare
ENTRY:
Person or being in a narrative, such as a [[Novel|novel]], [[Play (theatre)|play]] or [[Film|film]]. A character may be entirely fictional or based on a real person. The English word, derived from the Ancient Greek for an engraved stamp or mark, dates from the Restoration but became widely used after its appearance in *Tom Jones* (1749). Characters guide readers through stories, and a character's status is defined by its network of relations with other characters. Authors may base characters on real people, as George Orwell does in *Animal Farm*, where Soviet revolutionaries are portrayed as pigs, or draw on archetypes and stock characters. [[Carl Jung]]'s archetypes have served as a basis for character traits, and [[William Shakespeare]] built John Falstaff on the stock figure of the boastful soldier. In *Hedda Gabler* (1891) by [[Henrik Ibsen]] and *Miss Julie* (1888) by [[August Strindberg]], characters represent positions in class and gender relations.

=== KEY: Character_encoding_be02e4
HEADWORD: Character encoding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ASCII | Morse code | Telegraphy | UTF-8 | Unicode | World Wide Web
ENTRY:
Convention of using numeric values to represent the characters of a writing script. The numerical values are called code points, which together form a code space or code page. Early encodings originated with [[Telegraphy|telegraphy]]: [[Morse code]], introduced in the 1840s, used a system of four symbols, and the five-bit Baudot code was created in 1870. Later encodings include [[ASCII]], ISO/IEC 8859 and [[Unicode]] encodings such as [[UTF-8]] and UTF-16. Unicode, first published in 1991, assigned 159,801 characters from 172 scripts as of version 17.0 in 2025, and has replaced most earlier encodings. UTF-8 is used by 99.0% of surveyed web sites, as of September 2026, making it the most popular encoding on the [[World Wide Web]].

=== KEY: Charcoal_545992
HEADWORD: Charcoal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Central Europe | Coal | Deforestation | Iron | South America | Steel
ENTRY:
Lightweight black residue made of carbon, produced by heating wood in minimal oxygen to drive off water and volatile constituents. In traditional charcoal burning, the heat comes from burning part of the starting material in a kiln with limited oxygen. Charcoal is used as fuel for cooking, heating and industrial processes, notably as a reducing agent in [[Iron|iron]] and [[Steel|steel]] production, and in pyrotechnics, cosmetics, horticulture and medicine. The operation was delicate and was left to professional charcoal burners, who often lived alone in huts. Historically, wood charcoal production contributed to [[Deforestation]], particularly in [[Central Europe]], and as wood grew scarce it gave way to [[Coal|coal]]. Modern retort methods yield higher efficiencies. Illegal and unregulated production is prevalent in [[South America]] and [[Africa]].

=== KEY: Chard_888be4
HEADWORD: Chard
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beetroot | Calorie | Carl Linnaeus | Cultivar | Iron | Magnesium
ENTRY:
Green leafy vegetable, also called silver beet, perpetual spinach or leaf beet. Chard is the same species as [[Beetroot|beetroot]], *Beta vulgaris*, and was first described in 1753 by [[Carl Linnaeus]]. Two [[Cultivar|cultivar]] groups are recognized: the Flavescens Group, or Swiss chard, has large leaf stalks often prepared separately from the blade, while the Cicla Group, or spinach beet, has a leafier blade. Leaf blades can be green or reddish, and stalks are white, yellow or red. Chard is a biennial, and most species produce three or more crops. It is low in [[Calorie|calories]], about 7 per cup of raw leaves, and rich in vitamins A, C and K, [[Iron|iron]] and [[Magnesium|magnesium]].

=== KEY: Charge_coupled_device_3eed21
HEADWORD: Charge-coupled device
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Integrated circuit | Photon
ENTRY:
[[Integrated circuit]] containing an array of linked capacitors that transfer electric charge to their neighbors, used in digital imaging. In a CCD image sensor, each pixel is a metal–oxide–semiconductor (MOS) capacitor that converts incoming [[Photon|photons]] into electron charges, which are then read out. CCDs remain widely used in professional, medical and scientific imaging, and in [[Astronomy|astronomy]], where high-quality image data are required. Since the late 2010s, however, CMOS sensors have become the dominant technology in digital cameras and have largely replaced CCD image sensors. Willard Boyle and George E. Smith invented the device in 1969 at Bell Labs while researching MOS technology and semiconductor bubble memory. Their work earned them the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics, shared with Charles K. Kao.

=== KEY: Chariot_88ba90
HEADWORD: Chariot
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Rome | Bronze Age | Cart | Cavalry | Hittites | Wheel
ENTRY:
Light, two-wheeled, horse-drawn vehicle with spoked wheels, driven by a charioteer. The critical invention enabling light chariots was the [[Wheel|spoked wheel]]. The oldest known chariots were found in burials of the Sintashta culture in modern Russia, dated to c. 1950–1880 BC, although some scholars argue these vehicles were [[Cart|carts]]. The [[Hittites]] were renowned charioteers, and their chariots carried three warriors: one steered, one served as archer and one as shield-bearer. Chariots played a major role in [[Bronze Age]] warfare, including the Battle of Kadesh (c. 1274 BC). After being superseded by [[Cavalry|cavalry]] in war, chariots were used for processions, games and races. In [[Ancient Rome]], a biga described a chariot drawn by two horses and a triga one drawn by three.

=== KEY: Charisma_a47211
HEADWORD: Charisma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Holy Spirit | Management | Max Weber | Paul the Apostle | Psychology
ENTRY:
Personal quality of magnetic charm, persuasion or appeal. The English word derives from the [[Ancient Greek]] *charisma*, meaning a favor freely given, from *charis*, grace. In Christian theology, charisma is a spiritual gift, an extraordinary power given by the [[Holy Spirit]]; [[Paul the Apostle]] introduced this meaning in 1 Corinthians. In sociology and political science, [[Max Weber]] described charismatic authority, which derives legitimacy from a leader's demonstrated heroic or supernatural capabilities during crises, in contrast to traditional and rational-legal authority. Charisma is studied in [[Psychology]] as a multifaceted trait, and in [[Management]] as a style of leadership. Empirical studies suggest that moderate levels may enhance innovation and unity, while extremes risk overconfidence and manipulation.
