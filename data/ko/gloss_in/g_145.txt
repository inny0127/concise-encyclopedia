=== KEY: Water_buffalo_9861bb
HEADWORD: Water buffalo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Assam | Cattle | Egypt | Indian subcontinent | Milk | Protein | Southeast Asia
ENTRY:
Large domesticated bovine of South and Southeast Asia, descended from the wild water buffalo (*Bubalus arnee*). Two types are recognized: the river buffalo of the [[Indian subcontinent]], which extends west to [[Egypt]], the Balkans and Italy; and the swamp buffalo, from [[Assam]] through [[Southeast Asia]] to the Yangtze Valley of China. The river type was domesticated about 6,300 years ago and the swamp type about 3,000 to 7,000 years ago. Water buffaloes were traded from the Indus Valley Civilisation to Mesopotamia in 2500 BC. They are especially suited to tilling rice fields, and their [[Milk|milk]] is richer in fat and [[Protein|protein]] than that of [[Cattle|dairy cattle]]. Kept today in Italy, the Balkans, Australia, the Americas and parts of Africa, feral populations have naturalized in several countries.

=== KEY: Water_cycle_66a962
HEADWORD: Water cycle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Condensation | Deforestation | Evaporation | Ocean | Precipitation | Urbanization
ENTRY:
Biogeochemical cycle of water movement on, above and below Earth's surface. Water moves among reservoirs of ice, fresh water, salt water and atmospheric water through processes including [[Evaporation|evaporation]], transpiration, [[Condensation|condensation]], [[Precipitation|precipitation]], sublimation, infiltration, surface runoff and subsurface flow. The total mass of water on Earth remains fairly constant, but its distribution among reservoirs varies with climate. The [[Ocean|ocean]] is the source of 86% of global evaporation. The cycle is driven by heat exchanged during phase changes, and ocean circulation carries heat from the tropics to the poles. It reshapes landscapes through weathering, erosion and deposition, and it is essential to most life and ecosystems. Human actions, including [[Deforestation|deforestation]], [[Urbanization|urbanization]] and groundwater extraction, alter the cycle, and [[Climate change|climate change]] is intensifying it.

=== KEY: Water_pollution_4de357
HEADWORD: Water pollution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Drinking water | Ecosystem | Irrigation | Pathogen | Pesticide | Sewage treatment
ENTRY:
Contamination of water bodies by human activity that impairs their use. Contaminants arrive from four main sources: sewage discharges, industrial activities, agricultural activities and urban runoff, including stormwater. Point sources, such as a storm drain, a [[Sewage treatment|wastewater treatment plant]] or an oil spill, have one identifiable cause, while non-point sources, such as agricultural runoff, are diffuse. Pollution takes many forms, including toxic substances such as oil, metals, plastics and [[Pesticide|pesticides]]; stressful conditions such as changes in pH, low oxygen and higher temperatures; and [[Pathogen|pathogenic organisms]]. Power plants and industrial manufacturers that use water as a coolant cause thermal pollution. Pollution degrades [[Ecosystem|aquatic ecosystems]] and spreads water-borne diseases when polluted water is used for [[Drinking water|drinking]] or [[Irrigation|irrigation]]. Control requires infrastructure, management plans and legislation, including better sanitation, sewage treatment and control of urban runoff.

=== KEY: Water_purification_fa6093
HEADWORD: Water purification
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Distillation | Drinking water | Filtration | Groundwater | Reservoir | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Removal of undesirable chemicals, biological contaminants, suspended solids and gases from water. Purification aims to produce water fit for a specific purpose, and most water is treated for [[Drinking water|drinking]]. Methods include physical processes such as [[Filtration|filtration]], sedimentation and [[Distillation|distillation]]; biological processes such as slow sand filters; chemical processes such as flocculation and chlorination; and [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light. Deep [[Groundwater|groundwater]] is usually free of pathogenic bacteria but may need its iron or manganese content reduced. [[Reservoir|Upland reservoirs]] usually have low bacteria levels, though many have low pH that requires adjustment. Low-lying rivers and canals have a significant bacterial load. Governments or international bodies set drinking water standards. A visual inspection cannot establish quality, and boiling or a household filter does not treat all possible contaminants. Only chemical and microbiological analysis provides the information needed to choose a method.

=== KEY: Water_supply_982463
HEADWORD: Water supply
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Aquifer | Desalination | Irrigation | Lake | River | United States
ENTRY:
Provision of water, usually through pumps and pipes, by public utilities, commercial organizations, communities or individuals. Public systems supply drinking water to populations worldwide. Water is drawn from [[Aquifer|groundwater]], [[Lake|lakes]] and [[River|rivers]], or from the sea through [[Desalination|desalination]], then treated, usually by chlorination. Service quality depends on continuity of supply, water quality, pressure and responsiveness to complaints. === KEY: Water_tower_7eb39a
HEADWORD: Water tower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gravity | Pipe (fluid conveyance) | Reservoir | Steam locomotive
ENTRY:
Elevated structure supporting a water tank. Built high enough to pressurize a potable water distribution system, a water tower also provides emergency storage for fire protection. It often operates with underground or surface [[Reservoir|service reservoirs]]. Because [[Gravity|gravity]] produces hydrostatic pressure, a water tower can supply water during power outages, but a pump is needed to refill it. The water level typically falls during peak hours and is refilled overnight, which also keeps the water from freezing in cold weather. Modern water towers developed in the mid-19th century, as steam pumping and better [[Pipe (fluid conveyance)|pipes]] for higher pressures became common. Water towers also supplied water stops for [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotives]] on railroad lines. Steel and reinforced or prestressed concrete are the most common materials, and a standard tower is about 40 m (130 ft) high.

=== KEY: Water_vapor_336456
HEADWORD: Water vapor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Cloud | Condensation | Evaporation | Fog | Greenhouse gas | Industrial Revolution | Methane
ENTRY:
Gaseous phase of water (H₂O), one state of water within the hydrosphere. It is produced by [[Evaporation|evaporation]] or boiling of liquid water, or by the sublimation of ice, and is continuously generated and removed by [[Condensation|condensation]]. Water vapor is transparent and less dense than most air constituents, so it triggers convection currents that can lead to [[Cloud|clouds]] and [[Fog|fog]]. It is especially abundant in Earth's atmosphere, where it acts as a [[Greenhouse gas]] and warming feedback, contributing more to the greenhouse effect than non-condensable gases such as [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and [[Methane|methane]]. Steam has been important for cooking and, since the [[Industrial Revolution]], a major component of energy production and transport. Water vapor is also present on every planet in the Solar System and in many astronomical objects, including comets and large asteroids.

=== KEY: Watercolor_painting_5c2fa9
HEADWORD: Watercolor painting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albrecht Dürer | Leather | Paleolithic | Paper | Papyrus | Renaissance
ENTRY:
Painting method using pigments suspended in a water-based solution. Watercolor refers to both the medium and the resulting artwork. The most common support is special [[Paper|paper]], often partly made of cotton, although [[Papyrus|papyrus]], [[Leather|leather]], silk, stone and vellum have also been used. === KEY: Waterfall_09c9ea
HEADWORD: Waterfall
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bedrock | Glacier | Hydropower | Ice shelf | Iceberg | Species
ENTRY:
Point in a river or stream where water flows over a vertical drop or series of steep drops. Waterfalls also form where meltwater drops over the edge of a tabular [[Iceberg|iceberg]] or [[Ice shelf|ice shelf]]. The most common formation occurs when a river flows over resistant [[Bedrock|bedrock]] before falling onto softer rock, which erodes faster, leading to an increasingly high fall. Waterfalls can also form where a river flowing into a [[Glacier|glacier]] continues down a valley after the glacier has receded, creating a hanging valley. They can be formidable barriers to navigation along rivers. Humans have traveled to, explored and named waterfalls since prehistory, and many cultures regard them as religious sites. Since the 18th century, waterfalls have drawn tourists and provided [[Hydropower|hydropower]], and since the mid-20th century they have become subjects of research, including studies of their impact on [[Species|species]] living in and around them.

=== KEY: Watermill_cef8ca
HEADWORD: Watermill
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Flour | Gear | Lumber | Paper | River | Textile | Vitruvius
ENTRY:
Mill powered by a water wheel or water turbine. Watermills drive processes such as milling, rolling or hammering needed to make [[Flour|flour]], [[Lumber|lumber]], [[Paper|paper]], [[Textile|textiles]] and many metal products. Vertical waterwheels drive machinery through [[Gear|gears]], while horizontal wheels need no gearing. Vertical wheels are further classed by where water strikes their paddles: undershot, overshot, breastshot and pitchback. Tide mills use the movement of the tide. The earliest evidence of a water-driven wheel appears in the treatises of Philo of Byzantium (ca. 280–220 BC), and the Roman engineer [[Vitruvius]] gave the first technical description of a watermill. Watermills alter [[River|river]] dynamics: channels tend to silt up behind them, and where mills have been removed, rivers cut deeper.

Rotating column of air over a [[Body of water|body of water]], usually a funnel-shaped cloud in contact with the water. === KEY: Watt_a11e76
HEADWORD: Watt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ampere | Industrial Revolution | International System of Units | James Watt | Newton (unit) | Volt
ENTRY:
SI derived unit of power, equal to one joule per second. Named for the Scottish inventor [[James Watt]], whose improvements to the steam engine became fundamental to the [[Industrial Revolution]], the watt measures the rate of energy transfer and is symbolized W. One watt is the rate at which work is done when a constant opposing force of one [[Newton (unit)|newton]] acts on an object moving at one meter per second. In electricity, it is the rate of electrical work when a current of one [[Ampere|ampere]] flows across a potential difference of one [[Volt|volt]]. === KEY: Wave_d911b3
HEADWORD: Wave
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earthquake | Field (physics) | Frequency | Gravitational wave | Maxwell's equations | Polarization (waves) | Simple harmonic motion | Sound
ENTRY:
Propagating dynamic disturbance, a change from equilibrium, of one or more quantities in a medium or field. Periodic waves oscillate repeatedly about an equilibrium value at some [[Frequency|frequency]]. Two identical periodic waves traveling in opposite directions superimpose to make a standing wave, which has nulls where the amplitude is small or zero. Viewed macroscopically, a wave is the dynamic response of a [[Field (physics)|field]] to effects that can only propagate at finite speed.

The two types most studied in classical physics are mechanical and electromagnetic waves. In a mechanical wave, stress and strain fields oscillate about a mechanical equilibrium, and the disturbance passes from particle to particle. Examples include [[Sound|sound]] waves, seismic waves generated by an [[Earthquake|earthquake]], surface waves and string vibrations. In an electromagnetic wave such as light, coupling between electric and magnetic fields sustains propagation according to [[Maxwell's equations]]. Electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum; by frequency they are called radio waves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. Other types include [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]], disturbances in spacetime, and plasma waves. Mechanical and electromagnetic waves transfer energy, momentum and information.

The simplest wave mathematically is a sinusoidal plane wave, in which the field undergoes [[Simple harmonic motion|simple harmonic motion]] at one frequency. A wave is transverse if the disturbance is perpendicular to the direction of propagation, and longitudinal if aligned with it. Electromagnetic plane waves are strictly transverse; sound waves in fluids are longitudinal. The orientation of the oscillation is the wave's [[Polarization (waves)|polarization]]. Wave speed relates to frequency and wavelength by v = fλ. In quantum mechanics, wave–particle duality means that entities such as electrons show both wave-like and particle-like behavior.

=== KEY: Wave_equation_7580d4
HEADWORD: Wave equation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acoustics | Electromagnetism | Fluid dynamics | Laplace operator | Light | Partial differential equation | Superposition principle
ENTRY:
Second-order linear [[Partial differential equation|partial differential equation]] describing waves and standing wave fields. It governs mechanical waves, such as water, sound and seismic waves, and electromagnetic waves, including [[Light|light]]. It arises in [[Acoustics|acoustics]], [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]] and [[Fluid dynamics|fluid dynamics]]. In its scalar form, the second time derivative of a displacement or conserved quantity u equals c² times the [[Laplace operator|Laplacian]] of u, where c is the wave's propagation speed. Because the equation is linear, sums of solutions are also solutions, a property called the [[Superposition principle|superposition principle]]. Physical solutions require initial conditions, and enclosed spaces with boundary conditions produce standing waves, analogous to the harmonics of musical instruments.

=== KEY: Wave_function_b2c301
HEADWORD: Wave function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | Hilbert space | Photon | Quantum mechanics | Schrödinger equation | Wave equation | Wave–particle duality
ENTRY:
Mathematical description of the quantum state of an isolated system in [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. Usually denoted by the Greek letter ψ (psi), a wave function is complex-valued and can be added and multiplied by complex numbers to form a [[Hilbert space]]. Under the Born rule, the squared modulus of a position-dependent wave function gives the probability density of finding a particle at a given place. Its squared modulus integrates to 1 over all the system's degrees of freedom, a condition called normalization. Only relative phase and magnitude are measurable; observable quantities are calculated by applying quantum operators. The [[Schrödinger equation]] determines how wave functions evolve over time. Because it is a type of [[Wave equation|wave equation]], the wave function behaves like other waves, which gives rise to [[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]]. Particles such as [[Electron|electrons]] and [[Photon|photons]] carry spin, which is included as a discrete degree of freedom.

=== KEY: Wave_interference_39f383
HEADWORD: Wave interference
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anti-reflective coating | Diffraction grating | Double-slit experiment | Sound | Thomas Young (scientist)
ENTRY:
Phenomenon resulting from the superposition of two waves. In physics, interference occurs when coherent waves combine by adding their intensities or displacements, with due consideration for their phase difference. Constructive interference, where crests meet crests, gives greater amplitude when waves are in phase; destructive interference, where crests meet troughs, lowers amplitude when they are out of phase. The term was used around 1800 by [[Thomas Young (scientist)|Thomas Young]] in developing his theories of acoustics and optics. Interference occurs with light, [[Sound|sound]], radio and surface water waves, and with matter waves. Examples include the [[Double-slit experiment]], [[Anti-reflective coating|anti-reflective coatings]] that minimize glare on lenses, and [[Diffraction grating|diffraction gratings]] that separate light wavelengths. In acoustics, interference explains the standing waves of musical instruments.

=== KEY: Wave_particle_duality_4fa343
HEADWORD: Wave–particle duality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Christiaan Huygens | Electron | Isaac Newton | J. J. Thomson | Louis de Broglie | Max Planck | Photon
ENTRY:
Concept in quantum mechanics that fundamental entities, such as [[Photon|photons]] and [[Electron|electrons]], show particle or wave properties depending on the experimental circumstances. Classical concepts of particle or wave cannot fully describe quantum objects. Light was first found to behave as a wave and later as a particle. [[Isaac Newton]] favored a corpuscular theory of light, while [[Christiaan Huygens]] took a wave view, and Thomas Young's interference experiments in 1801 supported the wave view. [[Max Planck]]'s formula for black-body radiation assumed discrete energy increments, and [[Albert Einstein]]'s 1905 interpretation of the photoelectric effect also used discrete energies. Electrons, which behaved as particles in experiments by [[J. J. Thomson]] and others, were later found to have wave behavior. In 1924 [[Louis de Broglie]] proposed that electrons and all matter could be considered waves, and in 1927 the Davisson–Germer experiment confirmed electron waves by scattering electrons from nickel surfaces.

=== KEY: Waveguide_0b8892
HEADWORD: Waveguide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: J. J. Thomson | Loudspeaker | Microwave | Microwave oven | Optical fiber | Radio wave
ENTRY:
Structure that guides waves by restricting the direction of energy transmission. Common types include acoustic waveguides, which direct sound; optical waveguides, which direct light; and radio-frequency waveguides, which direct electromagnetic waves such as [[Radio wave|radio waves]]. Without one, waves would spread into three-dimensional space and their intensity would fall according to the inverse square law. The original and most common type is a hollow conductive metal pipe carrying high-frequency radio waves, particularly [[Microwave|microwaves]]. [[Optical fiber|Optical fibers]] transmit light over long distances with low attenuation. In a [[Microwave oven]], a waveguide transfers power from the magnetron to the cooking chamber. Each waveguide has a cutoff wavelength set by its size and will not conduct longer wavelengths. Air ducts and horns guide sound in musical instruments and [[Loudspeaker|loudspeakers]]. The first structure for guiding waves was proposed by [[J. J. Thomson]] in 1893.

=== KEY: Wavelength_b25b27
HEADWORD: Wavelength
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetic radiation | Frequency | Speed of sound | Violet (color)
ENTRY:
Distance over which a wave's shape repeats. In physics and mathematics, wavelength is the distance between consecutive corresponding points of the same phase, such as adjacent crests, troughs or zero crossings. It is commonly designated by the Greek letter λ and applies to both traveling and standing waves. For a sinusoidal wave at fixed speed, wavelength is inversely proportional to [[Frequency|frequency]]. [[Electromagnetic radiation]] in free space travels at about 3×10⁸ m/s, so a 100 MHz radio wave has a wavelength of about 3 m. Visible light ranges from roughly 700 nm in deep red to 400 nm in [[Violet (color)|violet]]. The [[Speed of sound]] in air is 343 m/s, so audible sound, from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, has wavelengths between about 17 m and 17 mm. In a standing wave, the wavelength is twice the distance between nodes.

=== KEY: Weak_interaction_549f04
HEADWORD: Weak interaction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beta decay | CERN | Higgs boson | Nuclear fusion | Quark | Standard Model | W and Z bosons
ENTRY:
One of the four known fundamental interactions, acting between subatomic particles. The others are electromagnetism, the strong interaction and gravitation. The weak interaction, also called the weak force or weak nuclear force, is responsible for the radioactive [[Beta decay|beta decay]] of atoms and participates in nuclear fission and [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]]. First suggested in 1933, it reached its current form in the 1950s and 1960s, and Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics for electroweak theory.

In the [[Standard Model]], the weak interaction has three carriers, the W⁺, W⁻ and Z bosons ([[W and Z bosons]]), which were not discovered until 1983 at [[CERN]]. Their large mass, approximately 90 GeV/c², makes them short-lived and gives the force a very short effective range of around 10⁻¹⁷ to 10⁻¹⁶ m, less than the diameter of a proton. The force is termed weak because its field strength over a set distance is typically several orders of magnitude less than that of electromagnetism; its coupling constant lies between 10⁻⁷ and 10⁻⁶, against about 10⁻² for electromagnetism. The carriers' masses are explained in the Standard Model by the [[Higgs boson|Higgs mechanism]].

The weak interaction is unique in several respects. It is the only interaction that changes the flavor of [[Quark|quarks]] and leptons and the only one that violates parity symmetry. In beta-minus decay, a down quark in a neutron changes into an up quark, converting the neutron to a proton and emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. This conversion makes the weak interaction important in the fusion of hydrogen into helium in stars. Neutrinos interact only through gravity and the weak interaction.

=== KEY: Weapon_18c836
HEADWORD: Weapon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze Age | Cavalry | Chariot | Gunpowder | Knight | Nuclear weapon | Trebuchet
ENTRY:
Any implement or tool used to deter, threaten, inflict physical damage on, harm or kill. Weapons serve hunting, crime, law enforcement, self-defense and warfare. Ordinary objects such as rocks and bottles can serve as weapons, but many objects are designed for the purpose, from clubs and swords to firearms, tanks, artillery, missiles, chemical, biological and [[Nuclear weapon|nuclear weapons]]. In a broader sense a weapon may be anything used to gain a tactical, strategic, material or mental advantage over an adversary.

Weapons have driven cultural evolution, since users adapt to enemies' weapons in a continuing arms race. The earliest unambiguous weapons are the Schöningen spears, eight wooden throwing spears more than 300,000 years old.

Metal tools began with copper about 3,300 BC and were followed by the [[Bronze Age]] and its swords. The battering ram was in use by 2500 BC. Early iron swords were not superior to bronze ones; more important was the domestication of the horse and widespread use of spoked wheels by c. 2000 BC, which produced the light, horse-drawn [[Chariot|chariot]]. Chariots ceased to be militarily relevant by the 4th century BC. [[Cavalry]] developed once horses were bred to carry a human, and warships such as the trireme were in use by the 7th century BC.

Post-classical European warfare was dominated by [[Knight|knights]] supported by massed infantry, with infantry using spears, bows and later pikes. The introduction of [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]] from Asia at the end of the period revolutionized warfare: musketeers protected by pikemen came to dominate open battles, and the cannon replaced the [[Trebuchet|trebuchet]] as the dominant siege weapon. Firearms differ from earlier weapons in releasing energy from combustible propellants rather than from a counterweight or spring.

=== KEY: Weapon_of_mass_destruction_9b25b3
HEADWORD: Weapon of mass destruction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 2003 invasion of Iraq | Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki | Cold War | September 11 attacks | Soviet Union | The Holocaust | Tokyo subway sarin attack
ENTRY:
Biological, chemical, radiological, nuclear or other weapon that can kill or significantly harm many people. Originally coined for aerial bombing with conventional explosives during World War II, the term now usually refers to large-scale biological, chemical, radiological and nuclear weapons, and its scope is disputed and often more political than technical. In World War II the United States developed nuclear weapons and used them twice, in the [[Atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki|atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki]]. Nazi Germany's use of gases during the [[The Holocaust|Holocaust]] killed approximately three million people, the largest death toll from chemical weapons in history. During the [[Cold War]], the [[Soviet Union]] and the United States ran the largest nuclear, chemical and biological weapons programs in history. Claims by a US-led coalition that Iraq was maintaining such programs were a major justification for the [[2003 invasion of Iraq]].

=== KEY: Weather_1441df
HEADWORD: Weather
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate | Extratropical cyclone | Great Red Spot | Hadley cell | Jet stream | Milankovitch cycles
ENTRY:
State of the Earth's atmosphere at a specific place and time, described in terms of temperature, humidity, cloud cover and stability. On Earth most weather occurs in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere. Weather refers to day-to-day atmospheric conditions, whereas [[Climate|climate]] is their average over longer periods.

Weather is driven by differences in air pressure, temperature and moisture from place to place. These differences arise partly from the Sun's angle, which varies with latitude. The strong temperature contrast between polar and tropical air gives rise to the largest atmospheric circulations: the [[Hadley cell]], the Ferrel cell, the polar cell and the [[Jet stream|jet stream]]. Mid-latitude systems such as [[Extratropical cyclone|extratropical cyclones]] are caused by instabilities of the jet stream flow. Because Earth's axis is tilted relative to the ecliptic, sunlight arrives at different angles through the year, producing seasons, and annual surface temperatures usually range ±40 °C. Over thousands of years, changes in Earth's orbit affect the solar energy received and influence long-term climate ([[Milankovitch cycles]]). Surface temperature differences create pressure differences, and air moving from higher to lower pressure forms wind.

Weather forecasting applies science and technology to predict the state of the atmosphere at a future time and place. Numerical weather prediction, which began in the mid-20th century, has raised short-term reliability to approximately 90% accuracy for five-day outlooks, but the weather system is chaotic: small changes can grow into large effects, which limits predictability beyond about two weeks.

Jupiter's [[Great Red Spot]] is an anticyclonic storm known to have existed for at least 300 years.

=== KEY: Weather_forecasting_e44b85
HEADWORD: Weather forecasting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astrology | Meteorology | Pressure
ENTRY:
Application of science and technology to predict atmospheric conditions for a given location and time. People attempted informal weather prediction for thousands of years, and formal prediction since the 19th century. The Babylonians predicted weather from cloud patterns and [[Astrology|astrology]] as early as 650 BC. Forecasts are made by collecting data on the current state of the atmosphere, land and ocean and applying [[Meteorology|meteorology]] to project changes. Forecasts were once calculated manually from [[Pressure|barometric pressure]], current conditions and cloud cover; today computer models dominate, though people still choose the best model. Accuracy falls as the forecast range grows, because the atmosphere is chaotic and measurements are imperfect, and ensembles and model consensus help narrow the error. Weather warnings protect lives and property, and forecasts of temperature and precipitation are important to agriculture and commodity traders. In 2009 the United States spent approximately $5.8 billion on forecasting, with benefits estimated at six times that amount.

=== KEY: Weather_front_62c7e9
HEADWORD: Weather front
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air mass | Fog | Humidity | Precipitation | Thunderstorm
ENTRY:
Boundary separating [[Air mass|air masses]] that differ in density, wind, temperature and [[Humidity|humidity]]. Disturbed, unstable weather often develops along it. Cold fronts can bring bands of [[Thunderstorm|thunderstorms]] and cumulonimbus [[Precipitation|precipitation]] or be preceded by squall lines, while warm fronts are usually preceded by stratiform precipitation and [[Fog|fog]]. Some fronts produce no precipitation and little cloudiness, though there is invariably a wind shift. Cold fronts generally move from west to east and warm fronts poleward, although any direction is possible. Cold fronts move faster than warm fronts because the dense air behind them can lift the warmer air ahead. Occluded fronts combine the two, and stationary fronts stall. Where the density contrast has diminished, for example after air flows over a uniformly warm ocean, a front can degenerate into a shear line, separating regions of differing wind velocity.

=== KEY: Weathering_ff1b1b
HEADWORD: Weathering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Erosion | Mineral | Sedimentary rock | Soil
ENTRY:
Deterioration of [[Soil|soils]], rocks and [[Mineral|minerals]] through contact with water, atmospheric gases, sunlight and living organisms. Weathering occurs in situ, with little or no movement, and is distinct from [[Erosion|erosion]], which transports material by water, ice, wind, waves and gravity. Physical weathering breaks rock apart mechanically through heat, water, ice and wind, while chemical weathering involves reactions with water, atmospheric gases and biologically produced chemicals; water is the principal agent in both. Frost weathering is the most important form of physical weathering, and a growing body of work suggests that ice segregation, in which water migrates into ice lenses within rock, is more important than the long-favored frost wedging. Atmospheric oxygen, [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and organisms are also important. Weathered material combines with organic matter to form soil, and weathering, erosion and redeposition shape many landforms. [[Sedimentary rock]], a product of weathering, covers 66% of Earth's continents and much of the ocean floor.

=== KEY: Weaving_42db97
HEADWORD: Weaving
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Loom | Textile | Yarn
ENTRY:
Method of [[Textile|textile]] production in which two sets of [[Yarn|yarns]] are interlaced at right angles to form cloth. The longitudinal threads are the warp and the lateral threads the weft, also called woof or filling. The pattern of interlacing, called the weave, determines the cloth's character; most woven products use plain, satin or twill weave. === KEY: Welfare_spending_526534

Varroa mites and colony collapse disorder threaten it, and the IUCN Red List rated it Data Deficient as of 2025.

HEADWORD: Western imperialism in Asia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dutch East India Company | East India Company | Industrial Revolution | Macau | Silk Road
ENTRY:
Colonial and commercial domination of Asia by Western powers, from the 16th century. It originated in the 15th-century search for trade routes to India and Southeast Asia, prompted by Ottoman control of the [[Silk Road|Silk Road]]. The Portuguese reached India by way of the Cape of Good Hope in the 16th century, and the rival [[Dutch East India Company]] later eclipsed them, wresting Malacca, Ceylon and the Japan trade from them between 1640–60. After the Seven Years' War in 1763, the British eliminated French influence in India and made the [[East India Company]] the most important political force there. Before the [[Industrial Revolution]] of the 19th century, demand for porcelain, silk, spices and tea drove trade. The 1870s Long Depression provoked a scramble for new markets and the New Imperialism of direct colonial control. Formal colonial rule ended with the independence of Portuguese [[Macau]] in 1999.

=== KEY: Western_philosophy_766dfc
HEADWORD: Western philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Eastern philosophy | Heraclitus | Plato | Pythagoras | Socrates | Socratic method | Thales of Miletus
ENTRY:
Philosophical thought, traditions, and works of the Western world, beginning with the ancient Greek philosophy of the pre-Socratics. The word *philosophy* derives from the Ancient Greek *philosophía*, literally "the love of wisdom." Western philosophy is usually contrasted with other traditions such as [[Eastern philosophy]].

Ancient Western philosophy covered today's philosophical problems and also pure mathematics and natural sciences such as physics, astronomy, and biology; [[Aristotle]] wrote on all of these. The pre-Socratics were interested in cosmology and sought the *arche*, the first principle of the world. [[Thales of Miletus]] identified water as the arche, and his use of observation and reason leads many to call him the first philosopher. [[Pythagoras]] and his followers held that "all is number." Parmenides argued that the world is singular, unchanging, and eternal, while [[Heraclitus]] held that everything is in flux. Empedocles proposed four classical elements, and Leucippus and Democritus proposed atomism. The Sophists, teachers of rhetoric, generally promoted subjectivism and relativism.

The Classical period centers on [[Socrates]] and two generations of students. His critical approach, now called the [[Socratic method]], examined people's views on justice, beauty, truth, and virtue. Socrates wrote nothing himself; his disciples Plato and Xenophon recorded some of his conversations. Tried by the Athenian democracy for impiety and corrupting the youth, he was executed with poison hemlock. [[Plato]] founded the Platonic Academy and, like Socrates, identified virtue with knowledge, raising questions of epistemology.

Later phases include medieval integration of Aristotelian logic with Christian theology, early modern thought, Enlightenment empiricism, and divergence in the 19th and 20th centuries into analytic and continental traditions.

=== KEY: Western_world_588594
HEADWORD: Western world
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crusades | East–West Schism
ENTRY:
Nations of Europe, the Americas and Australasia, also called the West and the Occident. Ancient Greece and Rome are regarded as the birthplaces of Western civilization. A geographical concept of the West took shape in the 4th century, when Constantine divided the Roman Empire between the Greek East and the Latin West. The [[East–West Schism]] of 1054 made the division definitive, and the [[Crusades]] failed to win eastern trade routes but instead "discovered" the Americas. After European colonization of the Americas, the idea of a Western world emerged as an heir to Latin Christendom; the earliest Oxford English Dictionary reference is from 1586. Australia and New Zealand are included in modern definitions, while Russia has been seen both as part of the West and in contrast to it. From the late 1960s, parts of the Western world became notable for diversity due to immigration.

=== KEY: Westminster_Abbey_651e54
HEADWORD: Westminster Abbey
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Church of England | Elizabeth I | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
[[Church of England|Anglican]] church in the City of Westminster, London, formally the Collegiate Church of Saint Peter at Westminster. Coronations of 40 English and British monarchs have taken place there since 1066, and 18 monarchs are buried there. More than 3,300 people are buried or commemorated in the abbey, including poets, scientists and the Unknown Warrior. Construction of the present Gothic church began in 1245 under Henry III. The monastery was dissolved in 1559, and [[Elizabeth I]] made the church a royal peculiar, directly accountable to the sovereign. The abbey has been a [[World Heritage Site]] since 1987.

=== KEY: Wet_season_bf25cd
HEADWORD: Wet season
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cassava | Intertropical Convergence Zone | Maize | Malaria
ENTRY:
Period of the year when most of a region's average annual rainfall falls, also called the rainy or monsoon season. Under the Köppen classification, a tropical wet-season month has average precipitation of 60 mm (2.4 in) or more. Wet seasons occur in warmer months in tropical savanna and monsoon climates, where rain falls mainly in the late afternoon and early evening as the [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]] moves. Vegetation grows and fresh water quality improves, but soil nutrients diminish, erosion increases, and [[Malaria|malaria]] and dengue rise where high temperatures coincide. Food shortages are common, since the previous dry season's crops have yet to mature. Crops planted in the wet season include [[Cassava]], [[Maize]], groundnut, millet, rice and yam. Tropical rainforests have no wet season, because their rainfall is spread evenly through the year.

=== KEY: Wetland_a585e5
HEADWORD: Wetland
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon | Mangrove | Marsh | Swamp
ENTRY:
Semi-aquatic ecosystem whose ground is flooded or saturated with water, permanently or seasonally. Flooding makes the soil oxygen-poor, and wetland plants have roots adapted to these waterlogged conditions. Wetlands form a transitional zone between water bodies and dry land and exist on every continent except Antarctica, with freshwater, brackish or saltwater. [[Marsh]]es are dominated by herbaceous plants such as reeds and cattails, [[Swamp]]s by trees and shrubs, and [[Mangrove]] forests by salt-tolerant woody plants. Wetlands are among the most biologically diverse ecosystems. They purify water, stabilize shorelines and control floods, and they store [[Carbon|carbon]] but can emit methane. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment of 2005 found them more threatened than any other ecosystem on Earth. Since 1971, an international treaty has worked to protect "wetlands of international importance."
