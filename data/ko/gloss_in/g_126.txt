=== KEY: Somatosensory_system_b2de89
HEADWORD: Somatosensory system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Reflex | Sensory nervous system | Skin | Spinal cord
ENTRY:
Subset of the [[Sensory nervous system|sensory nervous system]] that detects touch, temperature, pain and body position. Its main functions are perceiving external and internal stimuli and regulating body position and balance (proprioception). It has been thought of as having two subdivisions: one for mechanosensory information, such as light touch, vibration, pressure and tension in the [[Skin]], and one for nociception, the detection of pain and temperature deemed harmful. Mechanoreceptors such as tactile corpuscles, which respond to pressure and vibration, are among the cutaneous receptors. Signals travel to the [[Spinal cord]], where they may drive a [[Reflex|reflex]], and to the brain for conscious perception. Neighboring neurons in the somatosensory cortex represent nearby areas of skin, forming a sensory homunculus. As of 2024, debate continued over the underlying mechanisms of the model.

=== KEY: Sonar_0ca3d6
HEADWORD: Sonar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Leonardo da Vinci | Reflection (physics) | Submarine | World War I
ENTRY:
Method of using sound propagation, usually underwater, to navigate, measure distances and detect objects. It stands for sound navigation and ranging. Passive sonar listens for sounds made by vessels, while active sonar emits pulses of sound and listens for their [[Reflection (physics)|echoes]]. Sonar is also used for robot navigation and, in its upward-looking in-air form called sodar, for atmospheric research. The first recorded use, attributed to [[Leonardo da Vinci]] in 1490, involved a tube inserted into water to detect vessels by ear. Sonar was developed during [[World War I]] to counter the threat of [[Submarine|submarine]] warfare, and an operational passive system was in use by 1918.

Multi-movement instrumental composition, usually of three or four movements, which may be written for various instruments. Epic poem in Old French, the oldest surviving major work of [[French literature]] and the first example of the chanson de geste. Bird of the suborder Passeri, one of the two major lineages of [[Bird|perching birds]], with about 4,000 species. The other lineage, the Tyranni, has about 1,000 species. Recent estimates place the origin of songbirds about 50 million years ago, in Australia, and about 40 million years ago they began spreading to Eurasia, Africa and eventually the Americas. Sound produced by shock waves from an object traveling faster than the [[Speed of sound|speed of sound]]. ENTRY:
Fixed poetic form of fourteen lines, traditionally with a set rhyme scheme, originating in 13th-century [[Sicily]]. The word derives from the Italian *sonetto*, meaning "little song". Giacomo da Lentini is credited with inventing the form at the court of Frederick II in [[Palermo]]. It spread to many European-language areas, mainly to express romantic love at first, and Guittone d'Arezzo later brought it to [[Tuscany]]. === KEY: Soul_5a9fdd
HEADWORD: Soul
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Buddhism | Christianity | Hinduism | Immanuel Kant | Islam | Plato | Thomas Aquinas
ENTRY:
Immaterial aspect or essence of a living being in religion and philosophy, typically believed to be immortal and to exist apart from the material world. Most humans believe in a soul or spirit, distinguished across cultures from the material body.

Religions conceptualize souls differently. [[Buddhism]] generally teaches the non-existence of a permanent self (*anattā*), while [[Christianity]] holds that the soul is eternal and that death is a transition to God's presence in heaven. In some [[Hinduism|Hindu]] traditions the *Ātman* ('self', 'essence') is identical to Brahman. [[Islam]] uses two terms, *rūḥ* and *nafs*, to distinguish the divine spirit from personal disposition. Jainism considers the soul (*jīva*) an eternal but changing form until liberation, and Judaism employs terms such as *nefesh* and *neshamah*. and Taoism recognizes two soul types, *hun* and *po*.

The soul has been a central subject of philosophy since ancient times. Socrates envisioned it as possessing a rational faculty, whose practice is man's most godlike activity. [[Plato]] believed it to be the person's real self, immaterial and immortal, continuing to think after death. [[Aristotle]] described it as the "first actuality" of a naturally organized body. Ibn Sina distinguished soul from spirit and argued that its immortality follows from its nature. [[Thomas Aquinas]], following Aristotle, held that the soul could exist without a body because it has operations independent of corporeal organs. Modern philosophy offers three main theories of the relationship between soul and body: interactionism, parallelism, and epiphenomenalism. [[Immanuel Kant]] defined the soul as the "I" in the most technical sense.

=== KEY: Sound_9d07e0
HEADWORD: Sound
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acoustics | Ear | Hearing | Medical imaging | Music | Speed of sound | Vacuum | Viscosity
ENTRY:
Pressure disturbance propagating through an elastic material medium. In physics it is characterized as a mechanical wave of pressure or related quantities such as displacement; in physiological and psychological contexts the term implies a hearing process. The American national acoustical standard defines it both as an oscillation propagated in a medium and as the auditory sensation it evokes. The human [[Ear|ear]] is sensitive to frequencies from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, though [[Hearing|hearing]] sensitivity varies among organisms. Applications include [[Music|music]], [[Medical imaging|medical imaging]], oral language, and parts of science.

A sound source, such as the vibrating diaphragm of a loudspeaker, sets the surrounding medium in motion, and the disturbance travels away at the local [[Speed of sound|speed of sound]]. The medium's particles do not travel with the wave; the disturbance and its mechanical energy do. Any form of matter, whether solid, liquid, gas, or plasma, can carry sound, but it cannot propagate through a [[Vacuum|vacuum]]. Propagation depends on the relationship between the medium's density and pressure, which is also affected by temperature and determines the speed of sound; on motion of the medium, as when wind adds to or subtracts from the wave's speed; and on [[Viscosity|viscosity]], which sets the rate of attenuation and is negligible in many media, such as air or water.

In fluids sound travels as longitudinal, or compression, waves of alternating compression and rarefaction. In solids it can also travel as transverse waves of alternating shear stress. Sound waves exhibit reflection, transmission, refraction, diffraction, absorption, and attenuation. [[Acoustics]] is the interdisciplinary study of mechanical waves, vibrations, sound, ultrasound, and infrasound in gaseous, liquid, or solid media; its subfields include architectural acoustics, psychoacoustics, and underwater acoustics.

=== KEY: Sound_recording_and_reproduction_2f0285
HEADWORD: Sound recording and reproduction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric pressure | Binary number | Compact disc | Electromagnet | Magnetic tape | Microphone | Phonograph | Thomas Edison
ENTRY:
Process of inscribing sound waves on a medium and re-creating them. The two main classes of technology are analog and digital. In analog recording, a [[Microphone|microphone]] diaphragm senses changes in [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]] and records them mechanically, such as grooves cut into a [[Phonograph|phonograph]] record. On [[Magnetic tape|magnetic tape]], the signal becomes a varying electric current, which an [[Electromagnet|electromagnet]] converts to a varying magnetic field that magnetizes a coating on plastic tape. Digital recording samples the signal at equal intervals and stores it as [[Binary number|binary numbers]]. Automatic music reproduction dates to at least the 9th century, when the Banū Mūsā brothers built a water-powered organ that played interchangeable cylinders. Practical recording arrived with [[Thomas Edison|Thomas Edison's]] phonograph in 1877, and digital formats reached the market in the 1970s and 1980s through the [[Compact disc|compact disc]].

Primarily liquid food, generally served warm or hot, made by cooking meat or vegetables with stock, milk or water. === KEY: South_America_aab422
HEADWORD: South America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amazon River | Andes | Brazil | Drake Passage | Inca Empire | Maize | Mapuche | Treaty of Tordesillas
ENTRY:
Continent entirely in the Western Hemisphere and mostly in the Southern Hemisphere (pop., 2021: more than 434 million), the southern subregion of the Americas.

South America is bordered on the west by the Pacific Ocean, on the north and east by the Atlantic Ocean, and on the south by the [[Drake Passage]]; the Caribbean Sea lies to the northwest. It has an area of 17,840,000 sq km, ranking fourth in area and fifth in population. The continent includes twelve sovereign countries: Argentina, Bolivia, [[Brazil]], Chile, Colombia, Ecuador, Guyana, Paraguay, Peru, Suriname, Uruguay, and Venezuela; two dependent territories, the Falkland Islands and South Georgia and the South Sandwich Islands; and one internal territory, French Guiana. The ABC islands (Aruba, Bonaire, and Curaçao) and Trinidad and Tobago lie on the continental shelf and may be considered part of South America, as may Panama, Ascension Island, and Bouvet Island.

Brazil has almost half of the continent's population and has generated half of its GDP. Most people live near the western or eastern coasts, while the interior and far south are sparsely populated. The west is dominated by the [[Andes]]; the east contains highlands and vast lowlands where rivers such as the [[Amazon River|Amazon]], Orinoco, and Paraná flow. Most of the continent lies in the tropics, except for a large part of the Southern Cone. The great majority of South Americans speak Portuguese or Spanish. Since 1900 the continent has been relatively peaceful, though high rates of violent crime remain a concern in some countries.

Humans are thought to have first entered South America at least 15,000 years ago, crossing the Bering Land Bridge and migrating south through North America. The Monte Verde II site in Chile is suggested to date to around 14,500 years ago. A genomic study identified at least three major dispersals into the continent. [[Maize]] was present in northern South America by around 6,000 years ago, and llamas and alpacas were domesticated in the Andean highlands circa 3500 BC. Caral–Supe, on the central Peruvian coast, created one of the first cities of the world, generally dated to around 3500 BC. Later cultures included Chavín (900 BC to 300 BC), Moche, Nazca, Tiwanaku (100 BC–1200 AD, Bolivia), the Wari Empire (600–1200), and the Muisca of what is now Colombia. The Inca civilization, with its capital at Cusco, dominated the Andes from 1438 to 1533; the [[Inca Empire|Inca]] ruled some nine to fourteen million people connected by a 25,000 km road system. The [[Mapuche]] of central and southern Chile waged the Arauco War against European and Chilean settlers for more than 300 years. In 1494 Portugal and Spain signed the [[Treaty of Tordesillas]], dividing lands outside Europe along a meridian 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands: land to the west, comprising most of South America, went to Spain, and land to the east to Portugal.

=== KEY: South_Asia_403f70

Southern subregion of Asia (pop., 2.04 billion), defined in both geographical and ethnic-cultural terms and also called the Indian subcontinent. The 1971 [[Bangladesh Liberation War]] produced the most recent new nation in the region.

In 2022 South Asia had the world's largest populations of Hindus, Muslims, Sikhs, Jains, and Zoroastrians, including 90.47% of Hindus and 31% of Muslims worldwide.

=== KEY: Southeast_Asia_ef005a

Southeastern subregion of Asia (pop., about 676 million), lying south of China, east of the [[Indian subcontinent]], and northwest of mainland Australia. === KEY: Sovereign_state_ea8d6a
HEADWORD: Sovereign state
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Kosovo | Peace of Westphalia | Sovereignty | United Nations
ENTRY:
State having [[Sovereignty|sovereignty]], the highest authority over a defined territory. A sovereign state is generally considered independent and must have a permanent population, a defined territory, a government not under the control of another, and the capacity to interact with other sovereign states. Recognition by other states plays an important role in determining its status, and unrecognized states often have difficulty conducting diplomatic relations. Westphalian sovereignty, based on territoriality and the absence of external agents in domestic affairs, began with the [[Peace of Westphalia]] in 1648. More than 200 sovereign states exist, most of them represented in the [[United Nations]]. Disputes over Taiwan or [[Kosovo]] show the tension between legal criteria and political realities.

=== KEY: Sovereignty_c567a0
HEADWORD: Sovereignty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Peace of Westphalia | Thomas Hobbes
ENTRY:
Supreme, independent control and lawmaking authority over a territory. Sovereignty is expressed through the power to rule and make law, and it entails hierarchy within a state as well as external autonomy in international affairs. It is assigned to the person, body or institution with ultimate authority over a state's citizens. A state is generally considered sovereign over a territory when it has consistently exercised authority there without objection from other states. De jure sovereignty is the legal right to rule; de facto sovereignty is the factual ability to do so. Stephen D. Krasner distinguished domestic, interdependence, international legal and [[Peace of Westphalia|Westphalian]] sovereignty. The term entered English in the 14th century. [[Thomas Hobbes]] advanced the idea in *Leviathan* (1651).

=== KEY: Soviet_Union_984ccf
HEADWORD: Soviet Union
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boris Yeltsin | Cold War | Gulag | Joseph Stalin | Mikhail Gorbachev | October Revolution | Vladimir Lenin | Warsaw Pact
ENTRY:
Transcontinental federal union of national republics spanning much of Eurasia, 1922–91; officially the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics (USSR). It was the world's third-most populous country and the largest by area; its capital was Moscow. The Communist Party governed under Marxism–Leninism, and the economy was centrally planned.

The state grew out of the [[October Revolution]] of 1917, after which [[Vladimir Lenin]]'s government established the Russian Soviet Federative Socialist Republic. After the Bolshevik victory in the Russian Civil War, the republics merged into the Soviet Union in 1922. After Lenin's death in 1924, a six-year power struggle brought [[Joseph Stalin]] to power as undisputed personalist ruler. His rapid industrialization and forced collectivization produced growth but contributed to a famine in the 1930s that killed millions. The [[Gulag]] labor camp system was expanded, and the Great Purge of the late 1930s brought deportations and executions. Despite a 1939 non-aggression pact with Nazi Germany, it was invaded in 1941. It played a decisive role in defeating the Axis powers but suffered around 27 million casualties, the most of any country in World War II, and emerged a superpower.

Tensions with the United States led to the [[Cold War]]. NATO formed in 1949 and the [[Warsaw Pact]] in 1955. After 1953 Nikita Khrushchev began de-Stalinization, leading to a split with China. In 1985 [[Mikhail Gorbachev]] sought reform through *glasnost* and *perestroika*. A failed coup by hardliners in 1991 prompted Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus to secede. On December 26, 1991, Gorbachev recognized the dissolution of the Soviet Union. [[Boris Yeltsin]] oversaw the Russian SFSR's reconstitution as the Russian Federation, the successor state, and all fifteen republics became independent.

It had the world's second-largest economy and the largest standing military and nuclear arsenal.

=== KEY: Space_Race_10d911

ENTRY:
Competition between the United States and the Soviet Union in spaceflight during the [[Cold War|Cold War]]. === KEY: Space_Shuttle_629d42

LINK TARGETS: Hubble Space Telescope | International Space Station | Kennedy Space Center | NASA | Soyuz (spacecraft)
ENTRY:
Partially reusable low Earth orbital spacecraft system, operated by [[NASA]] from 1981 to 2011. Its official name was the Space Transportation System. === KEY: Space_Shuttle_Challenger_disaster_41f52f
HEADWORD: Space Shuttle Challenger disaster
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: NASA | Ronald Reagan | Space Shuttle
ENTRY:
Fatal failure of the [[Space Shuttle]] Challenger, which broke apart 73 seconds after launch on January 28, 1986. The orbiter disintegrated about 46,000 feet above the Atlantic Ocean, killing all seven crew members, including schoolteacher Christa McAuliffe. The cause was the failure of O-ring seals in a joint of the right solid rocket booster, which had stiffened in record-low launch-morning temperatures. === KEY: Space_Shuttle_Columbia_disaster_c936cc

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: International Space Station | Louisiana | Space Shuttle | Texas
ENTRY:
Destruction of the [[Space Shuttle]] orbiter Columbia during reentry on February 1, 2003, killing all seven astronauts on mission STS-107. === KEY: Space_d511f8
HEADWORD: Space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Aristotle | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Immanuel Kant | Isaac Newton | Plato | René Descartes | Spacetime
ENTRY:
Three-dimensional continuum containing positions and directions. In classical physics, physical space is often conceived in three linear dimensions; modern physicists usually consider it, with time, part of a boundless four-dimensional continuum known as [[Spacetime|spacetime]]. Philosophers continue to disagree over whether space is an entity, a relationship between entities, or part of a conceptual framework.

Debates about its nature date to antiquity: the *Timaeus* of [[Plato]], and the definition of *topos* (place) in the *Physics* of [[Aristotle]]. Galilean and Cartesian theories of space underlie the Scientific Revolution, understood to have culminated in Newton's *Principia Mathematica* in 1687. [[René Descartes|Descartes]] held that space was Euclidean in structure, infinite, uniform, and flat, and defined it as that which contains matter.

[[Isaac Newton]] viewed space as absolute, existing permanently and independently of whether any matter was in it. [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Gottfried Leibniz]] argued instead that space is a collection of relations between objects, given by their distance and direction from one another. This dispute underlies the continuing debate between substantivalism and relationalism. [[Immanuel Kant]] held that space and time are not empirical concepts but elements of a framework humans use to structure experience; in the *Critique of Pure Reason* he called space a subjective "pure a priori form of intuition".

In the 19th and 20th centuries mathematicians began to examine non-Euclidean geometries, in which space is curved rather than flat. According to [[Albert Einstein]]'s general relativity, space around gravitational fields deviates from Euclidean space, and experimental tests have confirmed that non-Euclidean geometries model its shape better. Reconciling quantum and gravitational descriptions of space remains unresolved.

=== KEY: Space_exploration_9867fe
HEADWORD: Space exploration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollo 11 | Galileo Galilei | International Space Station | Outer space | Space Race | Sputnik 1 | Yuri Gagarin
ENTRY:
Physical investigation of [[Outer space|outer space]] by uncrewed robotic probes and by human spaceflight. Astronomy predates recorded history, but large, efficient rockets of the mid-20th century made direct exploration possible. Stated rationales include scientific research, national prestige, uniting countries, ensuring humanity's long-term survival, and military and strategic advantage.

The first recorded telescopic astronomy was by [[Galileo Galilei]] in 1609; the Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, set a later milestone. The early era was driven by the [[Space Race]] between the Soviet Union and the United States. The Soviet Union launched [[Sputnik 1]], the first human-made object to orbit Earth, on October 4, 1957, and achieved many early firsts: the first living being in orbit in 1957, the first human spaceflight (by [[Yuri Gagarin]] aboard Vostok 1) in 1961, the first spacewalk (by Alexei Leonov) on March 18, 1965, the first soft landing on another celestial body (Luna 9) on February 3, 1966, and the first space station, Salyut 1, in 1971. The United States achieved the first crewed Moon landing with [[Apollo 11]] on July 20, 1969; six crewed landings took place, the last in 1972.

In the 1970s, focus shifted from one-off flights to reusable hardware such as the Space Shuttle, and from competition to cooperation, the foremost example being the [[International Space Station]], built between 1998 and 2011. China, the European Union, Japan, and India advanced their programs in the 2000s, and private launch vehicles, capsules, and satellite manufacturing rose in the 2010s. Robotic probes such as the Voyagers have mapped the outer planets and entered interstellar space, in 2012 and 2018. In the 2020s, the two leading programs are Moon-focused: the U.S.-led Artemis program and the Chinese-led International Lunar Research Station, each with international partners.

=== KEY: Space_station_000708
HEADWORD: Space station
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: International Space Station | Mir | Skylab | Tiangong space station
ENTRY:
Habitable artificial satellite that remains in orbit and hosts humans for extended periods. Most space stations have served as research laboratories, though some have had military or commercial uses, including hosting space tourists. The first was Salyut 1, launched by the Soviet Union in 1971, followed by [[Skylab]] (1973) and [[Mir]], occupied from 1987. === KEY: Spacecraft_0978cd
HEADWORD: Spacecraft
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Launch vehicle | Soyuz (spacecraft) | Space Shuttle | SpaceX | Sputnik 1 | Vostok 1 | Yuri Gagarin
ENTRY:
Vehicle designed to fly and operate in outer space. Spacecraft serve purposes including communications, Earth observation, meteorology, navigation, planetary exploration and the transport of humans and cargo. Most require a [[Launch vehicle|launch vehicle]] to reach space. Most orbital spacecraft are not recoverable, while recoverable designs may be reusable, like the [[SpaceX]] Dragon and the [[Space Shuttle]] orbiters, or expendable, like the [[Soyuz (spacecraft)|Soyuz]]. === KEY: Spaceflight_1380ef
HEADWORD: Spaceflight
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollo 11 | International Space Station | Outer space | Robert H. Goddard | Sputnik 1 | V-2 rocket | Wernher von Braun | Yuri Gagarin
ENTRY:
Flight of objects, usually spacecraft, into or through [[Outer space|outer space]], with or without humans aboard. Most spaceflight is uncrewed, conducted with satellites in Earth orbit and with space probes beyond it, under telerobotic or autonomous control. Human programs include Soyuz, Shenzhou, the past Apollo Moon landings, and the Space Shuttle; current crewed flights go to the [[International Space Station]] and China's Tiangong station.

Spaceflight is achieved conventionally with multistage rockets. Once a spacecraft reaches sufficient speed it falls around Earth rather than back to the surface, which defines orbit. Debris from spent stages and failed spacecraft threatens working satellites, a cascade known as Kessler syndrome.

The first theoretical proposal of rocket space travel was published in 1861 by William Leitch. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky's 1903 work set out the rocket equation, still used by engineers. [[Robert H. Goddard]] published *A Method of Reaching Extreme Altitudes* in 1919 and launched the first liquid-fueled rocket on March 16, 1926. During World War II, Nazi Germany developed the [[V-2 rocket]], the first guided rocket, which became the first human-made object to reach space. Most of its team, including [[Wernher von Braun]], surrendered to the United States. Sergey Korolev's R-7 missile derivatives launched [[Sputnik 1]], the first artificial satellite, on October 4, 1957. [[Yuri Gagarin]] flew Vostok 1 in 1961. President John F. Kennedy pledged to reach the Moon, leading to the Gemini and Apollo programs and the crewed lunar landing of [[Apollo 11]] on July 20, 1969.

The Space Shuttle program operated from 1981 to 2011 and conducted 135 missions. Reusable rockets have since reshaped launch economics; SpaceX's Falcon 9 has achieved over 300 successful launches by 2025, and the company's Starship is aimed at goals such as Mars colonization.

=== KEY: Spacetime_ed5df3
HEADWORD: Spacetime
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: General relativity | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Mathematical model in physics combining three dimensions of space and one of time into a single four-dimensional continuum. Until the turn of the 20th century, space and time were assumed to be distinct. In 1908, Hermann Minkowski presented a geometric interpretation of [[Special relativity|special relativity]] that fused time and space into Minkowski space, which proved vital to [[General relativity|general relativity]]. In special relativity, the rate at which time passes for an object depends on its velocity relative to an observer. A point in spacetime is an event specified by four coordinates, and the path of a particle through spacetime is its world line. In general relativity, spacetime is curved by mass and energy, and gravitational fields can slow the passage of time. Gravitational waves, ripples in spacetime, were detected in 2015.

=== KEY: Spain_907eba
HEADWORD: Spain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Andalus | European Union | Francisco Franco | Iberian Peninsula | Madrid | Reconquista | Spanish Civil War | Spanish Empire
ENTRY:
Country in Southern and Western Europe with territories in North Africa (pop., 2026: 49.8 million). Spain covers 505,983 sq km, most of the [[Iberian Peninsula]], and includes the Canary Islands, the Balearic Islands, and the autonomous cities of Ceuta and Melilla. It borders France, Andorra, Portugal, and Morocco. The capital and largest city is [[Madrid]].

The peninsula was home to Iberians, Celts, and other peoples before Roman conquest created the province of Hispania. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, the Visigoths established a kingdom centered on Toledo. In the early 8th century the Umayyad Caliphate conquered most of the peninsula, creating [[Al-Andalus]] centered on Córdoba. Northern Christian kingdoms launched the [[Reconquista]], which ended with the fall of the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada. The 1479 dynastic union of the Crowns of Castile and Aragon under the Catholic Monarchs is often seen as the de facto unification of Spain as a nation state.

During the Age of Discovery Spain led the exploration and conquest of the New World and built the [[Spanish Empire]], ruled by the Habsburg and Bourbon dynasties. The 19th century brought the victorious Peninsular War (1808–1814) and the loss of most American colonies. Conflicts culminated in the [[Spanish Civil War]] (1936–1939) and the dictatorship of [[Francisco Franco]] (1939–1975). A constitution adopted in 1978 established parliamentary rule and regional autonomy, and Spain joined the [[European Union]] and NATO.

Spain is a secular parliamentary democracy and a constitutional monarchy, with King Felipe VI as head of state. It is a unitary state of 17 autonomous communities; regional tensions, particularly in Catalonia, remain a political issue. Spanish is the national official language. Spain has a developed, high-income economy, the fourth largest in the European Union.

=== KEY: Spanish_American_wars_of_independence_808e3f

Series of early 19th-century conflicts that ended Spanish rule across most of mainland Spanish America. Conflict fought in Spain from 1936 to 1939 between the Republicans and the Nationalist rebels. === KEY: Spanish_Empire_08a952
HEADWORD: Spanish Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Aztec Empire | Christopher Columbus | Francisco Pizarro | Napoleon | Spanish–American War | Treaty of Tordesillas
ENTRY:
Global colonial empire, 1492–1976, ruled by the Crown of Castile and later by unified Spain. With Portugal, it ushered in the European [[Age of Discovery]], and it was known as "the empire on which the sun never sets." At its greatest extent, in the late 1700s and early 1800s, it covered 13.7 million sq km.

The empire began with the 1492 arrival of [[Christopher Columbus]], sponsored by Isabella of Castile after the Catholic Monarchs completed the conquest of Granada. The [[Treaty of Tordesillas]] (1494) divided the world between Spain and Portugal, giving Spain the Western Hemisphere. Spain expanded across the Caribbean, half of South America, most of Central America, and much of North America. Hernán Cortés defeated the [[Aztec Empire]] in 1521, and [[Francisco Pizarro]] overthrew the Inca Empire in 1533. From 1580 to 1582 Spain conquered Portugal, creating the Iberian Union, which lasted until 1640.

Silver and gold from mines at Zacatecas, Guanajuato, and Potosí enriched the crown and financed wars and expansion. Colonial rule operated through viceroyalties and audiencias under the Laws of the Indies (1573); the New Laws of 1542 sought to curb abuses of the encomienda system. Historians differ on how much colonial revenue reached Spain: critics stress extraction, while others note large local spending on civil and military needs within the colonies.

Bourbon rule, from 1700, brought the Nueva Planta decrees and centralization. Tensions between peninsular Spaniards and Creoles, and [[Napoleon]]'s 1808 invasion of Spain, set off independence wars that, by the mid-1820s, cost Spain Mexico, Central America, and South America. After the [[Spanish–American War]], it lost Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, and Guam by late 1898. The empire ended with Spain's withdrawal from Western Sahara in 1976.

System of tribunals established in 1478 to enforce Catholic orthodoxy in Spain. Authorized by Pope Sixtus IV and organized by the Catholic Monarchs, [[Ferdinand II of Aragon]] and [[Isabella I of Castile]], it became an effective instrument of state power. It prosecuted an estimated 150,000 people, of whom 3,000–5,000 were executed, mostly by burning at the stake, particularly in its first 50 years. Other punishments included penance, flogging, exile and imprisonment, and victims' property was often confiscated. Royal decrees of 1492 and 1502 ordered [[Jews]] and [[Muslims]] to convert or leave, and an estimated 40,000–100,000 Jews were expelled in 1492. Its targets later included Protestants, [[Witchcraft|witchcraft]] and Freemasonry.

=== KEY: Spanish_colonization_of_the_Americas_cb51cf
HEADWORD: Spanish colonization of the Americas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Christopher Columbus | Cuba | Hispaniola | Isabella I of Castile | Philippines | Puerto Rico | Spanish–American War
ENTRY:
Process of Spanish conquest and settlement in the Americas, beginning in 1493. It started on the Caribbean island of [[Hispaniola]] after a 1492 voyage by Genoese mariner [[Christopher Columbus]] under license from Queen [[Isabella I of Castile]]. Religion played an important role, as indigenous peoples were brought into the [[Catholic Church]] peacefully or by force. An estimated 1.86 million Spaniards settled in the Americas between 1492 and 1832. The indigenous population fell by an estimated 80% in the century and a half after Columbus's voyages, primarily through infectious diseases, while forced labor and slavery were used for resource extraction. The Spanish American wars of independence in the early 19th century led to the secession of most of Spanish America. [[Cuba]], [[Puerto Rico]] and the [[Philippines]] stayed under Spanish rule until they were lost to the United States in 1898, after the [[Spanish–American War]].

=== KEY: Spanish_conquest_of_the_Aztec_Empire_18400d
HEADWORD: Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hernán Cortés | Mesoamerica | Mexico City | Moctezuma II | New Spain | Smallpox | Spanish Empire | Tenochtitlan
ENTRY:
Campaign in which Spanish forces overthrew the Aztec Empire, 1519–1521. [[Hernán Cortés]] led a small army of European soldiers and a large allied army of Indian auxiliaries against the most powerful pre-Columbian empire in [[Mesoamerica]], which was ruled by [[Moctezuma II]]. The empire's downfall followed from European weaponry, alliances with rebellious Indigenous populations and European diseases. A first wave of [[Smallpox|smallpox]] in 1520 killed an estimated 5–8 million people. The final victory came on 13 August 1521, when Spanish and Tlaxcalan forces captured the Aztec emperor Cuauhtémoc and the capital, [[Tenochtitlan]]. Its fall established [[New Spain]], part of the [[Spanish Empire]], with its capital at [[Mexico City]], built on the ruins of the former capital.

=== KEY: Spanish_conquest_of_the_Inca_Empire_4e3a94
HEADWORD: Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atahualpa | Chile | Colombia | Francisco Pizarro | Inca Empire | Peru | Smallpox
ENTRY:
Campaign by which Spain conquered the [[Inca Empire]] in South America, 1532–1572. In 1532, 168 Spanish soldiers under [[Francisco Pizarro]] and their Indigenous auxiliaries captured the last Sapa Inca, [[Atahualpa]], at the Battle of Cajamarca. The campaign took decades of fighting and ended with Spanish victory in 1572 and colonization of the region as the [[Peru|Viceroyalty of Peru]]. Spin-off campaigns reached present-day [[Chile]] and [[Colombia]], and expeditions went into the Amazon Basin. The empire had grown from about 400,000 sq km in 1448 to 1,800,000 sq km by 1528, and scholars estimate its population at between 12 and 16 million. Huayna Capac and his heir died suddenly in 1528, probably of [[Smallpox|smallpox]], a disease introduced by the Spanish into the Americas, and a civil war between the princes Huáscar and Atahualpa followed that greatly aided the Spanish.

=== KEY: Spanish_flu_32ea59
HEADWORD: Spanish flu
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Censorship | Hygiene | Influenza | Kansas | Spain | World War I
ENTRY:
Exceptionally deadly global [[Influenza|influenza]] pandemic of 1918–1920, caused by the H1N1 subtype of influenza A virus. The earliest probable cases were documented in March 1918 in Haskell County, [[Kansas]]. Two years later, nearly a third of the global population, an estimated 500 million people, had been infected. Estimates of deaths range from 17 million to 50 million, and possibly as high as 100 million. The pandemic broke out near the end of [[World War I]], when wartime [[Censorship|censors]] suppressed bad news, but newspapers freely reported the outbreak in neutral [[Spain]], creating a misleading impression of Spain as the epicenter and leading to the name Spanish flu. Most influenza outbreaks kill mainly the young and old, but this pandemic had unusually high mortality among young adults, a claim that has been contested. World War I worsened malnourishment, overcrowding and poor [[Hygiene|hygiene]], promoting bacterial superinfection that killed most victims after a typically prolonged death bed.

=== KEY: Spanish_language_8cb317
HEADWORD: Spanish language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arabic | Kingdom of Castile | Mandarin Chinese | Mexico | Miguel de Cervantes | Romance languages | Spanish Empire | United Nations
ENTRY:
[[Romance languages|Romance language]] of the Indo-European family, spoken natively mainly in the Americas and Spain. Also called Castilian (*castellano*), it evolved from the Vulgar Latin of the Iberian Peninsula and originated in the [[Kingdom of Castile]], in what is now north-central Spain. It is the world's second-most spoken native language after [[Mandarin Chinese]] and its most widely spoken Romance language. Estimates of native speakers range from 498 million to 519 million. It is an official language of 20 or more countries and one of the six official languages of the [[United Nations]]. [[Mexico]] has the most native speakers, about 130 million.

Spanish descends from Vulgar Latin, which reached the peninsula with the Romans during the Second Punic War, beginning in 210 BC. The first documents showing traces of Spanish date from the 9th century, and the first systematic written use took place in Toledo in the 13th century. About 75% of modern vocabulary is Latin in origin. It borrowed words from neighboring Romance languages, Andalusi [[Arabic]], and Basque. Spread through the Catholic Monarchs' unification and the [[Spanish Empire]] made it dominant across the Americas and into parts of Africa, Asia, and Oceania, though its global reach stems from colonization.

In Spain the language is called both *español* and *castellano*, the latter contrasting it with Galician, Basque, Catalan, and other languages of Spain. The 1978 Constitution uses *castellano* for the official language of the state. The Royal Spanish Academy, which has standardized the language since 1713, called it *castellano* from 1713 to 1923 and now prefers *español*, while regarding both terms as valid. According to the Academy, *español* derives from Occitan *espaignol*, from Vulgar Latin *hispaniolus* ("of Hispania"). Its literary tradition includes *Don Quixote* by [[Miguel de Cervantes]].

=== KEY: Spanish_literature_48b20b
HEADWORD: Spanish literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epic poetry | Futurism | Iberian Peninsula | Miguel de Cervantes | Modernism | Novel | Prose | Spain
ENTRY:
Literature written in Spanish within the territory of present-day [[Spain]]. Its development intersected with Catalan and Galician literature and with Latin, Jewish and Arabic traditions of the [[Iberian Peninsula]]. The [[Epic poetry|epic poem]] *Cantar de Mio Cid*, composed between 1140 and 1207, is among its earliest notable works. [[Prose|Spanish prose]] gained popularity in the mid-13th century, and in the 16th century the first Spanish [[Novel|novels]], *Lazarillo de Tormes* and *Guzmán de Alfarache*, appeared. In the Baroque era of the 17th century, [[Miguel de Cervantes]] wrote *Don Quixote de la Mancha*, a novel that consolidated the picaresque form into a template for later fiction. Enlightenment, Romantic and Realist writers followed, and [[Modernism]] produced several currents, including [[Futurism]]. The literature of Spanish America is an important branch, with its own characteristics dating back to the earliest years of Spain's conquest of the Americas.

=== KEY: Spanish_moss_f675c8
HEADWORD: Spanish moss
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bromeliaceae | Florida | Flowering plant | Lichen | Mexico | Spain | Virginia
ENTRY:
Epiphytic [[Flowering plant|flowering plant]] (*Tillandsia usneoides*) of the [[Bromeliaceae|bromeliad]] family, often growing on large trees in tropical and subtropical climates. It is native to much of [[Mexico]], Bermuda, the Bahamas, Central America, South America and the Southern United States, and it has been naturalized in Queensland, Australia. It has the widest distribution of any bromeliad. In the United States it is common on southern live oak and bald cypress in lowland swamps and marshes from southeastern [[Virginia]] to [[Florida]] and west to southern Arkansas and Texas. Although it resembles the [[Lichen|lichen]] *Usnea*, it is neither a lichen nor a moss, and it is not native to [[Spain]]. The plant has no roots and is not parasitic, absorbing nutrients and water through its leaves from air and rain.

=== KEY: Sparta_1728d3
HEADWORD: Sparta
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Athens | Lycurgus | Peloponnesian War
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek city-state (polis) in Laconia, in the southeastern Peloponnese. From the 7th century BC it was the most prominent land military power in the Greek world, a status it kept until its defeat at Leuctra in 371 BC. In the 6th century BC it founded the Peloponnesian League, and its army was crucial at Thermopylae and Plataea during the second Persian invasion. Sparta led the League against the [[Athens|Athenian Empire]] in the [[Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BC) and emerged victorious. Its social system, supposedly introduced by the semi-mythical lawgiver [[Lycurgus]], was exceptionally militarized: full citizens, the Spartiates, trained under the harsh agoge, while helots, enslaved non-Spartan locals, made up the vast majority of the population and repeatedly revolted. Sparta was sacked in 396 AD by the Visigothic king Alaric and declined into the medieval period, when much of its population moved to nearby Mystras.

=== KEY: Spartacus_0443ed
HEADWORD: Spartacus
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Gladiator | Marcus Licinius Crassus | Plutarch | Roman Republic | Sicily
ENTRY:
Thracian gladiator and escaped slave leader of the Third Servile War, a major slave uprising against the [[Roman Republic]]. The main accounts of his life, by [[Plutarch]] and Appian, were written more than a century later and are contradictory. He came from near the Strymon River in Thrace, possibly of the Maedi tribe, and had served as a Roman soldier before being enslaved as a [[Gladiator|gladiator]]. In 73 BC he and about 70 other gladiators escaped a gladiatorial school near Capua, and his forces swelled to an estimated 70,000 enslaved people and others. After initial successes and attempts to negotiate and to escape to [[Sicily]], his army was defeated in 71 BC by [[Marcus Licinius Crassus]], who had been tasked with suppressing the revolt. Spartacus was presumed killed, though his body was never found, and about 6,000 captured rebels were crucified along the Appian Way.

=== KEY: Spear_1b84a9
HEADWORD: Spear
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axe | Bayonet | Bronze | Flint | Galago | Iron | Knife | Modern era
ENTRY:
Polearm consisting of a usually wooden shaft with a pointed head, which may be the sharpened shaft itself or a durable material such as [[Flint|flint]], bone, obsidian, copper, [[Bronze|bronze]], [[Iron|iron]] or steel. Spears are designed either for thrusting, as with lances and pikes, or for throwing, as with javelins. People have used them for at least 400,000 years for hunting, fishing and warfare, and they are among the earliest and most widespread tools, along with the club, [[Knife|knife]] and [[Axe|axe]]. Wooden spears found at Clacton in England and Schöningen in Germany are at least 400,000 years old, and by 15,000 BC hunters used wooden and bone spear-launchers. Spears were used in virtually every conflict until the [[Modern era|modern era]], and they survive as the [[Bayonet|bayonet]] fixed to the muzzle of a long gun. Western chimpanzees in Senegal have been observed making spears to hunt [[Galago|galagos]].
