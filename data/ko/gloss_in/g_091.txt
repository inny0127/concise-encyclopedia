=== KEY: Monopoly_4d6bfc
HEADWORD: Monopoly
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Competition (economics) | Copyright | Patent
ENTRY:
Market structure in which a single firm is the only supplier of a good or service. A monopoly lacks economic competition and viable substitutes, which allows a price well above marginal cost. In law, a monopoly is a business with significant market power, and size is not a defining characteristic. Monopolies may form through mergers, arise naturally where production is resource-intensive, or be established by a government, as with [[Patent|patents]] and [[Copyright|copyrights]]. A monopsony, with a single buyer, is a related situation, and a monopoly differs from a cartel, in which several providers coordinate. Many jurisdictions use [[Competition (economics)|competition]] laws to restrict monopolies, although holding a dominant position is often not illegal in itself, and certain abusive behaviors can be sanctioned.

=== KEY: Monopoly_game_aae9b2
HEADWORD: Monopoly
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Board game | Dice
ENTRY:
Multiplayer economics-themed [[Board game|board game]]. Players roll two [[Dice|dice]] to move around the board, buying and trading properties and railroads and developing them with houses and hotels. They collect rent from opponents and aim to drive them into bankruptcy. Players receive a salary when they pass "Go", and Chance and Community Chest cards and tax squares can also gain or cost money. The game is derived from *The Landlord's Game*, created in 1903 by Lizzie Magie to show that an economy rewarding individuals was better than one in which monopolies held all the wealth. Parker Brothers first published the game in 1935, and it was absorbed into Hasbro in 1991. It has been licensed in more than 113 countries and had sold an estimated 275 million copies by 2015.

=== KEY: Monorail_bc727a
HEADWORD: Monorail
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chongqing | Tram
ENTRY:
Form of urban rail transit with vehicles on or suspended from a single rail or beam. Monorails typically provide intermediate capacity, greater than [[Tram|trams]] and less than heavy metro systems. Two principal types exist: straddle-beam monorails, in which vehicles ride atop a concrete or steel beam, and suspended monorails, in which vehicles hang beneath the guideway. The straddle-beam design descends from the ALWEG system of the 1950s and dominates modern installations. The Wuppertal Schwebebahn, in service since 1901, is the oldest monorail in operation. Tokyo Monorail has carried over 1.5 billion passengers since 1964. [[Chongqing]] Rail Transit in China operates the world's largest and busiest monorail system, and its Line 3 carries over 680,000 passengers per day. Monorails are most often used in large cities, at airports and in theme parks.

Belief in only one deity, or God. Egg-laying [[Mammal|mammal]] of the order Monotremata. The five living species are the [[Platypus]] and four species of [[Echidna|echidnas]]. === KEY: Monroe_Doctrine_874b28
HEADWORD: Monroe Doctrine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: James Monroe | Organization of American States
ENTRY:
U.S. foreign policy position opposing foreign interference in the Western Hemisphere. President [[James Monroe]] first articulated the doctrine on December 2, 1823, in his seventh annual message to Congress; it was not named after him until 1850. Monroe declared that the New World and Old World were separate spheres of influence, and that European efforts to control or influence sovereign states in the Americas would be viewed as a threat to U.S. security. The United States, in turn, would not interfere with existing European colonies. Because the United States lacked a credible navy and army, the doctrine was largely ignored, though the United Kingdom partly enforced it. The United States enforced it itself early in the 20th century. After 1898 it was reinterpreted as promoting multilateralism and non-intervention, and in 1933 Franklin D. Roosevelt affirmed this view by co-founding the [[Organization of American States]].

=== KEY: Monsoon_7198f9
HEADWORD: Monsoon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arabian Sea | Bay of Bengal | Intertropical Convergence Zone
ENTRY:
Seasonal change in atmospheric circulation and precipitation. Traditionally a seasonal reversing wind, the term now describes changes associated with the annual latitudinal oscillation of the [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]]. It usually refers to the rainy phase of the pattern, although a dry phase also exists, and it can describe locally heavy but short-term rains. The term was first used in English in British India for the large seasonal winds that blew from the [[Bay of Bengal]] and the [[Arabian Sea]] in the southwest, bringing heavy rainfall.

=== KEY: Month_7cbb88
HEADWORD: Month
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gregorian calendar | Leap year | Moon
ENTRY:
Unit of time, used with calendars, that is approximately as long as a cycle of the [[Moon|Moon]]'s phases. The synodic month, from one new moon to the next, averages 29.53059 days, so there are roughly 12.37 such months in one Earth year. Calendars that developed from the Roman system, such as the [[Gregorian calendar]], divide the year into 12 months of 28 to 31 days. The names of months 9 to 12 come from Latin numerals 7 to 10, because they were originally the seventh through tenth months of the Roman calendar. In the Gregorian calendar only February varies, with 29 days in a [[Leap year|leap year]] and 28 otherwise. Astronomers also recognize the sidereal month, about 27.32166 days, and the anomalistic month, about 27.55455 days.

=== KEY: Moon_d502a5
HEADWORD: Moon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollo 11 | Lunar eclipse | Natural satellite | Soviet Union | Theia (hypothetical planet) | Tidal locking
ENTRY:
Earth's only [[Natural satellite|natural satellite]], a differentiated, terrestrial body with a diameter of 3,474 km.

The Moon orbits Earth in an elliptical path, completing an orbit relative to the Earth and Sun every 29.5 days (a lunar month) and relative to the stars every 27.3 days. Gravitational attraction between the two bodies produces tides on Earth and has pulled the Moon into [[Tidal locking|tidal locking]], so that it always shows the same near side to Earth. Libration makes about 59% of the surface visible from Earth.

Its mass is 1.2% that of Earth, and its diameter is roughly one-quarter of Earth's. It is larger and more massive than any known dwarf planet and is the fifth-largest moon in the Solar System, the largest relative to its parent planet. Its surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's, second-highest among moons after Io. It has only a minuscule hydrosphere, atmosphere, and magnetic field. The surface is covered with regolith dust and marked by impact craters, some with bright rays. The Moon was volcanically active until 1.2 billion years ago; lava filling ancient craters, mostly on the thinner near side, formed the dark basaltic plains called maria ('seas'). The reflectance of its surface is comparable to that of asphalt, yet it is the brightest object in Earth's night sky.

Isotope dating of lunar samples suggests the Moon formed in the first 200 million years after the Solar System's origin. The prevailing theory holds that a giant impact of a Mars-sized body named [[Theia (hypothetical planet)|Theia]] with the proto-Earth ejected material that accreted into the Moon. Its apparent size matches the Sun's, so it covers the Sun completely during a total solar eclipse, and when it passes through Earth's shadow a [[Lunar eclipse|lunar eclipse]] occurs. Its changing illuminated portion produces the lunar phases.

The first spaceflights to another body were to the Moon: Luna 1 flew past in 1959 and Luna 2 struck it, both launched by the [[Soviet Union]]; Luna 9 made the first soft landing in 1966, and Luna 10 the first orbital insertion. Humans first orbited it on Apollo 8 on December 24, 1968, and landed on [[Apollo 11]] on July 20, 1969. By 1972 six Apollo missions had landed twelve people, making the Moon the only celestial body beyond Earth that humans have visited. Plans to return, including the Artemis program and its Artemis II crewed flyby of 2026, are driven partly by robotic confirmation of water on the Moon.

=== KEY: Moraine_799556

Accumulation of unconsolidated debris carried and deposited by a [[Glacier|glacier]] or [[Ice sheet|ice sheet]]. === KEY: Morality_a84f47
HEADWORD: Morality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cicero | Consequentialism | Ethics | Geoffrey Chaucer | Immanuel Kant | Latin | Nihilism
ENTRY:
Standard, doctrine or system of conduct through which communities and individuals evaluate actions, character, and social arrangements; a quality found among social animals, including humans.

The criteria used vary across individuals, societies, social classes, religions, cultures, and traditions, and include rightness or wrongness, virtue or vice, honor or disgrace, reciprocity, and shame. Cross-cultural research suggests that attention to moral sentiments exists in all human societies. Moral universalism holds that some principles, such as honesty, loyalty, and fairness, are recognized by all societies, while moral relativism denies that any universal morality exists. Immorality is the violation of morality; [[Nihilism|nihilism]] rejects morality as a concept, and amorality is variously defined as unawareness of, indifference to, or disbelief in any set of moral standards.

The English word derives from the [[Latin]] *mōs* (genitive *mōris*), 'manner, character, proper behavior'. It entered Middle English around 1375 in the works of [[Geoffrey Chaucer]]. The Latin adjective *mōrālis* was coined by [[Cicero]] to translate a Greek term.

[[Ethics]], or moral philosophy, is the branch of philosophy that addresses questions of morality, and the two words are often used interchangeably. Simon Blackburn notes a usage that restricts morality to systems such as that of [[Immanuel Kant]], based on duty, obligation, and principles, and reserves ethics for the Aristotelian approach based on virtue. In its descriptive sense, morality refers to the values and codes of conduct a society is observed to accept; in its normative sense, it refers to whatever is actually right or wrong, which may be independent of those values. Three major approaches in normative ethics are [[Consequentialism|consequentialism]], deontological ethics, and virtue ethics, which differ in whether outcomes, duties, or character have the central role in evaluation.

Country, North Africa, officially the Kingdom of Morocco, with coastlines on the Mediterranean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean.

=== KEY: Morpheme_aa60df
HEADWORD: Morpheme
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin | Morphology (linguistics)
ENTRY:
Smallest meaningful constituent of a linguistic expression, especially within a word. A free morpheme can stand alone as a word, such as *town*, while a bound morpheme appears only within words, such as the *-s* in *cats*. Bound morphemes include affixes, such as prefixes and suffixes, and roots that cannot stand alone, such as the [[Latin]] *reg-* in *regis*. Derivational morphemes change meaning or part of speech, as the suffix *-ness* does in turning *happy* into *happiness*. Inflectional morphemes add grammatical information without changing word class, and English has eight inflections. Allomorphs are variants of one morpheme, such as the three pronunciations of the English plural marker. The study of morphemes is [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphology]].

Family of small [[Fly|flies]], the Culicidae, consisting of about 3,600 species. Mosquitoes have one pair of wings and a long proboscis adapted for piercing and sucking. === KEY: Motion_822658
HEADWORD: Motion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Classical mechanics | Frame of reference | General relativity | Isaac Newton | Kinematics | Newton's laws of motion | Quantum mechanics | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Change in the position of an object or fluid with respect to a [[Frame of reference|reference frame]] over a given time.

Motion is described mathematically by vector quantities such as displacement, velocity, and acceleration. The branch of physics describing motion without reference to its cause is [[Kinematics|kinematics]]; the branch studying forces and their effect on motion is dynamics. An object not moving relative to a given frame is said to be at rest. Because modern physics holds that there is no absolute frame of reference, [[Isaac Newton]]'s concept of absolute motion cannot be determined, and everything in the universe can be considered to be in motion.

[[Classical mechanics]] describes macroscopic objects moving much slower than the speed of light, from projectiles and machinery to spacecraft, planets, and stars. It rests on [[Newton's laws of motion]], first compiled in his *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, published on July 5, 1687. The first law states that a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in motion, unless acted on by an external force; the second relates force to the change in momentum, equal to mass times acceleration for constant mass; the third states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. These laws unified the motion of celestial bodies with that of objects on Earth.

Modern kinematics, developed alongside the study of electromagnetism, refers velocities to the speed of light and transforms them by Lorentz transformations; this is [[Special relativity|special relativity]]. W. K. Clifford and Albert Einstein used differential geometry to incorporate gravity, producing [[General relativity|general relativity]]. At the atomic and subatomic level, [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] applies. Because of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, a particle's location and velocity cannot be determined simultaneously, and matter and radiation show wave–particle duality.

Elevated portion of a planet's surface, generally with steep sides showing significant exposed [[Bedrock|bedrock]].

No universally accepted definition exists; elevation, volume, relief, steepness, spacing, and continuity have all been used as criteria. A mountain is usually considered higher than a hill and is often thought of as higher than 2,000 feet (610 m), the threshold the U.K. government uses for access purposes. By one UN Environment Programme classification, 24% of Earth's land mass is mountainous.

There are three main types of mountains: volcanic, fold, and block. All are formed through [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], when portions of the crust move, crumple, and dive. Volcanic mountains, such as [[Mount Fuji]] in Japan and [[Mount Pinatubo]] in the Philippines, are built where a plate is pushed below another or at a mid-ocean ridge or hotspot. Fold mountains arise when two plates collide, shortening the crust along thrust faults. Once building ceases, mountains are slowly leveled by weathering, mass wasting, and [[Erosion|erosion]] by rivers and glaciers.

High elevations produce colder climates than sea level at similar latitude, so different elevations support different plants and animals. Because of the less hospitable terrain and climate, mountains are used less for agriculture and more for resource extraction, such as mining and logging, and for recreation, such as climbing and skiing.

The highest mountain on Earth above sea level is [[Mount Everest]] in the Himalayas, whose summit is 8,850 m above mean sea level. The highest known mountain on any planet in the Solar System is [[Olympus Mons]] on Mars, at 21,171 m. Measured from base to peak, [[Mauna Kea]] in Hawaii rises 9,330 m from the ocean floor, and some scientists consider it the tallest on Earth.

Navigable route through a [[Mountain range|mountain range]] or over a ridge. Because ranges are formidable barriers to travel, passes have long played a key role in trade, war, and human and animal migration. Passes are typically formed between two volcanic peaks or created by erosion from water, ice or wind. The lowest point along a ridge, the saddle, often provides the route, and the top of a pass is frequently the only flat ground in the area. Roads and railways have long been built through passes, and some high ones have tunnels, such as the Eisenhower Tunnel beneath Loveland Pass in the Rockies. Where a national border follows a ridge, a pass is typically on the border; [[Argentina]] and [[Chile]] share a 5,300 kilometers (3,300 mi) border that includes 42 mountain passes. The Brenner Pass in the [[Alps]] is a typical historic trade route.
