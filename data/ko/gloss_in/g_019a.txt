ENTRY:
Spirit produced by distilling [[Wine|wine]], generally containing 35–60% alcohol by volume. It is typically drunk as an after-dinner digestif. Some brandies are aged in wooden casks, while others are colored with caramel to imitate aging. Cognac and Armagnac from [[France|southwestern France]] are among the most renowned. === KEY: Brass_ff6039
HEADWORD: Brass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Bronze | Copper | Corrosion | Musical instrument | Plumbing | Valve | Zinc
ENTRY:
[[Alloy]] of [[Copper|copper]] and [[Zinc|zinc]], in proportions that can be varied to achieve different colors and properties. Brass is similar to [[Bronze|bronze]], which uses tin instead of zinc. Its relatively low melting point (900 to 940 °C; 1,650 to 1,720 °F, depending on composition) and high workability make it easy to cast and machine. It is used for locks, hinges, gears, bearings, zippers, [[Plumbing|plumbing]] and [[Valve|valves]], where [[Corrosion|corrosion]] resistance and low friction are needed, and for horns and bells in [[Musical instrument|musical instruments]]. It is unsuitable for marine use because salt water leaches the zinc, but marine brass containing tin avoids this. The density of brass is 8.4 to 8.73 g/cm³, and almost 90% of brass alloys are recycled.

=== KEY: Brass_instrument_ae12e2
HEADWORD: Brass instrument
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Musical instrument | Saxophone | Trombone | Trumpet | Tuba | Woodwind instrument
ENTRY:
Class of [[Musical instrument|musical instruments]] that produce sound by sympathetic vibration of air in a tubular resonator, driven by the player's lips. Slides, valves, crooks or keys change the length of tubing, and the player's embouchure, lip tension and air flow select the harmonic produced. Classification follows how sound is made, not the material: wooden instruments such as the alphorn and didgeridoo are brass instruments, while the [[Saxophone|saxophone]] is a [[Woodwind instrument|woodwind]] made of brass. Valved instruments include the [[Trumpet|trumpet]], horn, euphonium and [[Tuba|tuba]], while the [[Trombone|trombone]] is a slide instrument. Natural instruments, such as the bugle, play only notes of the harmonic series and are used for period performances.

Economically important family of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] known as the mustards, crucifers or cabbage family. Most species are herbaceous, with simple leaves that lack stipules. === KEY: Brazil_42537f
HEADWORD: Brazil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: BRICS | Brasília | Empire of Brazil | Getúlio Vargas | Pedro Álvares Cabral | Portuguese language | Rio de Janeiro | São Paulo
ENTRY:
Largest country in South America (pop. over 214 million), with its capital at [[Brasília]]. Fifth-largest in the world by area (8,515,770 sq km) and seventh by population, Brazil is a federation of 26 states and a Federal District. Its most populous city is [[São Paulo]], followed by [[Rio de Janeiro]]. [[Portuguese language|Portuguese]] is the official language, and Brazil is the only Portuguese-speaking country in the Americas.

Bounded by the Atlantic Ocean on the east, with a coastline of 7,491 km, Brazil covers roughly half of the continent and borders every other South American country except Ecuador and Chile. It contains most of the Amazon basin, with the world's largest river system and most extensive virgin tropical forest, and ranks first among 17 megadiverse countries.

Indigenous peoples inhabited the territory before the Portuguese explorer [[Pedro Álvares Cabral]] landed in 1500. Portugal founded the first city, São Vicente, in 1532 and imported enslaved Africans to work plantations. Prince Pedro of Braganza declared independence in 1822 and established the [[Empire of Brazil]]. Slavery was finally abolished in 1888, and a military coup in 1889 made Brazil a presidential republic. A revolution in 1930 brought [[Getúlio Vargas]] to power. A military dictatorship ruled from 1964 until 1985, and the constitution of 1988 defines Brazil as a democratic federal republic.

Brazil has the largest economy in Latin America and one of the 10 largest in the world. It is a founding member of the United Nations and a member of the G20 and [[BRICS]].

=== KEY: Brazil_nut_56f287
HEADWORD: Brazil nut
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amazon rainforest | Bolivia | Brazil | Pollination | Selenium
ENTRY:
Edible seed of *Bertholletia excelsa*, a large South American tree. It is one of the largest and longest-lived trees in the [[Amazon rainforest]], reaching 50 m (160 ft) tall and possibly living 500 years or more. The fruit weighs as much as 2 kg (4.4 lb) and holds eight to 24 wedge-shaped seeds. Brazil nuts are notable for micronutrients, especially a high amount of [[Selenium|selenium]]. The trees fruit almost exclusively in pristine forests, because [[Pollination|pollination]] requires large bees that disturbed forests lack. Agoutis, large rodents, gnaw open the fruit and bury seeds, some of which germinate. In 2024 world production was 79,736 tonnes, with [[Brazil]] and [[Bolivia]] accounting for 87%. The wood is prized for carpentry, flooring and heavy construction.

=== KEY: Brazilian_Highlands_db319d
HEADWORD: Brazilian Highlands
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Basalt | Biome | Brazil | Cerrado | Erosion | Soil
ENTRY:
Extensive region covering most of eastern, southern and central [[Brazil]]. It covers some 4,500,000 km² (1,930,511 sq mi), approximately half of the country's land area. The vast majority of Brazil's population (203,062,512 in the 2022 census) lives in the highlands or on the narrow coastal region beside them. Ancient [[Basalt|basaltic]] lava flows formed much of the region, which has no seismic or volcanic activity today. [[Erosion]] has shaped it into sedimentary deposits and worn-down mountains. Its diversity includes several [[Biome|biomes]], varied climates, many [[Soil|soil]] types and thousands of animal and plant species. The highlands are usually divided into the Atlantic Plateau along the eastern coast, the Southern Plateau and the Central Plateau, where about 85% of the land was once covered by [[Cerrado]] vegetation. The highest point is Pico da Bandeira, 2,891 m (9,485 ft), in the Serra do Caparaó.

[[Martial arts|Martial art]] and combat sport based on grappling, ground fighting and submission holds, commonly abbreviated as BJJ. Practitioners take an opponent to the ground, gain a dominant position and use joint locks, chokeholds or compression locks to force submission. It rests on the idea that a smaller, weaker person can defeat a larger opponent through leverage and weight distribution. === KEY: Bread_1296fc
HEADWORD: Bread
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Neolithic | Pliny the Elder | Rye | Sourdough | Sumer | Wheat | Yeast
ENTRY:
Baked food made from water, flour, and often yeast. A staple food across the world, particularly in Europe and the Middle East, bread is one of the oldest human-made foods. The oldest evidence of bread-making comes from a 14,500-year-old Natufian site in Jordan's northeastern desert, and grains became its mainstay around 10,000 BC with the dawn of the [[Neolithic]] age and the spread of agriculture.

Bread may be leavened by naturally occurring microbes, as in [[Sourdough|sourdough]], by chemicals, by industrially produced [[Yeast|yeast]], or by high-pressure aeration; it may also be unleavened. In yeast fermentation, carbon dioxide and ethanol vapors produce the air pockets of the crumb. An early leavened bread was baked as early as 6000 BC in southern Mesopotamia, home of the [[Sumer|Sumerian]] civilization, which may have passed the knowledge to the Egyptians around 3000 BC. [[Pliny the Elder]] reported that the Gauls and Iberians used the foam skimmed from beer, called barm, for a lighter bread, and that the most common leavening was a piece of dough kept from the previous day. The Chorleywood process, developed in 1961, uses intense mechanical working to shorten fermentation and is widely used in large factories.

Bread is the staple of the Middle East, Central Asia, North Africa, Europe, and European-derived cultures, in contrast to parts of South and East Asia, where rice or noodles are the staple. Most is made from [[Wheat|wheat]], whose gluten gives dough sponginess and elasticity. Other cereals, including [[Rye|rye]], barley, maize, oats, sorghum, millet, and rice, have been used, usually mixed with wheat flour because they contain less gluten. Gluten-free breads use flours such as almond, rice, sorghum, corn, legumes, and cassava.

=== KEY: Breadfruit_e0f1b6
HEADWORD: Breadfruit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cultivar | Jackfruit | Moraceae | Oceania | Polynesia
ENTRY:
Edible fruit-bearing tree in the [[Moraceae|mulberry and jackfruit family]], *Artocarpus altilis*. Its name refers to the texture of the moderately ripe fruit when cooked, similar to freshly baked bread. The fruit is a staple in many cultures, and the light, sturdy timber is used for furniture, houses and surfboards. Austronesian expansion spread the tree into [[Oceania]], where it is believed to have been selectively bred in [[Polynesia]] from the breadnut. British and French navigators introduced seedless Polynesian varieties to the Caribbean in the late 18th century. A single tree can produce up to 200 or more grapefruit-sized fruits per season, and it grows to 26 m (85 ft) tall. Most selectively bred [[Cultivar|cultivars]] are seedless, and large-scale propagation uses root cuttings. The tree is closely related to the [[Jackfruit|jackfruit]].

=== KEY: Breakfast_cereal_160b6d
HEADWORD: Breakfast cereal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cereal | Mascot | Oat | Sugar
ENTRY:
Processed food made from [[Cereal|cereal]] grains, eaten mainly in the morning or as a snack, primarily in Western societies. Warm cooked cereals such as [[Oat|oatmeal]] have the longest history, but ready-to-eat cold cereals, many made by extrusion, appeared in the late 19th century. Ferdinand Schumacher, a German immigrant, began the cereal revolution in 1854 with a hand oats grinder in Akron, Ohio, and his German Mills American Oatmeal Company was the nation's first commercial oatmeal manufacturer. The number of types of breakfast cereal in the United States more than doubled, from about 160 in 1970 to around 340 in 1998. Many packaged cereals have a high [[Sugar|sugar]] content and are marketed to children with cartoon [[Mascot|mascots]] and toys. The industry has gross profit margins of 40–45%.

=== KEY: Breakfast_f2265d
HEADWORD: Breakfast
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Bacon | Bread | Coffee | Falafel | Middle Ages | Sausage
ENTRY:
First meal of the day, usually eaten in the morning. The English word refers to breaking the fasting period of the previous night, and by around the 15th century it was used in written English for a morning meal. In [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]], peasants ate soup, beer, bread and onions before work, and a traditional breakfast was fūl, made from fava beans, with emmer-wheat bread and [[Falafel|falafel]]. The Romans called the meal ientaculum, typically bread, cheese, olives and cold meat. In [[Middle Ages|medieval]] Europe breakfast was eaten mainly by working people, children, the elderly and the sick, when only two formal meals a day were eaten. Customs now vary: French breakfasts tend to be light, with [[Bread|bread]], butter, jam and [[Coffee|coffee]], while the full English breakfast includes eggs, [[Bacon|bacon]], [[Sausage|sausages]], grilled tomatoes, mushrooms, baked beans and toast.

=== KEY: Breakup_0f0d82

ENTRY:
Ending of an intimate relationship, romantic or platonic. Slang calls a breakup started by one partner "dumping." For a married couple the ending is typically called a separation or [[Divorce|divorce]]. Psychotherapist Susie Orbach (1992) has argued that the end of dating and cohabiting relationships can be as painful as divorce, because such relationships are less socially recognized. Platonic breakups are usually caused by failure to maintain a [[Friendship|friendship]]. In 1976 sociologist Diane Vaughan proposed an "uncoupling theory," in which one partner unconsciously knows the relationship is ending but may hold on for years. Breakups act as acute stressors, often producing [[Insomnia|insomnia]] and [[Depression (mood)|depression]].

=== KEY: Breast_cancer_4c0205
HEADWORD: Breast cancer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcohol (drug) | Biopsy | Chemotherapy | Ionizing radiation | Menopause | Obesity | Radiation therapy
ENTRY:
Cancer of the mammary glands, most commonly arising in cells lining the milk ducts or lobules. Risk factors include [[Obesity|obesity]], lack of exercise, [[Alcohol (drug)|alcohol]] consumption, hormone replacement therapy during [[Menopause|menopause]], [[Ionizing radiation|ionizing radiation]], older age and family history. About five to ten percent of cases result from inherited predisposition, including BRCA mutations. Diagnosis is confirmed by [[Biopsy|biopsy]]. Five-year survival rates are over 90% in the United States and the United Kingdom but lower in developing countries. Treatment may include surgery, [[Radiation therapy|radiation therapy]], [[Chemotherapy|chemotherapy]], hormonal therapy and targeted therapy. Worldwide it is the leading type of cancer in women, accounting for 25% of all cases; in 2018 it caused two million new cases and 627,000 deaths.

Either of two prominences on the upper front of the torso in humans and other [[Primate|primates]]. === KEY: Breastfeeding_bdf9dc
HEADWORD: Breastfeeding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Breast cancer | Cardiovascular disease | Diarrhea | Infant | Rheumatoid arthritis | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Feeding of an [[Infant|infant]] with breast milk, directly from the breast or with milk expressed by a breast pump. The [[World Health Organization|World Health Organization]] (WHO) recommends that breastfeeding begin within the first hour after birth and continue as the baby wants, with exclusive breastfeeding for six months, followed by complementary foods and breastfeeding for up to 2 years and beyond. Exclusive breastfeeding rose from 37.0% to 47.4% between 2012 and 2024. Breastfeeding reduces the risk of respiratory tract infections, ear infections, sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) and [[Diarrhea|diarrhea]] in babies. Increased breastfeeding in low- and middle-income countries could prevent approximately 820,000 deaths of children under five annually. For mothers, it reduces blood loss after delivery and the risk of postpartum depression, and over the long term, the risk of [[Breast cancer|breast cancer]], [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]] and [[Rheumatoid arthritis|rheumatoid arthritis]]. Medical conditions that prevent breastfeeding are rare.

=== KEY: Breathing_bd2879
HEADWORD: Breathing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cellular respiration | Circulatory system | Diffusion | Lung | Oxygen | Thermoregulation | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Rhythmic process of inhaling [[Oxygen|oxygen]]-rich air into the [[Lung|lungs]] and exhaling carbon dioxide, enabling gas exchange. All aerobic organisms require oxygen for [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]], which produces carbon dioxide as a waste product. In [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] with lungs, breathing consists of repeated cycles of inhalation and exhalation through a branched system of airways that lead to the alveoli, where gases move by [[Diffusion|diffusion]]. The [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]] transports the gases between the lungs and the tissues. Under normal conditions, homeostatic mechanisms control the depth and rate of breathing unconsciously, maintaining arterial partial pressures of carbon dioxide and oxygen and thereby keeping extracellular fluid pH stable. Breathing also supports speech and reflexes such as yawning and coughing, and contributes to [[Thermoregulation|thermoregulation]], as in panting. The lungs expand mainly through contraction of the diaphragm and, to a lesser extent, the intercostal muscles, while exhalation at rest is largely passive.

=== KEY: Breathless_1960_film_e533ec
HEADWORD: Breathless
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: François Truffaut | Jean-Luc Godard
ENTRY:
French New Wave crime drama directed by [[Jean-Luc Godard|Jean-Luc Godard]]. *Breathless* (*À bout de souffle*, lit. "Out of Breath") stars Jean-Paul Belmondo as Michel, a wandering criminal, and Jean Seberg as his American girlfriend, Patricia. It was Godard's first feature-length film and brought Belmondo his breakthrough as an actor. The film is an influential example of French New Wave cinema. Along with [[François Truffaut|François Truffaut]]'s *The 400 Blows* and Alain Resnais's *Hiroshima mon amour*, both released a year earlier, it brought international attention to new French filmmaking styles. Its bold visual style included unconventional jump cuts. On its initial release in France, it attracted over two million viewers. A fully restored version was released in the United States in May 2010 to mark the film's 50th anniversary. Critic Pauline Kael called it the most important New Wave film to reach the United States.
