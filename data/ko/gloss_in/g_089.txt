=== KEY: Middlemarch_87d039
HEADWORD: Middlemarch
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: George Eliot
ENTRY:
Novel by [[George Eliot]], the pen name of Mary Ann Evans, published in eight installments from December 1871 to December 1872. Subtitled *A Study of Provincial Life*, it is set in the fictional Midlands town of Middlemarch from 1829 to 1832 and follows many intersecting characters, including Dorothea Brooke, who searches for purpose, and the physician Tertius Lydgate. Its themes include the status of women, the nature of marriage, religion, hypocrisy, political reform and education. It approaches events such as the Reform Act 1832 and the early railways in a realist mode, leavened with comic elements. Its serial form borrowed a method used for Victor Hugo's *Les Misérables*. Initial reviews were mixed, but 70 years later it was considered Eliot's best work and one of the great English novels.

Small [[Fly|fly]] of any of several families of non-mosquito nematoceran Diptera. Midges are found on nearly every land area outside permanently arid deserts and the frigid zones. Some, such as sand flies and [[Black fly|black flies]], are vectors of disease. Others are prey for insectivores, including frogs and [[Swallow|swallows]]. The larvae of some non-biting midges contain [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]] and are called bloodworms.

=== KEY: Migraine_a4964a
HEADWORD: Migraine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ibuprofen | Menopause | Paracetamol | Puberty
ENTRY:
Neurological disorder that causes recurrent moderate-to-severe headaches, often on one side of the head. Attacks are generally associated with nausea, light sensitivity and sound sensitivity, and some begin with an aura, a sensory disturbance. Triggers include lack of sleep, skipped meals and hormonal fluctuations. It is believed to result from a combination of genetic, environmental and neurological factors. Mild to moderate attacks are first treated with over-the-counter pain relievers such as [[Ibuprofen|ibuprofen]] and [[Paracetamol|paracetamol]], and triptans are recommended for moderate to severe attacks. Preventive medications include beta blockers, anticonvulsants and certain antidepressants, while opioids should generally not be used. Approximately 14% (1.16 billion) of people have a migraine in a given year. Women experience more and longer migraines than men, beginning in [[Puberty|puberty]], and migraines tend to decrease after [[Menopause|menopause]].

=== KEY: Migration_Period_0cb7aa
HEADWORD: Migration Period
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Danube | Fall of Constantinople | Goths | Huns | Lombards | Vandals | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
European period of large-scale migrations, c. AD 300–600, also called the Barbarian Invasions. Tribes including the [[Vandals]], [[Goths]], Burgundians, Alemanni and [[Lombards]] migrated into and settled former Roman territories, establishing post-Roman kingdoms. Historians traditionally date the period from the invasion of Europe by the [[Huns]] in about AD 375 to the Lombard conquest of Italy in 568. Estimates for the Gothic crossing of the [[Danube]] in 376 range from 90,000–200,000 people, and total migration into Roman territory may have reached 500,000 to over 1,000,000 people. The fall of the [[Western Roman Empire]] led to competing barbarian kingdoms, while the Eastern Roman Empire survived until the [[Fall of Constantinople|fall of Constantinople]] in 1453. Scholars continue to debate whether migration or internal decline caused Rome's collapse.

=== KEY: Miguel_de_Cervantes_244718
HEADWORD: Cervantes, Miguel de
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Algiers | Don Quixote | Madrid | Novel | Rome
ENTRY:
Spanish novelist, poet, playwright, and soldier. He is best known for the two-part [[Novel]] *[[Don Quixote]]*, widely regarded as the first modern novel and often labeled the "best book of all time" by many authors. His reputation as the greatest writer in the Spanish language is reflected in the common description of Spanish as "the language of Cervantes."

Born in Alcalá de Henares to a surgeon father, he spent much of his life in relative poverty and obscurity, and many of his early works were lost. In 1569 he was forced to leave Spain for [[Rome]], where he worked in a cardinal's household. In 1570 he enlisted in a Spanish Navy infantry regiment and was badly wounded at the Battle of Lepanto in October 1571, losing the use of his left arm and hand. He served as a soldier until 1575, when Barbary pirates captured him. After five years in captivity in [[Algiers]], he was ransomed in 1580 and returned to [[Madrid]].

His first significant novel, *La Galatea*, appeared in 1585, while he worked as a purchasing agent and later as a government tax collector; he was imprisoned in 1597 over fiscal irregularities. Part One of *Don Quixote*, a parody of chivalric romances, was published in 1605 and Part Two in 1615. His other works include the 12 *Novelas ejemplares* (*Exemplary Novels*), the long poem *Viaje del Parnaso* (*Journey to Parnassus*), and *Ocho comedias y ocho entremeses* (*Eight Plays and Eight Interludes*). His final novel, *Los trabajos de Persiles y Sigismunda* (*The Travails of Persiles and Sigismunda*), was published posthumously in 1617. He was buried in the Convento de las Trinitarias Descalzas in Madrid.

=== KEY: Milankovitch_cycles_7b826d

Long-term climate cycles caused by variations in Earth's orbit and tilt. They are named after the Serbian geophysicist and astronomer Milutin Milanković, who in the 1920s gave a more quantitative analysis of James Croll's earlier hypothesis. Changes in orbital eccentricity, [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]] (obliquity) and precession alter the amount and location of solar radiation reaching Earth. Unit of length in the British imperial and U.S. customary systems, equal to 5,280 feet or 1,760 yards. === KEY: Militarism_122699

ENTRY:
National belief in a strong military capability and its aggressive use to expand national interests and values. The term may also imply glorification of the military and the "predominance of the armed forces in the administration or policy of the state." In Germany its roots lie in 18th- and 19th-century [[Prussia]]. === KEY: Military_7d2957
HEADWORD: Military

LINK TARGETS: Air force | Army | Classical antiquity | Navy | Qin Shi Huang | Sovereign state | Subculture | Terracotta Army
ENTRY:
Heavily armed, highly organized force primarily intended for warfare, also called the armed forces. A military is typically authorized and maintained by a [[Sovereign state]], and its members are identifiable by a distinct uniform. It may comprise one or more branches, such as an [[Army]], [[Navy]], [[Air force]], space force, marines, or coast guard. Its main task is usually defined as defending the state and its interests against external armed threats. In technical usage, a distinction is sometimes made in which a country's armed forces may also include paramilitary forces such as armed police.

Beyond warfare, militaries may be employed in other sanctioned and non-sanctioned functions within the state, including internal security, crowd control, emergency services, reconstruction, and ceremonies. A nation's military may function as a discrete social [[Subculture]], with dedicated infrastructure such as housing, schools, utilities, logistics, hospitals, legal services, food production, finance, and banking.

The profession of soldiering is older than recorded history. Images from [[Classical antiquity]] portray the feats of military leaders, and the Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC features in bas-relief monuments from the reign of Ramses II. The first emperor of a unified China, [[Qin Shi Huang]], created the [[Terracotta Army]] to represent his military might.

Militaries evolved from ad hoc tribal groups into structured organizations by antiquity, and professional standing armies emerged prominently in Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries. Core functions today include deterring potential aggressors, projecting power, and achieving superiority in battle. One analysis of engagements from 1600 to 1973 found that numerical advantage, leadership quality, and unit cohesion were decisive, more so than technology alone. Recurring controversies include coups in unstable states and debates over procurement inefficiencies.

=== KEY: Military_aircraft_5d0c8b
HEADWORD: Military aircraft
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Fixed-wing or rotary-wing aircraft operated by a military force. Combat aircraft, such as [[Fighter aircraft|fighters]] and [[Bomber|bombers]], are designed to destroy enemy equipment or personnel with their own ordnance. Non-combat aircraft, such as transports and tankers, mainly serve support roles, though they may carry weapons for self-defense. Balloons were used for observation from 1794, when the French Aerostatic Corps flew one at the Battle of Fleurus, and they continued in the [[Napoleonic Wars]]. In the [[Cold War]] era, jets exceeded Mach 2 and armament shifted mainly to missiles. Stealth aircraft entered development in the 1970s and saw combat in the 1980s.

=== KEY: Military_education_and_training_4deb1d
HEADWORD: Military education and training
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Military
ENTRY:
Process of establishing and improving the capabilities of [[Military|military]] personnel in their roles. Training may be voluntary or compulsory, and in some countries it forms part of compulsory education. It begins with recruit training, which uses conditioning techniques to resocialize trainees so that they obey all orders without hesitation, and teaches basic military skills. === KEY: Military_engineering_e09ac9
HEADWORD: Military engineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Battering ram | Catapult | Civil engineering | Logistics | Vitruvius
ENTRY:
Art, science and practice of designing and building military works and maintaining military transport and communication lines. Military engineers are also responsible for [[Logistics|logistics]] behind military tactics, and the discipline is a predecessor of [[Civil engineering]]. The word "engineer" was first used in warfare around c. 1380 to mean "a constructor of military engines". The Romans had a dedicated corps of military engineers, the architecti, who built a double wall of fortifications 30 miles (48 km) long to encircle Alesia in just six weeks in 52 BC. [[Vitruvius]] is the best known of these Roman army engineers. Military engineers also built siege machines such as [[Catapult|catapults]] and [[Battering ram|battering rams]]. In modern armies, soldiers trained to work under fire are called combat engineers.

=== KEY: Military_exercise_38aaa9
HEADWORD: Military exercise
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Warsaw Pact
ENTRY:
Employment of military resources in training for military operations. Exercises test tactics and strategies without actual combat and ensure the combat readiness of forces before deployment. War games differ mainly in that they do not involve actual military forces; they use artificial players and models to consider multiple possible outcomes, not to predict results. Joint exercises involve several branches of one military, while combined, coalition, bilateral or multilateral exercises involve two or more countries. A 2021 study found that exercises within well-defined alliances usually deter adversaries, while exercises outside alliances, which are extremely rare, usually lead to conflict escalation. Exercises have sometimes served as cover for invasions, such as the [[Warsaw Pact]] invasion of Czechoslovakia and the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine.

=== KEY: Military_history_d6ecc8
HEADWORD: Military history
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: War
ENTRY:
Study of armed conflict in human history and its impact on societies, cultures, and economies, together with the resulting changes in local and international relationships. It examines the organization of armed forces, strategic and tactical developments, and the broader social contexts of conflict, treating [[War]] as a driver of technological innovation, state formation, and geopolitical change.

Its essential subjects are the causes of war, the social and cultural foundations of the forces involved, military doctrine on each side, logistics, leadership, technology, strategy, and tactics, and how these changed over time. Just war theory, by contrast, explores the moral dimensions of warfare and seeks to establish a doctrine of military ethics that limits war's destructive reality. Defining conflicts include the Assyrian conquests, Roman expansions, Mongol invasions, and the World Wars.

Military history is taught at academies and service schools as an applied field, because commands aim to prevent past errors and improve performance by training commanders to recognize historical parallels. The Combat Studies Institute, when certifying instructors, deemphasizes rote memorization of detail in favor of themes and context relevant to current and future conflict, under the motto "Past is Prologue."

The discipline changes with military forces, notably during the Industrial Revolution and the nuclear and information ages. The Revolution in Military Affairs (RMA) is a concept that tries to explain how emerging technologies, such as gunpowder, have shaped warfare, highlighting short outbursts of rapid change followed by periods of relative stability. Within the field, debate continues between "operational" military history and a "new" military history that emphasizes cultural and social factors; critics of the latter argue that it dilutes attention to combat outcomes.

=== KEY: Military_industrial_complex_b48272
HEADWORD: Military–industrial complex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dwight D. Eisenhower | Vietnam War
ENTRY:
Relationship between a country's military and the defense industry that supplies it, seen as a vested interest that influences public policy. Both sides benefit: one obtains weapons, and the other is paid to supply them. The concept is associated mainly with the United States. The phrase is often linked to [[Dwight D. Eisenhower]], whose January 17, 1961, farewell address warned against "unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought" by the complex, although he was not the first to use it. The first known use was by Winfield W. Riefler in 1947. The phrase rose to prominence after the address, as part of opposition to the [[Vietnam War]]. The term has also been applied to Russia, especially since the beginning of the Russo-Ukrainian war.

=== KEY: Military_organization_27bdf9
HEADWORD: Military organization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air force | Army | Infantry | Navy
ENTRY:
Structuring of a state's armed forces to provide the military capability that national defense policy requires. Formal military organization tends to be hierarchical. In most countries the armed forces are divided into three branches: [[Army|army]], [[Navy|navy]] and [[Air force|air force]]. Some countries add cyber, medical, logistics, space or marine forces as independent services. Executive control is typically exercised by a ministry of defense, which in democracies is headed by an elected minister. The Roman Army used formal ranks, organized into legions of around 5,000 soldiers led by a legate and divided into centuries led by centurions. Most smaller countries have a single organization encompassing all their armed forces. Armies of developing countries tend to consist primarily of [[Infantry|infantry]], while developed countries' armies have larger units manning expensive equipment.

=== KEY: Military_rank_c766f5
HEADWORD: Military rank
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Battle of Marathon | Red Army
ENTRY:
System of hierarchical relationships within armed forces, police and other institutions organized along military lines. Rank defines authority and responsibility within the chain of command, and it also sets pay grade, so that responsibility and pay rise together. Uniform insignia usually denote rank. Ranking systems have long helped logistics, command and coordination, and as operations grew larger, more ranks were created. Communist states have abolished ranks on several occasions, including the [[Red Army|Soviet Red Army]] from 1918 to 1935, but they re-established them after encountering operational difficulties. In Athens, ten generals (*strategoi*) were elected annually from 501 BC, one for each tribe; the position was an elected office rather than a military rank. The generals decided the battle plan at the [[Battle of Marathon]] in 490 BC by majority vote.

=== KEY: Military_reserve_ad0d9b
HEADWORD: Military reserve
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Red Army | Russian Armed Forces | World War II
ENTRY:
Units held back from battle so that they remain available for unforeseen situations or sudden opportunities. Reserves may defend against attack, be committed where the enemy exposes a vulnerability, or relieve troops already fighting. Their size depends on the commander's uncertainty about the enemy's intentions, a "hedge against uncertainty". Reserves exist at tactical, operational and strategic levels, and a reserve for one headquarters may not be one for a higher headquarters. The British Reserve Army of World War I was to carry out the breakthrough phase of the Somme offensive. The Reserve of the Supreme High Command was the principal reserve of the [[Red Army|Soviet Red Army]] in [[World War II]]; it is now part of the [[Russian Armed Forces]]. A military reserve differs from a military reserve force, a long-term part-time organization of reservists.

=== KEY: Military_science_58c17e
HEADWORD: Military science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Industrial Revolution | Napoleonic Wars | World War II
ENTRY:
Study of military processes, institutions and behavior, and of the theory and application of organized coercive force. It focuses on the theory, method and practice of producing military capability consistent with national defense policy, and it identifies the strategic, political, economic and tactical elements that sustain military advantage. During the [[Industrial Revolution]] the term covered all matters of military theory and technology as a single academic discipline. In military education, departments with this name train junior officers rather than military scientists. Until the early 19th century, a British veteran of the [[Napoleonic Wars]] thought little had changed since the Greeks. Clausewitz argued that, unlike in other sciences or arts, "in war the object reacts." Operations research techniques pioneered in [[World War II]] optimized convoy routing and bombing patterns.

=== KEY: Military_strategy_6c88bb
HEADWORD: Military strategy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl von Clausewitz | Chanakya | Military tactics | Sun Tzu
ENTRY:
Set of ideas used by military organizations to pursue strategic goals. The term derives from the Greek *strategos*, and in the 18th century it was used narrowly for the "art of the general," or the arrangement of troops. [[Carl von Clausewitz]] defined military strategy as "the employment of battles to gain the end of war." B. H. Liddell Hart defined it as "the art of distributing and applying military means to fulfill the ends of policy," with less emphasis on battles. Strategy is broader than [[Military tactics|tactics]], which concern the disposition and maneuver of units on a battlefield. [[Sun Tzu]] is often considered the father of Eastern military strategy, which differs from the Western by focusing more on asymmetric warfare and deception. [[Chanakya]]'s *Arthashastra* has been an important compendium in Indian and Asian history.

=== KEY: Military_tactics_0b85af
HEADWORD: Military tactics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Civil War | Crimean War | Machine gun | World War II
ENTRY:
Art of organizing and employing fighting forces on or near the battlefield. Tactics involve four battlefield functions: firepower, mobility, protection or security, and shock action. They are separate from command and control and from logistics, and they form the lowest of three levels of warfare, below the operational and strategic levels. Firepower advanced from melee weapons and bows to artillery, the rifled musket of the [[Crimean War]] and [[American Civil War]], and [[Machine gun|machine guns]] at the turn of the 20th century. For most of history, armies could not travel more than 32 km (20 mi) a day unless on rivers. Tanks in World War I improved mobility, and full tactical mobility was achieved in [[World War II]]. Field obstacles, often created by military engineers, can still limit tactical mobility.

=== KEY: Military_technology_e95ecb
HEADWORD: Military technology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Assyria | Bronze Age | Cavalry | Song dynasty
ENTRY:
Application of technology to warfare, comprising technology that is distinctly military in nature. Such technology usually lacks civilian uses or is dangerous without military training, though the line is porous, and civilian innovations have often been put to military use. Many new technologies arose from military funding of science. Armament engineering draws on mechanical, electrical, aerospace, materials and chemical engineering. Large-scale analyses of pre-industrial societies identify population growth, interregional connectivity, and enabling technologies such as iron and cavalry as major drivers of innovation. Humans in the [[Bronze Age]] learned to smelt copper with tin to make weapons. The [[Assyria|Assyrians]] are credited with introducing horse [[Cavalry|cavalry]] and extensive use of iron weapons by 1100 BCE. The [[Song dynasty]]'s *Wujing Zongyao* (*Essentials of the Military Arts*), written around 1043, covers strategy, training and advanced weaponry.

=== KEY: Militia_e4a1ba
HEADWORD: Militia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conscription | Russia | Soviet Union | United States
ENTRY:
Military or paramilitary force of civilian members rather than a professional standing army of regular, full-time personnel. Militias may be raised in times of need to support regular troops or to serve as a pool of manpower for them. Acting alone, they are generally unable to hold ground against regular forces, so they commonly skirmish, hold fortifications or conduct irregular warfare, though they may also defend a community's territory, property and laws. In [[Russia]] and some countries of the former [[Soviet Union]], an official reserve army of citizen soldiers is known as the militsiya.

=== KEY: Milk_e89b2c
HEADWORD: Milk
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calcium | Cattle | Colorectal cancer | Dairy | India | Lactose | Pasteurization
ENTRY:
Usually white liquid food produced by the mammary glands of lactating mammals. It is the primary source of nutrition for young mammals before they can digest solid food. It is an emulsion of fat globules in a watery solution of proteins, carbohydrates (mainly [[Lactose]]), minerals, and vitamins. Bovine milk provides approximately 3.3 grams of protein per 100 milliliters, along with 3.7% fat, 4.8% lactose, and nutrients such as [[Calcium]] and vitamin D. The first milk, colostrum, contains antibodies that strengthen the immune system.

As an agricultural product, milk is collected from farm animals, mostly [[Cattle]], on a [[Dairy]]. Humans use it as a drink and as the base ingredient of dairy products. More than six billion people worldwide consume milk and milk products. In 2011, dairy farms produced around 730 million tonnes of milk from 260 million dairy cows; global production reached 965.7 million tonnes in 2023. [[Pasteurization]] reduces pathogens, though advocates of raw milk cite retained enzymes. Cohort studies link moderate dairy intake to greater bone mineral density and lower risks of [[Colorectal cancer]], while evidence for prostate cancer risk at high intakes is mixed. Health authorities consider dairy beneficial but not essential, since its nutrients are available from other sources, including fortified plant-based milks.

=== KEY: Milky_Way_84d9f6
HEADWORD: Milky Way
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Barred spiral galaxy | Dark matter | Edwin Hubble | Galileo Galilei | Laniakea Supercluster | Local Group | Sagittarius A* | Solar System
ENTRY:
[[Barred spiral galaxy]] that includes the [[Solar System]]; also called the Milky Way Galaxy or simply the Galaxy. The name describes its appearance from Earth: a hazy band of light in the night sky formed by stars in other arms of the galaxy, too distant to be distinguished individually by the naked eye.

[[Galileo Galilei]] first resolved the band into individual stars with his telescope in 1610. Until the early 1920s most astronomers thought it contained all the stars in the universe. After the 1920 Great Debate between Harlow Shapley and Heber Doust Curtis, observations by [[Edwin Hubble]] in 1923 showed it to be only one of many galaxies.

The galaxy has a D25 isophotal diameter estimated at 26.8 ± 1.1 kiloparsecs (87,400 ± 3,600 light-years), and is surrounded by a dark matter area whose total diameter may approach 613 kpc (2 million light-years). The stars in the innermost 10,000 light-years form a bulge, from which the bar radiates. At the center is the intense radio source [[Sagittarius A*]], a supermassive black hole with a mass of 4.100 (±0.034) million solar masses. It is estimated to contain 100–400 billion stars. Its total mass, including [[Dark matter]], is estimated at 1.5 trillion solar masses.

The galaxy is estimated to have formed around 13.6 billion years ago and has grown through mergers with smaller galaxies. The Solar System lies on the inner edge of the Orion Arm, about 27,000 light-years (8.3 kpc) from the Galactic Center, and takes about 240 million years to orbit it. The Milky Way has several satellite galaxies and belongs to the [[Local Group]], part of the Virgo Supercluster, itself a component of the [[Laniakea Supercluster]].

=== KEY: Millipede_11edab

ENTRY:
Arthropod of the class Diplopoda, with two pairs of jointed legs on most body segments. ENTRY:
Philosophical view that mental phenomena are non-physical, or that mind and body are distinct and separable. It is contrasted with [[Materialism|physicalism]], [[Monism|monism]] and enactivism in the mind–body problem. [[Plato]] held that the soul was not dependent on the physical body and believed in metempsychosis, the migration of the soul to a new body. [[Aristotle]] described a hierarchy of souls, of which only the intellective part of mind was immortal. Dualism is most closely associated with [[René Descartes]], who in *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641) held that the mind is a nonphysical, non-spatial substance and identified it with consciousness and self-awareness. Most substance dualists hold that mind and body can causally affect each other, a view called interactionism. Critics argue that dualism cannot explain how an immaterial mind affects a physical body.

=== KEY: Mind_d721fb
HEADWORD: Mind
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Imagination | Latin | Memory | Mental disorder | Middle English | Old English | Perception | Philosophy of mind
ENTRY:
Totality of psychological phenomena and capacities, encompassing both conscious and unconscious states. The mind is that which thinks, feels, perceives, imagines, remembers, and wills. Conscious processes make an individual aware of external and internal circumstances; unconscious processes can influence an individual without intention or awareness. Its exact nature is disputed: some characterizations stress internal aspects, saying that the mind transforms information and is not directly accessible to outside observers, while others understand mental phenomena as dispositions to engage in observable behavior. In a narrow sense the term covers only cognitive functions associated with perception, reasoning, awareness, and memory; in a broader sense it also includes feeling, motivation, and behavior.

The English word derives from [[Old English]] *gemynd*, meaning "memory", which gave rise to [[Middle English]] forms and slowly expanded to cover all mental capacities. Cognates include the ancient Greek μένος, the [[Latin]] *mens*, and the Sanskrit *manas*. Psyche and mentality are usually treated as synonyms, while soul, spirit, cognition, intellect, intelligence, and brain overlap with mind without meaning exactly the same. The brain is the physical organ responsible for most or all mental functions.

The mind encompasses [[Perception|perception]], [[Memory|memory]], thought, [[Imagination|imagination]], motivation, emotion, attention, learning, and consciousness. Perception interprets and organizes sensory information and is shaped by past experiences, cultural background, beliefs, knowledge, and expectations. Episodic memory concerns specific past events, semantic memory general knowledge, and procedural memory how to do things; short-term memory holds information briefly, while long-term memory can last a lifetime. Thinking is goal-oriented processing of information and includes logical reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making; some theorists hold that all thought happens through language. Imagination internally generates mental images and ideas without direct stimulation of the sense organs.

The mind–body problem is the challenge of explaining the relation between matter and mind. Traditionally the two were often thought of as distinct substances; René Descartes's 17th-century dualism conceived the mind as a non-spatial, spiritual substance interacting with the body through the pineal gland. The dominant philosophical position since the 20th century has been physicalism, which holds that everything is material, so that minds are aspects or features of certain material objects. The evolutionary history of the mind is tied to the development of nervous systems and brains, with particular brain areas dedicated to specific functions. Some people are affected by [[Mental disorder|mental disorders]], in which certain mental capacities do not function as they should. It is widely accepted that at least some non-human animals have some form of mind, but which animals is controversial, and theorists also discuss creating artificial minds with computers.

The main fields studying the mind are psychology, neuroscience, cognitive science, and [[Philosophy of mind|philosophy of mind]], which use methods ranging from empirical observation and neuroimaging to conceptual analysis and thought experiments.

=== KEY: Mineral_9637bb
HEADWORD: Mineral
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amethyst | Earth's crust | Ice | Mercury (element) | Olivine | Opal | Quartz | Rock (geology)
ENTRY:
Naturally occurring solid with a fairly well-defined chemical composition and a specific crystal structure. The geological definition normally excludes compounds that occur only in living organisms. A mineral is distinct from a [[Rock (geology)|rock]], which may consist of one mineral or an aggregate of several.

The International Mineralogical Association (IMA), the standard body for naming mineral species, recognizes 6,228 species. It requires that a mineral be formed by natural geological processes, be solid in its natural occurrence, and have a well-defined crystallographic structure and fairly well-defined composition. Substances made only by human activity or in living beings, such as tungsten carbide and seashells, are excluded. Native [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] is a classified exception, because it was included before the current rules. Water [[Ice|ice]] is a mineral; carbon dioxide is not. Amorphous materials such as [[Opal|opal]] and obsidian are called mineraloids.

A compound with different crystal structures yields different species: [[Quartz|quartz]] and stishovite are both silicon dioxide. Composition may vary within a species, as in mackinawite, (Fe,Ni)₉S₈. Continuous series are sometimes split arbitrarily, as in the [[Olivine|olivine]] group, whose end-members forsterite and fayalite count as separate minerals. Varieties can have their own names; [[Amethyst|amethyst]] is a purple variety of quartz.

Descriptions include hardness, luster, color, streak, cleavage, magnetism, and reaction to acid. Minerals are classified by key chemical constituents, chiefly under the Dana and Strunz systems. Groups include native elements, sulfides (galena, PbS), oxides, halides (rock salt, NaCl), carbonates (calcite, CaCO₃), sulfates, phosphates, and silicates, which make up approximately 90% of [[Earth's crust|Earth's crust]].

=== KEY: Mineralogy_c6f72e
HEADWORD: Mineralogy
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Branch of [[Geology|geology]] concerned with the chemistry, crystal structure and physical properties of minerals and mineralized artifacts. It also studies how minerals form, how they are classified, where they occur and how they are used. Early writing on minerals, especially on gemstones, came from Babylonia, the Greco-Roman world, China, India and the Islamic world. [[Pliny the Elder]]'s *Natural History* described many minerals and their properties. The modern discipline grew out of [[Crystallography|crystallography]] and the microscopic study of rock sections. In 1814 [[Jöns Jacob Berzelius]] introduced a classification of minerals based on their chemistry rather than their crystal structure. In 1912 Max von Laue demonstrated [[X-ray|X-ray]] diffraction, which was developed into a tool for analyzing crystal structure by [[William Henry Bragg]] and William Lawrence Bragg. Today the field overlaps with [[Materials science]] and solid-state physics.

=== KEY: Mining_efdd60
HEADWORD: Mining
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aqueduct (water supply) | Athens | Coal | Eswatini | Ore | Philip II of Macedon
ENTRY:
Extraction of valuable minerals and other geological materials from the Earth. Mining is required to obtain most materials that cannot be grown through agriculture or feasibly made artificially. Products include metals, [[Coal|coal]], gemstones, limestone, rock salt, potash, gravel, and clay; in a wider sense the term covers non-renewable resources such as petroleum and natural gas. An [[Ore|ore]] must contain a valuable constituent that can be extracted and sold for profit. Modern mining involves prospecting, assessing a proposed mine's profit potential, extraction, and reclamation of the land after closure.

The oldest known mine is the Ngwenya Mine in [[Eswatini]], about 41,000–43,000 years old according to radiocarbon dating, where Paleolithic people mined hematite to make red ochre. Flint mines such as Grimes Graves and Krzemionki are mostly Neolithic (c. 4000–3000 BC). The gold mines of Nubia were among the most extensive in ancient Egypt. The silver mines of Laurium, worked by over 20,000 slaves, helped support [[Athens]], and [[Philip II of Macedon]] captured the gold mines of Mount Pangeo in 357 BC to fund his campaigns. The Romans developed large-scale methods, using water brought by [[Aqueduct (water supply)|aqueducts]] for hydraulic mining.

Mining is associated with land degradation, water contamination, habitat loss, occupational health risks, and community displacement. Most nations regulate it, but governments frequently fail to enforce the rules fully, because mining often generates much of the business in remote or economically depressed communities. Unregulated or illegal mining, especially in developing economies, frequently contributes to human rights violations and environmental conflicts. Where enforced, modern practices have significantly improved safety.

Unlike a hallucination, a mirage is a real phenomenon that can be photographed, because light actually forms the false image at the observer's location. === KEY: Missile_e6eb77
HEADWORD: Missile
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | Jet engine | Maratha Empire | Nazi Germany | Rocket engine | V-2 rocket | World War II
ENTRY:
Airborne ranged weapon capable of self-propelled flight, usually aided by a propellant, [[Jet engine|jet engine]] or [[Rocket engine|rocket engine]]. Missiles are classified by launch source and target as surface-to-surface, air-to-surface, surface-to-air or air-to-air, and they typically have targeting, guidance, flight, engine and warhead systems. Rockets preceded missiles, and in the 18th century iron-cased rockets were used in India by the Kingdom of Mysore and [[Maratha Empire]] against the British. The first operational missiles were rocket-based weapons developed by [[Nazi Germany]] in [[World War II]], including the V-1 and [[V-2 rocket|V-2]]. The [[Cold War]] and the development of nuclear weapons then spurred faster, more accurate missiles with longer range. Since the 1980s, attempts to control the spread of long-range missiles, such as the Missile Technology Control Regime (1987), have been voluntary.

[[U.S. state]] in the [[Deep South]] region of the United States. It became the 20th state on December 10, 1817, and takes its name from the [[Mississippi River]], which forms its western boundary. === KEY: Mississippi_River_575ce5
HEADWORD: Mississippi River
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Civil War | Gulf of Mexico | Hernando de Soto | Levee | Louisiana Purchase | Mark Twain | Missouri River | Ojibwe
ENTRY:
River, central United States, flowing from northern Minnesota to the [[Gulf of Mexico]]. It is the primary river of the largest drainage basin in the U.S. and the second-longest in the country after the [[Missouri River|Missouri]]. From its traditional source at Lake Itasca it flows generally south for 2,340 miles (3,770 km) to the Mississippi River Delta. Its watershed drains all or parts of 32 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces; the total drainage basin is 1,151,000 square miles (2,980,000 sq km). It ranks as the world's tenth-largest river by discharge and the largest in North America. The name comes from *Misi-ziibi* ("Great River"), the Anishinaabe ([[Ojibwe]]) name rendered by the French as *Misi zipi*.

The river is divided into the Upper Mississippi, from the headwaters to the confluence with the Missouri at St. Louis; the Middle Mississippi, from there to the Ohio; and the Lower Mississippi, from the Ohio to the Gulf. Between Lake Itasca and St. Louis, 43 dams moderate the flow.

Native Americans have lived along the river for thousands of years; some, such as the Mound Builders, formed agricultural and urban civilizations. [[Hernando de Soto]] reached it in 1541. It formed borders for New Spain, New France, and the early United States. After the [[Louisiana Purchase]] in 1803 it and its tributaries became pathways for westward expansion, and it became the subject of American literature, notably the writings of [[Mark Twain]]. Its final capture by Union forces in the [[American Civil War]] marked a turning point toward Union victory.

In the 20th century, massive engineering works such as [[Levee|levees]], locks, and dams were built, partly to keep the lower river from shifting into the Atchafalaya River channel and bypassing New Orleans. Agricultural runoff is the primary contributor to the Gulf of Mexico dead zone.
