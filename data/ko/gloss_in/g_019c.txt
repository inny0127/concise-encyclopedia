=== KEY: British_Empire_f9ea6b
HEADWORD: British Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Revolution | Commonwealth of Nations | East India Company | Napoleonic Wars | Suez Crisis | World War I | World War II
ENTRY:
Dominions, colonies, protectorates, mandates, and other territories ruled or administered by the United Kingdom and its predecessor states. By 1913 it held sway over 412 million people, 23 percent of the world population, and by 1920 it covered 35.5 million sq km, 24 percent of the Earth's land area.

Britain became a major power in the Indian subcontinent after the [[East India Company]] conquered Mughal Bengal at the Battle of Plassey in 1757. The [[American Revolution]] cost it some of its oldest and most populous colonies by 1783, and expansion turned toward Asia, Africa, and the Pacific. After the defeat of France in the [[Napoleonic Wars]] (1803–15), Britain was the principal naval and imperial power of the 19th century. It abolished the slave trade in 1807 and slavery itself across its territories in 1833.

The empire's growth involved military campaigns, resource extraction, and policies that exacerbated famines and provoked local resistance, and historians debate the balance between exploitation and modernization in colonial rule.

The empire reached its largest extent after [[World War I]]. In [[World War II]], its colonies in East and Southeast Asia were occupied by Japan. India, its most valuable and populous possession, became independent in 1947, and most other territories followed. The [[Suez Crisis]] of 1956 confirmed Britain's decline as a global power, and the handover of Hong Kong to China on July 1, 1997, symbolized for many its end. Fourteen overseas territories remain British, and many former colonies joined the [[Commonwealth of Nations]].

=== KEY: British_Hong_Kong_1bc3b7

British colony in East Asia (1841–1997). Hong Kong Island was occupied in 1841 and ceded to Britain in perpetuity under the Treaty of Nanking of 1842, which followed the [[First Opium War]]. The Kowloon Peninsula was added in 1860, and the New Territories were leased for 99 years in 1898. The [[Empire of Japan]] occupied the territory from 1941 to 1945. Under the 1984 Sino-British Joint Declaration, Britain agreed to return the territory to China, which took place in July 1997, when Hong Kong became a special administrative region.

=== KEY: British_Indian_Ocean_Territory_3cffa9
HEADWORD: British Indian Ocean Territory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atoll | Diego Garcia | Donald Trump | International Court of Justice | Mauritius
ENTRY:
British Overseas Territory in the Indian Ocean, comprising seven [[Atoll|atolls]] of the Chagos Archipelago with a total land area of 60 square kilometers (23 square miles). The largest island, [[Diego Garcia]], hosts a joint military facility of the United Kingdom and the United States. Britain separated the archipelago from the Crown Colony of [[Mauritius]] in 1965. Between 1968 and 1973 the Chagossians, then about 2,000 people, were expelled to Mauritius and Seychelles, and the British government has repeatedly denied them the right of return. Mauritius claims sovereignty, and a 2019 advisory opinion of the [[International Court of Justice]] called for the islands to be given to Mauritius. A 2025 treaty would have transferred sovereignty to Mauritius while leasing the Diego Garcia base to Britain for 99 years, but its ratification was suspended after backlash from U.S. President [[Donald Trump]] in 2026.

=== KEY: British_Museum_c9534c

Public museum of human history, art and culture in the Bloomsbury area of London. Established by Act of Parliament in 1753, it was the world's first public national museum and opened to the public in 1759 at Montagu House. Fourteen dependent territories of the United Kingdom that lie outside the British Islands. === KEY: British_Raj_0505b9
HEADWORD: British Raj
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: East India Company | India | Indian Rebellion of 1857 | Myanmar | Pakistan
ENTRY:
Period of Crown rule in India, from 1858 to 1947, when the British Crown governed the Indian subcontinent. It was instituted on June 28, 1858, after the [[Indian Rebellion of 1857]] transferred power from the [[East India Company]] to the Crown. Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India in 1876. Directly administered areas were called British India, while the princely states were ruled by indigenous rulers under British paramountcy. The Raj covered almost all of present-day [[India]], [[Pakistan]], Bangladesh and [[Myanmar]]. In 1947 it was partitioned into the Union of India and the Dominion of Pakistan. Its economic legacy, including the so-called drain of wealth to Britain, is debated.

=== KEY: British_colonization_of_the_Americas_2d9e52

English and later British settlement of North America, the Caribbean and parts of Central and South America, from the late 16th century. Sir Walter Raleigh established the short-lived [[Roanoke Colony]] in 1585. The first permanent English colony in the Americas was founded at [[Jamestown]], Virginia, in 1607, and the English later established colonies in the Caribbean, including [[Barbados]] and [[Jamaica]]. Britain continued to colonize in the 19th century, taking control of [[British Columbia]] and establishing the Falkland Islands colony. Most remaining colonies gained independence between 1962 and 1983, and many joined the [[Commonwealth of Nations]].

=== KEY: Broadcasting_60ea88
HEADWORD: Broadcasting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cable television | Guglielmo Marconi | John Logie Baird | Philo Farnsworth | Radio wave | Vacuum tube | World War I
ENTRY:
Distribution of audio and audiovisual content to dispersed audiences through an electronic mass communications medium, typically by [[Radio wave|radio waves]] in a one-to-many model. Broadcasting began with AM radio, which became popular around 1920 with the spread of [[Vacuum tube|vacuum tube]] transmitters and receivers.

The term comes from the agricultural method of sowing seeds by casting them broadly. Applications to one-to-many radio transmissions appeared as early as 1898. Over-the-air, or terrestrial, broadcasting is usually associated with radio and television and in most countries requires a license. Transmission by wire, as in [[Cable television|cable television]], also counts, and since the 2000s streaming has increasingly been called broadcasting as well. U.S. federal regulations define broadcasting as transmissions intended for reception by the general public, so amateur and citizens band radio operators may not broadcast.

In 1894 Italian inventor [[Guglielmo Marconi]] began developing wireless communication using radio waves, and by 1901 had shown they could cross the Atlantic. In 1904 a commercial service began sending nightly news summaries to ships. [[World War I]] accelerated military radio, and commercial AM broadcasting began in the 1920s. Stereo FM broadcasting began in the 1930s in the United States and the 1970s in the United Kingdom.

[[John Logie Baird]] demonstrated moving pictures at Selfridges in London on March 25, 1925, and the British Broadcasting Corporation began experimental broadcasts on September 30, 1929. Most 20th-century televisions used the cathode-ray tube; a promising version was produced by [[Philo Farnsworth]] and demonstrated on September 7, 1927. Satellite broadcasting began in the 1960s, and in the 2000s broadcasters switched from analog to digital signals.

Edible green plant of the [[Brassicaceae|cabbage family]], grown for its large flower heads and stalk. === KEY: Bromeliaceae_c1bc2a
HEADWORD: Bromeliaceae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Guiana Shield | Pineapple | Spanish moss
ENTRY:
Family of monocot flowering plants, known as bromeliads, with about 80 genera and 3,700 known species. Nearly all are native to the tropical Americas, with one species in tropical West Africa. The family includes epiphytes such as [[Spanish moss]] and terrestrial species such as the [[Pineapple]]. Many "tank bromeliads" store water in tanks formed by their tightly overlapping leaf bases, and some species are desert succulents. The largest is *Puya raimondii*, whose flower spike can reach 9–10 meters. The family is thought to have originated in the tepuis of the [[Guiana Shield]] about 100 million years ago. Many species use crassulacean acid metabolism (CAM) photosynthesis, opening their stomata at night to reduce water loss.

=== KEY: Bromine_7fcff1
HEADWORD: Bromine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chlorine | Halogen | Iodine | Ozone depletion
ENTRY:
Chemical element with the symbol Br and atomic number 35, a volatile red-brown liquid at standard conditions and a member of the [[Halogen|halogen]] group. Its properties lie between those of [[Chlorine]] and [[Iodine]]. The name derives from the Greek *bromos*, meaning "stench." It was isolated independently by Carl Jacob Löwig in 1825 and Antoine Jérôme Balard in 1826. Because it is very reactive, bromine does not occur free in nature, and it is extracted commercially from brine evaporation ponds, mostly in the United States and Israel. More than half of the bromine produced worldwide each year is used as fire retardants, and the pesticide methyl bromide is no longer used because of [[Ozone depletion]]. Bromine is an essential trace element for collagen development in all animals.

=== KEY: Bronchitis_f7fdbd
HEADWORD: Bronchitis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease | Paracetamol | Tobacco smoking
ENTRY:
Inflammation of the bronchi, the large and medium-sized airways of the lungs, causing coughing. It usually begins as an infection of the nose, ears, throat or sinuses. Acute bronchitis, also called a chest cold, causes a cough that usually lasts about three weeks, and in more than 90% of cases the cause is a viral infection. Treatment typically involves rest, [[Paracetamol|paracetamol]] and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Chronic bronchitis is defined as a productive cough lasting three months or more per year for at least two years. [[Tobacco smoking]] is its most common cause, and many patients also have [[Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]] (COPD). Treatment includes quitting smoking, vaccinations, rehabilitation, and inhaled bronchodilators and steroids.

Polish-born anthropologist and ethnologist whose fieldwork in the Trobriand Islands helped establish modern ethnography. He popularized participant observation as a method. === KEY: Bronze_Age_b9e183
HEADWORD: Bronze Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze | Chalcolithic | Iron Age | Late Bronze Age collapse | Neolithic | Stone Age | Sumer
ENTRY:
Period in the development of material culture among ancient societies in Asia, the Near East, and Europe, marked by the use of [[Bronze|bronze]]. It is the middle period of the three-age system, following the [[Stone Age]] and preceding the [[Iron Age]]; a transition, the [[Chalcolithic]], separates it from the [[Neolithic]]. The Bronze Age had begun in much of the Old World by 3,000 BC. In the Near East it ran c. 3300–1200 BC, in Europe from about 2500 BC to 800 BC, and in East Asia it began around 2000 BC.

Bronze Age cultures were the first to develop writing: cuneiform in Mesopotamia and hieroglyphs in Egypt. The Americas are conventionally studied under a five-period system with no Bronze Age.

Bronze was harder and more durable than other metals then available. Iron is abundant, but smelting it requires 1,250 °C, and it was beyond common use until the end of the 2nd millennium BC. Tin occurs in few places, unlike copper, so bronze often stayed rare and expensive, and some societies used it mainly for weapons or elite art. The Maykop culture of the North Caucasus produced the oldest-known bronze, an arsenical bronze, by the mid-4th millennium BC.

The Near East entered the Bronze Age first, with the rise of [[Sumer]] in the mid-4th millennium BC. Its cultures developed city-states, written law codes, and the potter's wheel. The Hittites founded an empire in the 18th century BC at Hattusa, reaching its height in the 14th century BC. Widespread disruption in the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BC, the [[Late Bronze Age collapse]], ended the age there.

=== KEY: Bronze_a0df72
HEADWORD: Bronze
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Arsenic | Bronze Age | Copper | Cornwall | Iron Age | Tin
ENTRY:
[[Alloy]] consisting primarily of [[Copper|copper]], commonly with about 12–12.5% [[Tin|tin]]. Added metals such as aluminum, manganese, nickel, or zinc, and sometimes phosphorus, arsenic, or silicon, give alloys harder than copper alone or with other useful properties.

The period in which bronze was the hardest metal in widespread use is the [[Bronze Age]]. It began in western Eurasia in the mid-4th millennium BC and in China in the early 2nd millennium BC, and was followed by the [[Iron Age]], which started about 1300 BC. The earliest known tin-copper artifact, dated to c. 4650 BC, comes from the Vinča culture site at Pločnik, Serbia. Copper–[[Arsenic|arsenic]] alloys, stronger and with a lower melting point than pure copper, were made earlier; the earliest known artifacts, from Tal-i-Iblis on the Iranian plateau, date to 3800–3400 BC. Tin became the major non-copper ingredient in the late 3rd millennium BC; tin bronze was easier to control and cast and, unlike arsenic, not toxic.

Copper and tin ores are seldom found together, with exceptions such as [[Cornwall]], so bronze work has always involved long-distance trade. Cornish tin was traded as far as Phoenicia. An analysis of 324 metallic finds of the Indus Valley Civilisation (2600–1900 BC) found 67 (20%) with some tin content.

Bronze has a Vickers hardness of 60–258, against 30–80 for wrought iron, yet the Bronze Age gave way to iron after migrations around 1200–1100 BC disrupted tin supplies and raised prices. Bronze remains in use for sculpture, bells, bearings, and marine hardware.
