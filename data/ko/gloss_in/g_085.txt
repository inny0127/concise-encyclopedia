=== KEY: Marine_life_f162f6
HEADWORD: Marine life
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algae | Archean | Bacteria | Cyanobacteria | Hydrothermal vent | Photosynthesis | Shark | Whale
ENTRY:
Organisms that live in salt water, including seawater and brackish coastal habitats. Marine life spans microorganisms such as [[Bacteria|bacteria]] and plankton, [[Algae|algae]], and animals from invertebrates to [[Whale|whales]] and [[Shark|sharks]]. As of 2023, more than 242,000 marine species had been documented, and perhaps two million remain to be documented. Researchers describe about 2,300 new species each year. By volume, oceans provide about 90% of the living space on Earth. The earliest known life forms evolved in the [[Archean]] oceans around deep-sea [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vents]]. Marine primary producers, mainly [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] and chloroplastic algae, produce oxygen through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Marine vertebrates include fish, marine mammals, reptiles and seabirds.

=== KEY: Maritime_history_a7cf46

Study of human interaction with and activity at sea. The field often takes a global approach, although national and regional histories remain predominant. Its subjects include fishing, whaling, maritime law, naval history, ship design, shipbuilding, navigation, the history of [[Oceanography|oceanography]], [[Cartography|cartography]] and [[Hydrography|hydrography]], lighthouses, maritime trade and seafaring communities. === KEY: Maritime_transport_f5884c
HEADWORD: Maritime transport
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coke (fuel) | Ore | Port
ENTRY:
Transport of people or goods via waterways. Maritime transport, also called ocean or waterborne transport, can operate over any distance where navigable oceans, lakes, rivers and canals connect. It has been widely used throughout recorded history because it offers higher capacity than land transport, which is typically more costly per unit of payload. Watercraft are much cheaper than aircraft or road and rail vehicles but are slower over long journeys and depend on adequate [[Port|port]] facilities. According to UNCTAD, maritime transport accounts for roughly 80% of international trade in 2020. Containerization revolutionized the industry from the 1970s. Water transport is highly cost-effective for heavy or bulk cargo, such as coal, [[Coke (fuel)|coke]], [[Ore|ores]] and grains. According to the 2005 CIA World Factbook, there were 30,936 merchant ships of at least 1,000 gross register tons in the world; in 2010 the total was 38,988, an increase of 26%.

=== KEY: Market_economics_dacb94
HEADWORD: Market
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diamond | Market failure | Microeconomics | Price | Supply and demand
ENTRY:
In economics, any structure that allows buyers and sellers to exchange goods, services and information. Markets can be as small as a food market in a single building or as large as the worldwide [[Diamond|diamond]] trade. They differ by products, buyers, place of exchange, government regulation, taxes and size. Prices coordinate economic activity by conveying information among firms, households and individuals, and [[Supply and demand|supply and demand]] determine [[Price|prices]]. [[Microeconomics]] studies market structure and the efficiency of equilibrium; when equilibrium is not efficient, economists say a [[Market failure|market failure]] has occurred. In his 1937 article "The Nature of the Firm," Ronald Coase argued that the use of the price mechanism to convey information is the defining feature of the market.

=== KEY: Market_failure_7b98f0
HEADWORD: Market failure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Endangered species | John Stuart Mill | Monopoly
ENTRY:
Inefficient allocation of goods and services by a free market. In neoclassical economics, market failure is a situation in which the allocation is not Pareto efficient, often leading to a net loss of economic value. The term was first used by economists in 1958, although the concept has been traced to the Victorian writers [[John Stuart Mill]] and Henry Sidgwick. Market failures are associated with public goods, externalities, information asymmetries, failures of competition, principal–agent problems and unequal bargaining power. A [[Monopoly|monopoly]] can block mutually beneficial trade, and a natural monopoly arises where per-unit cost falls as output rises. Mainstream economists generally accept that governments or other organizations can sometimes improve market outcomes, as with building codes and [[Endangered species|endangered species]] laws. Critics argue that government interventions such as taxes, subsidies and regulations can also produce inefficiency, sometimes called government failure.

=== KEY: Marketing_7cb15e
HEADWORD: Marketing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Advertising | Commerce | Neuroscience | Psychology | Retail
ENTRY:
Process of acquiring, satisfying, and retaining customers, and a primary component of business management and [[Commerce|commerce]]. Marketing is usually conducted by the seller, typically a [[Retail|retailer]] or manufacturer, and products may be marketed to other businesses (B2B) or directly to consumers (B2C). Tasks are sometimes contracted to marketing, market research, or [[Advertising|advertising]] agencies.

The American Marketing Association (AMA) currently defines marketing as "the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large." The interests of society at large were added in 2008. Philip Kotler defined marketing in 1980 as "satisfying needs and wants through an exchange process".

The "marketing concept" holds that an organization should anticipate the needs and wants of potential consumers and satisfy them more effectively than its competitors. It originated in the work of [[Adam Smith]] but did not become widely used until nearly 200 years later. Market segmentation divides markets into groups of buyers with distinct needs, characteristics, or behaviors. The marketing mix is commonly framed as the four Ps: product, price, place, and promotion.

Historically, marketing shifted from a production focus during the Industrial Revolution to a consumer orientation after World War II. Because it draws on social science, [[Psychology|psychology]], sociology, mathematics, economics, anthropology, and [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], it is now widely recognized as a science, though it was once seen mainly as a creative industry. One estimate credits marketing investment with 0.18 percentage points of annual U.S. output growth from 1987 to 2020. Critics point to potentially manipulative persuasion, while defenders argue that it facilitates voluntary exchanges that enhance welfare.

=== KEY: Marketplace_675e31
HEADWORD: Marketplace
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: E-commerce | Phoenicia
ENTRY:
Location, physical or virtual, where people regularly gather to buy and sell goods. Such places are called souks, bazaars, mercados or palengkes in different regions. Some operate daily as permanent markets, while others meet weekly or on festival days. Open-air markets were known in ancient Babylonia, [[Phoenicia]], Greece and Egypt, and bazaars may have originated in Persia. Middle Eastern markets are often covered to shelter traders from the sun. Many marketplaces are now accessible online through [[E-commerce|e-commerce]], and many historic markets are listed sites of architectural significance.

=== KEY: Markov_chain_3cfc50
HEADWORD: Markov chain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bayesian statistics | Biology | Economics | Google | Physics | Probability distribution | Stochastic process
ENTRY:
[[Stochastic process|Stochastic process]] in which the probability of each event depends only on the state attained in the previous event. Named after the Russian mathematician Andrey Markov, Markov chains move between states at discrete time steps (discrete-time Markov chains) or continuously (continuous-time Markov chains). They serve as statistical models of real-world processes in fields such as [[Biology]], [[Economics]] and [[Physics]]. They provide the basis for Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, which sample from complex [[Probability distribution|probability distributions]] in [[Bayesian statistics]]. [[Google]]'s PageRank algorithm treats web links as transition probabilities, and hidden Markov models are used in speech recognition. Markov chains also appear in queueing theory in operations research.

It is nearly a cultural universal, but its definition varies across cultures, religions, and time. A marriage ceremony is called a [[Wedding|wedding]]. A marriage may be recognized by a state, an organization, a religious authority, a tribal group, a local community, or peers, and is often viewed as a legal [[Contract|contract]].

The word appeared around 1300, borrowed from Old French *mariage* and ultimately from [[Latin]] *maritātus*, "married". The earliest recorded evidence of marriage ceremonies dates to about 2350 BC in [[Mesopotamia]].

Anthropologists have proposed competing definitions. Edvard Westermarck, in 1936, defined marriage as a relation of one or more men to one or more women recognized by custom or law. Edmund Leach argued in 1955 that no single definition fits all cultures and listed ten rights marriage may establish, including rights over a spouse's labor and property, a joint fund for the children, and a relationship of affinity between the husband and his wife's brothers.

[[Monogamy|Monogamy]], one spouse at a time or in a lifetime, predominates across cultures, though some societies permit polygyny. Studies link marriage with better health outcomes, but debate continues over whether selection effects or causal benefits explain this. Worldwide trends include equal rights for women within marriage and greater tolerance of interethnic, interfaith, transnational, and same-sex couples, which have gained legal recognition in Western jurisdictions since the late 20th century. International norms stress free consent and a minimum age.

=== KEY: Mars_671f02
HEADWORD: Mars
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Carbon dioxide | Deimos (moon) | Exploration of Mars | Life on Mars | Olympus Mons | Phobos (moon)
ENTRY:
Fourth planet from the Sun, a desert-like rocky world known as the Red Planet for its orange-red appearance. Its mean diameter is 6,779 km, about half the Earth's, and its surface gravity is roughly a third of Earth's. The tenuous atmosphere is primarily [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], with surface pressure a few thousandths of Earth's. Temperature ranges from −153 to 20 °C. Mars retains some water in the ground and thinly in the atmosphere, forming clouds, frost, and polar ice caps, but it has no bodies of liquid surface water.

Terrain follows a north–south divide: the northern hemisphere is mainly flat, low-lying plains, the southern mainly cratered highlands. The planet hosts many enormous extinct volcanoes, the tallest being [[Olympus Mons]] at 21.9 km, and Valles Marineris, one of the largest canyons in the Solar System, 4,000 km long. Mars has two small, irregularly shaped moons, [[Phobos (moon)|Phobos]] and [[Deimos (moon)|Deimos]]. An [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]] of 25 degrees gives it seasons. A Martian year equals 1.88 Earth years (687 Earth days), and a solar day, or sol, lasts 24.6 hours.

Mars formed about 4.5 billion years ago, and its history is divided into the Noachian, Hesperian, and Amazonian periods, the last continuing to the present. Because of this history, past or present [[Life on Mars|life]] remains under active scientific investigation, with some possible traces needing further examination.

[[Exploration of Mars|Exploration]] began in 1963 with Mars 1, which lost communication en route. Mariner 4 made the first successful flyby in 1965. In 1971 Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit any body other than the Moon, Sun, or Earth, and Mars 3 made the first successful landing. Probes have been active on Mars continuously since 1997.

=== KEY: Martial_arts_0c7e39

ENTRY:
Codified systems and traditions of combat. They are practiced for self-defense, military and law enforcement training, exercise, ceremony, competition, physical, mental, and spiritual development, entertainment, and the preservation of national cultural heritage. The term originally referred to the traditions of East Asia but has since been applied to practices from elsewhere. It derives from an older [[Latin]] term meaning the "arts of [[Mars (mythology)|Mars]]", the Roman god of war, and was used for European combat systems as early as the 1550s, according to John Clements. Martial arts may be classified as armed or unarmed, traditional or contemporary, or by intent, such as self-defense, combat sport, demonstration of forms, fitness, or meditation. Armed traditions use bladed weapons and polearms and include eskrima, silat, kalaripayattu, kobudo, kenjutsu, kendo, and historical European martial arts. Japanese martial arts are called *budō*, the suffix meaning "way" or "path".

Cave paintings from eastern Spain dated between 10,000 and 6,000 BCE show organized groups fighting with bows and arrows,. [[Wrestling]] is the oldest combat sport; belt wrestling appears in [[Mesopotamia|Mesopotamian]] and ancient Egyptian art from c. 3000 BC, and the earliest known depiction of boxing is a Sumerian relief of the 3rd millennium BC.

[[Judo]] and taekwondo achieved Olympic status in the 20th century. Since the 1990s, [[Mixed martial arts]] has combined elements of styles such as Brazilian jiu-jitsu, Muay Thai, and wrestling. Debate continues over traditional forms versus reality-based training tested in unrestricted fights.

=== KEY: Martin_Luther_135ef7
HEADWORD: Luther, Martin
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bible | Catholic Church | German language | Lutheranism | Nazi Party | Ninety-five Theses | Reformation
ENTRY:
German priest, theologian, and author, the seminal figure of the Protestant [[Reformation]]. A former Augustinian friar and professor, he was born in Eisleben and ordained to the priesthood in 1507. He came to reject teachings and practices of the Roman [[Catholic Church]], in particular its view of indulgences and papal authority. In 1517 he wrote the [[Ninety-five Theses]], sent to Archbishop Albert of Mainz; tradition holds that he nailed them to the door of the All Saints' Church in Wittenberg, though modern scholarship considers this unlikely. In 1520 Pope Leo X demanded that he renounce his writings, and when he refused, excommunicated him in January 1521. That year Emperor Charles V condemned him as an outlaw at the Diet of Worms, where he refused to recant. Protected by Frederick the Wise, he translated the New Testament into German at Wartburg Castle.

Luther taught that justification is not earned by human acts or merit but received as the free gift of God's grace through faith in Jesus, and that the [[Bible]] is the only source of divinely revealed knowledge. His beliefs form the basis of [[Lutheranism]], though he himself insisted on the names Christian or Evangelical. His German Bible (1534) made scripture accessible to laypeople, fostered a standard German language, and influenced the English Tyndale Bible. His hymns shaped Protestant congregational singing, and his marriage to the former nun Katharina von Bora set a model for clerical marriage.

Luther's writings also include harsh polemics against Anabaptists, Zwingli, the pope, and Jews. He called for the expulsion of Jews and the burning of synagogues, notably in *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543), after having argued in 1523 against their mistreatment and for their conversion. He did not advocate murdering Jews, but some historians contend that his rhetoric encouraged antisemitism in [[German language|Germany]] and, centuries later, the [[Nazi Party]]. In 1525 he urged princes to suppress the peasants' uprising. His excommunication was still in effect at his death in 1546.

Political philosophy and method of socioeconomic analysis based on historical materialism, developed by [[Karl Marx]] and [[Friedrich Engels]] in the 19th century. Marxism treats class struggle as the central driving force of historical change and holds that a society's economic mode of production shapes its social, political and intellectual life. It argues that the [[Bourgeoisie|bourgeoisie]], who own the means of production, exploit the [[Working class|working class]], and that this leads to alienation, periodic economic crises and class conflict. Marx theorized that workers' revolutions would overthrow [[Capitalism|capitalism]] and establish [[Socialism|socialism]], a transition toward a classless and stateless society. The most prominent 20th-century form was [[Marxism–Leninism]], the official ideology of the Soviet Union. Other traditions, including Western Marxism and Marxist feminism, have often been critical of state socialism. Marxist concepts such as alienation and class struggle have become integral to the social sciences and humanities.

English writer and philosopher best known for her advocacy of [[Women's rights]]. === KEY: Masonry_aa4777
HEADWORD: Masonry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brick | Concrete | Construction | Friction | Granite | Limestone | Marble | Steel
ENTRY:
Craft of building structures from individual units of stone, [[Brick|brick]], [[Concrete|concrete]], or similar material, usually bound with mortar; the term also refers to the units themselves. Common materials are bricks and building stone; rocks such as [[Granite|granite]], [[Marble|marble]], and [[Limestone|limestone]]; cast stone, concrete blocks, glass blocks, and adobe. A person who builds masonry is called a mason or bricklayer, both classified as [[Construction|construction]] trades.

Masonry is one of the oldest building crafts, predating written history; Egyptian pyramids, Roman aqueducts, and medieval cathedrals are examples. Early structures relied on the weight of the masonry itself for stability against lateral movement. Since the mid-20th century, masonry has often included [[Steel|steel]]-reinforced elements to carry the tension in modern thin, light, tall building systems. Applications are structural (walls, columns, beams, foundations, load-bearing arches) and non-structural (fireplaces, chimneys, veneers). Filling the cores of hollow concrete blocks with concrete or reinforced concrete gives much greater tensile and lateral strength.

Bonds are the arrangements in which units and mortar are laid; patterns are the visual arrangements on the face. A stretcher is laid with its long side facing out, a header with its short end out, and a course is a single horizontal row. English bond alternates courses of stretchers and headers; Flemish bond alternates them within each course. Stonework using dressed stones is ashlar masonry, and that using irregular stones is rubble masonry. Some dry set structures omit mortar, relying on [[Friction|friction]] between interlocking blocks.

Masonry is durable, adds thermal mass, is non-combustible, and resists debris from hurricanes and tornadoes. Its drawbacks include freeze-thaw degradation, heavy weight that requires stable ground, and a need for skilled labor. Because walls rely mainly on weight and thin mortar layers, they perform poorly in earthquakes, and many collapses involve load-bearing masonry walls.

=== KEY: Mass_energy_equivalence_53a6d3
HEADWORD: Mass–energy equivalence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Henri Poincaré | Special relativity | Speed of light
ENTRY:
Physical principle that mass and energy are equivalent in a system's rest frame, expressed as E = mc². In the formula, E is the energy of a particle at rest, m is its mass and c is the [[Speed of light|speed of light]], so a small amount of mass corresponds to an enormous amount of energy. The principle arose from [[Special relativity|special relativity]] as a paradox described by the French polymath [[Henri Poincaré|Henri Poincaré]]. [[Albert Einstein|Einstein]] was the first to propose it as a general principle, in a paper on the inertia of bodies published in 1905. When mass is lost in a nuclear or chemical reaction, a corresponding amount of energy is released as light or heat, a principle fundamental to nuclear and particle physics.

=== KEY: Mass_ff2864
HEADWORD: Mass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equivalence principle | General relativity | Higgs boson | Inertia | Kilogram | Moon | Solar mass | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Intrinsic physical quantity of a body, in modern physics generally defined as the strength of its gravitational attraction to other bodies. The unit of mass in the International System of Units is the [[Kilogram|kilogram]] (kg). Mass can also be understood as a measure of the energy content of a body (E = mc²), and experimentally as a measure of [[Inertia|inertia]], the resistance to acceleration when a net force is applied.

Three phenomena can be used to measure mass: inertial mass (resistance to acceleration, F = ma), active gravitational mass (the strength of the gravitational field an object generates), and passive gravitational mass (the force exerted on an object in a known field). Experiments since the 17th century have shown inertial and gravitational mass to be identical, and since 1915 this has been incorporated in the [[Equivalence principle|equivalence principle]] of [[General relativity|general relativity]]. Mass differs from weight, which is a force: an object on the [[Moon|Moon]] weighs less than on Earth but has the same mass. In the [[Standard Model]], the mass of elementary particles is believed to result from their coupling with the [[Higgs boson]], in the Brout–Englert–Higgs mechanism.

The kilogram was first defined in 1795 as the mass of one cubic decimetre of water at the melting point of ice. In 1889 it was redefined as the platinum–iridium International Prototype of the Kilogram, whose mass and that of its national copies were found to drift over time. The redefinition that took effect on May 20, 2019, uses only the speed of light, the caesium hyperfine frequency, the Planck constant, and the elementary charge. Non-SI units still widely used include the tonne (1000 kg) and the dalton, approximately 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, convenient for atoms and molecules. Astronomers compare large masses with the [[Solar mass|solar mass]], approximately 1.99×10³⁰ kg.

=== KEY: Mass_media_9fe2ff
HEADWORD: Mass media
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: AOL | Advertising | Diamond Sutra | Johannes Gutenberg | Journalism | Propaganda | The Times | World War II
ENTRY:
Forms of media that reach large audiences through mass communication, including broadcast, digital, print, social, and streaming media, [[Advertising|advertising]], and events. Mass media encompasses news, advocacy, entertainment, and public service announcements, and intersects with the study of marketing, [[Propaganda|propaganda]], public relations, political communication, [[Journalism|journalism]], and technology. Its influence has also been analyzed from the standpoint of anthropology, economics, history, law, philosophy, psychology, and sociology. It is often controlled by media conglomerates and may be susceptible to media capture.

In the late 20th century mass media was classified into eight industries: books, the Internet, magazines, movies, newspapers, radio, recordings, and television. By the early 2000s a classification of "seven mass media" came into use: print (late 15th century), recordings (late 19th century), cinema (c. 1900), radio (c. 1910), television (c. 1950), the Internet (c. 1990), and mobile phones (c. 2000). The Internet and mobile phones are often called digital media, and radio and television broadcast media. The sociologist John Thompson identified mass communication as a "one-to-many" form in which products are mass-produced and disseminated to large audiences. The term is sometimes used as a synonym for mainstream media, though mass media may include alternative outlets that use mass communication technology.

The first dated printed book, the [[Diamond Sutra]], was printed in China in 868 AD, and movable type made of clay was invented there in 1041. [[Johannes Gutenberg|Gutenberg]] printed the Latin Bible with movable type in 1453, enabling books and newspapers on a far larger scale, though books stayed too expensive to be a mass medium for at least a century. Newspapers developed from c. 1612, with the first English example in 1620, and reached a mass audience in the 19th century; the first high-circulation papers, such as [[The Times]], arose in London in the early 1800s, made possible by the high-speed rotary steam printing press and railroads. Until after [[World War II]] the notion of mass media was generally restricted to print; radio, television, and video then followed, and television became widely used in the U.S. and England in the late 1940s.

Media consolidation has been extensive. Between 1985 and 2018 about 76,720 deals were announced in the media industry, with an overall value of around US$5,634 billion, in three major waves (2000, 2007, and 2015). The most active year by number was 2007, with around 3,808 deals. The largest deal in history was the acquisition of Time Warner by [[AOL]] Inc. for US$164,746.86 million. In democratic societies, media can inform the electorate about government and corporate entities, while some consider the concentration of media ownership a threat to democracy. In 1997 J. R. Finnegan Jr. and K. Viswanath identified theories of media effects, including the knowledge gap and agenda setting.

=== KEY: Mass_production_0f0d0e
HEADWORD: Mass production
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Manufacturing of standardized products in a constant flow, especially on assembly lines. It is one of three main production methods, along with job production and batch production. The term was popularized by a 1926 article in the [[Encyclopædia Britannica|Encyclopædia Britannica]] supplement, written from correspondence with [[Ford Motor Company|Ford Motor Company]]. Some techniques, such as standardized sizes and production lines, predate the [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]] by centuries, but modern mass production became possible only in the mid-19th century, when machine tools and [[Interchangeable parts|interchangeable parts]] were developed. Its core principle is that the skill is built into the tool, so the worker need not be highly skilled. The approach is capital- and energy-intensive, and the machinery for a line, such as robots and machine presses, is expensive, so a product must be assured of success. It is applied to bulk fluids and minerals as well as to clothing, textiles and automobiles.

=== KEY: Mass_spectrometry_13e1ec

Analytical technique that measures the mass-to-charge ratio of [[Ion|ions]]. Results are displayed as a mass spectrum, a plot of intensity against mass-to-charge ratio. Spectra reveal the elemental or [[Isotope|isotopic]] signature of a sample, the masses of particles and molecules, and the chemical structure of compounds. U.S. state in the [[New England|New England]] region of the Northeastern United States (pop., 2024: 7,136,171). Its capital and largest city is [[Boston|Boston]]. Massachusetts was an early site of English colonization, and the [[American Revolution|American Revolution]] originated there. Originally dependent on agriculture, fishing and trade, it became a manufacturing hub during the [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. Its economy shifted to services in the 20th century, and in the 21st century it became a global leader in [[Biotechnology|biotechnology]]. It is home to [[Harvard University|Harvard University]] and the [[Massachusetts Institute of Technology|Massachusetts Institute of Technology]], and it was the first U.S. state to legally recognize [[Same-sex marriage|same-sex marriage]].

Private research university in [[Cambridge, Massachusetts|Cambridge, Massachusetts]], United States. Founded by geologist William Barton Rogers to accelerate American industrialization through scientific knowledge, it adopted a German polytechnic model emphasizing laboratory instruction in applied science and engineering. Its first classes were held in Boston in 1865, and it moved to its current Cambridge campus in 1916. During World War II, its Radiation Laboratory made the institute the nation's largest non-industrial research and development contractor, and graduate enrollment and research funding grew rapidly in the postwar decades. It became closely associated with [[Computer science|computer science]], [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]] and [[Biotechnology|biotechnology]], although engineering remains its largest school. Tuition is generally not charged to undergraduates from families with incomes below $200,000.

English trip hop group formed in [[Bristol|Bristol]] in 1988. Its debut album, *Blue Lines* (1991), is generally considered the first trip-hop album. The single "Unfinished Sympathy" reached number one on the Dutch Top 40. *Mezzanine* (1998) gave the group their first number one on the UK Albums Chart. Robert "3D" Del Naja and Grant "Daddy G" Marshall have continued as a duo since Andrew "Mushroom" Vowles left in 1999. The group's five studio albums have sold over 13 million copies worldwide. Its awards include a Brit Award for Best British Dance Act, and it supports political, [[Human rights|human rights]] and environmental causes.

Postgraduate academic degree demonstrating mastery of a specific field or area of professional practice. A master's degree normally requires previous study at the [[Bachelor's degree|bachelor's]] level, either as a separate degree or as part of an integrated course. Graduates are expected to have advanced knowledge of a specialized body of theoretical and applied topics, along with high-order skills in analysis, critical evaluation or professional application. The degree descends from the medieval *licentia docendi*, or license to teach. In the United States, the first master's degrees were awarded at [[Harvard University|Harvard University]] soon after its foundation. In 2023, approximately 25.3 million Americans held one, representing about 11% of adults aged 25 and older. Holders earn a median weekly wage of $1,840, compared with $1,543 for bachelor's recipients.

Large, powerful type of [[Dog|dog]]. Mastiffs are among the largest dogs, with a short coat, a long low-set tail, large feet, a large bulky skull, a broad short muzzle and drooping ears. Records of mastiff-type dogs in Europe and Asia date back 3,000 years. Mastiffs have historically been guard dogs that protected homes and property, but they were also used for hunting, war and blood sports such as bull-baiting and bear-baiting. Although often grouped with livestock guardian dogs, mastiffs form separate genetic populations. Older English sources call them bandogs. The English Mastiff, a classic breed, is docile and dignified, aloof with strangers but loyal to its family. The American Kennel Club recognized the breed in 1885.

Extinct proboscidean genus, *Mammut*, endemic to North America. Mastodons lived from the late [[Miocene|Miocene]] to the early [[Holocene|Holocene]] and belong to the same order as [[Elephant|elephants]] and [[Mammoth|mammoths]], although they had stockier builds and lower-domed skulls. The best-known species, *Mammut americanum*, is thought to have stood 275–305 cm at the shoulder. Mastodons browsed on leaves and woody plants, with females and juveniles living in herds and adult males living largely solitary lives. [[Clovis culture|Clovis culture]] and other Paleoindians hunted them. Mastodons disappeared in the end-[[Pleistocene|Pleistocene]] extinction event, which also eliminated most of North America's largest animals.
