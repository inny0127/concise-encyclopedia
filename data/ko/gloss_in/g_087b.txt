=== KEY: Melanin_448e72
HEADWORD: Melanin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Melanoma | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Family of biomolecules organized as oligomers or polymers, forming the pigments of many organisms. Melanin is produced by melanocytes in a process called melanogenesis, in which the [[Amino acid]] tyrosine is oxidized and then polymerized. There are five basic types: eumelanin, pheomelanin, neuromelanin, allomelanin and pyomelanin. Eumelanin is more abundant in tissues requiring photoprotection, such as the epidermis, and can dissipate over 99.9% of absorbed [[Ultraviolet|UV]] radiation. In human skin, melanogenesis is initiated by UV exposure, which is associated with increased risk of [[Melanoma]]. Pheomelanin is largely responsible for the red or yellow tint of some skin and hair. Neuromelanin, found in the brain, has been studied for possible treatment of neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson's.

=== KEY: Melanoma_ea6e07
HEADWORD: Melanoma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biopsy | Chemotherapy | Melanin | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Skin cancer arising from [[Melanin|melanin]]-producing cells called melanocytes. It typically occurs in the skin but may rarely occur in the mouth, intestines or eye. The primary cause is [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light exposure in people with low levels of skin pigment; risk is higher for those with many moles, a family history, or poor immune function. About 30% of melanomas develop from moles. Diagnosis is by [[Biopsy]]. Treatment is typically surgical removal, with lymph node testing for larger cancers. Immunotherapy, radiation or [[Chemotherapy]] may improve survival once the disease has spread. Five-year survival rates in the United States are 100% for localized disease, 76% when the disease has spread to lymph nodes, and 35% for distant spread. Melanoma is the most dangerous type of skin cancer; worldwide cases are expected to rise from 331,722 in 2022 to 510,000 in 2040, although mortality is decreasing.

=== KEY: Melody_ce2f3a
HEADWORD: Melody
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Classical music | Fugue | Gamelan | Harmony | Indian classical music | Jazz | Rhythm | Rock music
ENTRY:
Linear succession of musical tones that the listener perceives as a single entity. In its most literal sense a melody combines pitch and [[Rhythm]], while more figuratively it can include tonal color. It is the foreground to a background accompaniment, and is often built from motifs repeated in various forms. Melodies are described by their melodic motion, pitch range, tension and release, and cadence. In [[Classical music]], composers often introduce an initial melody or theme and then create variations, and several melodic layers may form polyphony, as in a [[Fugue]]. [[Jazz]] musicians use the "lead" or "head" as the starting point for improvisation. [[Rock music]] and folk styles tend to pick one or two melodies and stick with them. [[Indian classical music]] relies more on melody and rhythm than on [[Harmony]], while Balinese [[Gamelan]] often plays complicated variations of a single melody simultaneously.

=== KEY: Melting_bf4376
HEADWORD: Melting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Helium-3 | Internal energy | Liquid | Molecule | Solid | Viscosity
ENTRY:
Phase transition of a [[Solid]] to a [[Liquid]]. It occurs when the [[Internal energy]] of the solid increases, typically through heat or pressure, raising its temperature to the melting point. At that point the ordering of ions or [[Molecule|molecules]] breaks down. Melting is a first-order phase transition: at the melting point the change in Gibbs free energy is zero, while enthalpy and entropy change. Substances in the molten state generally have reduced [[Viscosity]] as temperature rises; elemental sulfur is an exception, because its viscosity increases between 130 °C and 190 °C due to polymerization. Water on a very clean glass surface can supercool several degrees below its freezing point. Low-temperature [[Helium-3]] has a negative enthalpy of fusion below 0.3 K, so heat must be removed to melt it.

Cultural idea, behavior or style transmitted by imitation from person to person within a [[Culture]]. === KEY: Memento_mori_158544

ENTRY:
Artistic symbol or trope reminding viewers of the inevitability of [[Death]]. The [[Latin]] phrase means "remember [that you have] to die". === KEY: Memory_4789f2
HEADWORD: Memory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alzheimer's disease | Amnesia | Brain | Hippocampus
ENTRY:
Faculty of the mind by which data or information is encoded, stored, and retrieved when needed. It is the retention of information over time for the purpose of influencing future action. Without memory of past events, language, relationships, and personal identity could not develop. Memory loss is usually described as forgetfulness or as a disorder such as [[Amnesia|amnesia]].

Memory is often understood as an information processing system made up of a sensory processor, short-term (or working) memory, and long-term memory. Working memory serves as an encoding and retrieval processor, and long-term memory stores information through various categorical models or systems.

Declarative, or explicit, memory is the conscious storage and recollection of data and includes semantic memory, encoded with specific meaning, and episodic memory, encoded along a spatial and temporal plane. Non-declarative, or implicit, memory is the unconscious storage and recollection of information, as in procedural memory, the slow and gradual learning of skills, and priming.

Sensory memory holds information from the senses for less than one second and cannot be prolonged by rehearsal. In experiments published in 1963, George Sperling showed that its capacity is approximately 12 items but that it degrades within a few hundred milliseconds. Its three types are iconic (visual), echoic (auditory), and haptic (touch). Short-term memory allows recall for several seconds to a minute without rehearsal, with very limited capacity.

Memory is not a perfect processor. Pain impairs it, as do reduced attention to new stimuli and physical damage to areas of the [[Brain|brain]] associated with storage, such as the [[Hippocampus|hippocampus]]. Retrieval can also be disrupted by decay within long-term memory, and disruptions in memory networks, as in [[Alzheimer's disease]], show their vulnerability.

=== KEY: Mendelian_inheritance_14831e

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gene | Genetics | Gregor Mendel | Natural selection | Pea | Ronald Fisher
ENTRY:
Type of biological inheritance following the principles proposed by [[Gregor Mendel]] in 1865 and 1866. Its defining feature is a heavy association with a single [[Gene]]. Mendel's principles were rediscovered in 1900 by Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak. In 1915 Thomas Hunt Morgan integrated them with the chromosome theory of inheritance, forming the core of classical [[Genetics]]. [[Ronald Fisher]] combined them with [[Natural selection]] in *The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection* (1930), putting evolution on a mathematical footing. Between 1856 and 1863 Mendel tested some 28,000 [[Pea]] plants. Mendelian principles underpin the study of monogenic disorders, such as Huntington's disease and cystic fibrosis.

Acute or chronic [[Inflammation]] of the protective membranes covering the [[Brain]] and [[Spinal cord]]. Common symptoms are fever, intense headache, vomiting and neck stiffness; young children may show only irritability or poor feeding. === KEY: Menopause_caf900
HEADWORD: Menopause
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cardiovascular disease | Estrogen | Gabapentin | Osteoporosis
ENTRY:
Permanent end of menstrual periods, marking the close of the reproductive phase in female humans. Medically, menopause is defined as having occurred after a year without menstrual bleeding. The transition has three stages: perimenopause, menopause and postmenopause. Natural menopause typically occurs between the ages of 45 and 55, as production of [[Estrogen]] and progesterone declines. Surgical removal of both ovaries also causes menopause, and ovarian failure before age 40 is termed premature ovarian insufficiency. Symptoms include hot flashes, night sweats, vaginal dryness and mood changes. Physical consequences include bone loss and increased risk of [[Osteoporosis]] and [[Cardiovascular disease]]. Menopausal hormone therapy is effective for many symptoms and for preventing bone loss. Non-hormonal options for hot flashes include cognitive-behavioral therapy and [[Gabapentin]].

=== KEY: Menstrual_cycle_e87695
HEADWORD: Menstrual cycle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Egg cell | Embryo | Estrogen | Female reproductive system | Menopause | Menstruation | Pregnancy | Uterus
ENTRY:
Recurring series of hormonal and structural changes in the [[Female reproductive system]]. Its ovarian cycle controls the release of [[Egg cell|eggs]] and the cyclic release of [[Estrogen|estrogen]] and progesterone, while its uterine cycle prepares the lining of the [[Uterus]] to receive an [[Embryo]]. Cycles usually last 21 to 35 days, with a median of 28 days. Menarche usually occurs around age 12, and cycles continue for about 30–45 years until [[Menopause]]. Around day 14 an egg is usually released. Falling progesterone triggers [[Menstruation]], the shedding of the lining, which signals that [[Pregnancy]] has not occurred. Hormonal birth control can modify the cycle.

Regular discharge of blood and mucosal tissue from the uterine lining through the [[Vagina]], also called a period. === KEY: Mental_disorder_494326
HEADWORD: Mental disorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bipolar disorder | Dementia | Mental health | Psychiatric hospital | Psychotherapy | Schizophrenia
ENTRY:
Behavioral or mental pattern that causes significant distress or impairment of personal functioning; also called mental illness or psychiatric disability. It is characterized by a clinically significant disturbance in cognition, emotional regulation, or behavior, often in a social context. Disturbances may be single episodes, persistent, or relapsing–remitting. A mental disorder is one aspect of [[Mental health|mental health]].

The causes are often unclear. Disorders are usually diagnosed by a mental health professional, such as a clinical psychologist, psychiatrist, psychiatric nurse, or clinical social worker, often relying on observation and questioning, with cultural and religious beliefs and social norms taken into account. In 2013 the American Psychiatric Association redefined a mental disorder in the DSM-5 as "a syndrome characterized by clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotion regulation, or behavior that reflects a dysfunction in the psychological, biological, or developmental processes underlying mental functioning."

Services are usually based in [[Psychiatric hospital|psychiatric hospitals]], outpatient clinics, or the community. Common treatments are [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] or psychiatric medication, along with lifestyle changes, social interventions, and peer support; in a minority of cases there is involuntary detention or treatment.

In 2019 major depression affected about 264 million people worldwide; [[Dementia|dementia]], about 50 million; [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]], about 45 million; and [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]] and other psychoses, about 20 million. Neurodevelopmental disorders, including attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, autism, and intellectual disability, begin early in development. Stigma and discrimination add to the suffering, prompting social movements to challenge exclusion.

The biomedical framing is disputed. Critics such as Thomas Szasz have argued that many conditions are "problems in living" or value judgments rather than verifiable pathologies, and some analysts warn of overdiagnosis.

=== KEY: Mental_health_7bab96
HEADWORD: Mental health
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eugenics | Mental disorder | Psychiatric hospital | Taxonomy | Well-being | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Emotional, psychological, and social [[Well-being|well-being]], influencing cognition, perception, and behavior. The [[World Health Organization]] (WHO) defines it as a "state of well-being in which the individual realizes their abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively and fruitfully, and can contribute to their community." From the perspectives of positive psychology or holism, it is not merely the absence of mental illness but a broader state that includes the ability to enjoy life and to achieve psychological resilience. Cultural differences, personal philosophy, and competing professional theories all affect how it is defined.

Impairment is a risk factor for a [[Mental disorder|mental disorder]]; about 970 million people worldwide had one in 2019, anxiety and depression being the most common. Diet, exercise, stress, drug use, and social connections are associated with mental health. Constant online connection can increase stress, and cyberbullying has been linked to depression and anxiety.

In the mid-19th century William Sweetser coined the term mental hygiene. Dorothea Dix exposed sub-standard conditions for the mentally ill and, from 1840 to 1880, won federal support for over 30 state [[Psychiatric hospital|psychiatric hospitals]], which were nevertheless understaffed and accused of violating human rights. Emil Kraepelin developed a [[Taxonomy|taxonomy]] of mental disorders in 1896 that dominated the field for nearly 80 years. Clifford Beers, after publishing *A Mind That Found Itself* (1908), opened the first outpatient mental health clinic in the U.S. The mental hygiene movement was at times associated with advocating [[Eugenics|eugenics]] and sterilization. After World War II the term "mental health" gradually replaced it, shifting emphasis toward prevention.

Access to care remains uneven: the WHO African Region averages 2.2 mental health workers per 100,000 population against a global median of 13.5. Critics argue that diagnostic expansion has led to overmedicalization.
