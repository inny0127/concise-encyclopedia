=== KEY: Abelian_group_19030a

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Field (mathematics) | Integer | Niels Henrik Abel | Real number | Ring (mathematics) | Vector space
ENTRY:
Group in which the result of the operation does not depend on the order of its elements. Named after the Norwegian mathematician [[Niels Henrik Abel]], such groups are also called commutative groups. The [[Integer|integers]] and the [[Real number|real numbers]] form abelian groups under addition. The concept underlies many algebraic structures, including [[Field (mathematics)|fields]], [[Ring (mathematics)|rings]] and [[Vector space|vector spaces]]. The theory is generally simpler than that of non-abelian groups, and finite abelian groups are fully classified. === KEY: Abiogenesis_cbafe7
HEADWORD: Abiogenesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Hydrothermal vent | Last universal common ancestor | Organic compound | RNA | Western Australia
ENTRY:
Natural process by which life arises from non-living matter such as simple [[Organic compound|organic compounds]], and the field of study concerned with it. The prevailing scientific hypothesis is that the transition was not a single event but a process of increasing complexity: formation of a habitable planet, prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules, molecular self-replication, self-assembly, autocatalysis, and the emergence of cell membranes. The transition has not been observed experimentally. Life builds on four chemical families: lipids for cell membranes, carbohydrates for energy storage and structure, [[Amino acid|amino acids]] for protein metabolism, and the nucleic acids DNA and [[RNA]] for heredity.

Competing approaches address different aspects. Many researchers think life descends from an RNA world, though other self-replicating molecules may have preceded RNA. "Metabolism-first" hypotheses stress catalysis on the early Earth supplying precursors for self-replication, and hybrid "systems chemistry" models treat compartments, metabolism, and informational polymers as co-evolving. There is no consensus on which arose first. The 1952 Miller–Urey experiment showed that amino acids can form from inorganic compounds under early-Earth-like conditions, and amino acids have since been found in meteorites, comets, asteroids, and star-forming regions.

The [[Last universal common ancestor]] (LUCA) lived some time after life began. Genomic work comparing Archaea and Bacteria suggests 60 proteins common to all life and 355 prokaryotic genes tracing to LUCA, implying an anaerobic organism that used DNA, the genetic code, and ribosomes. Earlier cells may have been powered by a proton gradient near a deep-sea [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vent]], though inland crust or surface water have also been proposed. Earth formed 4.54 billion years ago; the earliest evidence of life, 3.8 billion years old, comes from [[Western Australia]]. Open questions include how replicators arose, how genetic and metabolic functions became coupled, and why biochemistry is homochiral.

[[Discrimination|Discrimination]] and social prejudice against disabled people. Ableism characterizes people by their disabilities, regards them as inferior to non-disabled people and assigns or denies perceived abilities on that basis. It rests on [[Stereotype|stereotypes]], such as the presumption that all disabled people want to be cured, and these stereotypes justify discriminatory practices. === KEY: Abortion_7cc72b
HEADWORD: Abortion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Angkor Wat | Embryo | Fetus | Pregnancy | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Termination of a [[Pregnancy|pregnancy]] by removal or expulsion of an [[Embryo|embryo]] or [[Fetus|fetus]]. The unmodified word usually means induced abortion, the deliberate ending of a pregnancy; abortion without intervention is called spontaneous abortion or miscarriage and occurs in roughly 30–40% of pregnancies. Common reasons include birth-timing, limiting family size, maternal health, and inability to afford a child.

Modern methods use medication or surgery. The drug mifepristone combined with a prostaglandin appears as safe and effective as surgery in the first and second trimesters. Where legal in industrialized societies, induced abortion is one of the safest medical procedures. Unsafe abortions, performed by unskilled people, with hazardous equipment, or in unsanitary conditions, cause between 22,000 and 44,000 deaths and 6.9 million hospital admissions a year, 5–13% of maternal deaths, mostly in low-income countries. The [[World Health Organization]] holds that access to legal, safe, comprehensive abortion care is essential to sexual and reproductive health. About 73 million abortions occur worldwide each year, roughly 45% of them unsafe.

Laws range from permission on request to prohibition, and some countries allow it only in specific cases such as rape or risk to the woman's health. Since 1973 there has been a global trend toward wider legal access, but moral, religious, ethical, and legal debate continues. Opponents often argue that an embryo or fetus is a person with a right to life; supporters often argue that abortion is a reproductive right and a matter of bodily autonomy, and public health researchers link legal access with lower maternal mortality.

Abortion has a long history. Methods including herbal medicines and sharp tools are recorded from China, India, [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] (the Ebers Papyrus, c. 1550 BC), and Rome, and a relief at [[Angkor Wat]] (c. 1150) depicts one technique. Abortion was not always illegal or controversial until the 19th century, when it was banned in the United States and United Kingdom.

=== KEY: Abraham_Lincoln_2f7cfa
HEADWORD: Lincoln, Abraham
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: American Civil War | Gettysburg Address | Habeas corpus | Illinois | John Wilkes Booth | Kentucky | Republican Party (United States)
ENTRY:
American lawyer and statesman, 16th president of the United States (1861–65). Born in a one-room log cabin in [[Kentucky]], he grew up on the frontier in Kentucky, Indiana, and [[Illinois]] and was largely self-educated. He became a lawyer, Illinois state legislator, and a one-term U.S. representative. Angered by the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which opened territories to slavery, he became a leader of the new [[Republican Party (United States)|Republican Party]] and won national attention in the 1858 Senate campaign debates with Stephen A. Douglas.

Lincoln won the 1860 presidential election as the first Republican president, and most slave states began to secede. A month after he took office, Confederate forces attacked Fort Sumter, beginning the [[American Civil War]] against the Confederate States. He closely supervised Union strategy. In April 1861 he suspended the writ of [[Habeas corpus|habeas corpus]]; Chief Justice Roger Taney held in *Ex parte Merryman* that only Congress could do so. Critics, then and later, saw his expansion of executive power as overreach, while supporters regarded it as necessary to save the Union. The war claimed over 620,000 lives.

On January 1, 1863, Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation, declaring slaves in states "in rebellion" free; it was a war measure and did not cover Union border states. On November 19, 1863, he delivered the [[Gettysburg Address]]. He promoted the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolished chattel slavery in 1865. Re-elected in 1864, he favored a lenient Reconstruction.

On April 14, 1865, five days after the Confederate surrender at Appomattox, Lincoln was shot by [[John Wilkes Booth]] at Ford's Theatre in Washington, D.C., and died the next day, the first U.S. president to be assassinated. He is ranked in popular and scholarly polls among the greatest American presidents.

=== KEY: Abraham_c9af96
HEADWORD: Abraham
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abrahamic religions | Book of Genesis | Canaan | Hebron | Isaac | Ishmael | Islam | Kaaba
ENTRY:
Patriarch and prophet revered in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam, and the namesake of the [[Abrahamic religions]]. Originally named Abram, he is regarded in Judaism as the first Hebrew patriarch, who began the covenantal relationship between God and the Jewish people. Christianity treats him as forebear of Jesus and spiritual ancestor of all Christians, and [[Islam]] as a prophet in a chain from Adam to Muhammad. He is seen as common forefather of the Arabs through [[Ishmael]] and the Jewish people through [[Isaac]].

His story, told in the [[Book of Genesis]] (chapters 11–25), turns on God's promise of progeny and land. Called to leave his father Terah's house, Abram left Haran at age 75 with his wife Sarai (Sarah), who was childless, and his nephew Lot, and traveled to [[Canaan]], where he built altars. Abram and Lot later separated over pasture. Key episodes include the covenant of circumcision (Genesis 17) and the near-sacrifice of Isaac (Genesis 22). Abraham fathered Ishmael with Hagar and Isaac with Sarah. He bought the Cave of the Patriarchs at [[Hebron]] as Sarah's tomb. On his death, Isaac received "all Abraham's goods" and the other sons only "gifts."

In Christian tradition, drawing on Romans 4 and Galatians 3, he exemplifies justifying faith. In the Quran, Ibrahim is the "friend of God" and a model of pure monotheism who rejected idolatry; with Ishmael he built the [[Kaaba]] in Mecca.

Nearly all scholars view the patriarchal age as a late literary construct that does not correspond to any historical era, though the stories may retain some historical qualities. Many believe the traditions developed orally for centuries before entering Genesis during the Persian period, where they helped shape post-exilic Jewish identity.

=== KEY: Absolute_zero_6ef3c1
HEADWORD: Absolute zero
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fermion | Ideal gas law | Kelvin | Pauli exclusion principle | Superconductivity | Superfluidity | Uncertainty principle | Zero-point energy
ENTRY:
Lowest theoretical temperature, at which a system's internal energy, and in ideal cases its entropy, reach their minimum values. On the [[Kelvin|Kelvin]] scale it is 0 K, equivalent to −273.15 °C or −459.67 °F. The limit can be estimated by extrapolating the [[Ideal gas law|ideal gas law]] to the temperature at which a classical gas's volume or pressure becomes zero. ENTRY:
Set of spectroscopic techniques that measure the absorption of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] by a sample as a function of frequency or wavelength. === KEY: Abstract_algebra_df2135
HEADWORD: Abstract algebra
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic structure | Arthur Cayley | Category theory | Elementary algebra | Emmy Noether | Field (mathematics) | Group (mathematics) | Ring (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics that studies [[Algebraic structure|algebraic structures]], sets with specific operations acting on their elements. These include [[Group (mathematics)|groups]], [[Ring (mathematics)|rings]], [[Field (mathematics)|fields]], modules, vector spaces, lattices, and algebras over a field. The term was coined in the early 20th century to distinguish the subject from [[Elementary algebra]], which uses variables to represent numbers in computation and reasoning. The abstract viewpoint has become so basic to advanced mathematics that it is simply called "algebra," and "abstract algebra" is seldom used outside teaching. Algebraic structures and their homomorphisms form mathematical categories, and [[Category theory]] gives a unified framework for studying them.

About 1700 BC, Babylonians solved quadratic equations posed as word problems. Al-Khwarizmi originated the word "algebra" in AD 830, but fully symbolic algebra appeared only with François Viète's *New Algebra* (1591). In the late 18th century Leonhard Euler accepted negative and imaginary roots, though most European mathematicians resisted such numbers until the mid-19th century. George Peacock's *Treatise of Algebra* (1830) was the first attempt to put algebra on a strictly symbolic basis.

Group theory grew from several sources: Lagrange's 1770 study of the quintic, Gauss's 1801 work on modular arithmetic, Klein's 1872 Erlangen program, and Lie's 1874 theory of Lie groups. Galois in 1832 was first to use the term "group," for a collection of permutations closed under composition. [[Arthur Cayley]] defined a group in 1854 as a set with an associative operation and an identity, and Walther von Dyck in 1882 first required inverses. Disparate results unified into axiomatic definitions of groups, rings, and fields in the early decades of the 20th century, with major contributions from [[Emmy Noether]] on ring theory. Applications include the classification of symmetries in geometry and chemistry, error-correcting codes, and cryptography.

=== KEY: Abstract_art_356258
HEADWORD: Abstract art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstract expressionism | Cubism | Expressionism | Impressionism | Kazimir Malevich | Paul Cézanne | Piet Mondrian | Wassily Kandinsky
ENTRY:
Art that uses shape, form, color, and line to create compositions that may exist with a degree of independence from visual references in the world. Abstraction lies on a continuum. Art that conspicuously alters color or form is partially abstract, as in fauvism, which deliberately alters color, and [[Cubism]], which alters the forms of real objects. Total abstraction bears no trace of anything recognizable, as in geometric and lyrical abstraction.

From the Renaissance to the mid-19th century, Western art rested on the logic of perspective and the illusion of visible reality. By the late 19th century many artists wanted an art that reflected changes in technology, science, and philosophy. [[Impressionism]] and [[Expressionism]], which stressed sensation and psychological states over subject matter, prepared the way. James McNeill Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket* (1872) put visual sensation ahead of the depiction of objects, and Georgiana Houghton exhibited abstract "spirit" drawings in 1871. [[Paul Cézanne]]'s aim of a logical construction of reality in flat areas of modulated color became the basis of Cubism. Mysticism and theosophy influenced pioneers such as Hilma af Klint and [[Wassily Kandinsky]], and the teachings of Georges Gurdjieff and P. D. Ouspensky influenced Mondrian.

Fauvism, led by Henri Matisse, and Cubism, notably through Pablo Picasso, opened the door to abstraction in the early 20th century. In 1912 František Kupka exhibited *Amorpha, Fugue in Two Colors*, and Guillaume Apollinaire named the work of Robert Delaunay and others Orphism. Kandinsky produced some of the earliest acknowledged abstract paintings around 1910–12; [[Kazimir Malevich]] introduced Suprematism with *Black Square* (1915); and [[Piet Mondrian]] developed geometric neoplasticism. Later movements include [[Abstract expressionism]].

=== KEY: Abstract_machine_db7e86
HEADWORD: Abstract machine
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Theoretical model of a computing system that receives inputs and produces outputs according to predefined rules. It is called a machine because it allows step-by-step execution of programs, and abstract because it ignores many aspects of physical hardware. A typical abstract machine is defined by its inputs, outputs and the allowable operations that turn one into the other. === KEY: Abstraction_ef974c

=== KEY: Accuracy_and_precision_fc5aee
HEADWORD: Accuracy and precision
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Standard deviation
ENTRY:
Two measures of observational error. Accuracy is how close a measurement or set of measurements is to the true value, and precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. Precision describes random error, quantified by the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] of a set of results. In its more common sense, accuracy describes systematic error, or bias. The International Organization for Standardization defines *trueness* as the closeness of the mean of many results to the reference value. A measurement system may be accurate, precise, both or neither. Increasing sample size improves precision but does not correct a systematic error, while removing the error improves accuracy without changing precision. A measurement system is considered valid only if it is both accurate and precise.

Ancient Iranian empire (550–330 BC) founded by [[Cyrus the Great]] and the largest empire of its time. At its peak it covered roughly 5.5 million sq km, stretching from the Balkans and Cyrenaica in the west to the Indus Valley in the east, and including Anatolia, Cyprus, Mesopotamia, the Levant, the South Caucasus, parts of eastern Arabia, and large parts of Central Asia. Alexander the Great began a Macedonian campaign in 336 BC, defeated the Persians at Issus (333) and Gaugamela (331), and conquered the whole empire by 330 BC. ENTRY:
Chemical reaction between an acid and a base, used in titration to determine pH. Several theoretical frameworks describe these reactions. The concept was first proposed in 1754 by Guillaume-François Rouelle, who introduced the word base into chemistry. Antoine Lavoisier defined acids by their oxygen content around 1776, a view that Humphry Davy undermined in 1810 by showing that hydrogen sulfide and the hydrohalic acids contain no oxygen. Justus von Liebig defined acids in 1838 as hydrogen-containing compounds whose hydrogen can be replaced by a metal. The Arrhenius definition describes an acid as a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions in aqueous solution, and a base as one that increases hydroxide ions; it led to Svante Arrhenius's Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1903. The Lewis model is the broadest, with the Brønsted–Lowry theory a subset of it.

=== KEY: Acid_ed84f0
HEADWORD: Acid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acetic acid | Citric acid | Gilbert N. Lewis | Hydrochloric acid | PH | Proton | Sulfuric acid | Svante Arrhenius
ENTRY:
Molecule or ion able either to donate a [[Proton|proton]] (hydrogen cation, H⁺), as a Brønsted–Lowry acid, or to form a covalent bond with an electron pair, as a Lewis acid. In modern usage, "acid" alone implicitly means a Brønsted acid. Proton donors in water form the hydronium ion H₃O⁺ and are called Arrhenius acids; [[Svante Arrhenius]] attributed acidity to hydrogen cations in 1884. In 1923 Johannes Brønsted and Thomas Lowry independently generalized this to any proton transfer, including in non-aqueous solvents. In the same year [[Gilbert N. Lewis]] proposed the electron-pair definition, exemplified by boron trifluoride (BF₃), whose boron atom bonds to the lone pair on the nitrogen of ammonia (NH₃).

Aqueous acids taste sour, turn blue litmus red, and react with bases and with certain metals, such as calcium, to form salts. The word comes from Latin *acidus*, "sour." An aqueous acid solution has a [[PH|pH]] below 7, meaning a hydronium concentration above 10⁻⁷ moles per liter; the lower the pH, the higher the acidity. Strong acids dissociate fully in water, while weak acids dissociate partially and reach equilibrium with their conjugate bases. Strong acids and some concentrated weak acids are corrosive, though exceptions include boric acid.

Common examples are [[Hydrochloric acid]], found in gastric acid in the stomach, where it activates digestive enzymes; [[Acetic acid]], the acid in vinegar; [[Sulfuric acid]], used in car batteries and fertilizer manufacture; and [[Citric acid]], found in citrus fruits. Acids are classed as binary acids (hydrogen bonded to a nonmetal, such as HCl), oxoacids (containing oxygen, such as H₂SO₄), and organic acids with a carboxyl group (–COOH).

=== KEY: Adam_Smith_c86b03

Scottish economist and moral philosopher, a pioneer of political economy and a key figure of the Scottish Enlightenment. These changes strengthen the drive to use and weaken the capacity to control it. Addiction is understood as a brain disorder arising from psychosocial and neurobiological factors, although debate continues over how far volition is impaired and whether framing it as a disease affects views of personal agency.

Substance forms include [[Alcoholism|alcoholism]], [[Cannabis|cannabis]], [[Amphetamine|amphetamine]], [[Cocaine|cocaine]], [[Nicotine|nicotine]], and [[Opioid|opioid]] addiction. The DSM-5-TR recognizes only gambling disorder as a behavioral addiction; the ICD-11 also classifies gaming disorder. The core features are impaired control, rising priority given to the substance or behavior, and continued use despite harm, often with tolerance and withdrawal. Complications include overdose, blood-borne infection such as HIV and [[Hepatitis C]] among injecting users, organ damage, and co-occurring psychiatric conditions. Deaths by [[Suicide|suicide]] are considerably more frequent among people with substance use disorders.

About half of the risk is genetic; alcohol use disorder has an estimated heritability of about 50%. Environmental contributors include childhood adversity, chronic stress, peer and family influences, and availability of substances. Diagnosis is clinical. Addiction is managed as a chronic, relapsing condition, usually with medication where an effective one exists (for opioid, alcohol, and tobacco disorders, but not stimulants) combined with psychological and social support. An estimated 35–54% of people with a substance use disorder reach remission, typically after many years. About 163 million people worldwide were estimated to have a substance use disorder in 2021, most commonly involving alcohol.

City (pop., 2025: 6,287,000), capital and largest city of [[Ethiopia]]. Menelik II founded it in 1886 after finding nearby Mount Entoto unpleasant, and it became the empire's capital in 1889. Its mineral springs attracted nobles, then artisans, merchants and foreign visitors. Urban development began in the 20th century without prior planning, and later masterplans were drawn up under Italian occupation and by French and British consultants. At an elevation of 2,355 meters (7,726 ft), it is the highest capital in Africa. It is the headquarters of the [[African Union]] and the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa, and home to Addis Ababa University. The name is [[Amharic]] for "new flower".

One of the four basic [[Arithmetic|arithmetic]] operations, usually denoted by the plus sign (+). The other three are subtraction, multiplication and division. Adding two whole numbers gives their total, or sum, as in 3 + 2 = 5. Addition is commutative and associative, and adding 0 leaves a number unchanged. It can be defined for abstract [[Integer|integers]], [[Real number|real numbers]] and [[Complex number|complex numbers]], and in algebra for vectors and [[Matrix (mathematics)|matrices]]. Children learn it in primary education, and mechanical aids for it range from the ancient abacus to the modern computer. The plus sign first appeared in a 1456 German manuscript.

Capital (pop., 2024: 1,341,430) and most populous city of [[South Australia]]. Adelaide is the fifth-most populous city in [[Australia]]. Founded in 1836 as a planned settlement for free British migrants, it was laid out on a grid by Colonel William Light, with wide boulevards and public squares. It was named for Adelaide of Saxe-Meiningen, wife of King [[William IV]]. Known as the City of Churches for its diversity of faiths, it hosted the first Australian Grand Prix under the [[Formula One]] banner from 1985 to 1995. The city is classed as a Gamma+ level [[Global city|global city]], and its Mediterranean climate supports wine production in the region.
