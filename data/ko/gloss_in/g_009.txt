=== KEY: Archaeology_9e53fd
HEADWORD: Archaeology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Mesopotamia | Ouyang Xiu | Paleolithic | Prehistory | Shen Kuo | Song dynasty
ENTRY:
Study of human activity through the recovery and analysis of material culture. The archaeological record consists of artifacts, architecture, biofacts or ecofacts, sites, and cultural landscapes. The discipline is considered both a social science and a branch of the humanities; in North America it is usually treated as a subdiscipline of [[Anthropology|anthropology]], though it may also be classified with history or geography. Its work involves surveying, excavation, and analysis of the data collected.

Archaeologists study the human past from the first stone tools at Lomekwi in East Africa, 3.3 million years ago, to recent decades. The field is distinct from palaeontology, the study of fossil remains. It is particularly important for [[Prehistory|prehistoric]] societies, which by definition left no written records and account for over 99% of the human past, from the [[Paleolithic]] until the advent of literacy.

The discipline grew out of antiquarianism. King Nabonidus of Babylon, c. 550 BC, excavated and dated the foundation deposits of Naram-Sin's temples in [[Mesopotamia]] and is known as the first archaeologist. In the [[Song dynasty]] (960–1279), [[Ouyang Xiu]] and Zhao Mingcheng established the tradition of Chinese epigraphy, and in 1088 [[Shen Kuo]] criticized scholars for misattributing ancient bronze vessels. In Renaissance Europe, Cyriacus of Ancona recorded Greek and Roman monuments and inscriptions,. Archaeology developed out of antiquarianism in Europe during the 19th century and is now practiced worldwide.

Nation-states have used archaeology to create particular visions of the past. Current problems include pseudoarchaeology, looting of artifacts, lack of public interest, opposition to the excavation of human remains, and disputes over repatriation of artifacts versus their preservation for wider study.

=== KEY: Archaeopteryx_4c1c27
HEADWORD: Archaeopteryx
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archipelago | Charles Darwin | Dinosaur | Feather | Limestone | On the Origin of Species
ENTRY:
Extinct genus of bird-like [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]] that lived in the Late Jurassic, about 150 million years ago. The name derives from Ancient Greek words meaning “ancient” and “feather, wing”. It lived in what is now southern Germany, when Europe was an [[Archipelago|archipelago]] in a shallow tropical sea, and it was about the size of a Eurasian magpie. Despite its [[Feather|feathers]] and inferred ability to fly or glide, it shared many features with small dromaeosaurid and troodontid dinosaurs, including sharp teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail. Fourteen body fossils have been found, all from [[Limestone|limestone]] deposits near Solnhofen, Germany, and most preserve feather impressions. A complete specimen announced in 1861 came just two years after [[Charles Darwin]] published [[On the Origin of Species]], and the genus has since become key evidence for the origin of birds. Recent discoveries of other small feathered dinosaurs have raised questions about which animals are the ancestors of modern birds.

=== KEY: Archean_31e5bc
HEADWORD: Archean
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cyanobacteria | Hadean | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Second of the four geologic eons of Earth’s history, spanning 4,031 to 2,500 million years ago, between the [[Hadean]] and the Proterozoic. Earth was mostly a water world, with continental crust largely under a superocean deeper than today’s oceans. The atmosphere was a reducing atmosphere rich in methane and lacking free oxygen. The earliest known life, mostly shallow-water microbial mats called stromatolites, remained simple prokaryotes throughout the eon. Early [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] carried out [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] in the mid-to-late Archean, causing a permanent chemical change in the ocean and atmosphere after the eon.

=== KEY: Archery_bb0640
HEADWORD: Archery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crossbow
ENTRY:
Sport, practice or skill of using a bow to shoot arrows at a target, historically used for hunting and combat. The word derives from the Latin *arcus*, meaning “bow”. The oldest known arrows, from South African sites such as Sibudu Cave, date to about 72,000 to 60,000 years ago, while the earliest complete bows and arrows come from northern Europe. Bows eventually replaced the spear-thrower as the main means of launching shafted projectiles on every continent except Australasia. The composite bow was likely invented in central Asia, and the Welsh longbow proved its worth in continental warfare at the Battle of Crécy. [[Crossbow|Crossbows]] were introduced in Europe around the tenth century. Gunpowder rendered bows obsolete in battle by the 16th century, after which archery became mainly a competitive sport and recreational activity.

=== KEY: Archimedes_d0efef
HEADWORD: Archimedes
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Antikythera mechanism | Archimedes' principle | Calculus | Cicero | Lever | Pi | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
Greek mathematician, physicist, engineer, astronomer, and inventor from Syracuse in Sicily. Few details of his life are known, but his surviving work places him among the leading scientists of classical antiquity. Using infinitesimals and the method of exhaustion, he anticipated modern [[Calculus|calculus]] and rigorously proved theorems on the area of a circle, the surface area and volume of a sphere, the area of an ellipse, the area under a parabola. He derived an approximation of [[Pi|pi]], and devised a system using exponentiation to express very large numbers.

He was among the first to apply mathematics to physical phenomena. In statics and hydrostatics he proved the law of the [[Lever|lever]], made wide use of the concept of center of gravity, and stated the law of buoyancy known as [[Archimedes' principle]]. According to Vitruvius, he found how to test whether a goldsmith had mixed silver into King Hiero II's golden wreath by observing water displaced in a bath, and ran into the street crying "Eureka!" A planetarium device attributed to him may have been a precursor to the [[Antikythera mechanism]].

His inventions include the screw pump, compound pulleys, and war machines that helped defend Syracuse against Rome. He was killed by a Roman soldier during the siege of Syracuse despite orders that he not be harmed. [[Cicero]] described visiting his tomb, surmounted by a sphere and cylinder representing his most valued discovery.

His writings, little known in antiquity, were compiled c. 530 AD by Isidore of Miletus, translated into Arabic in the 9th century and Latin in the 12th, and influenced scientists of the Renaissance and the [[Scientific Revolution]]. The discovery in 1906 of the Archimedes Palimpsest gave new insight into his methods.

=== KEY: Archimedes_principle_da6345
HEADWORD: Archimedes’ principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Fluid mechanics
ENTRY:
Law of [[Fluid mechanics|fluid mechanics]] stating that the upward buoyant force on a body immersed in a fluid, fully or partially, equals the weight of the fluid it displaces. Formulated by [[Archimedes]] of Syracuse, it was set out in *On Floating Bodies* (c. 246 BC). It allows the buoyancy of any floating object to be calculated: the object rises if the net force is positive, sinks if negative, and stays in place if zero. The buoyant force equals fluid density multiplied by submerged volume and gravitational acceleration, written F = ρgV. It explains the flotation of ships and submarines.

=== KEY: Archimedes_screw_2b9e27
HEADWORD: Archimedes’ screw
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Irrigation
ENTRY:
Hydraulic machine consisting of an outer cylinder and an inner cylinder with helical blades. It can operate as a pump or a power generator, and has been used for drainage, [[Irrigation|irrigation]] and sewage management. Its origins are unclear. [[Archimedes]] is traditionally credited with inventing it, but other historians argue that the Egyptians were already using the screw, and the earliest mention dates from between 250 and 220 BC. Modern screw pumps are used in wastewater treatment plants. Run in reverse, screw turbines act as small hydroelectric generators at low-head sites, operating at flows of 0.01 m³/s to 14.5 m³/s and heads of 0.1 m to 10 m.

Chain, cluster or collection of islands, in an ocean, a sea or a smaller body of water. === KEY: Architectural_drawing_349593

ENTRY:
[[Technical drawing]] of a building or building project, used to develop a design, communicate it to clients and builders, guide construction or record an existing building. Drawings follow conventions for views such as floor plans and sections, sheet sizes, units, scales and annotation. A floor plan is a horizontal section cut through a building at about 1.2 meters (four feet) above floor level. Drawings are made to scale so that dimensions can be read from them, and the note “Do not scale off dimensions” guards against copying errors. Drawings were once made in ink and copied by hand, and in the 20th century they moved to tracing paper for mechanical copying. Most architectural drawings are now made with [[Computer|computer]]-aided design (CAD) software.

=== KEY: Architectural_engineering_78eb09
HEADWORD: Architectural engineering

LINK TARGETS: Interior design | Lighting | Plumbing
ENTRY:
Discipline that deals with the engineering and construction of buildings, including environmental, structural, mechanical, electrical and computational systems. Also called building engineering, it is distinguished from [[Interior design|interior design]] and architectural design. Architectural engineers focus on structural integrity, mechanical systems and energy efficiency, and collaborate with architects. Mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) specialists design heating, ventilation and air conditioning, [[Plumbing|plumbing]], electric power distribution and [[Lighting|lighting]]. Emerging as a licensed profession in the 20th century, the field addresses greenhouse gas emissions and the construction of resilient buildings.

=== KEY: Architectural_theory_ac192a
HEADWORD: Architectural theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Architecture | Leon Battista Alberti | Vitruvius
ENTRY:
Act of thinking, discussing and writing about [[Architecture|architecture]]. It is taught in architecture schools and takes forms such as the lecture, the treatise and the competition entry. The Roman writer [[Vitruvius]] produced the most prominent surviving theory of antiquity in *De architectura*, probably written between 27 and 23 BC, which defined the classical orders and proposed three laws: *firmitas*, *utilitas* and *venustas*, translated in the 17th century as “firmness, commodity, and delight.” Its rediscovery in 1414 influenced Renaissance architects such as [[Leon Battista Alberti]]. Books, magazines and journals published an unprecedented number of works by architects and critics in the 20th century, so that styles and movements formed and dissolved more quickly than before.

=== KEY: Architecture_2d242b
HEADWORD: Architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Giorgio Vasari | Gothic architecture | Le Corbusier | Leon Battista Alberti | Louis Sullivan | Ludwig Mies van der Rohe | Vitruvius
ENTRY:
Art and technique of designing buildings and other structures, especially habitable ones. Architecture uses civil engineering techniques but is considered a visual art, and it is both the process and the product of sketching, planning, designing, and constructing. The word comes from Latin *architectura*, from Greek *arkhitéktōn*, "chief creator." Buildings are often perceived as cultural symbols, and historical civilizations are often identified with their surviving architecture.

Architecture began in the prehistoric era as rural, oral vernacular building that developed from trial and error. The T-shaped pillars of Göbekli Tepe, built c. 9600 BC, are among the oldest evidence of deliberate large-scale construction. Ancient urban architecture was preoccupied with religious structures and buildings symbolizing the power of rulers, until Greek and Roman architecture shifted focus to civic virtues. Indian and Chinese architecture influenced forms across Asia, and Buddhist architecture took on diverse local forms. In the Middle Ages the pan-European Romanesque and [[Gothic architecture|Gothic]] styles of cathedrals and abbeys emerged, while the Renaissance favored Classical forms realized by architects known by name. The roles of architect and engineer later separated.

Modern architecture began after World War I as an avant-garde movement seeking a completely new style for a new social and economic order, stressing modern techniques, materials, and simplified geometric forms, and paving the way for high-rise buildings. Many architects came to see modernism as ahistorical and anti-aesthetic, and postmodern and contemporary architecture followed. Sustainable architecture was introduced in the late 20th century.

The earliest surviving text on architectural theory is the 1st-century BC treatise *De architectura* by [[Vitruvius]], who held that a good building embodies *firmitas*, *utilitas*, and *venustas*: durability, utility, and beauty. [[Leon Battista Alberti]], in *De re aedificatoria*, saw beauty as an objective quality found in proportion. The notion of style in the arts was developed in the 16th century by [[Giorgio Vasari]], and Sebastiano Serlio wrote the first handbook stressing practice. In 1836 A. W. N. Pugin, in *Contrasts*, held Gothic to be the only true Christian architecture. John Ruskin, in *The Seven Lamps of Architecture* (1849), argued that a building is not architecture unless it is adorned. The skyscraper architect [[Louis Sullivan]] declared that "form follows function," a notion that came to include aesthetic, psychological, and cultural dimensions. [[Le Corbusier]] distinguished mere construction from architecture by its power to move the viewer, and [[Ludwig Mies van der Rohe]] is said to have remarked that architecture "starts when you carefully put two bricks together." Philosophers from Plato to Michel Foucault and Ludwig Wittgenstein have debated whether architecture differs from building.

=== KEY: Architecture_of_India_9e3a5a
HEADWORD: Architecture of India
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chandigarh | Delhi Sultanate | India | Le Corbusier | Mughal Empire | Taj Mahal
ENTRY:
Architectural traditions rooted in the history, culture and religion of [[India]]. Hindu, Jain, Buddhist and Sikh styles are the most prevalent, alongside Indo-Islamic, Rajput, Mughal, South Indian and Indo-Saracenic architecture. Early wooden structures did not survive, so the earliest surviving examples are rock-cut temples. Hindu temples are traditionally divided into the Dravidian style of southern India and the Nagara style of northern India, with regional variations. The [[Delhi Sultanate]] developed Indo-Islamic architecture, which the [[Mughal Empire]] brought to its height, with the [[Taj Mahal]] as its high point. British colonial rule introduced Neoclassical and Gothic Revival styles, which merged with Indo-Islamic forms into the Indo-Saracenic style. After independence, Swiss-French architect [[Le Corbusier]] was commissioned to design the city of [[Chandigarh]], which influenced a generation of architects toward modernism.

=== KEY: Architecture_of_Mesopotamia_686ff2
HEADWORD: Architecture of Mesopotamia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Assyria | Babylon | Lebanon | Sumer | Urban planning
ENTRY:
Ancient architectural style of the Tigris–Euphrates river region, spanning from the 10th millennium BC, when the first permanent structures were built, to the 6th century BC. Its cultures included the [[Sumer|Sumerians]], Akkadians, [[Assyria|Assyrians]] and [[Babylon|Babylonians]]. Its accomplishments included [[Urban planning|urban planning]], the courtyard house and ziggurats, and scribes drafted and managed construction for government, nobility or royalty. Brick was the ordinary building material, and Sumerian masonry was usually mortarless. Buildings were periodically destroyed and rebuilt on the same spot, raising the level of cities into mounds. Civic buildings protected their facades with cones of colored stone, terracotta panels and clay nails. Babylonian temples were massive structures of crude brick supported by buttresses, and Assyria imitated this brick architecture in its own palaces and temples. Prized imports included cedar from [[Lebanon]].

=== KEY: Archive_e727b0
HEADWORD: Archive
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ebla | Library | Ugarit
ENTRY:
Accumulation of historical records or materials in any medium, or the facility in which they are kept. Archives contain primary source documents that accumulate over an individual’s or organization’s lifetime, generated naturally through legal, commercial, administrative or social activity and kept to show that history and function. Archival records are usually unpublished and often unique, unlike books. Records are selected for permanent preservation for their enduring cultural, historical or evidentiary value, and the choice of what to include can be a political act. Clay-tablet archives from the third and second millennia BC have been found at [[Ebla]], [[Ugarit]] and other sites. A person who works in archives is an archivist, and the field is called archival science. Archives are distinct from [[Library|libraries]], although archival collections are often housed within library buildings.

=== KEY: Arctic_443531
HEADWORD: Arctic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alaska | Arctic Circle | Arctic Ocean | Greenland | Permafrost | Sea ice | Tundra
ENTRY:
Polar region of Earth surrounding the North Pole, lying north of the [[Arctic Circle]]. The region includes parts of northern Norway (including Svalbard and Jan Mayen), Sweden, and Finland; Russia; the United States ([[Alaska]]); Canada (Yukon, Northwest Territories, and Nunavut); the Danish Realm ([[Greenland]]); and northern Iceland, together with the [[Arctic Ocean]] and adjacent seas.

Its boundary is defined in several ways. The Arctic Circle (about 66° 34′ N) marks the approximate southern limit of the midnight sun and the polar night. Ecologists define it as the region where the warmest month's average temperature is below 10 °C, roughly the tree line.

The climate has cold winters and cool summers. Precipitation, mostly snow, is low, with most of the area receiving less than 50 cm. Average winter temperatures can fall to −40 °C, and the coldest recorded is approximately −68 °C. The land is predominantly treeless [[Permafrost|permafrost]] beneath [[Tundra|tundra]]. Herbivores include the lemming, muskox, and reindeer, preyed on by the Arctic fox and grizzly bear; the polar bear hunts mainly marine life from the ice, and about 200 bird species breed there.

The Arctic is warming, with consequences including decline of Arctic [[Sea ice|sea ice]], and methane emissions as permafrost thaws. Isotherms have migrated poleward about 56 km per decade over 30 years, so the region as defined by tree line and temperature is shrinking. Models project near-complete to complete loss of September sea ice between 2035 and around 2067. The region holds oil, gas, minerals, freshwater, and fish, and is home to indigenous peoples adapted to its conditions. Melting ice has increased interest in shipping routes such as the Northern Sea Route.

Island group lying north of the Canadian mainland, in the [[Arctic Ocean]]. === KEY: Arctic_Circle_443964
HEADWORD: Arctic Circle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctic Circle | Axial tilt | Midnight sun | Murmansk | Polar night | Summer solstice | Winter solstice
ENTRY:
Northernmost of the five major circles of latitude, and one of the two polar circles. Its southern counterpart is the [[Antarctic Circle]]. The circle lies at about 66° 34′ N and currently at 66°33′51.1″ N, a latitude that depends on [[Axial tilt|Earth's axial tilt]], which fluctuates by some 2° over a 41,000-year period. It is the southernmost latitude at which the Sun does not rise on the December solstice or does not set on the June solstice, phenomena called [[Polar night|polar night]] and [[Midnight sun|midnight sun]]. In [[Murmansk]], three degrees north of the circle, the Sun stays below the horizon for 20 days before and after the [[Winter solstice|winter solstice]], and above it for 20 days before and after the [[Summer solstice|summer solstice]]. The circle drifts toward the North Pole by about 14.5 m (48 ft) per year.

=== KEY: Arctic_Ocean_c57f1d
HEADWORD: Arctic Ocean
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bering Strait | Fridtjof Nansen | Inuit | Sea ice
ENTRY:
Smallest and shallowest of the world's five oceanic divisions, centered on the North Pole. It covers approximately 14,060,000 sq km and is the coldest ocean. The International Hydrographic Organization recognizes it as an ocean, though some oceanographers call it the Arctic Mediterranean Sea. It extends south to about 60°N and is surrounded by Eurasia and North America, bounded by the [[Bering Strait]] on the Pacific side and the Greenland–Scotland Ridge on the Atlantic side. Average depth is around 1,200 m.

The ocean is partially covered by [[Sea ice|sea ice]] throughout the year and almost completely in winter. Its salinity is the lowest on average of the five oceans because of low evaporation, heavy freshwater inflow from rivers, and limited exchange with saltier waters. Satellite records show continuing decline in ice extent: in September 2012 it reached a record minimum, 49% below the 1979–2000 average.

People have lived in the North American Arctic for at least 17,000–50,000 years. The Thule, ancestors of the modern [[Inuit]], spread across the region from c. 200 BC. Pytheas of Massilia recorded a journey north in 325 BC to a land he called "Eschate Thule." A persistent myth of an "Open Polar Sea" was considered disproven by the early 1900s. [[Fridtjof Nansen]] made the first nautical crossing in the *Fram* Expedition of 1893–96. The submarine USS *Nautilus* made the first nautical transit of the North Pole in 1958, and Wally Herbert led the first surface crossing, by dog sled, in 1969. Territorial claims by bordering states and interest in hydrocarbons, minerals, and shipping routes make the region geopolitically significant.

Small fox native to the Arctic regions of the [[Northern Hemisphere]], common across the [[Tundra|Arctic tundra]]. Breeding pairs are monogamous and raise their young in complex underground dens. Natural predators include [[Golden eagle|golden eagles]], [[Polar bear|polar bears]] and [[Wolverine|wolverines]].

=== KEY: Arcturus_8eda11

Red giant star, and the brightest star in the northern constellation Boötes. Its Bayer designation is α Boötis. === KEY: Area_deec4f
HEADWORD: Area
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acre | Calculus | Circle | Hectare | International System of Units | Polygon | Triangle
ENTRY:
Measure of a region's size on a surface. The area of a plane region refers to a shape or planar lamina, while surface area refers to an open surface or the boundary of a three-dimensional object. It is the two-dimensional analogue of the length of a curve and the volume of a solid.

Area is measured by comparing a shape to squares of a fixed size. The standard unit in the [[International System of Units]] is the square meter (m²), the area of a square with sides one meter long. In mathematics the unit square is defined to have area one. Every unit of length has a corresponding unit of area: 1 km² = 1,000,000 m², and 1 m² = 10,000 cm². The [[Hectare|hectare]] equals 10,000 m², and the [[Acre|acre]], also used for land, equals 4,840 square yards or 43,560 square feet. At the atomic scale area is measured in barns, 1 barn = 10⁻²⁸ m².

Well-known formulas give the areas of simple shapes such as [[Triangle|triangles]], rectangles, and [[Circle|circles]], and the area of any [[Polygon|polygon]] can be found by dividing it into triangles. Shapes with curved boundaries usually require [[Calculus|calculus]], and determining the area of plane figures was a major motivation for its historical development. Surface areas of simple solids were computed by the ancient Greeks; complicated surfaces usually require multivariable calculus.

Area can be defined axiomatically, as a function from measurable plane figures to real numbers, and it can be proved that such a function exists. In analysis, area is defined through Lebesgue measure, and it is related to determinants in linear algebra and is a basic property of surfaces in differential geometry.

=== KEY: Arecaceae_475a3d
HEADWORD: Arecaceae

ENTRY:
Palm family of perennial [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] in the [[Monocotyledon|monocot]] order Arecales. Its members include climbers, [[Shrub|shrubs]], stemless plants and tree-like palms. The family comprises 181 [[Genus|genera]] with around 2,600 [[Species|species]], most restricted to tropical and subtropical climates. Most palms have large, compound, evergreen leaves called fronds, arranged at the top of an unbranched stem; the genus Hyphaene is an exception, with branched palms. They are among the best-known and most extensively cultivated plant families, and a wide range of common products and foods are derived from them. In many historical cultures, palms were symbols of victory, peace and fertility.

=== KEY: Aristotle_798a01

ENTRY:
Ancient Greek philosopher and polymath whose writings span the natural sciences, philosophy, linguistics, economics, politics, psychology, and the arts.

He was born in Stagira, in northern Greece, the son of Nicomachus, physician to King Amyntas of Macedon. At about 17 or 18 he moved to [[Athens]] to study at [[Plato]]'s Academy, remaining nearly 20 years, until Plato's death. He then went to Assos in Asia Minor, where he and Theophrastus researched botany and marine biology, continuing on [[Lesbos]]. In 343/42 BC [[Philip II of Macedon]] invited him to tutor his 13-year-old son, [[Alexander the Great]]. Returning to Athens in 335 BC, he founded the Lyceum, whose members were called Peripatetics, and established a library there.

Only about a third of his written output survives. His work contains the earliest known systematic study of [[Logic|logic]], including the syllogism, which shaped the field into the 19th century. In biology he classified animals on the basis of dissection and field observation. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* he held that human flourishing, *eudaimonia*, arises from rational virtue practiced in community, and in politics he analyzed 158 constitutions and advocated polity, a blend of democracy and oligarchy. Some doctrines, such as spontaneous generation and geocentric cosmology, were later overturned.

His physical science dominated from late antiquity into the Renaissance and was not systematically replaced until the Enlightenment and the rise of classical mechanics. Medieval Muslim scholars called him “The First Teacher,” and medieval Christians such as [[Thomas Aquinas]] called him “The Philosopher.” His ethics has drawn renewed interest with the modern revival of virtue ethics.

=== KEY: Arithmetic_9b8f09
HEADWORD: Arithmetic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebra | Integer | Irrational number | Natural number | Number theory | Numeral system | Rational number | Real number
ENTRY:
Elementary branch of mathematics concerned with numbers and the operations performed on them.

Its core operations are addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division; in a wider sense it also includes exponentiation, extraction of roots, and taking logarithms. The word derives from the Latin *arithmetica*, from the Greek *arithmos*, “number,” and *arithmetike tekhne*, “the art of counting.” Definitions differ. A narrow view restricts arithmetic to the natural numbers, but the more common view extends it to integers, rational numbers, real numbers, and sometimes complex numbers. It is closely related to [[Number theory|number theory]], which studies the properties of integers such as divisibility, factorization, and primality and is traditionally known as higher arithmetic.

The main kinds of numbers are [[Natural number|natural numbers]], which start from 1 and are also called counting numbers; whole numbers, which include 0; [[Integer|integers]], which add the negative whole numbers; and [[Rational number|rational numbers]], which are ratios of two integers. Every rational number corresponds to a finite or a repeating decimal. [[Irrational number|Irrational numbers]], such as π, cannot be expressed as a ratio of two integers and have infinite decimal representations without repetition. Rational and irrational numbers together make up the [[Real number|real numbers]].

Numbers are written in [[Numeral system|numeral systems]], which are either positional or non-positional. All early systems were non-positional, meaning a digit's value does not depend on its position; the simplest is the unary system, which repeats one symbol. Decimal arithmetic, using the numerals 0 to 9, is the most common, while binary arithmetic, using 0 and 1, is used by most computers.

Arithmetic is at least thousands and possibly tens of thousands of years old. Egyptians and Sumerians invented numeral systems for practical problems in about 3000 BC. From the 7th and 6th centuries BC the ancient Greeks studied numbers more abstractly and introduced rigorous mathematical proof. The ancient Indians developed the concept of zero and the decimal system, which Arab mathematicians refined and spread to the Western world in the medieval period. The first mechanical calculators appeared in the 17th century, modern number theory and axiomatic foundations developed in the 18th and 19th centuries, and electronic calculators and computers transformed the speed and accuracy of calculation in the 20th.

Arithmetic underlies [[Algebra|algebra]], calculus, and statistics, plays a similar role in physics and economics, and is among the earliest forms of mathematics that students encounter. Its cognitive and conceptual foundations are studied by psychology and philosophy.

=== KEY: Armour_865800
HEADWORD: Armor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Celts | Firearm | Industrial Revolution | Kevlar | Sumer | World War I
ENTRY:
Covering that protects a person, animal, or vehicle from physical injury or damage, especially from weapons or projectiles in combat. The British spelling is armor.

Personal armor protects soldiers and war animals, while vehicle armor is used on warships, armored fighting vehicles, and some combat aircraft. The word also names armored forces: after the development of armored warfare, tanks and mechanized infantry came to be called collectively “armor.” It comes from Old French *armure*, from Latin *armatura*, and is dated in English from 1297.

Armor has been used throughout recorded history, evolving from leather and fabric through chain mail and metal plate to modern composites. The first record of body armor appears on the Stele of Vultures in ancient [[Sumer]], and scale mail is attested in New Kingdom Egypt, Zhou dynasty China, and dynastic India. Mail of interlocking iron rings is believed to have first appeared some time after 300 BC; its invention is credited to the [[Celts]], and the Romans are thought to have adopted it. Plate armor became widespread in medieval Europe when water-powered trip hammers made the forming of plates faster and cheaper. Heavy cavalry in several European countries wore breast and back plates until the first year of [[World War I]] (1914–15).

Armor drove ancient technologies including wood lamination, mining, metal refining, and leather processing, and influenced the [[Industrial Revolution]]. It was also an important factor in the development of [[Firearm|firearms]], which in turn revolutionized warfare and gradually made heavy plate obsolete on the battlefield. Modern body armor uses materials such as [[Kevlar]] and ceramics to stop bullets.

=== KEY: Art_978d7d
HEADWORD: Art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aesthetics | Architecture | Aristotle | Art criticism | Immanuel Kant | Painting | Plato | Sculpture
ENTRY:
Diverse range of human activity centered on works made as expressions of creativity or imagination, expected to give an audience a worthwhile experience of emotion, thinking, or beauty.

There is no strictly agreed definition of art, and its interpretation has varied greatly across history and cultures. In the Western tradition, the three classical branches of visual art are [[Painting|painting]], [[Sculpture|sculpture]], and [[Architecture|architecture]]; theater, dance, literature, music, and film belong to the broader “arts.” The word comes from a Latin sense meaning “skill” or “craft,” and until the 17th century art referred to any skill or mastery. After the 17th century, where aesthetic considerations are paramount, the fine arts were separated from acquired skills in general, such as the decorative or applied arts. Some argue that this distinction reflects value judgments more than any clear definitional difference.

The purposes of art include communicating ideas, creating beauty, exploring perception, giving pleasure, and generating strong emotions. [[Plato]], [[Aristotle]], Socrates, and [[Immanuel Kant]] were among the philosophers who questioned its meaning. Aristotle considered epic poetry, tragedy, comedy, and music to be imitative arts that differ in medium, object, and manner, and held that imitation is natural to humankind. Kant took science to represent the domain of knowledge and the arts the domain of the freedom of expression.

The nature of art is explored in the branch of philosophy known as [[Aesthetics|aesthetics]], and artworks are studied in [[Art criticism|art criticism]] and art history. Abstract markings on rocks in South Africa indicate symbolic behavior at least 73,000 years ago, and figurative depictions of animals and human-like figures appear around 51,200 years ago in caves in Sulawesi, Indonesia. AI-generated and AI-assisted work has intensified debates over authorship, creativity, and agency.

=== KEY: Arthritis_7aa485
HEADWORD: Arthritis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cartilage | Gout | Ibuprofen | Immune system | Osteoarthritis | Paracetamol | Rheumatoid arthritis
ENTRY:
General term for disorders of the joints, marked by pain and stiffness.

Symptoms generally include joint pain and stiffness, and may include redness, warmth, swelling, and decreased range of motion. Onset can be gradual or sudden, and in some types other organs, such as the skin, are also affected. The term derives from *arthr-*, “joint,” and *-itis*, “inflammation.” More than 100 conditions fall under it.

The most common form is [[Osteoarthritis]], which affects more than 3.8% of people. It usually occurs with age, often in the hips, knees, shoulders, and fingers, and results from damage to joint [[Cartilage|cartilage]] through prior injury or long-term wear. [[Rheumatoid arthritis]], the second most common at about 0.24%, is an autoimmune disorder that often affects the hands and feet, in which the [[Immune system|immune system]] attacks the lining of the joint capsule. [[Gout]] is caused by urate crystals depositing in joints after excessive uric acid production.

Risk factors include age, obesity, previous joint injury, and, for inflammatory types, female sex, family history, and exposure to tobacco smoke. Diagnosis rests on clinical examination, supported by imaging and blood tests.

There is no cure for most types. Early treatment commonly includes resting the joint and applying heat or ice; weight loss and exercise may reduce force across weight-bearing joints. Medications such as [[Ibuprofen|ibuprofen]] and [[Paracetamol|acetaminophen]] relieve symptoms, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs are used for autoimmune forms, and severe cases may require joint replacement surgery.

About 15% of people in Australia and more than 20% in the United States have a type of arthritis. It becomes more common with age and is a common reason people cannot work.

=== KEY: Arthropod_130818
HEADWORD: Arthropod
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian | Chelicerata | Chitin | Crustacean | Hexapoda | Insect | Moulting | Myriapoda
ENTRY:
Invertebrate of the phylum Arthropoda, with a segmented body, paired jointed appendages, and an exoskeleton.

The exoskeleton, or cuticle, is made of [[Chitin|chitin]] and is often mineralized with calcium carbonate. To grow, arthropods must go through stages of [[Moulting|molting]], shedding the exoskeleton to reveal a new one. The name comes from the Greek *arthron*, “joint,” and *pous*, “foot.”

Arthropoda is the largest animal phylum, with estimates of species ranging from 1,170,000 to 5–10 million and accounting for over 80 percent of all known living animal species. They live in marine, freshwater, land, and air ecosystems. The phylum is divided into four major groups: [[Chelicerata]] (including arachnids and horseshoe crabs), [[Myriapoda]] (centipedes and millipedes), [[Crustacean|Crustacea]] (crabs, shrimp, lobsters, and other mostly aquatic forms), and [[Hexapoda]], which includes the [[Insect|insects]], the group with the most described species of any class.

Hemolymph, the analogue of blood, circulates through an open circulatory system. The nervous system is ladder-like, with paired ventral nerve cords, and the brain encircles the esophagus. Vision relies on compound eyes and simple ocelli, although the main eyes of spiders are ocelli that can form images. All terrestrial species use internal fertilization, and almost all arthropods lay eggs, though a few, such as aphids and scorpions, are genuinely viviparous. Hatchlings range from miniature adults to grubs and caterpillars that undergo total metamorphosis.

Arthropod ancestry dates to the [[Cambrian]] period. Arthropods contribute to the human food supply directly as food and, more importantly, as pollinators of crops, while some species spread severe disease to humans, livestock, and crops.

Born in [[Łódź]], in Congress Poland, which was then part of the [[Russian Empire]], he moved to [[Berlin]] at age ten to study. He gave his first performance with the Berlin Philharmonic in 1900, at age 13. In 1904 he moved to Paris, where he met [[Maurice Ravel]]. In 1912 he made his London debut and found a musical home in the salon of Paul and Muriel Draper, where he associated with [[Igor Stravinsky]] and [[Pablo Casals]]. During World War I he stayed in London, and in 1916 and 1917 he made his first tours of Spain and South America.

=== KEY: Arthur_Schopenhauer_06d29d
HEADWORD: Schopenhauer, Arthur
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Asceticism | Frankfurt | Idealism | Immanuel Kant | Indian philosophy
ENTRY:
German philosopher and writer, known as the “Philosopher of Pessimism.” His main work, *The World as Will and Representation* (1818; expanded 1844), characterizes the phenomenal world as the manifestation of a blind and irrational noumenal will. Building on the transcendental idealism of [[Immanuel Kant]], he developed an atheistic, metaphysical, and ethical system that rejected the contemporaneous ideas of [[Idealism|German idealism]]. He was among the first Western philosophers to affirm significant tenets of [[Indian philosophy]], such as [[Asceticism|asceticism]], denial of the self, and the notion of the world as appearance. His work attracted little attention during his lifetime. His writing on aesthetics, morality, and psychology later influenced many thinkers and artists. He lived for much of his later life in [[Frankfurt]].

=== KEY: Arthur_Wellesley_1st_Duke_of_Wellington_7a61bf

British Army officer and statesman. Born in [[Dublin]] into an Anglo-Irish family, he fought in India during the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, winning at Seringapatam in 1799, and won a decisive victory over the Maratha Confederacy at the Battle of Assaye in 1803. He rose to prominence in the [[Peninsular War]] and was promoted to field marshal after the victory at Vitoria in 1813. A Tory, he served as prime minister from 1828 to 1830 and briefly in 1834, oversaw the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, and opposed the Reform Act 1832. === KEY: Artichoke_f3add9

ENTRY:
Variety of thistle (*Cynara cardunculus* var. *scolymus*) cultivated for its edible flower buds. The buds are eaten before the [[Flower|flowers]] bloom; the edible parts are the fleshy lower bracts and the base, known as the heart. The artichoke is a domesticated form of the wild cardoon, a perennial native to the Mediterranean region. === KEY: Artificial_intelligence_9d0996
HEADWORD: Artificial intelligence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artificial general intelligence | Chatbot | Computer science | Deep learning | Generative AI | Graphics processing unit | Large language model | Neural network (machine learning)
ENTRY:
Capability of computational systems to perform tasks typically associated with human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, problem-solving, perception, and decision-making; also the field of research that develops such systems.

The field spans [[Computer science|computer science]], engineering, and mathematics, and draws on psychology, linguistics, philosophy, and neuroscience. Its traditional goals include learning, reasoning, knowledge representation, planning, natural language processing, and perception. Techniques include state space search, mathematical optimization, formal logic, [[Neural network (machine learning)|artificial neural networks]], and methods from statistics, operations research, and economics. Applications include web search engines, [[Chatbot|chatbots]], virtual assistants, autonomous vehicles, play and analysis in strategy games such as chess and Go, and content generation.

The term was coined by John McCarthy in a 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth Conference of 1956, and AI was founded as an academic discipline in 1956. The field went through cycles of optimism alternating with periods of disappointment and lost funding known as AI winters. Interest and funding grew substantially after 2012, when [[Graphics processing unit|graphics processing units]] began to accelerate neural networks and [[Deep learning|deep learning]] outperformed earlier techniques. Growth accelerated further after 2017 with the transformer architecture, and advances in [[Generative AI|generative AI]], which creates and modifies media, spurred the AI boom of the 2020s. Reasoning models, a type of [[Large language model|large language model]] trained to generate intermediate chains of thought, emerged in 2024 and improved performance on mathematics and coding, though they can produce incorrect outputs known as hallucinations.

Some companies, including OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta, aim to create [[Artificial general intelligence]], AI able to complete nearly any cognitive task at least as well as a human; it has not been achieved. Safety, ethical concerns, environmental effects, and potential existential risks have prompted discussions of AI regulation.

=== KEY: Artificial_island_84e51f
HEADWORD: Artificial island
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bahrain | Edo period | Ellis Island | Land reclamation | Mexico City | Nagasaki | Neolithic | Tenochtitlan
ENTRY:
Island constructed by humans rather than formed through natural processes. Although there is no clear legal definition, artificial islands were built as early as the [[Neolithic]] era. In modern times they are usually formed by [[Land reclamation|land reclamation]]. Al-Sayah Island in [[Bahrain]] is described as the world’s first permanent artificial island, created at least 1,200 years ago around a water spring. The Aztec city of [[Tenochtitlan]], predecessor of [[Mexico City]], stood on a natural island surrounded by artificial islands. Dejima, a contained center for European merchants, was built in the bay of [[Nagasaki]] during Japan’s [[Edo period]]. [[Ellis Island]] was a former islet greatly expanded by land reclamation. Dubai’s Palm Jumeirah extends 5 kilometers into the Persian Gulf. China’s artificial islands in the South China Sea have intensified territorial disputes.

=== KEY: Artillery_49c409
HEADWORD: Artillery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catapult | Gunpowder | Howitzer | Joseph Stalin | Mortar (weapon) | Napoleonic Wars | World War I | World War II
ENTRY:
Ranged weapons that launch munitions far beyond the range and power of infantry firearms. The first known [[Catapult|catapult]] was developed in Syracuse in 399 BC, and early artillery was designed to breach walls and fortifications during sieges. Until the introduction of [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]], artillery depended on mechanical energy; a 1st-century BC Roman catapult launching 6.55 kg (14.4 lb) stones achieved 16 kilojoules, compared with 240 kilojoules for a mid-19th-century 12-pounder gun. Since the introduction of gunpowder and cannon, the term has largely meant shell-firing guns, [[Howitzer|howitzers]], [[Mortar (weapon)|mortars]], and rocket artillery. Artillery caused the majority of combat deaths in the [[Napoleonic Wars]], [[World War I]], and [[World War II]]. In 1944, [[Joseph Stalin]] called artillery “the god of war.”

=== KEY: Artiodactyla_111571
HEADWORD: Artiodactyla
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cattle | Cetacean | Deer | Eocene
ENTRY:
Order of placental mammals, typically ungulates that bear weight equally on two of their five toes. The name comes from Greek *ártios* (“even”) and *dáktylos* (“finger, toe”). Many artiodactyls digest plant cellulose in one or more stomach chambers rather than in the intestine. Molecular and fossil evidence places [[Cetacean|cetaceans]], such as whales, dolphins, and porpoises, within the group, most closely related to hippopotamuses. The roughly 270 land-based even-toed ungulate species include pigs, antelopes, [[Deer|deer]], camels, [[Cattle|cattle]], and hippopotamuses. The oldest fossils date to the early [[Eocene]], about 53 million years ago. Many artiodactyls are of great dietary, economic, and cultural importance to humans.

=== KEY: Artist_4047c5
HEADWORD: Artist
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Person who creates [[Art|art]] or practices one of the arts. The most common usage refers to a practitioner of the visual arts, such as [[Painting|painting]] or drawing. The term is also used in entertainment for [[Actor|actors]], musicians, singers, and dancers, and is less commonly applied to [[Writer|writers]], for whom “author” is generally used. === KEY: Artistic_gymnastics_03b87d

ENTRY:
Discipline of gymnastics in which athletes perform short routines on different types of apparatus. World Gymnastics governs the sport and assigns the Code of Points used to score elite international competition. The gymnastic system was mentioned in writings by [[Homer]], [[Aristotle]], and [[Plato]], and it evolved in Bohemia and Germany at the beginning of the 19th century. === KEY: Arts_and_Crafts_movement_dfa963
HEADWORD: Arts and Crafts movement
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Art Nouveau | British Empire | British Isles | Decorative arts
ENTRY:
International trend in the [[Decorative arts|decorative]] and fine arts that developed earliest and most fully in the [[British Isles]], then spread across the [[British Empire]] and to Europe and North America. It was a reaction against the perceived decline of design standards associated with machinery and factory production. Design reformers, including Henry Cole and Owen Jones, criticized the exhibits at the Great Exhibition of 1851 as excessively ornate. The term was first used in 1887 by T. J. Cobden-Sanderson, and the movement flourished between about 1880 and 1920. It drew on the ideas of Thomas Carlyle, John Ruskin, and William Morris. Some consider it the root of the Modern Style, while others regard it as opposed to [[Art Nouveau]], which it criticized for its use of industrial materials such as iron. In Japan, it emerged in the 1920s as the Mingei movement.

=== KEY: Arturo_Toscanini_5dac5a
HEADWORD: Toscanini, Arturo
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Milan | Rio de Janeiro
ENTRY:
Italian conductor. Known for his intensity, perfectionism, and eidetic memory, he was at various times music director of La Scala in [[Milan]] and of the New York Philharmonic. He began conducting at 19 in [[Rio de Janeiro]], when he took the podium to lead *Aida* after the audience booed the substitute conductor, and he conducted 18 operas over the rest of that season. He was the first music director of the NBC Symphony Orchestra (1937–1954), and his radio and television broadcasts and many recordings made him a household name, especially in the United States. In 1931 he resigned from La Scala rather than conduct the Fascist anthem *Giovinezza*, and he opposed Fascism.
