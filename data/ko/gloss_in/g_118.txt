Biogeographical region of semi-arid savanna in Africa, forming a transition zone between the [[Sahara]] to the north and the more humid Sudanian savannas to the south. The name derives from Arabic sāḥil, meaning "coast, shore," a figurative reference to the southern edge of the Sahara. The population was approximately 80 million in 2018, and since the 2010s coups, insurgencies and foreign interventions have led to the region being called the Coup Belt.

=== KEY: Sail_8e186b
HEADWORD: Sail
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Drag (physics) | Kevlar | Lift (force)
ENTRY:
Tensile structure of fabric or other membrane material that uses wind power to propel sailing craft. Sails are made from canvas or polyester cloth, laminated membranes or bonded filaments, usually in a three- or four-sided shape. They produce propulsive force through [[Lift (force)|lift]] and [[Drag (physics)|drag]], depending on their angle of attack to the apparent wind, and on points of sail close to the wind they act as an airfoil. Sails are attached to a mast, boom or other spar, raised by a halyard and angled to the wind by a sheet, and battens can extend the trailing edge. There are two main rig types: the square rig, with yards set perpendicular to the keel, and the fore-and-aft rig, with sails set along the keel. Modern sails often use high-performance materials such as [[Kevlar]] and Dyneema. The origin of the sail is unknown, and many do not consider sails to have been used before the 5th millennium BCE.

=== KEY: Sailing_f0d49e
HEADWORD: Sailing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Nile | Sail | Sailing (sport)
ENTRY:
Propulsion of a vehicle by wind power. Sailing uses wind acting on [[Sail|sails]], wing sails or kites to propel a craft on water, on ice or on land over a chosen course. From prehistory until the second half of the 19th century, sailing craft were the main means of maritime trade and transport, and the gun-armed sailing warships of the Age of Sail dominated navies. Steam gradually replaced sail over the late 19th century, as steamships ran scheduled services at higher speeds and gained fuel economy. In the 21st century most sailing is recreation or [[Sailing (sport)|sport]], divided into racing and cruising. Sailing relies on the lift and drag generated by the sails, resisted by the hull, keel and rudder, which lets a boat sail both upwind and downwind but not too close into the wind. It was probably invented independently in Island Southeast Asia and the Mediterranean, possibly including the [[Nile]] and the Guayaquil River in Ecuador.

=== KEY: Sailing_ship_9ed240
HEADWORD: Sailing ship
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Ancient Egypt | Austronesian peoples
ENTRY:
Sea-going vessel that uses sails mounted on masts to harness wind power. Sailing ships carried square-rigged or fore-and-aft sails, or a combination of both in barques, barquentines and brigantines. Early sailing ships were used on rivers and coasts in [[Ancient Egypt]] and the Mediterranean. The [[Austronesian peoples]] developed catamaran and outrigger hulls and the crab-claw sail, which enabled their expansion beginning in Taiwan c. 3000 BC across the islands of the Indo-Pacific. European ships with square rigs became prevalent during the [[Age of Discovery]], when they crossed oceans between continents. By the 1880s, ships with triple-expansion steam engines could compete with sail on all major routes, though commercial sail continued into the 20th century, with the last ceasing to trade by c. 1960.

=== KEY: Sailing_sport_8e5350
HEADWORD: Sailing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 
ENTRY:
Competitive sport in which sailing craft race in fleets, in pairs or in teams. Races are held on closed courses or point to point, in sheltered waters, along coasts or on the open ocean, and are sanctioned by federations and yacht clubs. A competition among multiple vessels is a regatta, made up of several races, and the crew that performs best over the series is the overall winner. In fleet racing a race can include anywhere from four boats to hundreds, and a regatta must comprise at least three races to count. Match racing pits two boats against each other, with the aim of finishing ahead of the opponent rather than sailing as fast as possible. Team racing usually involves two teams of three boats each, and the team that scores ten or fewer points wins. The Racing Rules of Sailing govern yacht racing, windsurfing, kitesurfing and other racing around a course. The Barcolana regatta in Italy holds the Guinness World Record for the largest sailing race, with 2,689 boats and over 16,000 sailors at the starting line.

Series of hearings and prosecutions of people accused of [[Witchcraft|witchcraft]] in colonial Massachusetts from February 1692 to May 1693. Tax paid to a [[Government|government]] on the sale of certain goods and services, usually collected by the seller from the consumer at the point of purchase. === KEY: Salmon_e30d21

Any of several commercially important ray-finned fishes of the family [[Salmonidae]]. Genus of rod-shaped, gram-negative bacteria in the family Enterobacteriaceae, named after the American veterinary surgeon Daniel Elmer Salmon. === KEY: Salmonidae_702eb3

Family of ray-finned fishes, the sole extant member of the suborder Salmonoidei, including [[Salmon|salmon]], [[Trout|trout]], char, graylings and whitefishes. It comprises 11 extant genera and over 200 species of coldwater predatory fish native to the subarctic and cool temperate Northern Hemisphere. === KEY: Salt_af334c
HEADWORD: Salt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Food preservation | Herodotus | Hypertension | Iodine | Mediterranean Sea | Sahara | Sodium chloride | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Mineral composed primarily of [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]] (NaCl), used as a seasoning and preservative. Granulated for food it is called table salt; as a natural crystalline mineral it is rock salt or halite. It supplies the essential dietary minerals sodium and chlorine, and saltiness is one of the basic human tastes. Salting, brining, and pickling are ancient methods of [[Food preservation|food preservation]].

Some of the earliest evidence of salt processing, around 6000 BC, comes from present-day Romania, where people boiled spring water to extract salts; the harvest of salt from Xiechi Lake, China, dates to at least 6000 BC. Salt was prized by the ancient Hebrews, Greeks, Romans, Byzantines, Hittites, Egyptians, and Indians. It was carried across the [[Mediterranean Sea]] by boat and across the [[Sahara]] by camel caravan; [[Herodotus]] described salt routes across Libya in the 5th century BC. Scarcity and universal need led nations to fight over salt and tax it, as in the El Paso Salt War of the late 1860s.

Salt is obtained from salt mines and by evaporating seawater and spring water. Of an annual global production of around three hundred million tonnes, only a small percentage is eaten; the greatest use is as a feedstock for chemicals such as caustic soda and chlorine, and for products such as polyvinyl chloride, plastics, and paper pulp. Table salt usually contains an anti-caking agent and may be [[Iodine|iodized]] to prevent iodine deficiency.

Sodium is essential to health as an electrolyte, but excessive salt consumption increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases such as [[Hypertension|hypertension]]. The [[World Health Organization]] recommends that adults consume less than 2,000 mg of sodium, equivalent to 5 grams of salt, per day.

=== KEY: Salt_chemistry_2ae930
HEADWORD: Salt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acid–base reaction | Crystal | Ion | Ionic bonding | Max Born | Sodium chloride
ENTRY:
Chemical compound consisting of positively charged [[Ion|ions]] (cations) and negatively charged ions (anions), giving no net electric charge. The ions are held together by electrostatic forces termed [[Ionic bonding|ionic bonds]]. Also called an ionic compound, a salt may contain inorganic ions, such as chloride (Cl⁻), or organic ones, such as acetate (CH₃COO⁻), and ions that are monatomic, such as Na⁺, or polyatomic, such as ammonium (NH₄⁺) and carbonate (CO₃²⁻).

Individual ions usually have several near neighbors, so they form a continuous three-dimensional network rather than molecules, and salts usually form [[Crystal|crystalline]] structures when solid. Salts of small ions typically have high melting and boiling points and are hard and brittle. As solids they are almost always electrical insulators, but when melted or dissolved they conduct well because the ions become mobile. Historically, the term referred only to the compound formed by neutralization of an acid and a base; as the definitions broadened, it became synonymous with ionic compound, though the older distinction persists in some literature.

In 1913 William Henry Bragg and his son William Lawrence Bragg determined the structure of [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]], showing six equidistant nearest neighbors for each atom and long-range crystalline order. Proof that such compounds are made of ions came in the mid-1920s from X-ray reflection experiments. [[Max Born]] predicted crystal energies from the assumption of ionic constituents, and they matched thermochemical measurements.

Salts form when metals react with halogen gases, when solvent evaporates from a solution, or by precipitation on mixing two solutions. An [[Acid–base reaction|acid–base reaction]] gives a salt and water, as in NaOH + HCl → NaCl + H₂O.

=== KEY: Sampling_statistics_c6caf5
HEADWORD: Sampling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bias | Bible | Census | Statistical population | Survey methodology
ENTRY:
Selection of a subset of individuals from a [[Statistical population|statistical population]], used to estimate characteristics of the whole. Sampling is central to statistics, [[Survey methodology|survey methodology]] and quality assurance. It costs less and yields data faster than a [[Census]] of the entire population, which is often impossible. Samples should be representative, and statisticians aim for an unbiased group; survey weights can adjust for the design, particularly in stratified sampling. Random sampling by lots is mentioned several times in the [[Bible]]. In 1786 Pierre Simon Laplace estimated the population of France from a sample. A famous failure came in 1936, when the Literary Digest predicted a Republican win in the U.S. presidential election, because its subscription and telephone lists were heavily [[Bias|biased]] toward Republicans. Probability methods include simple random, systematic, stratified and cluster sampling.

ENTRY:
Member of the professional warrior class in pre-industrial [[Japan]], who served as retainers to the lords. The word is thought to derive from a term meaning "one who serves."
