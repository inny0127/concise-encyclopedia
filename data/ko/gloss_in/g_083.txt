=== KEY: Magma_1b62e9
HEADWORD: Magma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Igneous rock | Magma chamber | Volcano
ENTRY:
Molten or semi-molten rock beneath the surface of Earth, the source of all [[Igneous rock|igneous rocks]]. It may also contain suspended crystals and gas bubbles. Magma is produced by melting of the mantle or crust in tectonic settings including subduction zones, continental rift zones, mid-ocean ridges and hotspots. It may be stored in a [[Magma chamber|magma chamber]] before it feeds a [[Volcano|volcano]] and erupts as lava, or it may solidify underground as intrusions such as dikes, sills and batholiths. Magmas are classified by silica content as felsic, intermediate, mafic or ultramafic, and silica content correlates with viscosity and temperature. Felsic magmas contain more than 63% silica and are highly viscous, so their lavas usually erupt explosively. Magma has been encountered directly during geothermal drilling only three times, twice in Iceland and once in Hawaii.

=== KEY: Magma_chamber_3abe06
HEADWORD: Magma chamber
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Supervolcano | Volcano
ENTRY:
Large pool of molten rock beneath the Earth's surface, often beneath [[Volcano|volcanoes]]. Magma rises through cracks because it is less dense than the surrounding rock, and it pools in a chamber when it cannot find a path upward. Chambers are commonly built up over time by successive injections, and most known chambers lie between 1 km and 10 km down. As the magma cools, denser minerals crystallize and sink. Gas released as crystals form can raise chamber pressure, possibly enough to produce an eruption. [[Supervolcano|Supervolcano]] eruptions are possible only when an extraordinarily large chamber forms at a shallow level.

=== KEY: Magnet_6c72aa
HEADWORD: Magnet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Compass | Electromagnet | Iron | Magnetite
ENTRY:
Material or object that produces a magnetic field. Magnets attract ferromagnetic materials such as [[Iron|iron]], steel, nickel and cobalt, and attract or repel other magnets. Permanent magnets keep their field without external energy and are made from hard ferromagnetic materials such as alnico and ferrite, processed in a strong magnetic field during manufacture. === KEY: Magnetic_field_96f64c
HEADWORD: Magnetic field
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric motor | Hall effect | Iron | Solar wind
ENTRY:
Physical property of space that quantifies the magnetic influence at a given location. Magnetic fields deflect moving electric charges and currents, apply torques that twist magnets, attract or repel magnets and magnetic materials such as [[Iron|iron]], and induce electric currents in conducting material. They are created by permanent magnetic materials and by moving electric charges, including electric current. Because strength and direction vary with location, the field is a vector field, described by two closely related quantities, B and H. In magnetostatics, the field of a steady current is given by the Biot–Savart law. Magnetic fields are central to [[Electric motor|electric motors]], generators, transformers and electromagnets, and the [[Hall effect]] reveals charge carriers in materials. Earth's magnetic field creates a magnetosphere that shields the planet from the [[Solar wind|solar wind]].

=== KEY: Magnetic_resonance_imaging_a9f19f
HEADWORD: Magnetic resonance imaging
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: CT scan | Hydrogen | Proton
ENTRY:
Medical imaging technique that generates pictures of the anatomy and physiological processes inside the body. MRI uses strong magnetic fields, field gradients and radio waves, not X-rays or ionizing radiation, which distinguishes it from [[CT scan|computed tomography]] and positron emission tomography. Because [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] atoms are abundant in water and fat, most scans essentially map where water and fat are located in the body. Pulses of radio waves excite the nuclear spin of [[Proton|protons]], and gradients localize the signal in space. MRI was developed in the 1970s and 1980s from nuclear magnetic resonance research, and Paul Lauterbur proposed using a magnetic field gradient to create images in a two-dimensional plane. Compared with CT, it gives better contrast for soft tissues, but scans are longer and louder, and metal implants may pose a risk.

=== KEY: Magnetism_64fd3e
HEADWORD: Magnetism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetism | Ferromagnetism | Hans Christian Ørsted | Iron | Magnetic field | Shen Kuo | Thales of Miletus | William Gilbert (physicist)
ENTRY:
Class of physical phenomena arising through a [[Magnetic field|magnetic field]], which allows objects to attract or repel each other. Because both electric currents and the magnetic moments of elementary particles give rise to a magnetic field, magnetism is one of two aspects of [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]]. The familiar effects occur in [[Ferromagnetism|ferromagnetic]] materials, which are strongly attracted by magnetic fields and can be magnetized to become permanent magnets. Only a few substances are ferromagnetic; the most common are [[Iron|iron]], cobalt, nickel, and their alloys.

All substances exhibit some type of magnetism, and materials are classified by bulk susceptibility. Paramagnetic substances, such as aluminum and oxygen, are weakly attracted to an applied field; diamagnetic substances, such as copper and carbon, are weakly repelled; antiferromagnetic materials, such as chromium, have a more complex relationship with a field.

Magnetism was first noticed in the ancient world when lodestones, naturally magnetized pieces of the mineral magnetite, were seen to attract iron. The word *magnet* comes from the Greek for "the Magnesian stone." Aristotle attributed the first scientific discussion of magnetism to [[Thales of Miletus]]. The earliest Chinese literary reference is in the 4th-century BC book *Guiguzi*, and the 11th-century scientist [[Shen Kuo]] was the first to write of the magnetic needle compass, in the *Dream Pool Essays*. By the 12th century the Chinese used the lodestone compass for navigation. In 1600 [[William Gilbert (physicist)|William Gilbert]] concluded from experiments with a model earth that the Earth is itself magnetic, which explains why compasses point north. Understanding of the relationship between electricity and magnetism began when [[Hans Christian Ørsted]] discovered that an electric current could create a magnetic field.

=== KEY: Magnetostatics_292f13
HEADWORD: Magnetostatics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electrostatics | Maxwell's equations
ENTRY:
Branch of physics concerned with magnetism in systems with steady electric currents. It is the magnetic analog of [[Electrostatics|electrostatics]], where charges are stationary. Its equations can predict fast magnetic switching events lasting nanoseconds or less, and it is even a good approximation when currents are not steady, provided they do not alternate rapidly. It is widely used in micromagnetics, such as models of computer memory storage devices. Starting from [[Maxwell's equations]] and assuming steady currents, the equations separate into independent sets for the electric and magnetic fields. For a known current distribution, the magnetic field follows from the Biot–Savart law, and it can also be derived from a vector potential. Magnetization in strongly magnetic materials must be included explicitly.

=== KEY: Magnitude_astronomy_014c59
HEADWORD: Magnitude
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hipparchus | Parsec | Sirius
ENTRY:
Logarithmic measure of the brightness of an astronomical object. The scale is logarithmic, so a magnitude 1 star is exactly 100 times brighter than a magnitude 6 star, and each step is about 2.512 times brighter than the one above. The brighter an object appears, the lower its magnitude, and the brightest objects have negative values. Apparent magnitude is an object's brightness, which depends on its intrinsic luminosity, distance and extinction. Absolute magnitude is the apparent magnitude an object would have at 10 [[Parsec|parsecs]] for stars. [[Hipparchus]] introduced an imprecise but systematic system of magnitudes in ancient times. The Sun has an apparent magnitude of −27, and [[Sirius]] is −1.46.

=== KEY: Mahayana_600248
HEADWORD: Mahayana
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Theravada | Tibet | Vajrayana | Zen
ENTRY:
Largest branch of [[Buddhism]], followed by the older [[Theravada]]. Its name is Sanskrit for "Great Vehicle." It is a broad group of Buddhist traditions, texts, philosophies, and practices that developed in ancient India from about the 1st century BC onward. It accepts the main scriptures and teachings of early Buddhism but also recognizes scriptures that Theravada does not accept as canonical. Scriptures such as the Lotus Sutra and the Vajra-cutter Sutra emphasize the ideal of the *bodhisattva*, who vows to attain buddhahood to save all living beings, a compassionate attitude called *bodhicitta*.

Mahayana generally holds that buddhahood through the bodhisattva path is available to all beings who take the bodhisattva vow, and it regards the state of the arhat as incomplete and a lesser aspiration. Its buddhology includes buddhas and bodhisattvas absent from Theravada, such as Amitabha and Vairocana, and distinctive conceptions of buddhahood such as *Trikaya* and *Upaya*. Its philosophy includes the Madhyamaka theory of emptiness (*sunyata*) and the idealistic Vijnanavada, the "doctrine of consciousness."

Initially a small movement in India, Mahayana became an influential force in Indian Buddhism, and large scholastic centers associated with it, such as Nalanda and Vikramashila, thrived between the 7th and 12th centuries. It spread from South Asia to Central Asia, East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Himalayan regions. Traditions such as [[Zen]], Pure Land, and Tiantai are the predominant forms of Buddhism in China, Korea, Japan, Taiwan, Singapore, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, and Indonesia. [[Vajrayana]], or Mantra traditions, a subset of Mahayana using tantric methods considered to lead swiftly to buddhahood, are dominant in [[Tibet]], Mongolia, Bhutan, and other Himalayan regions. As of 2010, 53% of Buddhists belonged to East Asian Mahayana and 6% to Vajrayana, compared with 36% to Theravada.

System for physically transporting postcards, letters, and parcels. A postal service can be private or public, though many governments restrict private systems. Since the mid-19th century, national postal systems have generally been government monopolies, with the fee prepaid. In ancient India the Mauryan Empire (322–185 BC) developed an early mail service. === KEY: Main_sequence_603e92

ENTRY:
Continuous band of stars on a [[Hertzsprung–Russell diagram|Hertzsprung–Russell diagram]] of stellar color against brightness, where stars spend most of their lives. During this phase core hydrogen fusion into helium dominates. The heat produced creates a pressure gradient that balances gravitational collapse, keeping the star in [[Hydrostatic equilibrium|hydrostatic equilibrium]]. Main-sequence stars, also called dwarf stars, are the most numerous true stars in the universe and include the [[Sun]]. A star's position on the band depends mainly on its mass. Stars below about 1.5 solar masses fuse hydrogen mainly through the proton–proton chain, while more massive stars use the CNO cycle, in which carbon, nitrogen and oxygen act as intermediaries. More massive stars leave the main sequence sooner, evolving into [[Red giant|red giants]] or [[Supergiant|supergiants]] once hydrogen is exhausted.

=== KEY: Maize_7e3a62

Tall, stout grass (*Zea mays*), also called corn, cultivated worldwide for its cereal grain. The leafy stalk bears male inflorescences, or tassels, that produce [[Pollen|pollen]], and female inflorescences called ears, which yield the grain, known as kernels. Plants range in height from 1.2 to 4 m, and kernels, arranged in 8 to 32 rows around the cob, are usually yellow or white in modern varieties but can be orange, red, brown, blue, purple, or black.

Maize protein is low in some essential amino acids, and its niacin becomes available only if freed by alkali treatment.

Maize is diploid with 20 chromosomes. [[Barbara McClintock]] used it to validate her transposon theory of "jumping genes," for which she won the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, and it remains an important [[Model organism|model organism]] for genetics. The genome contains 32,540 genes. Two insect pests, European corn borer and corn rootworms, have each caused annual losses of a billion dollars in the United States. Much maize is now genetically modified.

Their bodies have 20 segments (rarely 21), divided into a head, thorax and abdomen. Except for fairy shrimps, malacostracans are the only living arthropods with compound eyes on movable stalks. [[Decapod|Decapods]], such as shrimp and crabs, support major fisheries, and krill form a foundation of marine food webs. The name, from the Greek for soft shell, is a misnomer because the shell is usually hard. French zoologist Pierre André Latreille applied it in 1802.

=== KEY: Malaria_49dfef
HEADWORD: Malaria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equator | Liver | Polymerase chain reaction | Red blood cell | Southeast Asia | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Mosquito-borne infectious disease caused by single-celled parasites of the genus *Plasmodium*. It is transmitted by the bite of infected female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. The parasites first reproduce and mature in the [[Liver|liver]] without causing symptoms, then spread into the bloodstream, where they infect and destroy [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]]. Five species commonly infect humans. *P. falciparum*, responsible for the vast majority of deaths, *P. vivax*, and *P. knowlesi* are associated with more severe cases, while *P. ovale* and *P. malariae* generally cause milder illness.

Symptoms, which usually begin 10 to 15 days after the bite, include fever, chills, fatigue, vomiting, and headaches, and in severe cases jaundice, seizures, coma, or death. Cerebral malaria, if untreated, can lead to death within forty-eight hours of the first symptoms. Survivors may develop partial immunity, which fades over months to years without continuing exposure. Diagnosis is by microscopic examination of blood films or rapid diagnostic tests; methods using the [[Polymerase chain reaction]] are considered the gold standard but are costly and not widely available where malaria is common.

Prevention relies on mosquito nets, repellents, insecticide spraying, draining standing water, and prophylactic medications. As of 2023, two vaccines have been endorsed by the [[World Health Organization]]. Parasites have evolved resistance to several drugs, including chloroquine and, in parts of [[Southeast Asia]], artemisinin.

The disease is widespread in tropical and subtropical regions around the [[Equator|equator]]. In 2024, some 282 million cases resulted in an estimated 610,000 deaths. The WHO African Region accounted for approximately 94% of cases and 95% of deaths. Malaria is commonly associated with poverty; in 2005 Africa was estimated to lose US$12 billion a year to it.

=== KEY: Malay_language_6a19af

Austronesian pluricentric macrolanguage native to several islands of Maritime Southeast Asia and the Malay Peninsula. === KEY: Mali_Empire_b6b84f
HEADWORD: Mali Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ghana Empire | Ibn Battuta | Mansa Musa | Mecca | Niger River | Songhai Empire
ENTRY:
Empire in West Africa, c. 1235 to 1610. Founded by Sundiata Keita, it became renowned for the wealth of its monarchs, especially [[Mansa Musa]]. At its peak it was the largest empire in West Africa, influencing the region's culture through the spread of its language, laws, and customs.

It began as a small Mandinka kingdom at the upper reaches of the [[Niger River]], centered on the Manding region, and developed during the 11th and 12th centuries as the [[Ghana Empire]] declined and trade shifted southward. Sundiata Keita, a warrior-prince of the Keita dynasty, freed the local people from the king of the Sosso Empire, Soumaoro Kanté; the conquest of Sosso in c. 1235 marked Mali's emergence as a major power, with the Kouroukan Fouga as its constitution. After Sundiata's death in c. 1255, rulers bore the title *mansa*. In 1285, Sakura, a former slave of the imperial family who had risen to general, carried out a military coup, and the Keita line was later restored.

Mansa Musa took the throne in c. 1312 and made a famous pilgrimage to [[Mecca]] from 1324 to 1326, where his expenditure of gold caused significant inflation in Egypt. Maghan I succeeded him in c. 1337 but was deposed by his uncle Suleyman in 1341. During Suleyman's 19-year reign [[Ibn Battuta]] visited Mali; his death marked the end of Mali's Golden Age.

From 1507 neighboring states, including the [[Songhai Empire]], chipped away at Mali's borders. A disastrous defeat outside Djenne in 1599 ended hopes of recovery after Songhai's collapse, and the empire disintegrated into independent chiefdoms; the Keitas retreated to Kangaba as provincial chiefs.

HEADWORD: Mamluk
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abbasid Caliphate | Crusades | Egypt | Ilkhanate | Levant | Mamluk Sultanate | Syria
ENTRY:
Slave-soldier or enslaved mercenary in the Muslim world. Mamluks were purchased as military slaves, converted to Islam and trained in warfare. After training they were freed but remained in the ruling military caste, forming elite regiments that in some periods rose to sovereign power. The institution developed under the [[Abbasid Caliphate]] in the 9th century and spread to include Circassians, Mongols, Georgians and Armenians. Mamluk factions seized the sultanate centered on [[Egypt]] and [[Syria]] in 1250. The [[Mamluk Sultanate]] defeated the Mongol [[Ilkhanate]] at Ain Jalut in 1260 and expelled the last [[Crusades|Crusaders]] from the [[Levant]] in 1302. In Egypt, Mamluks held status above the general population until their massacre by Muhammad Ali in 1811.

=== KEY: Mamluk_Sultanate_807ad4
HEADWORD: Mamluk Sultanate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baybars | Cairo | Crusader states | Levant | Mongols | Ottoman Empire | Selim I
ENTRY:
State in Egypt, the [[Levant]] and the Hejaz (1250–1517), with [[Cairo]] as its capital. It was ruled by a military caste of mamluks, freed slave-soldiers, headed by a sultan. The sultanate was established in 1250 with the overthrow of the Ayyubid dynasty. Under sultans [[Baybars]] and Qalawun, the Mamluks defeated the [[Mongols]] in 1260 and conquered the [[Crusader states]] by the end of the 13th century. They then expanded into Nubia, Cyrenaica, the Hejaz and southern Anatolia. Internal strife and financial distress weakened the sultanate under later rulers. In 1516, Qansuh al-Ghuri was killed in battle against the [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] sultan [[Selim I]], who conquered Egypt in 1517 and ended Mamluk rule. Cairo was one of the largest cities in the world at the time, and Mamluk architecture still makes up much of its historic fabric.

=== KEY: Mammal_374261
HEADWORD: Mammal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bat | Blue whale | Carnivora | Neolithic Revolution | Primate | Rodent | Synapsida
ENTRY:
Vertebrate animal of the class Mammalia, characterized by milk-producing mammary glands for feeding the young, a broad neocortex region of the brain, fur or hair, and three middle ear bones. These traits distinguish mammals from reptiles and birds, from which their ancestors diverged in the Carboniferous period over 300 million years ago. About 6,640 extant species have been described and divided into 27 orders.

The largest orders by number of species are the [[Rodent|rodents]], [[Bat|bats]], and eulipotyphlans (hedgehogs, moles, and shrews), followed by the [[Primate|primates]], the even-toed ungulates (including pigs, bovids, and whales), and the [[Carnivora]]. Mammals are the only living members of the clade [[Synapsida]]. They originated from cynodonts, an advanced group of therapsids, during the Late Triassic to Early Jurassic, and achieved their modern diversity in the Paleogene and Neogene periods of the Cenozoic era, after the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs. They have been the dominant terrestrial animal group from 66 million years ago to the present.

Mammals range in size from the 30–40 mm bumblebee bat to the 30 m [[Blue whale]], possibly the largest animal ever. All modern mammals give birth to live young except the five species of monotremes, which lay eggs. Most are intelligent, and they communicate through ultrasound, scent marking, alarm signals, singing, and echolocation; in humans, through complex language.

Domestication of mammals played a major role in the [[Neolithic Revolution]]. Domesticated mammals supply power for transport and agriculture, as well as meat, dairy products, fur, and leather. The decline and extinction of many mammals is driven primarily by poaching and habitat destruction, chiefly deforestation.

=== KEY: Mammoth_f0ecad
HEADWORD: Mammoth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arctic Ocean | Asian elephant | Elephant | Georges Cuvier | Holocene | Miocene | Siberia | Woolly mammoth
ENTRY:
Extinct elephantid genus, *Mammuthus*, related to modern [[Elephant|elephants]]. Mammoths lived from the late [[Miocene]] (around 6.2 million years ago) into the [[Holocene]] and inhabited Africa, Asia, Europe, and North America at various times. They are distinguished from living elephants by spirally twisted tusks and, in some later species, a thick coat of fur. The oldest species, *Mammuthus subplanifrons*, appeared in southern and eastern Africa around 6 million years ago. The [[Woolly mammoth]] (*M. primigenius*) evolved 700–400,000 years ago in [[Siberia]], and survived on Wrangel Island in the [[Arctic Ocean]] until about 4,000 years ago. Mammoths and [[Asian elephant|Asian elephants]] are more closely related to each other than either is to African elephants. In 1796 the French biologist [[Georges Cuvier]] first identified woolly mammoth remains as an extinct species.
