=== KEY: Totalitarianism_678e6e
HEADWORD: Totalitarianism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolf Hitler | Authoritarianism | Cold War | Islamism | Joseph Stalin | Nazi Germany | Soviet Union
ENTRY:
Form of government that completely controls public and private life, prohibits opposing political parties and suppresses opposition to the state. A totalitarian government uses an ideology to control the economy, education, the arts and sciences, and the private morality of its citizens. The difference from [[Authoritarianism|authoritarian]] rule is one of degree: totalitarianism features a charismatic dictator and a fixed worldview. The word was first used in the early 1920s to describe Italy's Fascist government. During the early [[Cold War]], it came to compare the [[Soviet Union]] under [[Joseph Stalin]] with [[Nazi Germany]] under [[Adolf Hitler]]. Some historians have challenged the concept, arguing that total control was never achieved and that Nazism and Stalinism differed substantially. In the 21st century the term has also been applied to [[Islamism|Islamist]] movements, a use that is also contested.

ENTRY:
Neotropical [[Bird|bird]] of the family Ramphastidae, marked by large, often colorful bills. Toucans are arboreal and usually found in pairs or small flocks. They nest in tree hollows, often excavated by [[Woodpecker|woodpeckers]], and typically lay two to four white eggs. === KEY: Touchscreen_588711

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capacitance | OLED | Smartphone
ENTRY:
Display that detects touch input from a user, typically with a finger or stylus. A touchscreen combines a touch panel, layered over an electronic visual display, so that the screen serves as both input and output. The display is often an LCD or [[OLED]] screen. Users tap, swipe or pinch to act directly on on-screen content rather than through a mouse or touchpad, and touchscreens are common in [[Smartphone|smartphones]], tablets, laptops, point-of-sale systems and automated teller machines (ATMs). ENTRY:
Neurodevelopmental disorder involving tics, which typically begin in childhood or adolescence. Tics are sudden, repetitive movements or sounds, often preceded by an unwanted premonitory urge. About 1% of school-age children and adolescents are estimated to have it; coprolalia, involuntary swearing, occurs only in a minority. There is no cure. === KEY: Tourism_e8dce7
HEADWORD: Tourism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: COVID-19 pandemic | Great Recession | League of Nations | Sustainability | Travel | United Nations
ENTRY:
[[Travel]] for pleasure, and the commercial activity of providing and supporting such travel. UN Tourism defines it more generally as people "staying in places outside their usual environment for not more than one consecutive year for leisure and not less than 24 hours, business and other purposes." The English words *tourist* (1772) and *tourism* (1811) derive from *tour*, from Old French *torner*, from Latin *tornare*, "to turn on a lathe."

Definitions have shifted over time. In 1936 the [[League of Nations]] defined a foreign tourist as someone traveling abroad for at least 24 hours, and the [[United Nations]] amended this in 1945 to include a maximum stay of six months. In 1994 the UN identified three forms: domestic tourism, by residents within their own country; inbound tourism, by non-residents entering a country; and outbound tourism, by residents traveling to another country. The terms tourism and tourist are sometimes used pejoratively to imply a shallow interest in the places visited, whereas traveler is often a mark of distinction.

International tourism receipts grew to US$1.03 trillion in 2005, and international tourist arrivals passed 1 billion for the first time in 2012. Numbers declined between the second half of 2008 and the end of 2009 because of the [[Great Recession]] and the 2009 H1N1 influenza outbreak, then recovered until the [[COVID-19 pandemic|COVID-19 pandemic]] ended the growth. The World Tourism Organization estimated that international arrivals might have fallen by 58% to 78% in 2020, a potential loss of US$0.9–1.2 trillion in receipts. Global tourism accounts for approximately 8% of greenhouse-gas emissions, and many organizations are shifting toward [[Sustainability|sustainable]] tourism to balance economic benefits with environmental and social responsibility.

=== KEY: Tower_8f8b35
HEADWORD: Tower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Castle | Cooling tower | Eiffel Tower | Great Wall of China | Qin dynasty | Ziggurat of Ur
ENTRY:
Tall structure, taller than it is wide, built to serve purposes other than habitation. Towers are distinguished from masts by their lack of guy-wires and, like tall buildings, are self-supporting. Height serves functions such as clock visibility, surveillance from a [[Castle|castle]], observation, leisure and telecommunications. The oldest known may be a circular stone tower in the walls of Neolithic Jericho (8000 BC). Ziggurats, such as the Sumerian [[Ziggurat of Ur]] built in the 3rd millennium BC, were among the earliest towers. The Chinese used towers as part of the [[Great Wall of China]] in 210 BC during the [[Qin dynasty]], and medieval fortified buildings made wide use of them. Modern examples include the [[Eiffel Tower]] in Paris, completed in 1889 as a temporary entrance arch for the World's Fair, and [[Cooling tower|cooling towers]] at industrial plants.

=== KEY: Town_c16dac
HEADWORD: Town
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: City | Human settlement | Village
ENTRY:
Type of [[Human settlement|human settlement]], generally larger than a [[Village|village]] but smaller than a [[City|city]]. Criteria for distinguishing a town vary globally and may depend on population size, economic character, administrative status, or historical significance. In some regions towns are defined by legal charters or government designations, while elsewhere the term is informal. Towns typically have centralized services, infrastructure, and governance, and serve as hubs for commerce, education, and culture.

Usage differs by country. In the U.K. a town may derive its status from a market-town designation or royal charter, and is typically served by a town council. In the U.S. the term is often applied to incorporated municipalities, which have legally defined borders and local government; an unincorporated town lacks its own governance. In Australia and Canada, distinctions between towns, cities, and rural areas rest on population thresholds. The UN-endorsed Degree of Urbanization framework treats a town as a contiguous area of at least 5,000 inhabitants with a density of 300 or more people per sq km. Population alone is an unreliable guide: in India a large village may hold several times the population of a small town, and some U.K. historical cities are far smaller than its larger towns. Today's towns usually differ from villages and hamlets in that most residents earn a living from manufacturing, commerce, and public services rather than agriculture.

The word shares an origin with German *Zaun* ("fence"), Dutch *tuin* ("garden, yard; fence, enclosure"), and Old Norse *tún* ("enclosure, as for a homestead"). The original sense in Germanic and Celtic was a fortress or enclosure. Old English *tūn* became a common place-name suffix in England and southeastern Scotland.

=== KEY: Toxicity_688250
HEADWORD: Toxicity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cell (biology) | Liver | Pathogen | Venom
ENTRY:
Degree to which a chemical substance or mixture can damage an organism, whether a whole animal, bacterium or plant, a [[Cell (biology)|cell]] or an organ such as the [[Liver|liver]]. Toxicity is dose-dependent: even water can cause intoxication in too high a dose, whereas a very toxic substance such as [[Venom|snake venom]] has a dose below which no toxic effect is detectable. Toxicity is species-specific, which complicates cross-species analysis. The word derives from the Greek *toxon*, "arc", in reference to poisoned arrows. Toxicity is generally classified as chemical, biological, physical, radioactive or behavioral. Biological toxicants include [[Pathogen|pathogens]] and the toxins of microorganisms, plants and fungi. Physical toxicants, such as asbestos fibers and coal dust, can be fatal when inhaled.

=== KEY: Toxicology_c89ec1
HEADWORD: Toxicology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biology | Cancer | Chemistry | Leukemia | Maimonides | Medicine | Paracelsus | Pharmacology
ENTRY:
Scientific discipline, overlapping with [[Biology|biology]], [[Chemistry|chemistry]], [[Pharmacology|pharmacology]] and [[Medicine|medicine]], that studies the adverse effects of chemical substances on living organisms. It also covers diagnosing and treating exposure to toxins and toxicants. The relationship between dose and effect is central, and factors include dosage, duration of exposure, route, species, age, sex and environment. Egyptian records of poisons date only to about 300 BC. [[Maimonides]] wrote a treatise on poisons and their treatment in the 12th century. The 16th-century Swiss physician [[Paracelsus]] is considered the father of modern toxicology. The field now contributes to [[Cancer|cancer]] research, since some toxins can be used as drugs to kill tumor cells, such as ribosome-inactivating proteins tested in the treatment of [[Leukemia|leukemia]].

ENTRY:
Parasitic disease caused by the [[Protozoa|protozoan]] *Toxoplasma gondii*. Infection is usually spread by eating poorly cooked food containing cysts, by exposure to infected [[Cat|cat]] [[Feces|feces]], or from mother to baby during [[Pregnancy|pregnancy]]. === KEY: Toy_0f8102
HEADWORD: Toy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Ancient Rome | Board game | Cognitive development | Doll | Kite | Middle class | Puzzle
ENTRY:
Object used primarily to provide entertainment, most often by children. Simple examples include toy blocks, [[Board game|board games]], and [[Doll|dolls]], though many toys are designed for adults and pets. Materials include wood, clay, paper, and plastic; newer forms include interactive digital entertainment and smart toys, and some are made as collector's items for display only.

Toys have prehistoric origins: dolls representing infants, animals, and soldiers are readily found at archaeological sites, and the oldest known doll is thought to be 4,000 years old. Toys from the Indus Valley Civilization (3010–1500 BC) include small carts, bird-shaped whistles, and toy monkeys that slid down a string. In [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]] and [[Ancient Rome|ancient Rome]], children played with dolls of wax or terracotta, sticks, bows and arrows, and yo-yos. The Ostomachion of Archimedes, from the 3rd century BC, is the oldest known put-together mechanical [[Puzzle|puzzle]].

Toys became more widespread during the Enlightenment, as Western attitudes shifted away from treating children as small adults expected to work. Popular toys included hoops, toy wagons, [[Kite|kites]], and puppets, and John Jefferys produced many board games in the 1750s. In the 19th century educational toys were favored, and growing prosperity among the [[Middle class|middle class]] led to the application of industrial methods to toy manufacture.

Research in developmental psychology indicates that play with toys supports early [[Cognitive development|cognitive development]], including problem-solving, memory, and symbolic thinking, and contributes to social learning and emotional regulation. Open-ended toys such as blocks are reported to promote sustained creativity more effectively than an excess of specialized items.

=== KEY: Trade_5f390d
HEADWORD: Trade
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Austronesian peoples | Currency | Great Depression | Lapis lazuli | Obsidian | Phoenicia | Retail | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Exchange of goods, services, or capital between parties, agreed in advance and made in return for something of value.

Trade involves the transfer of goods and services from a seller to a buyer. In one modern view, it exists because of specialization and the division of labor: individuals and groups concentrate on a small aspect of production and exchange their output for other needs. Trade between regions arises because regions may hold a comparative advantage in producing certain goods, and trading at market price can benefit both. The spice trade and the grain trade have both been important in the development of a global economy. [[Retail|Retail]] trade is the sale of goods from a fixed location, online, or by mail, in small lots for direct consumption.

Prehistoric peoples exchanged goods in a gift economy before the innovation of [[Currency|currency]]. Early humans built trade networks for [[Obsidian|obsidian]] 15,000 years ago and for ostrich egg shell beads 50,000 years ago. Because obsidian deposits are rare in the Mediterranean region, its use there required exchange, and early traders moved it over distances of 900 km. The Sari-i-Sang mine in Afghanistan was the largest source of [[Lapis lazuli|lapis lazuli]], traded most heavily in the Kassite period of Babylonia beginning in 1595 BC.

Ebla was a prominent trading center in the third millennium BC, when Sumerians in Mesopotamia also traded with the Harappan civilization of the Indus Valley. The [[Phoenicia|Phoenicians]] traveled across the Mediterranean Sea and as far north as Britain for tin to make bronze, founding trading colonies that the Greeks called emporia. Until the fall of the [[Roman Empire|Roman Empire]] in the 5th century, a lucrative trade brought spice to Europe from India and China, and Rome imported grain from Sicily and Egypt. Afterward trade in Western Europe nearly collapsed, but it continued to flourish in Africa, the Middle East, India, China, and Southeast Asia. The first true maritime trade network in the Indian Ocean was built by the [[Austronesian peoples|Austronesian peoples]] of Island Southeast Asia; its Maritime Jade Road carried jade mined in Taiwan and processed mostly in the Philippines.

Openness to free trade increased substantially from 1815 until 1914, collapsed in Europe and North America during the [[Great Depression|Great Depression]] of the 1930s, and rose again from the 1950s onward. Economists and economic historians contend that current levels of openness are the highest ever. Supporters of free trade argue that it raises growth and productivity and lowers consumer prices. Controversy persists over tariffs: critics say they distort resource allocation and raise costs for consumers, while proponents appeal to the protection of particular industries.

=== KEY: Trade_union_438242
HEADWORD: Trade union
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constitution | Industrial Revolution | Julius Caesar | Karl Marx | Marxism | Nordic countries | Trajan
ENTRY:
Organization of workers formed to maintain or improve the conditions of their employment. Also called a labor or labor union, it seeks better wages and benefits, improved working conditions and safety standards, complaint procedures, and greater bargaining power for workers.

Unions fund their head office and legal functions through regular fees called union dues. An elected leadership and bargaining committee negotiates labor contracts, called collective bargaining agreements, on behalf of the membership, known as the rank and file. Unions traditionally have a [[Constitution|constitution]]. A union may organize skilled or unskilled workers of one craft (craft unionism), workers from various trades (general unionism), or all workers in an industry (industrial unionism).

Trade unions originated in the United Kingdom and spread widely during the [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]], when employment rather than subsistence farming became the main way of earning a living. Union density is highest in the [[Nordic countries|Nordic countries]]. The Webbs' *History of Trade Unionism* (1894) defined a union as a continuous association of wage earners for maintaining or improving their conditions of employment. [[Karl Marx|Karl Marx]] saw unions as aiming to prevent wages from falling below their traditional level.

Ancient Rome had *collegia*, associations that acted as legal entities and needed official approval after the Lex Julia under [[Julius Caesar|Julius Caesar]]; a shipyard inscription from the reign of [[Trajan|Trajan]] indicates a shipbuilders' guild. Modern unionism is not a product of [[Marxism|Marxism]]: the earliest modern unions predate the *Communist Manifesto* (1848) by almost a century, emerging in 18th-century Britain as industrial work drew peasants and immigrants into cities.

Supporters credit unions with raising wages, shortening hours, and winning workplace protections. Critics argue that these gains come at the cost of employment, firm profitability, and productivity.

=== KEY: Trade_winds_88b104
HEADWORD: Trade winds
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hadley cell | Latin America | Sahara | Southeast Asia
ENTRY:
East-to-west prevailing winds in Earth's equatorial region, blowing mainly from the northeast in the Northern Hemisphere and from the southeast in the Southern Hemisphere. They strengthen in winter and form part of the [[Hadley cell]], in which surface air flows toward the equator. Captains of sailing ships have used them to cross the world's oceans for centuries, enabling European colonization of the Americas. In meteorology, they steer tropical storms over the Atlantic, Pacific, and southern Indian oceans, which cause rainfall in East Africa, Madagascar, North America, and [[Southeast Asia]]. Trade winds also carry nitrate- and phosphate-rich [[Sahara|Saharan]] dust to [[Latin America]] and the Caribbean Sea.

=== KEY: Tradition_6c8f9e
HEADWORD: Tradition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Folklore | Greeting | Holiday | Homer | Inheritance | Latin | Oral tradition
ENTRY:
System of beliefs or behaviors passed down within a group or society, with symbolic meaning or special significance and origins in the past. A component of cultural expressions and [[Folklore|folklore]], traditions include [[Holiday|holidays]], socially meaningful but impractical clothing such as lawyers' wigs or military officers' spurs, and social behaviors such as [[Greeting|greetings]].

The word derives from the [[Latin|Latin]] *tradere*, "to transmit, to hand over, to give for safekeeping"; the related *traditio* was used in Roman law for legal transfers and [[Inheritance|inheritance]]. According to Anthony Giddens and others, the modern meaning developed during the Enlightenment, in opposition to modernity and progress. The unifying idea is that customs or beliefs originating in the past are taught from one generation to the next and are still practiced in the present. Traditions are often presumed ancient and unalterable, yet many were invented deliberately, for political or cultural reasons, over short periods.

Before writing, traditions were passed on orally, aided by devices such as rhyme, epic stories, and alliteration; the stories so preserved are called [[Oral tradition|oral tradition]]. The phrase "according to tradition" usually signals information known only through such transmission and unsupported, or even refuted, by solid evidence: according to tradition, [[Homer|Homer]] was born on Chios, though many other places have claimed him.

Industrialization, globalization, and the assimilation or marginalization of cultural groups can erode them, and preservation initiatives, for example for traditional languages, now exist in many countries.

Views of tradition differ. Edmund Burke regarded it as an inheritance embodying the distilled wisdom of earlier generations, warning that abandoning it for abstract rationalism risks social disintegration. Modernist critics portray it as a barrier to innovation and rational progress.

=== KEY: Traditional_Chinese_medicine_c0b7d5
HEADWORD: Traditional Chinese medicine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cultural Revolution | Mao Zedong | Qi | Traditional medicine
ENTRY:
Alternative medical practice drawn from [[Traditional medicine|traditional medicine]] in China. Most of its claims are pseudoscientific, and most treatments lack robust evidence of effectiveness or a logical mechanism of action. Some ingredients are toxic and can cause disease, including cancer. Its modern form is largely a 20th-century invention: early 20th-century modernizers tried to eliminate traditional practices, and practitioners then organized selected elements of philosophy and practice as "Chinese medicine." During the [[Cultural Revolution]] of the 1960s, the government promoted it as inexpensive and popular, a policy largely spearheaded by [[Mao Zedong]], who according to one account did not believe in its effectiveness. Its core concept, that [[Qi|qi]] circulates through meridians linked to organs, has no supporting evidence: there is no evidence that meridians or vital energy exist. The demand for traditional medicines in China is a major driver of illegal wildlife smuggling.

=== KEY: Traditional_animation_e828c4

ENTRY:
Animation technique in which each frame is drawn by hand, also called classical, cel, or hand-drawn animation. It was the dominant form of animation in the United States through the 20th century, until the industry shifted to [[Computer animation|computer animation]]. Production usually begins with a script and a storyboard, and dialogue is recorded before animation so that character movement can be synchronized to the soundtrack. === KEY: Traffic_collision_4b4a5e

Incident in which a vehicle collides with another vehicle, a pedestrian, an animal, road debris, or a stationary obstruction. === KEY: Traffic_e7935a
HEADWORD: Traffic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Stochastic process | United Nations
ENTRY:
Movement of vehicles and pedestrians along land routes. Organized traffic has established priorities, lanes, right-of-way rules, and traffic control at intersections, which generally improves both safety and efficiency. Traffic is often classified as heavy motor vehicles, such as cars and trucks, other vehicles such as mopeds and bicycles, and pedestrians. The basic rules of the road are set by the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic, an international treaty under the authority of the [[United Nations]], although not all countries are signatories. === KEY: Tragedy_1df880
HEADWORD: Tragedy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aeschylus | Ancient Greece | Aristotle | Arthur Miller | Euripides | Henrik Ibsen | Samuel Beckett | Sophocles
ENTRY:
Genre of drama based on human suffering, in which terrible or sorrowful events befall a main character or cast. Traditionally, tragedy aims to produce catharsis, a "pain [that] awakens pleasure," in its audience. The form originated in [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]] about 2,500 years ago, where [[Aeschylus]], [[Sophocles]], and [[Euripides]] wrote the best-known surviving works. In his *Poetics* (335 BCE), [[Aristotle]] distinguished tragedy from comedy and epic poetry. Later playwrights extended the form: Shakespeare's *Hamlet* and *King Lear* blend fate with human choice, and [[Henrik Ibsen]]'s naturalistic tragedy shaped the modern stage. Modern dramatists such as [[Samuel Beckett]] and [[Arthur Miller]] applied the form to despair and to the common man.

=== KEY: Tragedy_of_the_commons_5050ef

ENTRY:
Dilemma in which individuals overuse a shared resource, depleting it for everyone. The term is the title of a 1968 essay by ecologist Garrett Hardin, published in [[Science (journal)|Science]], though the idea goes back to [[Aristotle]]. === KEY: Train_e41273
HEADWORD: Train
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: George Stephenson | Greenhouse gas | High-speed rail | Locomotive | Maglev | Railway track | Rapid transit | Steam locomotive
ENTRY:
Series of connected vehicles that run along a railway track and transport people or freight. The word comes from Old French *trahiner*, from Latin *trahere*, "to pull, to draw."

Trains are typically pulled or pushed by [[Locomotive|locomotives]], though some are self-propelled, such as multiple units or railcars. Most run on steel wheels on steel tracks, whose low friction makes them efficient. Conventional trains run on two rails of a [[Railway track|track]]; monorails use one.

Trains evolved from wagons running on stone wagonways, the earliest built by Babylon c. 2,200 BC. In 1804 British inventor Richard Trevithick built a [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]] that powered the first steam train. The opening of the Stockton and Darlington Railway in 1825 proved the value of steam outside coal mines: [[George Stephenson|George Stephenson]] ran *Locomotion No. 1* on the 40-km (25-mile) line, hauling over 400 passengers at up to 13 km/h (8 mph). Stephenson's *Rocket* followed in 1829, and a stock market bubble known as "Railway Mania" spread across the United Kingdom within a decade.

[[Rapid transit|Rapid transit]] and tram systems were first built in the late 1800s. From the 1920s, accelerating after World War II, diesel and electric locomotives replaced steam. Cars, trucks, highways, and faster airplanes then reduced the market share of trains, and many rail lines were abandoned.

Since the 1970s, governments and environmentalists have promoted rail for its fuel efficiency and lower [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]] emissions. [[High-speed rail|High-speed rail]], first built in the 1960s, competes with cars and planes over short to medium distances. [[Maglev|Maglevs]], which float above a guideway by magnetic levitation, have been under development since the 1970s.

=== KEY: Trajan_acdfdd

ENTRY:
Roman emperor (r. AD 98–117). Born in Italica in Hispania Baetica, present-day [[Spain]], Trajan was the first emperor born outside Italy and the second of the Five Good Emperors of the [[Nerva–Antonine dynasty]]. He rose under [[Domitian]], was adopted as heir by the elderly Nerva, and was given the title *optimus princeps* ("the best ruler") by the Senate. === KEY: Trans_Saharan_trade_987784

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Berbers | Ibn Battuta | Niger River | Sahara
ENTRY:
Trade between North Africa and the rest of Africa, conducted by camel caravans across the [[Sahara]]. Its peak ran from the 8th century until the early 17th century. The desert once separated the Mediterranean economy from the [[Niger River]] basin, and crossing it paid only when exceptional circumstances outweighed the cost and danger. === KEY: Transantarctic_Mountains_da75ec
HEADWORD: Transantarctic Mountains
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Gondwana
ENTRY:
Mountain range of uplifted, mostly sedimentary rock crossing [[Antarctica]]. About 3,500 km (2,000 mi) long, it divides East and West Antarctica and is one of the longest mountain ranges on Earth. Its highest peak, Mount Kirkpatrick, rises 4,528 m (14,856 ft). The range was uplifted beginning about 65 million years ago during the opening of the West Antarctic Rift System. Its sedimentary layers, including Beacon Supergroup sandstones and coal, rest on granites and gneisses. The McMurdo Dry Valleys, the largest stretch of Antarctic snow and ice-free terrain, lie within the range. Forests once covered the continent, and the last trees are believed to have grown here before cooling linked to the breakup of [[Gondwana]] eliminated them. The range was first crossed during the British National Antarctic Expedition of 1901–1904.

=== KEY: Transcription_biology_dabf90
HEADWORD: Transcription
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Gene expression | Protein | RNA | Virus
ENTRY:
Process of duplicating a segment of [[DNA]] into [[RNA]] for [[Gene expression|gene expression]]. RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand and produces a complementary RNA strand, called a primary transcript. Some transcripts are messenger RNA (mRNA), which encodes [[Protein|proteins]], while others are non-coding RNAs. The RNA uses uracil wherever DNA uses thymine. === KEY: Transformer_bbca27
HEADWORD: Transformer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Eddy current | Electromagnetic induction | Faraday's law of induction
ENTRY:
Passive electrical component that transfers energy between circuits through [[Electromagnetic induction|electromagnetic induction]]. A varying current in one coil produces a varying magnetic flux in the core, which induces a voltage across the other coils. [[Faraday's law of induction]], discovered in 1831, describes this effect. The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio between windings, so transformers step voltage up or down, and they can also provide galvanic isolation between circuits. Since the first constant-potential transformer in 1885, they have been essential to transmitting and distributing [[Alternating current|alternating current]] power. Their size ranges from RF units smaller than a cubic centimeter to units weighing hundreds of tons on the power grid. Real units lose energy through hysteresis and [[Eddy current|eddy current]] losses in the core and resistance in the windings.

=== KEY: Transgender_2d632e

Gender identity differing from the sex assigned at birth. === KEY: Transport_f5f0c4
HEADWORD: Transport
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air pollution | Airport | Canal | Economic growth | Fixed-wing aircraft | Globalization | Unmanned aerial vehicle
ENTRY:
Intentional movement of humans, animals, and goods from one location to another. The term is British English; American English uses transportation.

Modes of transport include air, land (rail and road), water, cable, pipelines, and space. The field divides into infrastructure, vehicles, and operations, and transport enables the trade that is essential to the development of civilizations. Infrastructure consists of fixed installations such as roads, railways, waterways, [[Canal|canals]], and pipelines, together with terminals including [[Airport|airports]], stations, warehouses, and seaports. Means of transport are the facilities used to carry people or cargo: vehicles such as wagons, automobiles, bicycles, buses, trains, trucks, helicopters, watercraft, and aircraft, as well as riding and pack animals.

A journey may use one mode or several, a case called inter-modal or multi-modal transport. Each mode is chosen on the basis of cost, capability, and route. Governments regulate how vehicles are operated, and ownership of infrastructure and operations may be public, private, or a partnership, depending on the country and mode. Freight transport has become focused on containerization, though bulk transport is used for large volumes of durable items.

Human-powered transport, a form of sustainable transport, uses muscle power in walking, running, and swimming, extended by vehicles such as bicycles and wheelchairs. Animal-powered transport uses working animals as mounts or pack animals or to pull sleds and wheeled vehicles, and remains useful in rough terrain.

A [[Fixed-wing aircraft|fixed-wing aircraft]], commonly called an airplane, uses the movement of air over its wings to generate lift. Most need an airport, though some take off and land on ice, snow, or calm water. Remotely piloted [[Unmanned aerial vehicle|unmanned aerial vehicles]] are used for package delivery. Aircraft are the second fastest method of transport after the rocket: commercial jets reach up to 955 km/h (593 mph), piston-powered general aviation aircraft up to 555 km/h (345 mph) or more. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change estimates that a commercial jet flight has 2–4 times the climate effect of the same CO₂ emissions at ground level.

Transport plays an important part in [[Economic growth|economic growth]] and [[Globalization|globalization]], by connecting producers to markets and reducing costs through scale. Railroads during the Industrial Revolution accelerated industrialization, and 20th-century aviation shortened distances for trade and travel. Machine-propelled transport, however, causes [[Air pollution|air pollution]], uses large amounts of land, and relies on fossil fuels; about 28% of U.S. greenhouse gas emissions come from vehicle exhaust and related activities. Governments heavily subsidize transport, and good planning is regarded as essential to keep traffic flowing and restrain urban sprawl. Debate continues over decarbonization through electrification and alternative fuels and over the effectiveness of infrastructure investment.

NP-hard problem in combinatorial optimization. Given cities and the distances between each pair, the traveling salesman problem asks for the shortest route that visits each city exactly once and returns to the origin city. === KEY: Tree_3b0c14
HEADWORD: Tree
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bark (botany) | Carbon dioxide | Conifer | Photosynthesis | Shrub | Tropical rainforest | Trunk (botany) | Vascular tissue
ENTRY:
Perennial plant with an elongated stem, or trunk, usually supporting branches and leaves. There is no universally recognized precise definition: broad definitions include taller palms, tree ferns, bananas, and bamboos, while narrower ones require a woody trunk thickened by secondary growth. Tree guide author Alan Mitchell defined a tree as a woody perennial plant that can attain a stature of 6 m or more on a single stem. There is no consistent distinction from [[Shrub|shrubs]], which are smaller.

Trees are not a single taxonomic group. The tree form evolved independently in unrelated plants as a way to tower above competitors for sunlight. The earliest trees evolved around 400 million years ago, and an estimated three trillion mature trees exist today. An estimated 60,000–100,000 species may make up twenty-five percent of all plant species, most in tropical regions. Most species are angiosperms, or hardwoods; many of the rest are gymnosperms, or softwoods, including most [[Conifer|conifers]].

The [[Trunk (botany)|trunk]] contains woody tissue for strength and [[Vascular tissue|vascular tissue]] that carries materials through the tree, and is generally surrounded by [[Bark (botany)|bark]] as a protective barrier. Leaves convert light energy into sugars by [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Most trees reproduce by seeds: flowering plants enclose them in fruit, conifers carry them in cones, and tree ferns produce spores.

Trees reduce erosion and moderate the climate, removing [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] from the atmosphere and storing carbon in their tissues. Trees provide habitat for many species, and [[Tropical rainforest|tropical rainforests]] are among the most biodiverse habitats in the world. In much of the world forests are shrinking as trees are cleared for agriculture. Trees have long been revered, with sacred groves in various cultures.

=== KEY: Triangle_5e5500
HEADWORD: Triangle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclid's Elements | Euclidean geometry | Geometry | Great Pyramid of Giza | Polygon | Polyhedron | Tetrahedron | Trigonometry
ENTRY:
[[Polygon|Polygon]] consisting of three sides connected at three corners, the simplest of polygons and one of the basic shapes of [[Geometry|geometry]]. The corners, or vertices, are points, and the sides are line segments. The three internal angles always sum to a straight angle (180 degrees, or π radians). The area equals one-half the product of height and base length.

In [[Euclidean geometry|Euclidean geometry]], any three points not on one straight line determine a unique triangle in a unique plane; four points in three-dimensional space determine a solid figure called a [[Tetrahedron|tetrahedron]].

The terminology for categorizing triangles is more than two thousand years old, defined in Book One of [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]]. By side lengths, a triangle with two equal sides is isosceles, one with three equal sides is equilateral, and one with all sides different is scalene. By angles, a right triangle has one 90° angle, an acute triangle has all angles less than 90°, and an obtuse triangle has one angle greater than 90°.

Isosceles triangles appear in gables and pediments, and equilateral triangles in the yield sign. The faces of the [[Great Pyramid of Giza|Great Pyramid of Giza]] are sometimes considered equilateral, but more accurate measurements show them to be isosceles. A [[Polyhedron|polyhedron]] whose faces are all equilateral triangles is a deltahedron.

Every triangle has special points associated with it, such as the circumcenter, where the three perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet; it is the center of the circumcircle through all three vertices. Relations between angles and side lengths are a major focus of [[Trigonometry|trigonometry]].

Figurate number counting the dots in an equilateral triangle. The nth triangular number is the sum of the integers 1 through n, equal to n(n+1)/2, giving the sequence 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, 21. The formula may originate with the Pythagoreans in the 5th century BC and was described by the Irish monk Dicuil in about 816. An apocryphal story credits [[Carl Friedrich Gauss|Gauss]] with finding the relationship as a youth. Proof is by [[Mathematical induction|induction]], and computing large triangular numbers can overflow an integer before the final [[Division (mathematics)|division]] by 2.

=== KEY: Triangulum_Galaxy_c0b531
HEADWORD: Triangulum Galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andromeda Galaxy | H II region | Large Magellanic Cloud | Light-year | Local Group | Milky Way | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Spiral galaxy 2.878 million [[Light-year|light-years]] from Earth, in the constellation Triangulum. Also designated Messier 33 (M33) or NGC 598, it is the third-largest member of the [[Local Group]], after the [[Andromeda Galaxy]] and the [[Milky Way]], with a D25 isophotal diameter of 18.74 kiloparsecs (61,120 light-years). It is the second-smallest spiral galaxy in the Local Group, after the [[Large Magellanic Cloud]], and is believed to be a satellite of the Andromeda Galaxy or to be rebounding into it. To some observers with fully dark-adapted eyes it is the farthest permanent entity visible without magnification. The Italian astronomer Giovanni Battista Hodierna probably discovered it before 1654. Charles Messier cataloged it independently on August 25–26, 1764, and [[William Herschel]] in 1784. Its brightest [[H II region]], NGC 604, was cataloged separately.

=== KEY: Triassic_151669
HEADWORD: Triassic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dinosaur | Jurassic | Mammal | Pangaea | Permian–Triassic extinction event | Pterosaur | Reptile | Triassic–Jurassic extinction
ENTRY:
First and shortest period of the Mesozoic Era, from 251.902 to 201.4 million years ago. It began after the [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]] and ended with the [[Triassic–Jurassic extinction]]. Its three epochs are Early, Middle and Late Triassic. [[Reptile|Reptiles]], especially archosaurs, were the chief land vertebrates; the first [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]] appeared in the Late Triassic but did not dominate until the [[Jurassic]]. Some archosaurs became the [[Pterosaur|pterosaurs]], the first vertebrates to fly, and the first stem-group [[Mammal|mammals]] appeared and survived the extinction event. The supercontinent [[Pangaea]] dominated the globe but began splitting into Laurasia and Gondwana in the latest Triassic. The climate was mostly hot and dry, with deserts across Pangaea's interior. The name, from Greek *triás* ('triad'), was coined in 1834 by Friedrich Alberti for three rock layers in southern Germany.

=== KEY: Triassic_Jurassic_extinction_d4857f
HEADWORD: Triassic–Jurassic extinction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid | Carbon dioxide | Coral | Dinosaur | Mercury (element) | Pterosaur
ENTRY:
Mass extinction at the boundary of the Triassic and Jurassic periods, 201.4 million years ago. It was one of five major extinction events of the Phanerozoic. About 23–34% of marine genera disappeared; [[Coral|corals]], bivalves, brachiopods, bryozoans and radiolarians suffered severe losses, and conodonts were wiped out. On land, all archosauromorph reptiles other than crocodylomorphs, [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]] and [[Pterosaur|pterosaurs]] became extinct, while crocodylomorphs, dinosaurs, pterosaurs and mammals were largely untouched and dominated land for the next 135 million years. The cause is generally considered to be volcanic eruptions in the Central Atlantic magmatic province, which released [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and toxic [[Mercury (element)|mercury]], causing global warming and ocean acidification. Older hypotheses proposed gradual climate and sea-level change or [[Asteroid|asteroid]] strikes.

=== KEY: Tribe_ffec4c
HEADWORD: Tribe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Civilization | Clan | Ethnicity | Hunter-gatherer | Kinship
ENTRY:
Human social group larger than a lineage or [[Clan|clan]] but smaller than a chiefdom, [[Ethnicity|ethnicity]], nation or state. The term is used most in [[Anthropology|anthropology]], where its definition is contested because of differing theories of [[Kinship|kinship]] and the difficulty of applying one concept to very diverse societies. Membership may rest on kinship, ethnicity, language, dwelling place, political group, religion or oral tradition. Anthropologist Elman Service classified societies as egalitarian [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherer]] bands, tribal societies with limited rank, stratified tribal societies led by chieftains, and [[Civilization|civilizations]] with complex hierarchies. Morton Fried argued in 1967 that bands organized into tribes to resist the violence and exploitation of early kingdoms and states. In the United States, Native American tribes have "domestic dependent nation" status and a government-to-government relationship with the federal government.

=== KEY: Triceratops_1ad864
HEADWORD: Triceratops
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cretaceous | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Dinosaur | Tyrannosaurus
ENTRY:
Genus of large chasmosaurine [[Dinosaur|dinosaur]] of the Late [[Cretaceous]] period, about 69 to 66 million years ago, in what is now western North America. Its name means "three-horned face" in Greek, for its three skull horns. With a bony frill and a four-legged body, it was one of the largest and best-known ceratopsians, measuring 8–9 m (26–30 ft) long and weighing up to 6–10 t. It was one of the last non-avian dinosaurs, living until the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]]. It shared its range with [[Tyrannosaurus]], which most likely preyed on it. The frill and horns were long viewed as defenses against predators, but more recent interpretations favor species identification, courtship and dominance display. Two species, *T. horridus* and *T. prorsus*, are considered valid, although 17 have been named.

=== KEY: Trick_or_treating_56f0bc
HEADWORD: Trick-or-treating
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Columbia | Canada | Ontario
ENTRY:
Halloween custom in which costumed children and adults go door to door asking for treats with the phrase "trick or treat." The "treat" is usually candy, or money in some cultures, and the "trick" is an idle threat of mischief if none is given. Its roots lie in guising, performing for food at Halloween, recorded in Scotland and Ireland from at least the 16th century. The earliest known North American guising was in [[British Columbia]], [[Canada]], in 1898, and the phrase was first recorded in [[Ontario]] in 1917. In Mexico, children ask for a calaverita, a small sugar or chocolate skull.

=== KEY: Trieste_1d8b2d
HEADWORD: Trieste
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adriatic Sea | Habsburg monarchy | Italy | Silk Road | Vienna | World War II
ENTRY:
Seaport in northeastern [[Italy|Italy]], capital of the Friuli-Venezia Giulia region, at the head of the Gulf of Trieste on the [[Adriatic Sea|Adriatic]]. A prosperous trading hub under the [[Habsburg monarchy]] from 1382 until 1918, it became the fourth largest city of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, after [[Vienna]], Budapest and Prague. After [[World War II|World War II]] it became the Free Territory of Trieste, a major site of struggle between the Eastern and Western blocs. Today it is a deep-water port considered the end point of the maritime [[Silk Road]], and it hosts many international organizations and research institutions.
