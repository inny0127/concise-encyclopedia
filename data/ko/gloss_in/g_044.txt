Longest river of [[West Asia|West Asia]]. Together with the [[Tigris]], it defined [[Mesopotamia]], "the land between the rivers." It rises in eastern Turkey, flows through Syria and Iraq, and joins the Tigris in the Shatt al-Arab, which empties into the [[Persian Gulf]]. Largest continental area on Earth, comprising all of [[Europe|Europe]] and [[Asia|Asia]]. Some models treat it as a single physiographic continent, although the division into Europe and Asia is largely cultural and its borders have shifted over time. === KEY: Europe_912d59
HEADWORD: Europe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Cold War | European Union | Gulf Stream | Industrial Revolution | Renaissance | Ural Mountains | Western Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Continent lying entirely in the Northern Hemisphere and mostly in the Eastern Hemisphere, forming the western part of Eurasia. It is bordered by the Arctic Ocean to the north, the Atlantic Ocean to the west, the Mediterranean Sea to the south, and Asia to the east. Europe is commonly considered to be separated from Asia by the watershed of the [[Ural Mountains]], the Ural River, the Caspian Sea, the Greater Caucasus, the Black Sea, and the Turkish straits. Islands are generally grouped with the nearest continental landmass, so Iceland is part of Europe while Greenland is usually assigned to North America.

Europe covers approximately 10,186,000 sq km, or 2% of Earth's surface (6.8% of its land area), making it the second-smallest continent in the seven-continent model. It is politically divided into about fifty sovereign states, of which Russia is the largest and most populous, spanning 39% of the continent and comprising 15% of its population. Europe had a total population of about 745 million, about 10% of the world population, in 2021, the third-largest after Asia and Africa. Warm Atlantic currents such as the [[Gulf Stream]] produce a temperate climate across much of the continent.

The name derives from Europa, a Phoenician princess of Greek mythology. One view derives it from Greek elements meaning "wide" and "eye" or "face"; Robert Beekes argued for a pre-Indo-European origin.

European culture consists of national and regional cultures that form central roots of Western civilization and commonly reference ancient Greece and ancient Rome. The fall of the [[Western Roman Empire]] in 476 CE and the Christian consolidation that followed the Migration Period marked the Middle Ages. The Italian [[Renaissance]] spread across many Western European countries. Since the [[Age of Discovery]], led by Spain and Portugal, Europe played a predominant role in global affairs, and between the 16th and 20th centuries European powers colonized the Americas, almost all of Africa and Oceania, and the majority of Asia. The [[Industrial Revolution]], which began in Great Britain at the end of the 18th century, brought radical economic, cultural, and social change.

Both world wars began and were fought largely in Europe, contributing to a decline in Western European dominance by the mid-20th century as the Soviet Union and the United States took prominence. The [[Cold War]] divided the continent along the Iron Curtain, with NATO in the West and the Warsaw Pact in the East. The divide ended with the Revolutions of 1989, the fall of the Berlin Wall, and the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991, which allowed European integration to advance. Integration has proceeded institutionally since the founding of the Council of Europe in 1949, and above all through the [[European Union]], which represents the majority of the continent. A majority of its members use the euro, and the Schengen Area has abolished internal border controls.

=== KEY: European_Union_9a1896
HEADWORD: European Union
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euro | European integration | Schengen Area | United Kingdom | United Nations | World Trade Organization
ENTRY:
Supranational political and economic union of 27 member states located primarily in Europe. It has a total area of 4,233,255 km² and an estimated population of just under 452 million as of 2026. Its member states generated a nominal gross domestic product of around €18.802 trillion in 2025, approximately one sixth of global economic output. The EU is often described as a *sui generis* entity combining characteristics of a federation and a confederation.

Its cornerstone, the Customs Union, paved the way for an internal single market. EU policies aim to ensure the free movement of people, goods, services, and capital. Passport controls have been abolished within the [[Schengen Area]]. The eurozone comprises the 21 member states that use the [[Euro|euro]]. The EU represents itself at the [[United Nations]] and the [[World Trade Organization]], and it was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 2012.

Its beginnings can be traced to the Inner Six states (Belgium, France, Italy, Luxembourg, the Netherlands, and West Germany) at the start of modern [[European integration]] in 1948, and to bodies such as the European Coal and Steel Community and the European Economic Community, established by treaties. The EU was established, along with its citizenship, when the Maastricht Treaty came into force in 1993, and it acquired a single international legal personality with the Treaty of Lisbon in 2009. A further 22 states joined from 1973 to 2013. In 2020 the [[United Kingdom]] became the only member state to leave, and ten countries are aspiring or negotiating to join. Critics cite a democratic deficit, including the appointed European Commission's monopoly on legislative initiative, while supporters credit the Union with decades of peace among former rivals.

Badger species of the family [[Mustelidae|Mustelidae]], native to Europe, West Asia and parts of Central Asia. Also called the Eurasian badger, it is classified as least concern on the IUCN Red List. Its weight ranges from 7–13 kg in spring to 15–17 kg in autumn. It is nocturnal and social, living in burrows called setts with multiple chambers and entrances. === KEY: European_bison_1b3e5c

ENTRY:
Bison species native to Europe, the heaviest wild land animal on the continent. Also called the wisent, it is one of two living bison species, alongside the [[American bison|American bison]]. [[Carl Linnaeus|Carl Linnaeus]] first described it scientifically in 1758. It was hunted to extinction in the wild in the early 20th century; the last wild animals of the Białowieża subspecies were shot in 1921, and the last Caucasian wisent in 1927. It is not to be confused with the [[Aurochs|aurochs]], the extinct ancestor of domestic cattle.

=== KEY: European_colonization_of_the_Americas_d473dc
HEADWORD: European colonization of the Americas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Christopher Columbus | Columbian exchange | Jamestown | Manifest destiny | Quebec | Santo Domingo | Treaty of Tordesillas
ENTRY:
Large-scale colonization of the Americas by European powers, primarily between the late 15th century and early 19th century, during the [[Age of Discovery]]. The Norse had earlier colonized Greenland and created a short-lived settlement near the northern tip of Newfoundland c. 1000 AD.

Systematic colonization began in 1492, when a Spanish expedition led by [[Christopher Columbus]], seeking a new trade route to the Orient, landed in The Bahamas. Under the [[Treaty of Tordesillas]] of 1494, Portugal and Spain agreed to divide the world between them; Spanish claims covered essentially all the Americas, while Portugal received the eastern tip of South America, where it established Brazil in the early 1500s. [[Santo Domingo]], founded in 1496, is credited as the oldest continuously inhabited European-established settlement in the Americas.

By the 1530s other Western European powers had begun to participate. The British and French colonized the northeast of the continent, with [[Jamestown]] founded in 1607 and [[Quebec]] in 1608. The Dutch founded New Amsterdam in 1624, and by the 1700s Russia had begun to claim the Pacific Coast from Alaska to California.

The [[Columbian exchange]] transferred crops, livestock, and technologies between hemispheres, and introduced pathogens such as smallpox, measles, and influenza to Indigenous populations without prior exposure. Indigenous peoples suffered epidemic disease, displacement, and enslavement, and some accounts characterize the violence as genocide; accounts differ over the relative weight of violence and disease in population decline. Conflict between European empires and Indigenous nations remained a leading dynamic into the 1800s, and the United States pursued a settler colonial policy of [[Manifest destiny]] and Indian removal. By the 19th century, independence movements had reshaped much of the Americas into nation-states.

=== KEY: European_integration_845c87
HEADWORD: European integration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Union | First French Empire | Hanseatic League | Holy Roman Empire | Roman Empire | Winston Churchill
ENTRY:
Process of political, legal, social and economic integration among states in or near Europe. It has been pursued largely through the [[European Union|European Union]], though it also includes cultural assimilation. The [[Roman Empire|Roman]] consolidation of the continent set a precedent for unity, echoed by the [[Holy Roman Empire|Holy Roman Empire]], the [[Hanseatic League|Hanseatic League]] and the [[First French Empire|First French Empire]]. After World War II, the 1951 Treaty of Paris established the European Coal and Steel Community among six nations, the first transfer of national powers to a common institution. The 1992 Maastricht Treaty transformed the European Economic Community into the European Union. [[Winston Churchill|Winston Churchill]] called for a "United States of Europe" in 1946. Critics argue that supranational rulemaking dilutes national sovereignty and that unelected EU bodies have limited accountability, while supporters credit integration with curbing war between European states.

Extinct order of marine [[Arthropod|arthropods]], informally called sea scorpions. The earliest known eurypterids date to the Tremadocian stage of the Ordovician period, about 480 million years ago. With approximately 250 species, the order is the most diverse Paleozoic chelicerate order. Eurypterids were major components of [[Silurian]] marine faunas, and the genus *Eurypterus* accounts for more than 90% of known specimens. The order includes the largest known arthropods, *Jaekelopterus*, at 2.5 m (8.2 ft) long. Their extinction is dated either to the early Middle [[Permian]] or to the [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]], 251.9 million years ago.

=== KEY: Evolution_42f0d7
HEADWORD: Evolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Charles Darwin | Genetic drift | Last universal common ancestor | Mendelian inheritance | Mutation | Natural selection | On the Origin of Species
ENTRY:
Change in the heritable characteristics of biological populations over successive generations. It occurs when individuals in a population vary in their genetic characteristics and in the numbers of their surviving offspring. [[Mutation]] introduces new genetic variation, and processes such as [[Genetic drift|genetic drift]], gene flow, and [[Natural selection|natural selection]] make some variants more or less common over time. Evolution is often defined as a change in the frequency of alleles, the variants of a gene found at a specific locus, in a population. In a strict biological sense it can occur even when the visible phenotype is unchanged. The process has given rise to biodiversity at every level of biological organization.

The scientific theory of evolution by natural selection was conceived independently by two British naturalists, [[Charles Darwin]] and [[Alfred Russel Wallace]], in the mid-19th century, as an explanation for why organisms are adapted to their environments. Their ideas were jointly presented in 1858, and the theory was first set out in detail in Darwin's 1859 book [[On the Origin of Species|*On the Origin of Species*]]. Natural selection rests on four observable facts: more offspring are often produced than can survive; traits vary among individuals; different traits confer different rates of survival and reproduction; and traits can be passed from generation to generation. Members of a population are therefore more likely to be replaced by the offspring of parents with favorable characteristics for that environment.

In the early 20th century, competing ideas of evolution were refuted and evolution was combined with [[Mendelian inheritance]] and population genetics to give rise to modern evolutionary theory. In this synthesis, the basis of heredity is in DNA molecules that pass information from generation to generation. Portions of DNA that specify a single functional unit are genes, and the different forms of a sequence at a locus are alleles. The complete set of genes in an organism is its genotype, and its set of observable traits is its phenotype. Some observable characteristics are not inherited; a suntan, for example, results from the interaction of genotype and sunlight and is not passed to children.

Variation comes from mutations in the genome, reshuffling of genes through sexual reproduction, and migration between populations. About half of the mutations in the coding regions of protein-coding genes are deleterious and the other half neutral, with a small percentage conferring a benefit. Duplication of large sections of a chromosome introduces extra copies of genes, a major source of raw material for new genes.

All life on Earth shares a [[Last universal common ancestor|last universal common ancestor]], which lived approximately 3.5–3.8 billion years ago. Existing biodiversity has been shaped by repeated formation of new species, changes within species, and extinction. Supporting evidence includes fossil records documenting transitional forms, comparative genomics revealing shared ancestry, and observable changes in real time, such as antibiotic resistance in bacteria. Evolutionary discoveries have influenced agriculture, medicine, and computer science.

=== KEY: Exercise_ef8fb9

=== KEY: Explosive_e7f88a

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ammonium nitrate | Dynamite | Greek fire | Gunpowder | Mining | Nitroglycerin | TNT | Uranium-235
ENTRY:
Reactive substance containing a great amount of potential energy that can produce an explosion if released suddenly, usually accompanied by light, heat, sound, and pressure. An explosive may be a single ingredient or a mixture, and can be combined with mechanical or electronic components to make a bomb. The energy is most commonly stored chemically, as in [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]], [[TNT]], or [[Nitroglycerin|nitroglycerin]], less commonly as pressurized gas, or as nuclear energy in fissile isotopes such as [[Uranium-235|uranium-235]].

Explosives are categorized by reaction speed. High explosives detonate, with the reaction front moving through the material faster than the speed of sound; low explosives deflagrate, with the front moving slower, producing a rapid burn rather than a proper explosion. By sensitivity, primary explosives can be initiated by a small amount of heat or pressure, while secondary and tertiary explosives are relatively insensitive and need a detonator.

Early thermal weapons such as [[Greek fire]] existed in ancient times. Taoist alchemists in China, searching for an elixir of eternal life, created the earliest gunpowder, which first saw use in warfare in 1161. Nitroglycerin, developed in 1847, was the first explosive stronger than black powder to see widespread use. Because it is liquid and unstable, it was replaced by nitrocellulose and trinitrotoluene in 1863, and by smokeless powder and [[Dynamite|dynamite]] in 1867. World War I saw the adoption of TNT in artillery shells.

The largest commercial application is [[Mining|mining]], which favors nitrate-based explosives such as mixtures of [[Ammonium nitrate|ammonium nitrate]] and fuel oil. Explosives are also used in explosion welding, which clads carbon steel or aluminum plate with a harder or more corrosion-resistant layer.

=== KEY: Exponential_distribution_357d8a
HEADWORD: Exponential distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Expected value | Gamma distribution | Geometric distribution | Median | Variance
ENTRY:
Probability distribution of the distance between events in a Poisson point process, in which events occur independently at a constant average rate. It is a particular case of the [[Gamma distribution|gamma distribution]] and the continuous analogue of the [[Geometric distribution|geometric distribution]]. Its probability density function is f(x) = λe^(−λx) for x ≥ 0, where the rate parameter λ is greater than 0. The mean is 1/λ, the [[Variance|variance]] is 1/λ², and the standard deviation equals the mean. The [[Median|median]] is ln(2)/λ, which is less than the [[Expected value|expected value]]. The distribution is memoryless: the remaining waiting time has the same distribution whatever time has already passed.

=== KEY: Exponential_function_df6294
HEADWORD: Exponential function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Complex number | Derivative | Euler's formula | Natural logarithm
ENTRY:
Mathematical function, usually written e^x or exp(x), that maps zero to one. It is the unique real function that equals its own [[Derivative|derivative]] and takes the value one at zero. It converts sums to products, exp(x+y) = exp(x)·exp(y), and its inverse is the [[Natural logarithm|natural logarithm]]. === KEY: Exponentiation_f66a8b
HEADWORD: Exponentiation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arithmetic | Complex number | Exponential function | Infinity | Integer | Multiplication | Real number
ENTRY:
[[Arithmetic]] operation on two numbers, the base *b* and the exponent *n*, written bⁿ. When *n* is a positive [[Integer|integer]], bⁿ is the product of [[Multiplication|multiplying]] *b* by itself *n* times, so that 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8. The exponent is also called the power or, in British English, the index; b² and b³ are read "b squared" and "b cubed" because they give the area of a square and the volume of a cube.

The operation can be defined by induction as b¹ = b and bⁿ⁺¹ = bⁿ · b, and it obeys bᵐ⁺ⁿ = bᵐ · bⁿ and (bᵐ)ⁿ = bᵐⁿ. A number raised to the 0 power is generally defined as 1, which keeps the first rule valid when m = 0, although zero to the power of zero may be an exception depending on context. For nonzero b, negative exponents are defined by b⁻ⁿ = 1/bⁿ. Raising 0 to a negative exponent is typically undefined, though in some circumstances it may be interpreted as [[Infinity|infinity]].

Unlike addition and multiplication, exponentiation is neither commutative nor associative: 2³ = 8 but 3² = 9, and (2³)² = 64 whereas 2^(3²) = 2⁹ = 512.

The definition extends to any positive [[Real number|real]] base and real exponent, and a more involved definition supports [[Complex number|complex numbers]] as base and exponent. René Descartes introduced modern superscript notation in 1637, initially for positive integers greater than two. Exponentiation models [[Exponential function|exponential]] growth and decay, and modular exponentiation is used in cryptography.

=== KEY: Extinction_4ce18a
HEADWORD: Extinction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Competition (biology) | Fossil | Habitat | Species | Zoo
ENTRY:
Termination of a [[Species|species]] through the death of its last member. A taxon may become functionally extinct earlier if it loses the capacity to reproduce and recover. Because a species' range may be very large, the moment is usually identified retrospectively; this leads to Lazarus taxa, species presumed extinct that abruptly "reappear," typically in the [[Fossil|fossil]] record. Local extinction, in which a species vanishes from a study area but survives elsewhere, may be reversed by reintroduction, as with wolves.

Species become extinct when they can no longer survive changing conditions or superior [[Competition (biology)|competition]]. A typical species becomes extinct within 10 million years of its first appearance, although so-called living fossils survive with little change for hundreds of millions of years, a claim that has been disputed. Over five billion species are estimated to have died out, against about 8.7 million eukaryote species alive today. Isolated extinctions are common and natural, while mass extinctions are rare. The loss of a keystone species can cause further extinctions, called "chains of extinction." Species surviving only in [[Zoo|zoos]] or other artificial environments are listed by the International Union for Conservation of Nature as "extinct in the wild."

Contemporary extinctions are attributed largely to human activity, chiefly destruction of natural [[Habitat|habitats]]. A 2019 assessment by IPBES reported that the biomass of wild mammals had fallen by 82%, natural ecosystems had lost about half their area, and a million species were at risk. At least 571 plant species have been lost since 1750. Whether this amounts to a sixth mass extinction is debated; critics cite undocumented extinctions and incomplete taxonomic inventories as sources of uncertainty.

=== KEY: Extinction_astronomy_22a6c9
HEADWORD: Extinction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetic radiation | Milky Way
ENTRY:
Absorption and scattering of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] by dust and gas between an astronomical object and the observer. Interstellar extinction was first documented as such in 1930 by Robert Julius Trumpler, though its effects on the colors of stars had been noted earlier. Because blue light is attenuated more strongly than red, extinction makes objects appear redder, a phenomenon called interstellar reddening. Interstellar reddening differs from redshift, which shifts spectra without distortion. Near the plane of the [[Milky Way|Milky Way]], visual extinction is roughly 1.8 magnitudes per kiloparsec. Strong atmospheric extinction in the X-ray, ultraviolet and infrared is overcome by space-based observatories.

=== KEY: Eye_ae160b
HEADWORD: Eye
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian explosion | Camera | Cephalopod | Compound eye | Mantis shrimp | Neuron | Trilobite
ENTRY:
Sensory organ that detects light and converts it into electrochemical impulses in [[Neuron|neurons]], forming part of an organism's visual system. In higher organisms it is a complex optical system that regulates the intensity of light, focuses it through adjustable lenses to form an image, and sends electrical signals along the optic nerve to the visual cortex and other brain areas.

Eyes with resolving power have come in ten fundamentally different forms, classified as compound or non-compound. Non-compound, or simple, eyes have a single lens that focuses light onto the retina and are common in mammals, including humans. In most vertebrates and some molluscs, cone cells (for color) and rod cells (for low-light contrasts) in the retina convert light into neural signals. The eyes of most [[Cephalopod|cephalopods]], fish, amphibians, and snakes focus by telescoping a fixed-shape lens, as in a [[Camera|camera]]. The simplest eyes, such as the ocelli of some snails, distinguish only light from dark.

[[Compound eye|Compound eyes]], common in insects and crustaceans, consist of many small visual units, each with its own lens and photosensitive cells. Some have up to 28,000 sensors arranged hexagonally, giving a full 360° field of vision; they are very sensitive to motion, but their physics limits resolution to no better than 1°. The extinct [[Trilobite|trilobites]] had clear calcite lenses, from one to thousands per eye. The [[Mantis shrimp|mantis shrimp]] has the most complex color vision system known.

The first proto-eyes evolved about 600 million years ago, about the time of the [[Cambrian explosion]].

ENTRY:
Short fictional story in prose or verse that illustrates a moral lesson through animals, plants or objects that speak and act like humans. Fables differ from parables in using such non-human actors. The best-known Western fables are attributed to the legendary [[Aesop's Fables|Aesop]], supposed to have been a slave in [[Ancient Greece]] around 550 BCE. In the first century AD, Phaedrus produced [[Latin]] verse versions that were transmitted through the [[Middle Ages]]. In [[India]], the [[Panchatantra]] sets beast fables inside a frame of instruction in practical conduct. Anansi stories originate with the tribes of [[Ghana]], and [[George Orwell]]'s *Animal Farm* uses the form for political satire.

=== KEY: Face_8af586
HEADWORD: Face
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beauty | Emotion | Eye color | Human | Mammal | Neanderthal | Primate
ENTRY:
Front part of the head of a [[Human|human]] or other [[Mammal|mammal]], featuring the eyes, nose and mouth. Mammals have evolved diverse facial forms, especially among [[Primate|primates]], whose forward-facing eyes allow binocular vision and depth perception. The human chin is unique to *Homo sapiens*, distinguishing modern humans from [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]]. Facial muscles allow humans to express [[Emotion|emotions]], and the face is the feature that best distinguishes an individual. Facial shape, influenced by the skull, is an important determinant of [[Beauty|beauty]], including facial symmetry. Surface differences such as [[Eye color|eye color]] may also contribute to racist ideologies and discrimination.

=== KEY: Facebook_d85544
HEADWORD: Facebook
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Harvard University | Mark Zuckerberg | Self-esteem | Smartphone | Social networking service
ENTRY:
American [[Social networking service|social networking service]] owned by Meta Platforms. Founded in 2004 by [[Mark Zuckerberg]] and fellow [[Harvard University|Harvard]] students, it was initially limited to Harvard before expanding to other universities. It was the most downloaded mobile application of the 2010s, and as of December 2023 it reported approximately 3.07 billion monthly active users. Users can create profiles and share text, photos and video, and access the service from [[Smartphone|smartphones]] and personal computers. The service has been criticized over user privacy, including the Cambridge Analytica data scandal, political manipulation, addiction and [[Self-esteem|low self-esteem]], and content such as fake news and hate speech.
