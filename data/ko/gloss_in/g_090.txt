=== KEY: Mississippian_period_archaeology_2d8827
HEADWORD: Mississippian period
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cahokia | Clan | Illinois | Maize | Mississippi
ENTRY:
Archaeological cultural era in the Southern United States and adjacent Eastern Woodlands, from the end of the 10th century to the 16th century. Mississippian peoples lived in large villages and relied on a diverse agricultural system centered on [[Maize|maize]]. Like their Woodland ancestors, most retained the Hopewell [[Clan|clan]] system, in which each clan functioned like a ritual society. The city of [[Cahokia]], founded in 1050 in [[Illinois]], likely had at least 22,400 people at its peak around 1124. A decades-long drought in Illinois (1150–1200) brought political instability, and Cahokia declined. Mound centers such as Moundville in Alabama and the Yazoo basin in [[Mississippi]] rose in prestige. In the Late Mississippian period (1400–1600), larger paramount chiefdoms emerged, with more defenses and archery.

=== KEY: Missouri_River_fd03bb
HEADWORD: Missouri River
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mississippi River
ENTRY:
Major river in the central United States, the nation's longest. It rises in the Rocky Mountains of southwestern Montana and flows 2,341 mi (3,767 km) before entering the [[Mississippi River]] north of St. Louis, Missouri. It drains more than 500,000 sq mi (1,300,000 km²). Although a tributary, it is slightly longer than the Mississippi and carries a comparable volume of water. For over 12,000 years people relied on it for sustenance and transport, and in the 19th century it was one of the main routes of westward expansion. Fifteen dams impound its main stem, and the river has been channelized for navigation, reducing its length by almost 200 mi (320 km). Development has taken a toll on wildlife, fish populations and water quality.

Ancient Hurrian-speaking kingdom in northern Syria and southeast [[Anatolia]], with Indo-Aryan linguistic and political influences. === KEY: Mite_8e0631
HEADWORD: Mite
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Honey bee | Lyme disease
ENTRY:
Small eight-legged [[Arthropod|arthropod]] of the class Arachnida, found in virtually every terrestrial and aquatic ecosystem. Mites belong to two large orders, the Acariformes and Parasitiformes, and about 48,000 species have been described. Most are less than 1 mm (0.04 in) long and have a simple, unsegmented body plan. Mites act as decomposers, predators and plant feeders, and some are parasites, including the Varroa parasite of [[Honey bee|honey bees]] and the scabies mites of humans. Most species are harmless to people, but a few cause allergies or transmit diseases, and many mites and ticks transmit pathogens that cause diseases such as [[Lyme disease]]. Genetic studies have questioned whether mites form a natural group, since Parasitiformes may be more closely related to other chelicerates than to Acariformes. The study of mites is called acarology.

=== KEY: Mitochondria_3a4976
HEADWORD: Mitochondria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Heart failure | Symbiogenesis
ENTRY:
Organelle found in most eukaryotic cells, where it carries out aerobic respiration. Mitochondria have a double membrane and generate [[Adenosine triphosphate|adenosine triphosphate]] (ATP), the chemical energy used throughout the cell. Their compartments include the outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner membrane, cristae (infoldings that increase surface area) and matrix. Mitochondria also take part in signaling, cell differentiation, cell death and control of the cell cycle. The number per cell varies widely: a mature red blood cell has none, while a liver cell can have more than 2,000. Mitochondria have their own genome, similar to bacterial genomes, which supports the [[Symbiogenesis|endosymbiotic]] origin of the organelle from free-living prokaryotes. Mitochondrial diseases, [[Heart failure|heart failure]] and autism are among the conditions in which mitochondria are implicated.

=== KEY: Mitosis_8ee3eb
HEADWORD: Mitosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Cell cycle | Chromosome
ENTRY:
Process of cell division in eukaryotes in which replicated [[Chromosome|chromosomes]] separate into two new nuclei, producing two genetically identical daughter cells. Mitosis is part of the [[Cell cycle|cell cycle]] and follows the S phase of interphase, when DNA is replicated. Through the stages of prophase, prometaphase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase, the duplicated chromosomes condense and attach to spindle fibers that pull one copy of each to opposite sides of the cell, and cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm. Animal cells usually undergo open mitosis, in which the nuclear envelope breaks down, whereas fungal cells generally undergo closed mitosis. Errors can produce three or more daughter cells, cause mutations or trigger apoptosis, and some [[Cancer|cancers]] arise from such mutations. Prokaryotes, which lack a true nucleus, divide by binary fission instead.

=== KEY: Mixture_cdf63a

ENTRY:
Material made of two or more substances combined physically, without chemical bonding. Each constituent keeps its own chemical identity and properties, so a mixture can usually be separated by physical methods such as [[Filtration|filtration]], [[Distillation|distillation]] or evaporation. Its physical properties, such as melting point, may differ from those of its components. === KEY: Moa_490f09
HEADWORD: Moa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Extinction | Haast's eagle | Herbivore | New Zealand | Tinamou
ENTRY:
Extinct order of flightless birds (Dinornithiformes) endemic to [[New Zealand]]. The nine species, in six genera, were the largest terrestrial animals and dominant [[Herbivore|herbivores]] in New Zealand's forests until the arrival of the Māori, and the two largest stood about 3.6 meters (12 ft) tall. Unlike other ratites, moa lacked even vestigial wings. Genetic studies place their closest living relatives among the flighted [[Tinamou|tinamous]] of South America. Fossil evidence shows they diversified during the Pleistocene and thrived in isolation until human arrival. Moa were hunted only by [[Haast's eagle]], but Polynesian settlers hunted them intensively for food, and they were driven to [[Extinction|extinction]] primarily through overhunting.

=== KEY: Moai_5ff0b9
HEADWORD: Moai
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Easter Island | Polynesia
ENTRY:
Monolithic human figures carved by the Rapa Nui people on [[Easter Island]] in [[Polynesia|eastern Polynesia]] between about 1250 and 1500. Hundreds were carved from volcanic stone at the Rano Raraku quarry, and many were moved to coastal stone platforms called ahu, where they faced inland toward clan lands. The moai are considered the living faces of deified ancestors. The tallest erected, Paro, was almost 10 meters (33 ft) high and weighed 82 metric tons. The statues were moved without wheels or draft animals. Europeans first visited in 1722 and found the statues still gazing inland, but many were toppled in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, possibly because of European contact or tribal warfare.

=== KEY: Moat_a6e10c
HEADWORD: Moat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angkor Wat | Artillery | Bridge | Castle | Dam | Siege
ENTRY:
Deep, broad ditch dug around a [[Castle|castle]], fortification or town, either dry or filled with water. Moats gave a site a preliminary line of defense, especially against siege engines. They usually denied direct access except at a few narrow points crossed by drawbridges, [[Bridge|bridges]] or causeways, where a [[Siege|siege]] could be funneled and more easily defended. Moats could be expanded into more extensive water defenses, including lakes, [[Dam|dams]] and sluices. They appeared in fortifications from ancient Egypt to medieval Europe, [[Angkor Wat]] and colonial forts in the Americas. The development of gunpowder [[Artillery|artillery]] made static water barriers less effective, and later moats were often ornamental.

=== KEY: Mobile_phone_41c2ff
HEADWORD: Mobile phone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: AT&T | Bluetooth | Finland | Japan | Samsung | Smartphone | Text messaging | Wi-Fi
ENTRY:
Portable wireless telephone that makes and receives calls over a radio frequency link while moving within a designated service area. The link connects to a mobile phone operator's switching systems, giving access to the public switched telephone network. Modern mobile telephony relies on a cellular network architecture, hence the term "cell phone" in North America. Digital phones support [[Text messaging|text]] and multimedia messaging, email, internet access (via LTE, 5G NR, or [[Wi-Fi]]), [[Bluetooth]], photography, video recording, gaming, streaming, satellite navigation, and payments. Phones offering only basic features are called feature phones; those with advanced computing power are [[Smartphone|smartphones]].

In 1917 the Finnish inventor Eric Tigerstedt filed a patent for a "pocket-size folding telephone with a very thin carbon microphone". The earliest zeroth-generation (0G) services were not cellular, supported few simultaneous calls, and were very expensive. In 1973 John F. Mitchell and Martin Cooper of Motorola demonstrated the first handheld cellular phone, weighing about 2 kg (4.4 lb); Cooper made the first call, to Joel S. Engel, a rival at [[AT&T]]. Nippon Telegraph and Telephone launched the first commercial automated cellular network, analog (1G), in [[Japan]] in 1979. The DynaTAC 8000x, the first commercially available handheld phone, appeared in 1983. In 1991 Radiolinja launched second-generation (2G) digital cellular service on the GSM standard in [[Finland]].

From 1993 to 2024, worldwide subscriptions grew to over 9.1 billion, enough for one for every person on Earth. In 2024 the top smartphone manufacturers were [[Samsung]], Apple, and Xiaomi, and smartphones were about 50 percent of mobile phone sales. Agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and National Cancer Institute have found no causal evidence that radiofrequency radiation causes cancer beyond potential thermal effects at high exposure, but distracted use causes accidents.

=== KEY: Moche_culture_132610
HEADWORD: Moche culture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Human sacrifice | Peru
ENTRY:
Pre-Columbian civilization that flourished on the northern coast of [[Peru|Peru]] from about AD 100 to 800. Many scholars contend that the Moche were not a monolithic empire but a group of autonomous polities sharing a common culture, evident in their iconography and monumental architecture. They built irrigation systems from rivers to support intensive agriculture, and constructed platform mounds called huacas, such as the Huaca del Sol and Huaca de la Luna near present-day Trujillo. Elite tombs, such as those of the Lords of Sipán, contained gold, silver and turquoise artifacts. Moche ceramics depicted portraits, daily life and warfare, and religious practice included [[Human sacrifice|human sacrifice]] of combatants. The culture declined around AD 800, possibly as a result of climatic disruption such as prolonged El Niño events.

=== KEY: Mode_statistics_d74f34
HEADWORD: Mode
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Median | Normal distribution | Random variable
ENTRY:
Value that occurs most often in a data set or probability distribution. The mode is one of three main measures of central tendency, along with the [[Median|median]] and mean. For a discrete [[Random variable|random variable]] it is the value with the highest probability. A data set may be unimodal (one mode), bimodal or multimodal, or amodal if all values occur equally often. In the set {1, 2, 2, 3, 4}, the mode is 2. Because it can be applied to nominal and categorical data, where arithmetic operations do not apply, the mode is widely used in fields such as epidemiology and market research. In a symmetric unimodal distribution such as the [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]], the mean, median and mode coincide.

=== KEY: Model_organism_be34b1
HEADWORD: Model organism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Insulin | Polio | Yeast
ENTRY:
Non-human species or strain studied intensively to reveal biological principles that apply to other organisms. Model organisms are chosen for traits such as short generation times, small size, ease of cultivation and well-characterized genomes. They allow research into human disease when experiments on people would be unfeasible or unethical, because living things share common descent and conserved pathways. Common examples include *Escherichia coli*, [[Yeast|baker's yeast]], *Drosophila melanogaster*, *Caenorhabditis elegans* and the mouse. Thomas Hunt Morgan's work with *Drosophila* from 1910 to 1927 showed that chromosomes carry genes. Animal research contributed to the 1922 discovery of [[Insulin|insulin]] and to the near-eradication of [[Polio|polio]]. Animal models have poor predictive accuracy for clinical toxicity and efficacy, and their use remains ethically contested.

=== KEY: Modern_architecture_89eb20
HEADWORD: Modern architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Art Deco | Concrete | Glass | Le Corbusier | Postmodern architecture | Skyscraper | Steel | Walter Gropius
ENTRY:
Architectural movement and style of the 20th century, between [[Art Deco]] and [[Postmodern architecture|postmodern architecture]]. It was based on new construction technologies, particularly [[Glass]], [[Steel]] and [[Concrete]]; functionalism, the principle that form should follow function; minimalism; and a rejection of ornament. Its roots lie in late 19th-century use of cast iron, plate glass and reinforced concrete, which led to the first [[Skyscraper|steel-framed skyscraper]] in Chicago. The movement became dominant after World War II until the 1980s, when postmodern architecture gradually replaced it as the principal style for institutional and corporate buildings. Leading figures included [[Le Corbusier]], [[Walter Gropius]] and Ludwig Mies van der Rohe. Critics argue that its universalism and minimalism overlooked evidence of user dissatisfaction and structural problems, contributing to a backlash by the late 20th century.

LINK TARGETS: Ballet | Isadora Duncan | Martha Graham | Performance art
ENTRY:
Genre of Western concert or theatrical dance that arose in Europe and the United States in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It was considered a rejection of, or rebellion against, classical [[Ballet]], and a way to express social concerns. Pioneers such as [[Isadora Duncan]], Maud Allan and Loie Fuller developed improvisational or free dance, discarding ballet's strict movement vocabulary, corsets and pointe shoes. American modern dance is often divided into three periods. In the Central Modern period (c. 1923–1946), [[Martha Graham]], Doris Humphrey and Lester Horton developed distinctively American movement styles and clearly defined training systems, and techniques by Graham and Horton are still taught worldwide. In the Late Modern period (c. 1946–1957), choreographers introduced abstraction and avant-garde movements and paved the way for postmodern dance, which rejected modern dance's formalism and drew on [[Performance art]] and contact improvisation.

=== KEY: Modern_era_a4b512
HEADWORD: Modern era
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Discovery | Contemporary history | Early modern period | French Revolution | Industrial Revolution | Postmodernism | Renaissance
ENTRY:
Current period of human history, originally applied to Europe and the West for events after classical antiquity and the Middle Ages, often from around the year 1500. Since the 1990s historians have more commonly called the period from the end of the Middle Ages to the 19th century the [[Early modern period|early modern period]], and the modern period is now more often used for events from the 19th century to the present. The time since the end of World War II (1945) can also be described as [[Contemporary history|contemporary history]]. The common definition is associated with the [[French Revolution]], the [[Industrial Revolution]], and the transition from nationalism toward the liberal international order.

Periodization is imprecise. The year 1500 is an approximate start because many major events changed the Western world around then: the fall of Constantinople (1453), Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (1450s), Christopher Columbus's voyage to the Americas (1492), and the Reformation, begun with Martin Luther's Ninety-five Theses (1517). The term "modern" was coined shortly before 1585 to describe the beginning of a new era, and the difference between "early modern" and "modern" was defined by the French and Industrial Revolutions. The European [[Renaissance]] (14th–16th centuries), which started in Italy, is an important transition period. "Modernity" and "modernism" refer to a new way of thinking distinct from medieval thinking, while "postmodernism", coined in 1949, describes a movement in art. These terms stem from European history; in China, India, and the Islamic world they are applied differently, often in the context of contact with European culture during the [[Age of Discovery]].

Early modern milestones include Niccolò Machiavelli's *The Prince* (c. 1513); Nicolaus Copernicus's *De revolutionibus orbium coelestium* (1543), proposing that the Earth revolves around the Sun; Johannes Kepler's *Astronomia nova* (1609), with the first mention of elliptical planetary orbits; Galileo Galilei's *Starry Messenger* (1610), the first published work of telescopic observations; the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648); the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which established the principle of state sovereignty; Isaac Newton's *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* (1687), formulating the laws of motion and universal gravitation; and the Glorious Revolution (1688), which affirmed parliamentary supremacy in England.

The period has seen significant development in science, politics, warfare, and technology, and the strengthening of European, and later colonial, political, economic, and cultural dominance over the rest of the world. It brought a systematic re-evaluation of value systems, monarchical regimes, and feudal economic systems, often replaced by democratic and liberal ideas, and is closely associated with individualism, capitalism, socialism, urbanization, and belief in technological and political progress. The world wars and the Cold War prompted reactions against modern development: [[Postmodernism|postmodernism]] has criticized the belief in constant progress, and postcolonial theory has criticized the dominance of Western Europe and North America.

=== KEY: Modern_paganism_13c63d

Range of [[New religious movement|new religious movements]] influenced by the indigenous beliefs of pre-modern peoples across Europe, North Africa and the Near East. Contemporary pagan movements are diverse and share no single set of beliefs, practices or sacred texts. Scholars differ on whether they form many religions or one decentralized religion with many denominations. Movements range from reconstructive groups, which seek to revive historical pagan religions, to eclectic ones that blend elements of various religions and philosophies. === KEY: Modern_philosophy_ee8166
HEADWORD: Modern philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baruch Spinoza | David Hume | Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Immanuel Kant | John Locke | Karl Marx | Søren Kierkegaard
ENTRY:
Philosophy developed in the modern era, roughly from the 17th to the early 20th century. It is not a single doctrine or school, but shares assumptions that distinguish it from earlier philosophy. Rationalists, mostly in France and Germany, argued that knowledge must begin from innate ideas; they included Descartes, [[Baruch Spinoza]] and [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Gottfried Leibniz]]. Empiricists, including [[John Locke]], George Berkeley and [[David Hume]], held that knowledge begins with sensory experience. In the late 18th century [[Immanuel Kant]] claimed to unite the two traditions, and his work sparked German idealism, culminating in [[Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel]]. [[Karl Marx]] transformed Hegel's ideas into a materialist form, while [[Søren Kierkegaard]] dismissed systematic philosophy as an inadequate guide to life. Bertrand Russell and George Edward Moore moved toward analytic philosophy, an updating of empiricism that drew on Gottlob Frege's logic.

=== KEY: Modernism_524ac6
HEADWORD: Modernism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cubism | David Hume | Ezra Pound | Postmodernism | Romanticism | W. B. Yeats | World War I
ENTRY:
Early 20th-century movement in literature, visual arts, performing arts, and music that emphasized experimentation, abstraction, and subjective experience. Philosophy, politics, architecture, and social issues were also aspects of it. It emerged during the late 19th century in response to secularization, the growing influence of science, technological innovation, industrialization, and urbanization, and was further shaped by the cultural and geopolitical shifts after [[World War I]].

Modernism was characterized by a self-conscious rejection of tradition and a search for new means of cultural expression, and it took a critical stance toward Enlightenment rationalism. It rejected the idea of absolute originality, *creatio ex nihilo*, upheld in the 19th century by realism and [[Romanticism]], replacing it with collage, reprise, rewriting, and parody. Associated techniques include abstract art, stream-of-consciousness, cinematic montage, atonality and twelve-tone music, modern dance, modernist architecture; [[Cubism]], *Ulysses*, and the International Style are often cited as breakthroughs. [[Ezra Pound]]'s imperative to "make it new" is often taken as its motto, and [[W. B. Yeats]]'s line "Things fall apart; the center cannot hold" sums up literary modernism, which often searches for a metaphysical center and experiences its collapse. That collapse of metaphysics can be traced philosophically to [[David Hume]].

Historian Roger Griffin defined modernism as a broad cultural, social, or political initiative sustained by the ethos of "the temporality of the new", seeking to restore a "sense of sublime order and purpose" to a fragmenting, secularizing world.

Critics have charged modernism with elitism, incomprehensibility to broad audiences, and, in built environments, a sterile inhumanity. [[Postmodernism]] rejects many of its principles and celebrates the collapse of metaphysics. Debate about the movement's timeline continues, with some scholars arguing that it evolved into late or high modernism.

Seismic intensity scale for the effects of an earthquake at a given location. Unlike magnitude, which measures the energy released at the source, it rates the intensity of shaking at the surface. Harry O. Wood and Frank Neumann developed it in 1931 from Giuseppe Mercalli's 1902 scale. Ratings run from I (imperceptible shaking) to XII (total destruction) and rely on observed effects rather than instruments. === KEY: Modular_arithmetic_90c68f
HEADWORD: Modular arithmetic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 12-hour clock | Cryptography | Equivalence relation | Number theory
ENTRY:
System of integer arithmetic in which results wrap around at a fixed value, the modulus. It replaces the results of addition, multiplication and subtraction with their remainders after division by the modulus. A notable property is that the final result is the same whether the reduction is applied after every operation, only at the end, or at intermediate steps. Carl Friedrich Gauss developed the modern approach to [[Number theory|number theory]] using it, in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). Congruence is an [[Equivalence relation|equivalence relation]] that partitions the integers into residue classes, as a [[12-hour clock]] does with hours. Applications include [[Cryptography|cryptography]], such as the RSA algorithm, which relies on the difficulty of factoring the product of two large primes, as well as hash functions. Andrew Wiles's 1994 proof of Fermat's Last Theorem used connections to elliptic curves and modular forms.

=== KEY: Module_mathematics_de6562
HEADWORD: Module
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abelian group | Algebraic geometry | Algebraic topology | Commutative algebra | Homological algebra | Representation theory
ENTRY:
Generalization of the vector space in which scalars come from a ring, not necessarily commutative, rather than a field. Like a vector space, a module is an additive [[Abelian group|abelian group]], and scalar multiplication distributes over addition and is compatible with ring multiplication. The concept generalizes abelian groups, which are exactly the modules over the ring of integers. Modules are closely related to the [[Representation theory|representation theory]] of groups and are central to [[Commutative algebra|commutative algebra]] and [[Homological algebra|homological algebra]]. They are used widely in [[Algebraic geometry|algebraic geometry]] and [[Algebraic topology|algebraic topology]]. Free modules, submodules and quotient modules are key tools for studying their structure.

=== KEY: Modulo_4cde24
HEADWORD: Modulo
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Binary operation giving the remainder of the Euclidean division of one number by another, the divisor being called the modulus. Programming languages differ in how they define the result when exactly one operand is negative. Many languages write the operation as mod or use a percent sign. The operation underlies [[Modular arithmetic|modular arithmetic]].

Qualitative ordinal scale from 1 to 10 for the scratch resistance of [[Mineral|minerals]]. Introduced in 1812 by German geologist and mineralogist Friedrich Mohs, it ranks minerals by whether a harder one can scratch a softer one. [[Talc]] is 1 and [[Diamond]] is 10. LINK TARGETS: Atom | Avogadro constant | Chemistry | International System of Units | Ion | Litre | Molecule
ENTRY:
Base unit of amount of substance in the [[International System of Units]] (SI), symbol mol. One mole is an aggregate of exactly 6.02214076×10²³ elementary entities, which can be [[Atom|atoms]], [[Molecule|molecules]], [[Ion|ions]] or other particles. The number of particles in a mole is the Avogadro number, and the [[Avogadro constant]] has units of mol⁻¹. The mole was historically defined by the number of atoms in 12 grams of carbon-12, making molar mass numerically equal to average molecular mass in daltons; the 2019 SI revision made that equivalence approximate. The concept resembles a dozen, but the number is huge because atoms are tiny. In [[Chemistry|chemistry]], it expresses reactant and product amounts: in 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O, 2 mol of hydrogen and 1 mol of oxygen react to form 2 mol of water. Concentrations are given in moles per [[Litre|liter]].

Branch of [[Biology|biology]] that seeks to understand the molecular structures and chemical processes underlying biological activity within and between cells. === KEY: Molecular_cloud_f21e96
HEADWORD: Molecular cloud
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon monoxide | Interstellar medium | Parsec | Solar mass | Star formation | Supernova
ENTRY:
Type of [[Interstellar medium|interstellar]] cloud dense enough for molecules, most commonly molecular hydrogen (H₂), to form. Typically 10 to 100 [[Parsec|parsecs]] across, with masses of 10⁴ to 10⁶ [[Solar mass|solar masses]], molecular clouds are sometimes called stellar nurseries when [[Star formation|star formation]] occurs within them. === KEY: Molecular_orbital_24d8a9
HEADWORD: Molecular orbital
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Atomic orbital | Electron | Pauli exclusion principle | Schrödinger equation
ENTRY:
Mathematical function describing the location and wave-like behavior of an [[Electron|electron]] in a molecule. Molecular orbitals are approximate solutions to the [[Schrödinger equation]] for electrons in the field of a molecule's nuclei. They are usually built from [[Atomic orbital|atomic orbitals]] of the constituent [[Atom|atoms]], forming bonding orbitals, which have lower energy and hold the molecule together; antibonding orbitals, which have higher energy and oppose bonding; and nonbonding orbitals, which have the same energy as their atomic orbitals. In general, molecular orbitals are delocalized throughout the entire molecule. Under the [[Pauli exclusion principle|Pauli principle]], two electrons in the same orbital have opposite spin. Robert S. Mulliken introduced the terms atomic orbital and molecular orbital in 1932.

=== KEY: Molecular_orbital_theory_44e734
HEADWORD: Molecular orbital theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic orbital | Quantum mechanics | Schrödinger equation | Valence bond theory
ENTRY:
Method for describing the electronic structure of molecules using [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. Proposed early in the 20th century, it treats electrons as moving under the influence of the nuclei of the whole molecule rather than being assigned to individual bonds. Its molecular orbitals are approximated as linear combinations of [[Atomic orbital|atomic orbitals]] (LCAO), derived from the [[Schrödinger equation]] using density functional theory or Hartree–Fock methods. The theory explains the paramagnetism of O₂, which [[Valence bond theory|valence bond theory]] cannot. Developed after valence bond theory was established in 1927, primarily by Friedrich Hund, Robert Mulliken, John C. Slater and John Lennard-Jones, it is used to interpret ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy.

=== KEY: Molecule_1003cf
HEADWORD: Molecule
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amedeo Avogadro | Chemical bond | Covalent bond | Ion | Linus Pauling | Noble gas | Polymer | Robert Boyle
ENTRY:
Electrically neutral group of two or more atoms held together by attractive forces known as [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]]. Depending on context, the term may or may not include [[Ion|ions]]; in quantum physics, organic chemistry, and biochemistry the distinction is dropped. A molecule may be homonuclear, consisting of atoms of one element, as in oxygen (O₂), or heteronuclear, a chemical compound, as in water (H₂O). In the kinetic theory of gases, the term is used for any gaseous particle, which relaxes the two-atom requirement because the [[Noble gas|noble gases]] are single atoms. Atoms and complexes joined by non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds or ionic bonds are typically not considered single molecules, as are large crystalline networks such as rocks, salts, and metals.

In [[Covalent bond|covalent bonds]], atoms share electron pairs. The three-dimensional structure that results, predicted by valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory, gives methane a tetrahedral shape and water a bent one, and it influences polarity and reactivity. Macromolecules such as proteins and DNA are large [[Polymer|polymers]].

Leucippus and Democritus argued in antiquity that the universe is composed of atoms and voids. The modern concept traces to [[Robert Boyle]]'s 1661 hypothesis, in *The Sceptical Chymist*, that matter is composed of clusters of particles and that chemical change results from their rearrangement. [[Amedeo Avogadro]]'s 1811 paper held that the smallest particles of gases are not necessarily simple atoms but groups of atoms united by attraction. In 1926 Jean Perrin received the Nobel Prize in physics for proving conclusively the existence of molecules, calculating the Avogadro constant by three methods. In 1927 Fritz London and Walter Heitler applied quantum mechanics to the hydrogen molecule, bringing chemistry under quantum mechanics; their work influenced [[Linus Pauling]], who published on the chemical bond in 1931.

=== KEY: Mollusca_3928a0
HEADWORD: Mollusca
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Blue-ringed octopus | Cambrian | Cephalopod | Colossal squid | Gastropod | Giant squid | Pearl
ENTRY:
Phylum of invertebrate animals including snails, slugs, clams, oysters, squid, cuttlefish, and octopuses. Some 89,857 extant species are recognized, with estimates ranging over 251,000, making it the second-largest animal phylum after [[Arthropod|Arthropoda]]. Molluscs are the largest marine phylum, about 23% of all named marine organisms, and include many freshwater and terrestrial species. The phylum is typically divided into 7 or 8 classes, two entirely extinct. [[Gastropod|Gastropods]] (snails, slugs, and abalone) account for 80% of classified species. [[Cephalopod|Cephalopods]] are among the most neurologically advanced invertebrates, and either the [[Giant squid|giant squid]] or the [[Colossal squid|colossal squid]] is the largest known extant invertebrate.

The four most universal features are a soft body composed almost entirely of muscle, a mantle with a cavity used for breathing and excretion, a radula, or rasping "tongue" (absent in bivalves), and the structure of the nervous system. The hypothetical ancestral mollusc has a single limpet-like shell of proteins and chitin reinforced with calcium carbonate, secreted by the mantle, and a muscular foot beneath. Circulation is mainly open. Evidence for gastropods, cephalopods, and bivalves appears in the [[Cambrian]], 541–485.4 million years ago, but their evolutionary history remains debated.

Molluscs have long been a food source, though toxins can accumulate in some. They have yielded luxury goods, notably [[Pearl|pearls]], mother of pearl, and Tyrian purple dye, and their shells served as money in some preindustrial societies. The bite of the [[Blue-ringed octopus|blue-ringed octopus]] is often fatal. Schistosomiasis, transmitted by water snails, affects about 200 million people, and snails and slugs can be serious agricultural pests.

The name derives from French *mollusque*, from Latin *mollis*, "soft". The study of molluscs is called malacology.

=== KEY: Moment_of_inertia_29073f
HEADWORD: Moment of inertia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christiaan Huygens | Leonhard Euler | Tensor | Torque
ENTRY:
Scalar measure of rotational inertia with respect to a fixed axis of rotation. It is the ratio between the applied [[Torque|torque]] and the resulting angular acceleration about that axis, and it plays the same role in rotation that mass plays in linear motion. A body's moment of inertia depends on its mass and on how that mass is distributed relative to the axis. For a point mass, it is the mass times the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis. [[Christiaan Huygens]] introduced the parameter in 1673 in his study of the compound pendulum, and [[Leonhard Euler]] named it in his 1765 treatise. A body free to rotate in three dimensions is described by a symmetric 3 × 3 [[Tensor|inertia tensor]], whose principal axes act independently. A flywheel's moment of inertia resists variations in applied torque, smoothing rotation.

=== KEY: Momentum_373237
HEADWORD: Momentum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: General relativity | Isaac Newton | Quantum mechanics | Special relativity | Uncertainty principle | Velocity
ENTRY:
Vector quantity equal to the product of an object's mass and [[Velocity|velocity]]. In SI units it is measured in kilogram meters per second, which is dimensionally equivalent to the newton-second. [[Isaac Newton|Newton]]'s second law states that the rate of change of a body's momentum equals the net force acting on it. In an inertial frame, the total momentum of a closed system is conserved when no external forces act on it. This conservation reflects translational symmetry and holds, in modified form, in [[Special relativity|special relativity]], [[General relativity|general relativity]], electrodynamics and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. In quantum mechanics, generalized momentum becomes an operator on a wave function, and the [[Uncertainty principle|Heisenberg uncertainty principle]] relates momentum and position operators.

=== KEY: Monarchy_dfbb07
HEADWORD: Monarchy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Absolute monarchy | American Revolution | Charles III | Constitutional monarchy | French Revolution | Narmer | Roman Republic | World War I
ENTRY:
Hereditary form of government in which political power is legally passed to family members of the monarch, a head of state who rules for life. Monarchs may be entirely ceremonial, hold executive powers, or have absolute power, and may also gain authority through election. There are conventionally two types: [[Absolute monarchy]], governed as autocracy, and [[Constitutional monarchy]], in which a constitution leaves the monarch legal and ceremonial roles with limited or no political power. Most modern monarchies are constitutional. The word comes from the Greek *monarkhia*, "absolute rule", from *monos* ("alone") and *arkhein* ("to rule").

Among the oldest recorded monarchies were those of [[Narmer]], pharaoh of Egypt c. 3100 BC, and Enmebaragesi, Sumerian king of Kish c. 2600 BC. In several ancient cultures the monarch held sacral functions connected to sacrifice and was sometimes identified with divine ancestry, possibly establishing the notion of the divine right of kings. Some monarchies were abolished in antiquity, as in the [[Roman Republic]] (509 BC). By the 17th century monarchy was challenged by parliamentarism and anti-monarchism, including the temporary overthrow of the English monarchy in 1649, the [[American Revolution]] of 1776, and the [[French Revolution]] of 1789. Advocacy of abolition is called republicanism, and advocacy of monarchy monarchism.

Nearly half of independent states at the start of the 19th century were monarchies. The proportion peaked in the middle of that century and has steadily declined, with republics replacing many monarchies, notably at the end of [[World War I]] and World War II. Today 43 sovereign nations have a monarch, including fifteen Commonwealth realms that have [[Charles III]] as head of state. In Morocco, Qatar, Liechtenstein, and Thailand the hereditary monarch has more political influence than any other single source of authority.

=== KEY: Money_a93312
HEADWORD: Money
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Herodotus | Marco Polo | Paper money | Song dynasty | Tax | United States dollar | World War II | Yuan dynasty
ENTRY:
Object or record generally accepted as payment for goods and services and repayment of debts, such as [[Tax|taxes]], in a particular country or socio-economic context. Its distinguishing functions are medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value and, sometimes, standard of deferred payment; most modern textbooks list only the first three.

Money was historically an emergent market phenomenon with intrinsic value as a commodity. Nearly all contemporary systems rest on unbacked fiat money, whose value derives from social convention and a government's declaration of legal tender. The money supply comprises currency in circulation and, depending on the definition, bank money, which forms by far the largest part of broad money in developed countries.

The word derives from Latin *moneta* 'coin', named for the temple of Juno Moneta, site of the Roman mint. The Mesopotamian shekel was a unit of weight based on about 160 grains of barley. According to [[Herodotus]], the Lydians first used gold and silver coins, which modern scholars date to c. 650–600 BC. [[Paper money]] first appeared in China during the [[Song dynasty]] as *jiaozi*, evolving from promissory notes used since the 7th century; [[Marco Polo]] described it under the [[Yuan dynasty]]. Stockholms Banco issued Europe's first banknotes in 1661. By the early 20th century almost all countries had adopted the gold standard. After [[World War II]], most countries fixed fiat currencies to the [[United States dollar|U.S. dollar]], itself fixed to gold until the U.S. government suspended convertibility in 1971.

=== KEY: Mongol_Empire_47dbea
HEADWORD: Mongol Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Genghis Khan | Golden Horde | Ilkhanate | Jochi | Kublai Khan | Mongolia | Qing dynasty | Yuan dynasty
ENTRY:
Largest contiguous empire in history, originating in present-day [[Mongolia]] and at its height stretching from the Sea of Japan to Eastern Europe, north into Siberia and south into the Indian subcontinent.

The empire emerged from the unification of several nomadic tribes under Temüjin, whom a council proclaimed ruler of all Mongols in 1206 with the title [[Genghis Khan]]. It grew rapidly under him and his descendants, who sent armies in every direction, conquering the Iranian plateau, invading Southeast Asia and reaching the Levant and the Carpathian Mountains. Its mobile cavalry armies were organized on a decimal system of units of 10, 100, 1,000, and 10,000. The conquests caused widespread depopulation in affected regions, with some estimates of 40 million or more deaths. The resulting *Pax Mongolica* allowed trade, technologies, commodities, and ideologies to move across Eurasia.

Succession disputes split the empire, as Genghis Khan's grandchildren contested whether the line should follow his son Ögedei or other sons such as Tolui, Chagatai, or [[Jochi]]. After Möngke Khan died in 1259, rival councils elected his brothers Ariq Böke and [[Kublai Khan]], who fought the Toluid Civil War (1260–1264). By Kublai's death in 1294 the empire had fractured into four khanates: the [[Golden Horde]], the Chagatai Khanate, the [[Ilkhanate]], and the [[Yuan dynasty]] in China, so named by Kublai in an edict of December 18, 1271.

The Ilkhanate disintegrated between 1335 and 1353. The Yuan lost China in 1368 to Ming forces and retreated north as the Northern Yuan, which survived until conquest by the [[Qing dynasty]] in the 1630s. The Golden Horde's rule in Eastern Europe is traditionally considered to have ended in 1480, and the Chagatai Khanate lasted until 1687.

Small terrestrial carnivorous [[Mammal|mammal]] of the family Herpestidae, found in Africa and Asia. The family has two subfamilies: the Herpestinae, with 23 living species native to southern Europe, Africa and Asia, and the Mungotinae, with 11 species native to Africa. Herpestidae originated about 21.8 ± 3.6 million years ago in the Early Miocene. Mongooses have long faces and bodies, short legs, long tapering tails and nonretractable claws. Their head-to-body length ranges from 24 to 58 cm, and they weigh from 320 g to 5 kg. Their diets consist mainly of insects, rodents, reptiles and birds. Mongooses are one of at least four mammalian taxa with mutations in the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor that protect against snake venom. Some species are kept as pets to control vermin, and a large introduced population lives on [[Hawaii]].

=== KEY: Monism_c1bf9b
HEADWORD: Monism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baruch Spinoza | Philosophy
ENTRY:
Philosophical view that reality is ultimately one kind of thing. The term attributes oneness to a concept such as existence and is used primarily in [[Philosophy]], where it sometimes overlaps with nondualism. Priority monism holds that all things derive from a distinct source, as in Neoplatonism, where everything derives from the One. Existence monism holds that strictly only the universe exists. Substance monism explains existing things by a single substance, such as matter or mind, while dual-aspect monism treats the mental and the physical as two aspects of one substance. Neutral monism holds that the fundamental nature of reality is neither mental nor physical. The term was introduced in the 18th century by Christian von Wolff in *Logic* (1728). [[Baruch Spinoza]] argued that "God or Nature" is the only substance of the universe.

=== KEY: Monitor_lizard_a066cb
HEADWORD: Monitor lizard
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Komodo dragon
ENTRY:
Lizard of the genus *Varanus*, the only living genus in the family Varanidae. About 94 species are recognized. They are native to Africa, Asia and Oceania, and one invasive species is also found in the southern United States. Monitor lizards have long necks, powerful tails and claws. Living species range from 20 cm (7.9 in) in length to over 3 m (10 ft) in the case of the [[Komodo dragon]]. Most are terrestrial, though many are arboreal or semiaquatic. Most are carnivorous, eating small reptiles, fish, birds, insects, small mammals and eggs, and a few also eat fruit and vegetation.

=== KEY: Monkey_412696
HEADWORD: Monkey
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baboon | Old World monkey | Rhesus macaque | Tarsier
ENTRY:
Simian primate other than an ape. Traditionally, all simians except apes are called monkeys, and apes are deep within the same family tree. The Old World monkeys ([[Old World monkey|Catarrhini]]), named by Étienne Geoffroy in 1812, are native to Africa and Asia. The New World monkeys (Platyrrhini) are native to Central and South America and are thought to have reached South America by ocean. Many species are arboreal, though some, such as [[Baboon|baboons]], live mainly on the ground. Most are diurnal, and monkeys are generally considered intelligent, especially the Old World monkeys. Simians diverged from [[Tarsier|tarsiers]] some 70 million years ago. Some species, such as the [[Rhesus macaque|rhesus macaque]], serve as models for human physiology in biomedical research.

=== KEY: Monocotyledon_172638
HEADWORD: Monocotyledon
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Clade of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] whose seeds contain only one embryonic leaf, or cotyledon. Monocotyledons, or monocots, are contrasted with dicotyledons, which have two cotyledons. The APG IV system recognizes the monocots as a monophyletic group without assigning it a rank. Monocots are of major economic importance and make up a majority of the plant biomass used in agriculture. Crops such as dates, onions, garlic, [[Rice]], [[Wheat]], [[Maize]] and [[Sugarcane]] are monocots. The grasses cover over 40% of Earth's land area. [[Orchid|Orchids]], [[Tulip|tulips]], daffodils and lilies are common houseplants. The group comprises about 60,000 to 85,000 species, roughly one-quarter of angiosperm diversity.

=== KEY: Monogamy_a04f21
HEADWORD: Monogamy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Polyamory | Polygamy
ENTRY:
Form of dyadic relationship with one partner at any one time. The term, derived from Greek for "one marriage", has genetic, sexual, social and marital meanings. Biologists distinguish social, sexual and genetic monogamy, which do not always coincide, and serial monogamy describes successive exclusive relationships. It contrasts with non-monogamy such as [[Polygamy|polygamy]] or [[Polyamory|polyamory]]. Monogamy in humans varies across cultures. A minority of societies are strictly monogamous, while many others practice serial monogamy or tolerate extramarital sex. Genetic monogamy is relatively unstudied and is often contradicted by evidence of extrapair paternity. Human monogamy likely evolved from factors such as the need for paternal care, together with cultural developments such as [[Agriculture|agriculture]], property inheritance and religious norms.
