=== KEY: Cicero_c6dd4a
HEADWORD: Cicero
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: John Locke | Latin | Mark Antony | Montesquieu | Petrarch | Pompey | Renaissance | Roman Republic
ENTRY:
Roman statesman, lawyer, scholar, philosopher, orator, and writer. He sought to uphold optimate principles during the political crises of the [[Roman Republic]] that led to the establishment of the Roman Empire. His extensive writings include treatises on rhetoric, philosophy, and politics, and he is considered one of Rome's greatest orators and prose stylists.

Cicero came from a wealthy municipal family of the equestrian order and was educated in Rome and Greece. He adapted the arguments of the main schools of Hellenistic philosophy into [[Latin]] and coined a large part of Latin philosophical vocabulary, almost 150 terms of it by translating Greek ones. As consul in 63 BC he suppressed the Catilinarian conspiracy, but his summary and controversial execution of five conspirators without trial brought exile in 58 and recall the next year. He governed Cilicia in 51 and, supporting [[Pompey]] in Caesar's civil war, was pardoned after Caesar's victory. After Caesar's assassination in 44 BC he led the Senate against [[Mark Antony]] in a series of speeches, the *Philippicae*, and elevated Caesar's heir Octavian to rally support against him. When Octavian and Antony reconciled to form the Second Triumvirate with Lepidus, Cicero was proscribed and executed in late 43 BC while trying to escape Italy. His hands and head were displayed on the Rostra.

[[Petrarch]]'s rediscovery of Cicero's letters is often credited with initiating the 14th-century [[Renaissance]] revival of humanism and classical Roman culture. His authority peaked in the 18th-century Enlightenment, and his impact on thinkers such as [[John Locke]] and [[Montesquieu]] was substantial. His works remain among the most important primary material for the history of the last days of the Republic.

=== KEY: Ciliate_53e555
HEADWORD: Ciliate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Protist
ENTRY:
Single-celled [[Protist|protist]] of the phylum Ciliophora, characterized by hair-like organelles called cilia. Cilia are structurally identical to flagella but are shorter and more numerous. They are used for swimming, crawling, attachment, feeding and sensation. About 4,500 free-living species have been described, and the potential number of extant species is estimated at 27,000–40,000. Ciliates range in size from 10 μm to 4 mm and are common in lakes, ponds, oceans and wet soils. Most are free-living or commensal, but some are parasites, including *Balantidium coli*, the only ciliate known to infect humans. Reproduction is by binary fission or conjugation, and contractile vacuoles regulate osmotic balance in freshwater habitats.

=== KEY: Cinematography_d8c215

ENTRY:
Art of motion picture photography, including electronic video photography. ENTRY:
Natural oscillation that repeats roughly every 24 hours. Circadian rhythms originate within an organism and are adjusted to the environment by external cues called zeitgebers, such as light and temperature. === KEY: Circle_30954d
HEADWORD: Circle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Euclid's Elements | Gear | Pi | Wassily Kandinsky | Wheel
ENTRY:
Shape consisting of all points in a plane at a given distance from a given point, the center. That distance is the radius; a line segment through the center joining two points on the circle is a diameter, twice the length of a radius; and the perimeter is the circumference. A circle bounds a region of the plane called a disc, though everyday usage often treats the two as one. The ratio of circumference to diameter is the constant [[Pi|pi]], so the circumference is C = 2πr and the area is A = πr².

Related terms include the arc (any connected part of a circle), the chord (a segment whose endpoints lie on the circle), the secant, the tangent (a line touching the circle at a single point), the sector, and the segment. The word derives from the Greek *kirkos*/*kuklos*, meaning "hoop" or "ring."

The circle has been known since before recorded history. Natural circles include the full moon, and the circle is the basis for the [[Wheel|wheel]] and related inventions such as [[Gear|gears]]. The Egyptian Rhind papyrus, dated to 1700 BC, gives a method for finding a circle's area that corresponds to an approximate value of π of 256/81 (3.16049...). Book 3 of [[Euclid's Elements]] deals with the properties of circles. In the 3rd century BC, [[Archimedes]] refined the value of π to between 3 10/71 and 3 1/7 using inscribed and circumscribed polygons. In 1880 Ferdinand von Lindemann proved that π is transcendental, showing that the ancient problem of squaring the circle cannot be solved with straightedge and compass. With the rise of abstract art in the early 20th century, circles became an artistic subject in their own right, notably in the work of [[Wassily Kandinsky]].

=== KEY: Circuit_breaker_30b547
HEADWORD: Circuit breaker
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fuse (electrical) | Thomas Edison
ENTRY:
Automatic electrical safety device protecting a circuit from overcurrent. Unlike a [[Fuse (electrical)|fuse]], which must be replaced after it operates, a circuit breaker can be reset manually or automatically to resume normal operation. Its basic function is to interrupt current flow to protect equipment and prevent fire, and it is often used as a main switch to disconnect and reconnect power to a whole sub-network. Designs range from devices for low-current circuits and household appliances to switchgear protecting high-voltage circuits that feed an entire city. [[Thomas Edison]] described an early form in an 1879 patent application. A modern miniature circuit breaker was patented by Brown, Boveri & Cie in 1924, and by 1935 breakers at the Boulder Dam project interrupted faults of up to 2,500 MVA in three AC cycles.

=== KEY: Circuit_design_ad313c
HEADWORD: Circuit design
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer simulation | Integrated circuit | Transistor
ENTRY:
Engineering process for designing electrical and electronic circuits, from complex systems down to individual [[Transistor|transistors]]. Traditional design begins with a specification that states what the circuit must do without indicating how, covering signals, power supplies, and physical limits such as size, weight and temperature range. === KEY: Circulatory_system_d36f5a
HEADWORD: Circulatory system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artery | Arthropod | Blood | Capillary | Heart | Lung | Lymphatic system | Vein
ENTRY:
Organ system that circulates blood through the body and, in vertebrates, comprises the [[Heart|heart]], blood vessels, and blood. Its cardiovascular, or vascular, portion consists of the heart and vessels and has two divisions: the pulmonary circulation and the systemic circulation.

The system is closed in vertebrates, meaning blood never leaves the network of vessels, made up of [[Artery|arteries]], [[Capillary|capillaries]], and [[Vein|veins]]. Many invertebrates, such as [[Arthropod|arthropods]], have an open system in which a heart pumps hemolymph that returns through the body cavity, while sponges and comb jellies lack a circulatory system.

[[Blood]] consists of plasma, red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. It carries oxygen and nutrients to tissues and removes waste. These substances nourish tissues, help the immune system fight disease, and help maintain homeostasis by stabilizing temperature and pH. An average adult holds five to six quarts (roughly 4.7 to 5.7 liters) of blood, about 7% of body weight.

The human heart has four chambers: left and right atria and ventricles. In the pulmonary circulation, oxygen-poor blood passes from the right heart to the [[Lung|lungs]], where carbon dioxide is released and oxygen absorbed, and returns to the left atrium. In the systemic circulation, oxygenated blood leaves the left heart through the aorta to the rest of the body and returns to the right heart through the venae cavae.

The [[Lymphatic system]] complements the circulatory system. An open system, it returns excess fluid filtered from capillaries to the blood as lymph and works with the immune system to defend against pathogens. Cardiovascular diseases can affect it.

=== KEY: Circumcision_78512e

ENTRY:
Surgical removal of the [[Foreskin|foreskin]] from the [[Human penis|human penis]]. It plays a major role in [[Judaism]] and [[Islam]], and it is considered obligatory in Judaism. Prophylactic use originated in England during the 1850s. Chronic disease of the [[Liver|liver]] characterized by fibrosis. Scar tissue and regenerative nodules replace functioning liver tissue, increasing resistance to blood flow and causing portal hypertension. The disease typically develops slowly over months or years. Early symptoms include tiredness, weakness, loss of appetite and unexplained weight loss; later symptoms include [[Jaundice|jaundice]], abdominal fluid build-up and easy bruising. Common causes include alcohol-related liver disease, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), heroin abuse, and chronic hepatitis B and C. Diagnosis relies on blood tests, medical imaging and [[Biopsy|liver biopsy]]. Avoiding alcohol is recommended in all cases, and a liver transplant may be an option once liver failure develops. In 2015 cirrhosis affected about 2.8 million people and caused 1.3 million deaths. [[Hippocrates]] described the condition in the fifth century BCE.

=== KEY: City_57d056
HEADWORD: City
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Industrial Revolution | Mesopotamia | United Nations | Urbanization
ENTRY:
Large, permanent, densely populated human settlement with administratively defined boundaries, whose members work primarily in non-agricultural occupations. The term has different meanings around the world, and there is no universally agreed lower limit of size. Common population definitions for an urban area range from 1,500 to 50,000 people. A modern metric, the degree of urbanization, was endorsed in March 2021 by the [[United Nations]] largely for international statistical comparison; it requires at least 50,000 inhabitants in contiguous dense grid cells of more than 1,500 inhabitants per square kilometer.

A typical city has professional administrators, regulations, and taxation, and extensive systems for housing, transportation, and sanitation. Cities emerged around 3500 BC in river valleys such as [[Mesopotamia]], the Indus Valley, and Mesoamerica, where agricultural surpluses enabled specialization and trade. Following two centuries of rapid [[Urbanization|urbanization]] since the [[Industrial Revolution]], more than half of the world population lives in cities, which cover about 3% of the land surface; projections put the urban share at 68% by 2050. Compact cities can have a smaller ecological footprint per inhabitant and are often cited as a crucial element in fighting [[Climate change|climate change]]. Concentration can also form urban heat islands, concentrate pollution, and stress water supplies. The international community has prioritized sustainable cities through Sustainable Development Goal 11.

=== KEY: Civil_engineering_cbea29
HEADWORD: Civil engineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Bridge | Canal | Cornell University | Dam | Great Wall of China | Mesopotamia | Parthenon
ENTRY:
Professional engineering discipline concerned with the design, construction, and maintenance of the physical and naturally built environment. Its work includes public works such as roads, [[Bridge|bridges]], [[Canal|canals]], [[Dam|dams]], airports, sewage systems, pipelines, railways, and the structural components of buildings. Traditional sub-disciplines include structural, geotechnical, environmental, transportation, water resources, and construction engineering. It is considered the second-oldest engineering discipline after military engineering and was defined to distinguish non-military engineering from military engineering.

The earliest practice may date to between 4000 and 2000 BC in ancient Egypt, the Indus Valley, and [[Mesopotamia]], when people abandoned a nomadic existence and needed shelter and transport. Early large works include the pyramids of Egypt (c. 2700–2500 BC), the [[Parthenon]] (447–438 BC), the Appian Way (c. 312 BC), and the [[Great Wall of China]] (c. 220 BC). A scientific approach appears in the 3rd century BC with [[Archimedes]], whose principle underlies the understanding of buoyancy.

The term civil engineering was coined in the 18th century to cover all things civilian as opposed to military. The first institution for teaching it, the École Nationale des Ponts et Chaussées, was established in France in 1747. John Smeaton, builder of the Eddystone Lighthouse, was the first self-proclaimed civil engineer. The Institution of Civil Engineers was founded in London in 1818, with Thomas Telford as first president from 1820, and received a Royal Charter in 1828 that formally recognized the profession. Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute awarded the first U.S. degree in civil engineering in 1835; the first such degree to a woman went to Nora Stanton Blatch at [[Cornell University]] in 1905.

=== KEY: Civil_law_legal_system_4691b7
HEADWORD: Civil law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Common law | Corpus Juris Civilis | Napoleonic Code | Precedent | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Legal system rooted in the [[Roman Empire]]. Today it is the world's most common legal system, practiced in about 150 countries. It was comprehensively codified starting in the 19th century, most notably in France's [[Napoleonic Code]] (1804) and Germany's *Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch* (1900). Unlike [[Common law|common law]] systems, which rely on [[Precedent|judicial precedent]], civil law treats legal codes as the primary source of law and gives case law a secondary role. === KEY: Civilization_048ba9
HEADWORD: Civilization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Ancient Egypt | Hunter-gatherer | Mesopotamia | Neolithic Revolution | Slavery | Writing system
ENTRY:
Complex society characterized by the development of the state, urbanization, and standardized symbolic communication in the form of [[Writing system|writing systems]]. Civilizations are organized around densely populated settlements and divided into more or less rigid hierarchical social classes, often with a ruling elite and subordinate urban and rural populations. They engage in intensive agriculture, mining, small-scale manufacture, and trade, and are marked by elaborate architecture, infrastructure, technological advancement, currency, taxation, regulation, and specialization of labor. Civilization concentrates power and extends human control over nature, including over other human beings. The term is often applied to a larger and supposedly more advanced culture, in implied contrast to non-centralized tribal societies, nomadic pastoralists, Neolithic societies, and hunter-gatherers.

The word relates to the Latin *civitas*, "city," and the National Geographic Society has described its most basic definition as "a society made up of cities." The earliest civilizations are generally connected with the final stages of the [[Neolithic Revolution]] in West Asia, culminating in rapid urban revolution and state formation associated with a governing elite. Among the earliest were those of [[Mesopotamia]] (c. 3500 BC), [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] (c. 3100 BC), Caral–Supe in Peru (c. 3000 BC), the Indus Valley (c. 2600 BC), and the Yellow River in China (from c. 2000 BC).

The abstract noun came from French in the 1760s; its first known French use is from 1757, and its first English use is attributed to Adam Ferguson in 1767. In the [[Age of Enlightenment|Enlightenment]] it was opposed to barbarism and meant the progress of humanity as a whole, and it was used only in the singular. Use of "civilizations" as a countable noun became much more common in the later 20th century. In 1996 Samuel P. Huntington defined a civilization as simply a "culture writ large." Already in the 18th century the idea was not always seen as an improvement: Rousseau held that civilization is not fully in accord with human nature, and later German thinkers, beginning with Herder, regarded cultures as natural organisms and civilization as unnatural and productive of vices such as guile, hypocrisy, envy, and avarice. Leo Strauss argued during World War II that this view lay behind Nazism and German militarism and nihilism.

Social scientists such as V. Gordon Childe have listed traits that distinguish civilizations, including subsistence, settlement patterns, forms of government, social stratification, economic systems, and literacy. All civilizations have depended on agriculture, with the possible exception of some early Peruvian ones that may have relied on maritime resources, and most depended on cereal agriculture. The traditional surplus model holds that stored grain surpluses allowed soldiers, artisans, priests, and other specialists to emerge. One study in the *Journal of Political Economy* contradicts this, arguing that only cereal farming produced civilization because its yearly harvest could be taxed. Some [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherers]], such as indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, have also had food surpluses. Andrew Nikiforuk argues that civilizations relied on the energy of enslaved people and considers [[Slavery|slavery]] a common feature of pre-modern civilizations.

=== KEY: Class_field_theory_dd3e9d
HEADWORD: Class field theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic number theory | David Hilbert
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Algebraic number theory|algebraic number theory]] concerned with abelian extensions. It describes all abelian Galois extensions of local and global fields using objects associated with the ground field. A key result is Artin reciprocity, which gives a canonical isomorphism between the Galois group of a finite abelian extension and a quotient of the idele class group. The theory takes its name from class fields, the abelian extensions that correspond to open subgroups of finite index. === KEY: Classical_antiquity_fba979
HEADWORD: Classical antiquity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Ancient Olympic Games | Carthage | Fall of the Western Roman Empire | Homer | Justinian I | Late antiquity | Renaissance
ENTRY:
Period of cultural European history between the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD, comprising the interwoven civilizations of ancient Greece and Rome, known together as the Greco-Roman world. It followed the Greek Dark Ages and was succeeded by [[Late antiquity]]. Its influence reached much of Europe, North Africa, and West Asia.

The period is conventionally considered to begin with the earliest recorded Greek epic poetry of [[Homer]] (8th–7th centuries BC) and to end with the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire|fall of the Western Roman Empire]] in 476 AD.

The Archaic period saw advances in political theory and the beginnings of democracy, philosophy, theater, and poetry. The traditional date for the founding of the [[Ancient Olympic Games]] is 776 BC. [[Carthage]] was founded in 814 BC. According to legend, Rome was founded on 21 April 753 BC by Romulus and Remus. The monarchy was expelled in 510 BC and Rome became a republic in 509 BC. Classical Greece corresponds to most of the 5th and 4th centuries BC, from the end of the Athenian tyranny in 510 BC to the death of [[Alexander the Great]] in 323 BC. Rome codified legal principles in the Twelve Tables, later compiled in the *Corpus Juris Civilis* under [[Justinian I]] in the 6th century.

Greco-Roman culture has been immensely influential on the language, politics, law, education, philosophy, science, literature, ethics, art, and architecture of the Western world and, through it, the modern world. Surviving fragments of classical culture helped produce a revival beginning in the 14th century that became known as the [[Renaissance]], and neoclassical revivals followed in the 18th and 19th centuries.

=== KEY: Classical_mechanics_4de01c
HEADWORD: Classical mechanics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chaos theory | Hamiltonian mechanics | Isaac Newton | Lagrangian mechanics | Leonhard Euler | Noether's theorem | Quantum mechanics | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Branch of physics describing the effect of forces on the motion of macroscopic objects and bulk matter, without considering quantum effects and often without relativistic effects.

It is used to describe projectiles, machinery, spacecraft, planets, stars, galaxies, deformable solids, and fluids. The earliest formulation, often called Newtonian mechanics, rests on the 17th-century work of [[Isaac Newton]], whose three laws of motion and law of universal gravitation appeared in 1687, and on mathematical methods developed by Newton, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, [[Leonhard Euler|Euler]], and others. Energy-based methods developed in the 18th and 19th centuries by Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, William Rowan Hamilton, and others produced analytical mechanics, which includes [[Lagrangian mechanics]] and [[Hamiltonian mechanics]]. The two are equivalent by a Legendre transformation. All equations of motion can be derived from the principle of least action, one consequence being [[Noether's theorem]], which connects conservation laws to symmetries.

Traditionally the subject is divided into statics (force and torque on systems in equilibrium), kinematics (the "geometry of motion"), and dynamics, which also considers the forces involved. It may alternatively be divided by region of application: celestial, continuum, relativistic, and statistical mechanics.

If the present state of an object obeying its laws is known, its future and past motion can be determined, although [[Chaos theory]] shows that long-term predictions are unreliable. The theory is accurate for objects that are not extremely massive and whose speeds are far below that of light. Atom-sized objects require [[Quantum mechanics]], speeds approaching that of light require [[Special relativity]], and extremely massive objects require general relativity.

=== KEY: Classical_music_25e50a
HEADWORD: Classical music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baroque | Carolingian Empire | Gregorian chant | Johann Sebastian Bach | Ludwig van Beethoven | Richard Wagner | Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart
ENTRY:
Tradition of art music of the Western world, distinct from Western folk and popular music.

It is characterized by formality and complexity of musical form and harmonic organization, notably the use of polyphony. Since at least the ninth century it has been chiefly a written tradition, with a sophisticated notational system and an accompanying critical and scholarly literature. The English *classical* derives from the Latin *classicus*, originally referring to the highest class of Roman citizens.

Surviving early medieval music is chiefly religious, monophonic, and vocal. The earliest extant manuscripts date from the [[Carolingian Empire]] (800–887), when Western plainchant gradually unified into [[Gregorian chant]]. Staff notation developed in the 11th century. By the mid-12th century France was the major musical center, where the Notre-Dame school first fully explored organized rhythms and polyphony. In the early 15th century the Franco-Flemish School brought choral music, particularly the mass and motet, to new standards.

The [[Baroque]] period (1580–1750) saw standardization of common-practice tonality and growth of instrumental ensembles. Italy was the birthplace of opera, the concerto, sonata form, the oratorio, and the cantata, and the fugue technique championed by [[Johann Sebastian Bach]] exemplified the period's complexity. In the Classical period (1750–1820), [[Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart]], Joseph Haydn, and [[Ludwig van Beethoven]] produced symphonies, string quartets, and concertos. Romantic music (1800–1910) emphasized programmatic works, virtuosity, and nationalism, converging in the operas of [[Richard Wagner]].

In the 20th century stylistic unification dissolved and popular music grew in prominence. Many composers adopted modernism, some abandoning tonality for serialism. After World War II audiences for the first time valued older music over contemporary works. Practitioners from the Americas, Africa, and Asia now hold crucial roles.

=== KEY: Cleopatra_f8cbe5
HEADWORD: Cleopatra
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Queen of Ptolemaic Egypt (r. 51–30 BC) and the last active Hellenistic pharaoh.

Formally Cleopatra VII, she was a descendant of the dynasty's founder, [[Ptolemy I Soter]], a Macedonian Greek general under [[Alexander the Great]]. She is the only Ptolemaic ruler known to have learned the Egyptian language, among several others.

Born in Alexandria, she was the daughter of Ptolemy XII Auletes, who named her his heir before his death in 51 BC. She began her reign alongside her brother Ptolemy XIII, but a falling-out led to civil war. [[Pompey]], defeated at Pharsalus in 48 BC, fled to Egypt and was ambushed and killed. [[Julius Caesar]] then occupied Alexandria and was besieged with Cleopatra at the palace; after the siege was lifted, Ptolemy XIII drowned in the Nile. Caesar declared Cleopatra and her brother Ptolemy XIV joint rulers, and their private affair produced a son, Caesarion.

In the civil war of 43–42 BC she sided with the Second Triumvirate of Octavian, [[Mark Antony]], and Lepidus. After meeting at Tarsos in 41 BC she and Antony had an affair that produced three children. The Donations of Alexandria declared their children rulers over territories under Antony's authority, which Octavian portrayed as treason. He declared war on Cleopatra, defeated her and Antony's fleet at the 31 BC Battle of Actium, and invaded Egypt in 30 BC. Antony committed suicide; Cleopatra reportedly killed herself, probably by poisoning, to avoid being displayed in a Roman triumphal procession. Egypt became a Roman province, ending the Hellenistic period.

Roman historiography and Latin poetry produced a generally critical view of her that pervaded medieval and Renaissance literature. She has been depicted in ancient busts and coinage and in Renaissance and Baroque art.

ENTRY:
City (pop., 2020: 372,624) in northeastern [[Ohio]], on the southern shore of [[Lake Erie]] and county seat of Cuyahoga County. Founded in 1796 near the mouth of the Cuyahoga River, it was named after General Moses Cleaveland and became a major commercial and industrial city by the late 19th century. It was among the top 10 largest U.S. cities by population for much of the 20th century. From the 1960s its economy slowed as manufacturing declined and suburbanization occurred. The city is a port connected to the Atlantic Ocean via the [[St. Lawrence Seaway]]. Its economy now relies on higher education, healthcare and financial services. Cultural institutions include the Cleveland Museum of Art, the Cleveland Orchestra and the [[Rock and Roll Hall of Fame]].

=== KEY: Cliff_9b3411
HEADWORD: Cliff
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Steep, nearly vertical rock face. Cliffs form through [[Weathering|weathering]] and [[Erosion|erosion]], with the effect of gravity, and are common on coasts, in mountainous areas and along rivers. They are usually composed of resistant rock, such as [[Sandstone]], limestone, [[Chalk]] and dolomite, while igneous rocks such as [[Granite]] and [[Basalt]] also often form cliffs. An escarpment is a cliff formed by movement along a [[Fault (geology)|fault]], by landslides or by differential erosion of rock layers. Most cliffs have a scree slope at their base. Coastal erosion may create sea cliffs along a receding coastline.

=== KEY: Clifford_Geertz_b7688b
HEADWORD: Geertz, Clifford
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bali | Harvard University | Java | Morocco
ENTRY:
American anthropologist and leading figure of symbolic and interpretive anthropology. After a PhD from [[Harvard University]] in 1956, he conducted fieldwork in [[Java]], [[Bali]] and [[Morocco]], analyzing religion, economy and social structure through symbols. His 1973 essay collection *The Interpretation of Cultures* argued that anthropological insight comes from "thick description" that interprets the meanings people attach to their actions. Critics argued that this approach sacrificed empirical testability and universality. For three decades he was considered "the single most influential cultural anthropologist in the United States." He ended his career as professor emeritus at the Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton.

=== KEY: Climate_96ebd2
HEADWORD: Climate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmosphere | Carbon dioxide | Köppen climate classification | Methane | Plate tectonics | Thermohaline circulation
ENTRY:
Long-term weather pattern of a region, typically averaged over 30 years.

More rigorously, climate is the mean and variability of meteorological variables over a span from months to millions of years. The variables most commonly measured are temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, wind, and precipitation. In a broader sense, climate is the state of the whole climate system: the [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]], hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere and the interactions among them. The word derives from Ancient Greek *klima*, 'inclination'. The standard averaging period of 30 years was defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), whose technical commission designated 1901–30 as the reference period for standard climate normals in 1934. The 1961–90 normals serve as the baseline reference period. The popular phrase "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get" summarizes the difference between climate and weather.

A location's climate is affected by its latitude, longitude, terrain, altitude, land use, and nearby bodies of water and their currents. Over historical time spans these determinants are nearly constant and change only over millions of years through processes such as [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Others are more dynamic: [[Thermohaline circulation|thermohaline circulation]] of the ocean warms the northern Atlantic Ocean by 5 °C (9 °F) compared with other ocean basins, other ocean currents redistribute heat regionally, and vegetation affects heat absorption, water retention, and rainfall. Changes in atmospheric greenhouse gases, particularly [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and [[Methane|methane]], determine how much solar energy the planet retains.

Climates are classified by average and typical variables, most commonly temperature and precipitation. The most widely used scheme is the [[Köppen climate classification]], first developed in 1899, which was designed to identify climates associated with certain biomes. The Thornthwaite system, in use since 1948, adds evapotranspiration, and the Bergeron and Spatial Synoptic systems focus on the origin of air masses. Originally, climes were defined in Ancient Greece by a location's latitude. A common shortcoming of all such schemes is that they draw distinct boundaries, while real climates change gradually.

Paleoclimatology studies past climates. Because few direct observations exist before the 19th century, it relies on proxy evidence: sediments, ice cores, pollen, and rocks, as well as tree rings and coral. Climate models are mathematical models of past, present, and future climates.

=== KEY: Climate_change_c07e50
HEADWORD: Climate change
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arctic | Fossil fuel | Greenhouse gas | Industrial Revolution
ENTRY:
Long-term shift in Earth's temperatures and weather patterns, now chiefly the human-driven rise in global average temperature and its wider effects.

Present-day climate change includes global warming, the ongoing increase in global average temperature, and its effects on the climate system. The modern rise is driven by human activities, especially burning of [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] since the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Fossil fuel use, deforestation, and some agricultural and industrial practices release [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gases]], which absorb heat radiated by the Earth. The atmosphere now holds roughly 50% more carbon dioxide than at the end of the pre-industrial era, at levels not seen for millions of years.

The year 2024 was the warmest on record, at +1.60 °C (2.88 °F) since regular tracking began in 1850. Heat waves and wildfires are becoming more common. Amplified warming in the [[Arctic]] has contributed to thawing permafrost, retreat of glaciers, and sea ice decline. Higher temperatures also cause more intense storms and droughts, and force many species to relocate or become extinct. The World Health Organization calls climate change one of the biggest threats to global health in the 21st century.

The scientific literature shows broad agreement that human activities are the main driver of recent warming. Under the 2015 Paris Agreement, nations agreed to keep warming "well under 2 °C", but with current pledges warming would still reach about 2.8 °C (5.0 °F) by the end of the century. Proposed responses include ending fossil fuel subsidies, conserving energy, switching to wind, solar, hydro, and nuclear power.

=== KEY: Climate_variability_and_change_7c239a
HEADWORD: Climate variability and change
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | Industrial Revolution | Sun
ENTRY:
Variations in climate that persist longer than individual weather events, from seasons to millennia. [[Climate change|Climate change]] refers to variations persisting for decades or more and is now often used for contemporary global warming. The climate system receives nearly all its energy from the [[Sun|sun]]; when incoming energy exceeds outgoing energy, the climate warms. Internal variability arises from natural processes, such as the Pacific decadal oscillation and Atlantic multidecadal oscillation, while external forcing includes changes in solar output and volcanism. Since the [[Industrial Revolution]], human activities have increasingly affected the climate, and atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm to over 420 ppm. Some analyses emphasize human dominance in recent warming, while others highlight natural variability.
