HEADWORD: Isomorphism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic structure | Category theory | Logarithm | Metric space | Topological space
ENTRY:
Structure-preserving, reversible mapping between two mathematical structures of the same type. Isomorphic structures share the same properties with respect to the structure considered, so they may often be identified. The term is mainly used for [[Algebraic structure|algebraic structures]] and [[Category theory|categories]], where a homomorphism is an isomorphism if and only if it is bijective. Specialized names include an isometry for [[Metric space|metric spaces]], a homeomorphism for [[Topological space|topological spaces]], and a permutation as an automorphism of a set. The [[Logarithm|logarithm]] is an isomorphism that translates multiplication of positive real numbers into addition of real numbers.

Colorless, flammable [[Organic compound|organic compound]], the simplest secondary alcohol. It is miscible with [[Water|water]], ethanol and chloroform, and adding sodium chloride separates it from salt solutions in a process called salting out. It forms an azeotrope with water. === KEY: Isotope_1c4163
HEADWORD: Isotope
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic number | Carbon | Chemical element | Periodic table | Proton | Technetium
ENTRY:
Nuclear species of the same [[Chemical element|chemical element]], with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons. Isotopes have virtually the same chemical properties but different atomic masses and physical properties. The name, from Greek roots meaning equal and place, reflects that they occupy the same place on the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. Margaret Todd suggested the term in 1913 to Frederick Soddy, who popularized it. The [[Atomic number|atomic number]] is fixed for an element, while the mass number is the total of its [[Proton|protons]] and neutrons. For example, [[Carbon|carbon]]-12, carbon-13 and carbon-14 have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. Some isotopes are radioactive, and [[Technetium|technetium-99m]] is used in medical imaging.

Country in the Southern Levant region of [[West Asia]], bordered by Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, and Egypt, with coasts on the Mediterranean Sea and the Red Sea. The Knesset is a proportionally elected parliament; the prime minister leads the government, and the president is largely ceremonial. Israel has one of the Middle East's largest economies, ranks 12th in nominal GDP per capita, devotes a larger share of its economy to research and development than any other state, and is believed to possess nuclear weapons.

=== KEY: Istanbul_0ef8f8
HEADWORD: Istanbul
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constantine the Great | Mehmed II
ENTRY:
Largest city (pop. over 15 million) of Turkey, a transcontinental megacity on the Bosphorus and its economic, cultural, and historical center. Formerly Constantinople, it lies in northwestern Turkey between the Sea of Marmara and the Black Sea; about two-thirds of its people live in Europe and the rest in Asia. It holds 18% of Turkey's population and accounts for about 30% of the country's economy.

Neolithic artifacts indicate that the historic peninsula was settled as far back as the 6th millennium BC. Greek colonists, led according to tradition by Byzas, founded Byzantium in the seventh century BC, around 657 BC. The Roman emperor [[Constantine the Great]] refounded it as Constantinople in AD 330. For nearly 16 centuries it was the capital of four empires: the Roman Empire (330–395), the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire (395–1204 and 1261–1453), the Latin Empire (1204–1261), and the Ottoman Empire (1453–1922). It was instrumental in the advancement of Christianity in Roman and Byzantine times. [[Mehmed II]] captured it on May 29, 1453, after a 53-day siege, and the Ottomans made it the seat of their caliphate. The Republic of Turkey established its capital in Ankara in 1923, and Istanbul became the official name in 1930.

The population has increased tenfold since the 1950s as migrants from across Anatolia arrived. Most citizens are ethnic Turks; Kurds are the largest minority. The Istanbul–İzmit area is one of Turkey's main industrial regions, and the city has two international airports, multiple ports, and numerous universities. The historic center is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Because of its proximity to the North Anatolian Fault, the city is vulnerable to earthquakes.

Series of European conflicts from 1494 to 1559, centered on the Italian Peninsula. Mediterranean culinary tradition developed in [[Italy]] since Roman times and spread worldwide with Italian emigration. Country (pop. nearly 59 million) in Southern and Western Europe, officially the Italian Republic, with Rome as its capital. It consists of a peninsula extending into the [[Mediterranean Sea]], with the [[Alps]] on its northern land border, and nearly 800 islands, notably Sicily and Sardinia. It covers 301,340 sq km and borders France, Switzerland, Austria, and Slovenia, and surrounds the enclaves of Vatican City and San Marino.

Italy's history reaches back to numerous Italic peoples, notably the ancient Romans, who conquered the Mediterranean world under the Roman Republic and ruled it for centuries under the Roman Empire. The Western Roman Empire fell between late antiquity and the Early Middle Ages. By the 11th century, Italian city-states and maritime republics expanded, bringing renewed prosperity through commerce. The Italian [[Renaissance]] flourished in the 15th and 16th centuries and spread across Europe. After centuries of political division, the Kingdom of Italy was established in 1861. Italy fought in World War I from 1915 to 1918 on the side of the Entente. A fascist dictatorship was established in 1922. In World War II Italy was first part of the Axis, then a co-belligerent of the Allies after an armistice (1940–43). In 1946 the monarchy was replaced by a republic.

Italy is a developed country with one of the world's largest economies by nominal and PPP-adjusted GDP and the second-largest manufacturing sector in Europe. It is a founding member of the [[European Union]] and of NATO. It has the highest number of World Heritage Sites (62) and is the fifth most visited country in the world by international tourist arrivals.

=== KEY: Ivory_5aea7c

ENTRY:
Hard, white material from the tusks and teeth of animals, consisting mainly of dentin. The chemical structure of mammalian teeth and tusks is the same regardless of species, so the term ivory can describe any mammalian teeth or tusks of commercial interest that are large enough to carve. Elephant ivory is the most important source, but ivory from [[Mammoth|mammoth]], [[Walrus|walrus]], hippopotamus, [[Sperm whale|sperm whale]], [[Narwhal|narwhal]], and warthog is also used. Trade in finished ivory products has its origins in the Indus Valley, where pins, combs, and dice were found at Harappan sites. In Japan, ivory carvings became popular in the 17th century during the [[Edo period]]. Trade in natural ivory of threatened species, such as African and Asian elephants, is illegal.

ENTRY:
Swedish chemist, one of the founders of modern chemistry. === KEY: Jainism_d4dfa3
HEADWORD: Jainism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diwali | India | Karma | Magadha | Mahavira | The Buddha | Vedas
ENTRY:
Indian religion, also called Jain Dharma, teaching a path to spiritual purity and liberation through disciplined nonviolence (*ahimsa*) toward all living creatures. It is guided by 24 *tirthankaras* ("ford-makers"), supreme teachers who have conquered the cycle of rebirth and attained omniscience (*kevala jnana*). Its goal is *moksha*, liberation from [[Karma|karma]].

Three principles form the core of Jain philosophy: *ahimsa*, *anekantavada* (non-absolutism, the view that reality has many aspects and no single viewpoint is complete), and *aparigraha* (non-possession). Purification of the soul is held to be achievable through the three jewels: right vision, right knowledge, and right conduct. Jainism teaches five vows: nonviolence, truth, not stealing, celibacy, and non-possession. They are called *mahavratas* (great vows) for monks and nuns, and *anuvratas* (small vows) for laypersons.

In tradition, the first *tirthankara* of the current cycle was Rishabhanatha. Historians date the 23rd, Parshvanatha, to the 8th or 7th century BCE. The 24th and last, [[Mahavira]], lived in the 6th or 5th century BCE, was a contemporary of [[The Buddha|the Buddha]], and was a central figure in the Śramaṇa movement of Greater [[Magadha]], which rejected the authority of the [[Vedas]].

Applying nonviolence, the community avoided agriculture and warfare, and many Jains became merchants and bankers in ancient and medieval [[India]]. It supported networks of temples, libraries, and charitable institutions. Jainism has between four and five million followers, mostly in India, with communities in North America, Europe, and East Asia. Two sub-traditions, the Digambaras ("sky-clad") and Śvētāmbaras ("white-clad"), differ on ascetic practices, gender, and canonical texts. Major festivals include Paryushana and [[Diwali]].

American writer and civil rights activist, known for essays, novels, plays and poems. His 1953 novel *Go Tell It on the Mountain* was ranked by [[Time (magazine)|Time]] among the top 100 English-language novels. *Notes of a Native Son* (1955) established him as a voice for human equality. He moved to [[Paris]] in 1948 to escape racial hostility in the United States. [[Homosexuality|Gay]] and [[Bisexuality|bisexual]] men feature prominently in his fiction, as in *Giovanni's Room* (1956), and his work influenced both the [[Civil rights movement]] and the gay liberation movement. His 1965 debate with William F. Buckley is regarded as one of the most influential debates.
