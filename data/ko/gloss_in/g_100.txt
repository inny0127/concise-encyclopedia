=== KEY: Organic_chemistry_f15c9d
HEADWORD: Organic chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aspirin | Biochemistry | Carbon | Friedrich Wöhler | Hydrocarbon | Justus von Liebig | Organometallic chemistry
ENTRY:
Subdiscipline of chemistry concerned with the structure, properties, and reactions of organic compounds and organic materials, those containing [[Carbon|carbon]] atoms. It includes determining structural formulas, analyzing physical and chemical properties, evaluating reactivity, and the synthesis of natural products, drugs, and polymers.

The field covers [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbons]] (compounds of only carbon and hydrogen) and compounds that also contain oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and the halogens. Carbon's valence of four, with single, double, and triple bonds and delocalized electrons, makes organic compounds structurally diverse. They form the basis of all known life and underlie pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, agrichemicals, lubricants, solvents, plastics, fuels, and explosives. The field overlaps [[Organometallic chemistry|organometallic chemistry]], [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], medicinal chemistry, polymer chemistry, and materials science.

Before the 18th century, chemists generally believed that compounds from living organisms held a "vital force" (vitalism). Around 1816 Michel Chevreul showed that fats could be chemically changed into new compounds. In 1828 [[Friedrich Wöhler|Friedrich Wöhler]] produced urea from potassium cyanate and ammonium sulfate, a synthesis now generally accepted as disproving vitalism, though Wöhler himself was cautious. [[Justus von Liebig|Justus von Liebig]] is considered one of the field's principal founders. In 1856 William Henry Perkin accidentally produced the dye known as Perkin's mauve, and in 1858 Friedrich August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper independently developed the concept of chemical structure.

Bayer first manufactured [[Aspirin|aspirin]] in the last decade of the 19th century. By 1910 Paul Ehrlich's group was developing the arsenic-based Salvarsan against syphilis, beginning chemotherapy. Natural indigo production fell from 19,000 tons in 1897 to 1,000 tons by 1914 thanks to Adolf von Baeyer's synthetic methods. Total synthesis later reached molecules as complex as vitamin B12.

=== KEY: Organic_compound_c7669c
HEADWORD: Organic compound
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alkane | Chemical compound | Cyanide | Ethane | Organic chemistry | Petrochemical
ENTRY:
Subclass of [[Chemical compound|chemical compounds]] containing carbon. Little consensus exists on the exact definition, and the only universally accepted one is that organic compounds are the subject matter of [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]]. Generally, any large compound containing a carbon-hydrogen or carbon-carbon bond counts as organic, so [[Alkane|alkanes]] such as [[Ethane|ethane]] and their derivatives are included. Small carbon-containing molecules such as carbon dioxide, [[Cyanide|cyanide]] and carbonates are generally excluded for historical and disciplinary reasons. Carbon's ability to form chains accounts for the millions of known organic compounds. Although they make up a small share of Earth's crust, all known life is based on them. Synthetic organic compounds are ultimately derived from [[Petrochemical|petrochemicals]], mainly hydrocarbons.

=== KEY: Organic_farming_4b235d
HEADWORD: Organic farming
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biodiversity | Climate change | European Union | Fertilizer | Genetically modified organism | Pesticide
ENTRY:
Agricultural system that emphasizes naturally occurring, non-synthetic inputs. Also called organic agriculture, ecological farming or biological farming, it uses compost, manure and green manure, and emphasizes crop rotation, companion planting and biological pest control. Organic standards allow some naturally occurring substances, such as pyrethrin, but prohibit most synthetic [[Pesticide|pesticides]] and [[Fertilizer|fertilizers]], [[Genetically modified organism|genetically modified organisms]], and growth hormones. The system originated early in the 20th century. Its standards are set largely by the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM), founded in 1972, and are legally enforced by the [[European Union]] and individual nations. About 96,000,000 hectares were farmed organically worldwide as of 2022, approximately 2 percent of world farmland. Organic farming can benefit local [[Biodiversity|biodiversity]], but its lower yields can increase pressure to convert other land, with negative effects on biodiversity and [[Climate change|climate]].

=== KEY: Organism_e69d46
HEADWORD: Organism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anglerfish | Claude Shannon | Erwin Schrödinger | Immanuel Kant | Lichen | Metabolism | Richard Dawkins | Virus
ENTRY:
Living thing that functions as an individual, a definition that raises more problems than it solves because the concept of an individual is itself difficult. Several criteria have been proposed, few widely accepted. The most common is autonomous reproduction, growth, and [[Metabolism|metabolism]], which would exclude [[Virus|viruses]]: they cannot reproduce, grow, or metabolize on their own, and cannot synthesize the organic compounds of which they are formed.

The English term appeared in the 1660s, from the Ancient Greek *organismos*, related to *organon* ("instrument, implement, tool"). In his 1790 *Critique of Judgment*, [[Immanuel Kant]] defined an organism as "both an organized and a self-organizing being". Other proposed criteria include noncompartmentability, stated by [[Richard Dawkins]] as "the quality of being sufficiently heterogeneous in form to be rendered non-functional if cut in half" (though many organisms can be cut into pieces that regrow); individuality; an immune response; "anti-entropy", the ability to maintain order, first proposed by [[Erwin Schrödinger]]; the capacity to maintain information content, as in [[Claude Shannon]]'s information theory; and having cells. Some scientists consider the concept inadequate for biology.

Colonial organisms are problematic cases. A colony of eusocial insects shows adaptive organization and germ-soma specialization, and a siphonophore is composed of organism-like zooids. David Queller and Joan Strassmann propose cooperation as the defining trait, which would count a [[Lichen|lichen]], a partnership of fungi with algae or cyanobacteria, and the permanent sexual partnership of an [[Anglerfish|anglerfish]] as organisms. Others argue that autonomy, genetic homogeneity, and genetic uniqueness should be examined separately.

Unicellular organisms (protists, bacteria, archaea) consist of a single cell; multicellular organisms (animals, plants, fungi, algae) of many. Organisms are commonly classified into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.

=== KEY: Organization_d6b258
HEADWORD: Organization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Company | Corporation | Government | Institution | Jury | Non-governmental organization
ENTRY:
Entity comprising one or more people with a particular purpose. Organizations include [[Company|companies]], [[Corporation|corporations]], [[Government|governments]], [[Institution|institutions]], associations and [[Non-governmental organization|non-governmental organizations]]. Some operate secretly, as with secret societies, or illegally, as with criminal organizations. Governments recognize an organization through incorporation, through societal pressure such as advocacy groups, or through its role as a negotiating spokesperson. Jack Knight writes that organizations are a narrow version of institutions, since they contain internal institutions that govern relations among members. Management science holds that most organizations fall into four types: [[Jury|juries]] or committees, ecologies, matrix organizations, and pyramids or hierarchies. Condorcet's jury theorem holds that adding members improves majority decisions only if the average member votes better than chance, so staffing is crucial.

=== KEY: Organization_for_Security_and_Co_operation_in_Europe_a2d9fb
HEADWORD: Organization for Security and Co-operation in Europe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belgrade | Cold War | Eastern Bloc | Human rights | Madrid | Soviet Union | Vienna
ENTRY:
Security-oriented intergovernmental organization with 57 participating states in Europe, North America and Asia. It grew out of the 1975 Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, whose predecessors provided a forum between the Western Bloc and the [[Eastern Bloc|Eastern Bloc]] during the [[Cold War|Cold War]]. The Helsinki Final Act was signed by 35 participating states in 1975, and follow-up meetings were held in [[Belgrade|Belgrade]], [[Madrid|Madrid]] and [[Vienna|Vienna]]. After the fall of the [[Soviet Union|Soviet Union]], the CSCE was renamed the OSCE on January 1, 1995. Its mandate includes arms control, the promotion of [[Human rights|human rights]], freedom of the press, and free and fair elections. It employs around 3,460 people, mostly in field operations, and its secretariat is in Vienna. Its concerns include early warning, conflict prevention, crisis management and post-conflict rehabilitation. Critics argue that its consensus rules have hindered responses to aggression by a member state, pointing to Russian vetoes that ended the Special Monitoring Mission to Ukraine in 2022.

=== KEY: Organized_crime_1b5e74
HEADWORD: Organized crime
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Mafia | Gang
ENTRY:
Structured criminal groups that profit from illegal activities. Centralized enterprises of this kind supply banned goods such as illegal drugs or firearms, or force people to do business with them, as when [[Gang|gangs]] extort protection money from shopkeepers. In the United States, the Organized Crime Control Act (1970) defines organized crime as the unlawful activities of a highly organized, disciplined association, and structured criminal activity is called racketeering. The U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration's 2017 National Drug Threat Assessment classified Mexican transnational criminal organizations as the greatest criminal drug threat to the United States. UN Sustainable Development Goal 16 includes a target to combat all forms of organized crime as part of the 2030 Agenda.

=== KEY: Organometallic_chemistry_f48e37
HEADWORD: Organometallic chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon monoxide | Cyanide | Inorganic chemistry | Magnesium | Organic chemistry | Polymer | Transition metal | Vitamin B12
ENTRY:
Study of compounds with at least one carbon-metal bond. The field combines aspects of [[Inorganic chemistry|inorganic]] and [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]]. Organometallic compounds contain bonds between a carbon atom of an organic molecule and a metal, including alkali, alkaline earth and [[Transition metal|transition metals]], and sometimes metalloids such as boron, silicon and selenium. Bonds to inorganic carbon, as in [[Carbon monoxide|carbon monoxide]] or [[Cyanide|cyanide]], are generally considered organometallic too. Examples include Grignard reagents, which contain [[Magnesium|magnesium]], and Gilman reagents, which contain lithium and copper. Ferrocene and tetracarbonyl nickel are transition-metal examples. Methylcobalamin, a form of [[Vitamin B12|vitamin B12]], is a naturally occurring organometallic complex. Organometallic compounds are used in research and industrial reactions, and as catalysts that increase reaction rates, in producing [[Polymer|polymers]] and pharmaceuticals.

=== KEY: Organon_394cbe
HEADWORD: Organon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Averroes | Dialectic | Logic | Maimonides | Proposition | Thomas Aquinas
ENTRY:
Standard collection of six works by [[Aristotle]] on logic. The works are *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, *Prior Analytics*, *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics* and *On Sophistical Refutations*. The name is Greek for instrument or tool, reflecting the works' role as tools of philosophical inquiry. Their order is not chronological but was chosen to form a well-structured system. *Categories* classifies existence into ten types, from substance and quantity to action and passion. *On Interpretation* introduces the [[Proposition|proposition]], and *Prior Analytics* introduces Aristotle's syllogistic method. Only *Categories* and *On Interpretation* were widely available in the early Middle Ages, translated into Latin by Boethius. The rest reached Western Christendom in the 12th century. [[Averroes|Averroes]] and [[Maimonides|Maimonides]] studied the works in Arabic translations, and [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] and other scholastic philosophers wrote commentaries on the [[Dialectic|dialectic]] and [[Logic|logic]] they contain.

=== KEY: Organophosphorus_chemistry_ce2b37
HEADWORD: Organophosphorus chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Nitrogen | Organic compound | Osteoporosis | Phosphate | Phosphorus
ENTRY:
Study of [[Organic compound|organic compounds]] containing [[Phosphorus|phosphorus]]. These compounds are used mainly in pest control as an alternative to chlorinated hydrocarbons that persist in the environment. Some are highly effective insecticides, but some are extremely toxic to humans, including the nerve agents sarin and VX. Like [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]], phosphorus is in group 15 of the periodic table, so the two form compounds with similar properties. In industrial and environmental chemistry the term is broad, and many pesticides, such as malathion, are included even without a direct phosphorus-carbon bond. [[Phosphate|Phosphate]] esters, which lack that bond, are used as flame retardants and plasticizers. Glyphosate, widely known as Roundup, is a phosphonate and one of the most widely used herbicides. Bisphosphonates treat [[Osteoporosis|osteoporosis]]. The Michaelis-Arbuzov reaction is the main method for synthesizing phosphonates and phosphinates.

=== KEY: Organosilicon_chemistry_aec470
HEADWORD: Organosilicon chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diatom | Silicon carbide | Silicone
ENTRY:
Study of organometallic compounds containing carbon-silicon bonds. Most organosilicon compounds resemble ordinary organic compounds: they are colorless, flammable, hydrophobic and stable to air, although [[Silicon carbide|silicon carbide]] is considered inorganic. Chemists Charles Friedel and James Crafts prepared the first organosilicon compound, tetraethylsilane, in 1863. Frederic S. Kipping pioneered the field in the early 20th century and coined the term [[Silicone|silicone]] in 1904. Eugene G. Rochow first described the Müller-Rochow process in 1945. Most organosilicon compounds derive from chlorides made by the direct process, which reacts methyl chloride with a silicon-copper alloy; about 1 million tons are produced annually this way. Silicones are common in antifoamers, sealants, adhesives and coatings. Carbon-silicon bonds are absent in biology, though silicates occur in [[Diatom|diatoms]].

=== KEY: Organosulfur_chemistry_185d2b
HEADWORD: Organosulfur chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Coal | Fossil fuel | Penicillin | Petroleum | Sulfur | Thiol
ENTRY:
Study of organic compounds that contain [[Sulfur|sulfur]]. Organosulfur compounds are often associated with foul odors, but some of the sweetest known compounds, such as saccharin, are organosulfur derivatives. Sulfur is vital to life: two of the 20 common [[Amino acid|amino acids]], cysteine and methionine, are organosulfur compounds, and the antibiotic [[Penicillin|penicillin]] contains sulfur. Sulfur mustard is a deadly chemical warfare agent. [[Fossil fuel|Fossil fuels]] such as [[Coal|coal]] and [[Petroleum|petroleum]] necessarily contain organosulfur compounds, whose removal is a major focus of oil refineries. Sulfur shares the chalcogen group with oxygen, selenium and tellurium. Organosulfur compounds include sulfides, [[Thiol|thiols]], disulfides and polysulfides, and aromatic sulfur-containing heterocycles such as thiophene. Thiols are more nucleophilic, more acidic and more readily oxidized than alcohols.

Peak of sexual excitement, marked by rhythmic involuntary contractions in the pelvic region. Also called sexual climax, an orgasm is the sudden release of accumulated sexual excitement during the sexual response cycle. It brings intense pleasure, a general euphoric sensation and often body movements and vocalizations, and it is controlled by the involuntary, or autonomic, nervous system. Human orgasms usually result from stimulation of the [[Penis|penis]] in males, typically accompanied by [[Ejaculation|ejaculation]], and of the [[Clitoris|clitoris]] and [[Vagina|vagina]] in females. Debate continues over whether sensations such as female orgasm from [[G-spot|G-spot]] stimulation alone should count. Anorgasmia is a sexual dysfunction involving orgasm.

=== KEY: Oriental_Orthodox_Churches_ea89ec
HEADWORD: Oriental Orthodox Churches
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Armenia | Christianity | Coptic Orthodox Church | Eastern Orthodox Church | Egypt | Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church
ENTRY:
Communion of Eastern Christian churches adhering to Miaphysite Christology. Estimated at 72 million members in 2020, the Oriental Orthodox Churches are among the oldest branches of [[Christianity|Christianity]]. Their bishops are equal by virtue of episcopal ordination, and their doctrines recognize only the first three ecumenical councils. The communion comprises six autocephalous churches: the [[Coptic Orthodox Church|Coptic Orthodox Church]] of Alexandria, the Syriac Orthodox Church of Antioch, the Armenian Apostolic Church, the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church, the [[Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church|Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church]] and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church. They shared communion with the imperial Roman church before the Council of Chalcedon in AD 451 and separated primarily over differences in Christology. Most members live in [[Egypt|Egypt]], Ethiopia, Eritrea, India, Syria, Turkey and [[Armenia|Armenia]]. The name was formally adopted at the Conference of Addis Ababa in 1965. The churches are in full communion with one another but not with the [[Eastern Orthodox Church|Eastern Orthodox Church]].

=== KEY: Origami_50f4f4
HEADWORD: Origami
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edo period | Sengoku period | Square
ENTRY:
Art and technique of folding paper. Origami typically transforms a flat [[Square|square]] sheet, without cuts or glue, into figures such as the Japanese paper crane, the best-known model. The word combines *ori*, meaning to fold, and *kami*, meaning paper. Though the word has Japanese roots, the practice is universal, and paper-folding traditions arose separately in Europe, China and Japan. Ceremonial origami, called origata in Japan, is distinct from recreational origami. Play-made paper models existed in Japan by the [[Sengoku period|Sengoku period]] or the early [[Edo period|Edo period]]. *Hiden senbazuru orikata* (1797) is the oldest known technical book on origami for play. In the 20th century Akira Yoshizawa developed wet-folding techniques and standardized diagramming symbols. Origami principles are also used in stents, packaging and other engineering applications.

=== KEY: Origen_ac6cb6
HEADWORD: Origen
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Free will | Hebrew Bible | Justinian I | Logic | Soul | Trinity
ENTRY:
Early Christian theologian and ascetic. A prolific writer, he is said to have produced roughly 2,000 treatises on textual criticism, biblical exegesis, homiletics and spirituality. He founded the Christian School of Caesarea, where he taught [[Logic|logic]], cosmology, natural history and theology. He was tortured during the Decian persecution in 250 and died a few years later from his injuries. *On the First Principles* laid out Christian theology systematically, and *Contra Celsum* is one of the most influential works of early Christian apologetics. He produced the *Hexapla*, a critical edition of the [[Hebrew Bible|Hebrew Bible]] in parallel columns, and interpreted many passages allegorically. He was the first to propose the ransom theory of atonement in its fully developed form and contributed to the development of the [[Trinity|Trinity]]. He defended [[Free will|free will]] and advocated Christian pacifism. The Church rejected his teaching on the pre-existence of [[Soul|souls]], and in 543 Emperor [[Justinian I|Justinian I]] condemned him as a heretic.

=== KEY: Origin_of_language_39b18a
HEADWORD: Origin of language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Darwin | Human evolution | Language acquisition | Noam Chomsky | Primate
ENTRY:
Relationship between language and [[Human evolution|human evolution]]. Researchers draw inferences from the fossil record, archaeological evidence, contemporary language diversity, [[Language acquisition|language acquisition]] and comparisons with the communication of other primates. Many argue that the origin of language is closely related to the origins of modern human behavior, but experts disagree about the facts. In 1866 the Linguistic Society of Paris banned debate on the subject, a prohibition influential in much of the Western world until the late 20th century. [[Charles Darwin|Charles Darwin]]'s theory of evolution by natural selection provoked a surge of speculation. Continuity theories hold that language evolved from [[Primate|primate]] precursors, while discontinuity theories hold that it appeared fairly suddenly. [[Noam Chomsky|Noam Chomsky]] argues that a single change in the human language faculty occurred approximately 100,000 years ago, before humans left Africa. Michael Tomasello, by contrast, considers language socially learned, developing from the mostly gestural aspects of primate communication.

=== KEY: Orinoco_df0ccd
HEADWORD: Orinoco
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander von Humboldt | Christopher Columbus | South America
ENTRY:
River of northern South America, flowing mainly through Venezuela and Colombia. At 2,140 km (1,330 mi), the Orinoco is one of the longest rivers in [[South America|South America]]. Its drainage basin covers approximately 1,000,000 km² (390,000 sq mi), with 65% in Venezuela and 35% in Colombia. It is the third-largest river in the world by discharge, at 39,000 m³/s (1,400,000 cu ft/s) at its delta. The river and its tributaries are the main transportation system for eastern and interior Venezuela and the Llanos plain of Colombia. [[Christopher Columbus|Christopher Columbus]] documented its mouth on August 1, 1498, and [[Alexander von Humboldt|Alexander von Humboldt]] explored stretches of it in 1800. The source was discovered in 1951 by a French-Venezuelan expedition.

=== KEY: Orion_Nebula_ec9f5a
HEADWORD: Orion Nebula
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brown dwarf | Earth | Orion (constellation) | Orion's Belt | Star formation | Sun
ENTRY:
Diffuse nebula in the [[Orion (constellation)|constellation Orion]]. Also known as Messier 42, M42 or NGC 1976, it lies south of [[Orion's Belt|Orion's Belt]] and is visible to the naked eye, with an apparent magnitude of 4.0. At 1,267.0 ± 5.4 light-years away, it is the closest region of massive [[Star formation|star formation]] to [[Earth|Earth]]. It is estimated to be 25 light-years across and to have a mass about 2,000 times that of the [[Sun|Sun]]. Astronomers have directly observed protoplanetary disks and [[Brown dwarf|brown dwarfs]] within it, which has revealed much about how stars and planetary systems form from collapsing clouds of gas and dust. Its red hue comes from hydrogen emission, while the blue-violet coloration is reflected light from massive O-class stars. The green hue, long a puzzle, was later traced to a forbidden transition in doubly ionized oxygen.

=== KEY: Orion_constellation_7e60f6
HEADWORD: Orion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Betelgeuse | Greek mythology | Halley's Comet | Meteor shower | Orion Nebula | Orion's Belt | Ptolemy | Rigel
ENTRY:
Prominent constellation of the Northern Hemisphere's winter sky, near the celestial equator. One of the 88 modern constellations, Orion was among the 48 listed by the 2nd-century AD astronomer [[Ptolemy|Ptolemy]]. It is named after a hunter in [[Greek mythology|Greek mythology]]. Its two brightest stars, [[Rigel|Rigel]] and [[Betelgeuse|Betelgeuse]], are both supergiants and among the brightest stars in the night sky. Three stars form [[Orion's Belt|Orion's Belt]], a short straight asterism. Orion is also the radiant of the Orionids, the strongest [[Meteor shower|meteor shower]] associated with [[Halley's Comet|Halley's Comet]], and it contains the [[Orion Nebula|Orion Nebula]]. Covering 594 square degrees, it ranks 26th of the 88 constellations in size. Its boundaries, set by Belgian astronomer Eugène Delporte in 1930, form a 26-sided polygon. It is most visible in the evening sky from January to April.

Asterism of three bright stars in the constellation [[Orion (constellation)|Orion]]. Alnitak, Alnilam and Mintaka appear nearly equally spaced in a line from Earth, but each is a star system: Alnilam is a single star, Alnitak is triple and Mintaka quintuple. === KEY: Ornithischia_f740b4
HEADWORD: Ornithischia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bird | Clade | Cretaceous | Dinosaur | Triassic | Triceratops
ENTRY:
Extinct [[Clade|clade]] of mainly herbivorous [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]] with a pelvic structure superficially similar to that of [[Bird|birds]]. The name, meaning bird-hipped, is misleading, since birds are theropod dinosaurs and only distantly related to the group. Members include the ceratopsians, such as [[Triceratops]]; the armored thyreophorans, such as stegosaurs and ankylosaurs; the pachycephalosaurs; and the ornithopods. Early forms were small, bipedal and fast runners, and some groups lived in herds. Many were partly covered in filamentous pelts, and debate continues over whether these were primitive feathers. The clade ranged from the Late [[Triassic]] until the [[Cretaceous]]-Paleogene extinction about 66 million years ago, contributing significantly to Mesozoic biodiversity.

=== KEY: Ottoman_Empire_699e55

State (c. 1299–1922) that spanned much of Southeastern Europe, West Asia, and North Africa, centered in modern-day Turkey. It also controlled parts of southeastern Central Europe between the early 16th and early 18th centuries.

The empire emerged from a principality founded in northwestern Anatolia in c. 1299 by the Turkoman tribal leader [[Osman I]]. His successors conquered much of Anatolia and expanded into the Balkans by the mid-14th century. The empire joined [[World War I]] on the side of the Central Powers and engaged in genocide against Armenians, Assyrians, and Greeks. The Allies occupied and partitioned it afterward. The Turkish War of Independence, led by [[Mustafa Kemal Atatürk]], ended the sultanate in 1922; the Grand National Assembly of Turkey abolished it on November 1, 1922.

=== KEY: Outer_space_940b80
HEADWORD: Outer space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Dark matter | International Space Station | Kármán line | Sputnik 1 | Vacuum | Yuri Gagarin
ENTRY:
Expanse beyond Earth's atmosphere and between celestial bodies. It is a near-perfect [[Vacuum|vacuum]] of predominantly hydrogen and helium plasma, permeated by electromagnetic radiation, cosmic rays, neutrinos, magnetic fields, and dust. Its baseline temperature, set by background radiation from the [[Big Bang]], is 2.7 kelvins (−270 °C). The plasma between galaxies is thought to account for about half of the ordinary matter in the observable universe, at fewer than one hydrogen atom per cubic meter. Most of the remaining mass-energy is made up of unknown forms called [[Dark matter|dark matter]] and dark energy.

Space has no definite starting altitude. The [[Kármán line]], 100 km above sea level, is conventionally used as its start in space treaties and aerospace records. The Outer Space Treaty, in force since October 10, 1967, precludes claims of national sovereignty and permits all states to explore freely.

According to Big Bang theory, the universe was extremely hot and dense about 13.8 billion years ago and rapidly expanded. About 380,000 years later it had cooled enough for protons and electrons to combine into hydrogen. Remaining matter collapsed into stars and galaxies, leaving the deep vacuum now called outer space.

The term "outer space" was first applied in astronomy in 1845 by Alexander von Humboldt. Exploration began with high-altitude balloons, then rockets. The Soviet Union launched [[Sputnik 1]] on October 4, 1957, and [[Yuri Gagarin]] achieved crewed Earth orbit in 1961. Apollo 11 landed on the Moon in 1969, and the [[International Space Station]] has been continuously occupied since 2000. High cost limits human flight to low Earth orbit and the Moon, while uncrewed craft have reached all known planets. Vacuum, radiation, and microgravity, which causes muscle atrophy and bone loss, endanger humans.

=== KEY: Oven_482690
HEADWORD: Oven
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cooking | Indus Valley Civilisation | Mesopotamia | Çatalhöyük
ENTRY:
Tool with a hollow chamber that exposes materials to a controlled hot environment. Ovens have been used since antiquity and differ by purpose and by how they generate heat. Most often they are used for [[Cooking|cooking]], usually baking and sometimes broiling. Ovens for ceramics and pottery are sometimes called kilns, metallurgical furnaces make metals, and glass furnaces make glass.

Some ovens burn fuel such as wood, coal, or natural gas; many use electricity. Electric ovens and furnaces use resistive heating, while microwave ovens expose materials to microwave radiation, which excites water molecules in food. Some ovens use forced convection, the movement of gases inside the chamber, to speed heating.

The earliest known ovens, in Central Europe, date to 29,000 BC: roasting and boiling pits inside yurts used to cook mammoth. In Ukraine from 20,000 BC, people used pits of hot coals covered in ashes. Clay ovens appeared in homes at [[Çatalhöyük]] by 6600 BC, in pre-dynastic Egypt c. 5000–4000 BC, and throughout [[Mesopotamia]] by 4000 BC, with examples at Ur, Nippur, and Eridu. By 3200 BC cultures of the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] used ovens to cook food and make bricks. The tandır, a cylindrical oven that bakes unleavened flatbread stuck to its walls, was common in Anatolia and remains central to rural food culture there.

In the Middle Ages, Europeans used fireplaces with large cauldrons. Cast-iron stoves came into use in the early 1700s, and the coal oven was developed in the early 19th century. James Sharp patented one of the first gas stoves in 1826, and Gustaf Dalén invented the AGA cooker in 1922. Percy Spencer discovered microwave cooking in 1946, leading to the microwave oven.

=== KEY: Oxygen_990bf4
HEADWORD: Oxygen
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antoine Lavoisier | Combustion | Earth's crust | Joseph Priestley | Ozone | Periodic table | Photosynthesis | Respiration (physiology)
ENTRY:
Chemical element, symbol O, atomic number 8, a nonmetal of the chalcogen group in the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. It is highly reactive and a potent oxidizing agent, readily forming oxides with most elements. It is the most abundant element in [[Earth's crust]], making up almost half of it, chiefly as water, carbon dioxide, iron oxides, and silicates, and the third most abundant in the universe after hydrogen and helium.

At standard temperature and pressure, two atoms bind covalently to form dioxygen (O₂), a colorless, odorless gas that is currently about 20.95% of Earth's atmosphere by molar fraction. The rarer allotrope [[Ozone|ozone]] (O₃) absorbs UVB and UVC radiation, forming a protective layer in the lower stratosphere, but at the surface it is a corrosive air pollutant. Oxygen is too reactive to remain free in air without continuous replenishment by [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] in cyanobacteria, algae, and land plants.

Aerobic [[Respiration (physiology)|respiration]] extracts energy by reacting oxygen with organic molecules from food, releasing carbon dioxide. Proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and fats contain oxygen, as do shells, teeth, and bone, and most of the mass of living organisms is oxygen in water.

The modern concept developed over five centuries, and no one person discovered oxygen. Michael Sendivogius isolated the substance before 1604, calling it *cibus vitae* ("food of life"). John Mayow proposed in 1668 that part of the air is consumed in both respiration and [[Combustion|combustion]]. Carl Wilhelm Scheele in Uppsala (1773 or earlier) and [[Joseph Priestley]] in Wiltshire (1774) discovered it independently; Priestley called it "dephlogisticated air" and did not recognize it as an element. In 1777 [[Antoine Lavoisier]] first recognized it as a chemical element and correctly described its role in combustion.

=== KEY: Oxytocin_87fbbd
HEADWORD: Oxytocin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Autism | Childbirth | Nobel Prize | Peptide | Pituitary gland
ENTRY:
[[Peptide]] hormone and neuropeptide, produced in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior [[Pituitary gland|pituitary]]. Oxytocin stimulates uterine contractions during [[Childbirth|childbirth]] and milk ejection during lactation, and it plays roles in social bonding, love and reproduction. Its release is controlled by positive feedback: release during a contraction stimulates further release, intensifying labor until the stimulus ceases. Its name derives from Greek words meaning "sharp" and "childbirth," and it is colloquially called the "love hormone." In 1953 Vincent du Vigneaud synthesized it, the first polypeptide hormone to be synthesized, and he won the [[Nobel Prize]] in Chemistry in 1955. Intranasal oxytocin has shown mixed results in trials for [[Autism|autism]], and it is being studied for anxiety disorders.

=== KEY: Oyo_Empire_8f09c7
HEADWORD: Oyo Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benin | Cavalry | Nigeria | Suicide | West Africa
ENTRY:
Former Yoruba empire in [[West Africa]], located in present-day western [[Nigeria]] and [[Benin]]. It grew into the largest Yoruba-speaking state through administrative organization, trade and the military use of [[Cavalry|cavalry]]. From the late 16th to the early 18th century it was among the most politically important states in West Africa, holding sway over most other Yoruba kingdoms and over the Fon Kingdom of Dahomey. According to legend, Ọranmiyan, a prince of Ile-Ife, founded Oyo where a snake disappeared into the ground. The Alaafin was a sacred king, and a council of nobles could compel him to commit [[Suicide|suicide]] if he was judged tyrannical. Around 1535 the Nupe occupied Oyo and drove the dynasty into exile in Borgu. It returned in the early 17th century as a more centralized state.

=== KEY: Oyster_7da119
HEADWORD: Oyster
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gill | Heart | Kidney | Pearl
ENTRY:
Common name for several families of saltwater bivalve mollusks that live in marine or brackish habitats. True oysters, family Ostreidae, include the edible oysters, such as the European flat oyster and the Pacific oyster, and they are a major aquaculture product, with global production exceeding 5 million metric tons a year. Oysters breathe mainly through [[Gill|gills]]. A small, three-chambered [[Heart|heart]] pumps colorless blood, and two [[Kidney|kidneys]] remove waste. There is no evidence that oysters have a brain. Pearl oysters belong to the family Pteriidae and are not closely related to true oysters. They make [[Pearl|pearls]] by covering an irritant with nacre. Cultured pearls have far outgrown natural pearls in the market since the early 20th century. The largest pearl-bearing oyster, *Pinctada maxima*, is roughly the size of a dinner plate.

=== KEY: Ozarks_0d574c
HEADWORD: Ozarks
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Appalachia | Arkansas | Missouri | Rocky Mountains | Tourism
ENTRY:
Highland region of the central United States, covering most of southern [[Missouri]] and northern [[Arkansas]]. At nearly 47,000 square miles (120,000 sq km), it is the largest highland region between the [[Appalachia|Appalachians]] and the [[Rocky Mountains|Rockies]]. Its highest point, Wahzhazhe Summit, rises to 2,561 feet (781 m). Together with the Ouachita Mountains it forms the U.S. Interior Highlands. The name derives from the French *aux Arcs*, an abbreviation of *aux Arkansas*, which referred to the trading post at Arkansas Post. Branson, Missouri, just north of the Arkansas border, is a [[Tourism|tourist]] destination where Ozark culture is popularized.

=== KEY: Ozone_486d0a
HEADWORD: Ozone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Lightning | Oxygen | Stratosphere | Ultraviolet
ENTRY:
Triatomic [[Oxygen|oxygen]] molecule (O₃), a pale-blue gas with a pungent odor. It is much less stable than dioxygen (O₂) and is formed from it by ultraviolet light and electrical discharges. Christian Friedrich Schönbein noticed the smell after [[Lightning|lightning]] and named the gas in 1839 from the Greek for "to smell." Most ozone lies in the [[Stratosphere|stratosphere]], where it absorbs most of the Sun's [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] radiation. At ground level, above about 0.1 ppm, it damages mucous and respiratory tissues. As a powerful oxidizing agent, ozone is used as a disinfectant and bleach, but concentrated gas and liquid can decompose explosively. Its O₃ structure was determined in 1865.
