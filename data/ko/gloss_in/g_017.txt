=== KEY: Blackcurrant_216d0b

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Drought | Frost | Vitamin C
ENTRY:
Deciduous shrub in the family Grossulariaceae, grown for its edible berries. *Ribes nigrum* is native to temperate parts of central and northern Europe and northern Asia, where it prefers damp, fertile soil. It is winter hardy, but cold weather at flowering can reduce the crop, and [[Frost|frost]] below −1.9 °C (28.6 °F) can damage flowers. The raw fruit is particularly rich in [[Vitamin C|vitamin C]] and polyphenols. Blackcurrants are used in jams, preserves and syrups and are grown commercially for juice, and the fruit also flavors alcoholic beverages and dyes. Cultivation in Europe is thought to have started in the last decades of the 17th century. Plants are intolerant of [[Drought|drought]], and an established bush can produce about 4.5 kilograms (10 pounds) of fruit each year.

=== KEY: Blackjack_c997a4
HEADWORD: Blackjack
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Miguel de Cervantes
ENTRY:
Casino banking card game, the most widely played such game in the world. It descends from a family of games known as twenty-one and uses decks of 52 cards. Players try to create totals higher than the dealer's without exceeding 21; number cards count at face value, face cards count as 10, and aces count as 1 or 11. A two-card total of 21 is called a 'blackjack' or 'natural.' The first written reference is in the work of [[Miguel de Cervantes|Miguel de Cervantes]], whose novella *Rinconete y Cortadillo* (written 1601–1602) describes card cheats playing the Spanish game veintiuno. Its immediate precursor was the English vingt-un, whose first rules appeared in Britain in 1800. The name 'blackjack' is linked to prospectors during the Klondike Gold Rush (1896–99), though French card historian Thierry Depaulis found no evidence for a bonus payout on an ace of spades and a black jack.

=== KEY: Bladder_48f562

ENTRY:
Hollow muscular organ that stores urine from the [[Kidney|kidneys]] of [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]]. Urine enters the bladder through the ureters and exits through the [[Urethra|urethra]]. In humans the bladder sits on the pelvic floor and holds 300 to 500 ml (10 to 17 fl oz) before the urge to urinate occurs, though it can hold considerably more. French mathematician, physicist, inventor, philosopher and Catholic writer. === KEY: Blame_a483e1

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Censure of a person or group for actions regarded as socially or morally irresponsible, the opposite of praise. Philosophers classify blame as one of the reactive attitudes, a term coined by P. F. Strawson that also includes praise, gratitude, resentment and [[Forgiveness|forgiveness]]. Psychologists note that blaming others can devalue them and leave the blamer feeling superior. Victims of manipulation and abuse often develop self-blame, which is either behavioral, based on actions, or characterological, based on character. === KEY: Blazar_c7c5d7
HEADWORD: Blazar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Active galactic nucleus | Gamma ray | Quasar | Supermassive black hole
ENTRY:
[[Active galactic nucleus|Active galactic nucleus]] with a relativistic jet directed very nearly toward an observer. The jet is composed of ionized matter moving at nearly the speed of light, and relativistic beaming makes the source appear much brighter than it would if the jet pointed away from Earth. Blazars are powerful sources across the electromagnetic spectrum and are observed as sources of high-energy [[Gamma ray|gamma-ray]] photons. They vary rapidly, with brightness fluctuations on timescales of hours to days, and some jets appear to show superluminal motion. Blazars are divided into BL Lac objects, which have weak or no emission lines, and flat-spectrum radio quasars, which show strong emission lines. The name was coined in 1978 by astronomer Edward Spiegel from *BL Lacertae* and [[Quasar|quasar]]. Like all active galactic nuclei, they are thought to be powered by material falling into a [[Supermassive black hole|supermassive black hole]].

=== KEY: Bleach_1bff57

ENTRY:
Chemical product used to remove color from fabric, fiber or food, or to disinfect. The term often refers specifically to a dilute solution of sodium hypochlorite, called liquid bleach. === KEY: Bleeding_6eaa38
HEADWORD: Bleeding
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood transfusion | Circulatory system
ENTRY:
Blood escaping from the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]] through damaged blood vessels. Bleeding, also called hemorrhage, can be internal or external, either through a natural opening such as the nose or mouth or through a puncture in the skin. Hypovolemia, a massive decrease in blood volume, can lead to death by exsanguination, but a healthy person can endure a loss of 10–15% of total blood volume without serious difficulty. Controlling bleeding, called hemostasis, is central to first aid and surgery. Causes include traumatic injuries such as lacerations, punctures and crush injuries, as well as medical conditions such as clotting disorders, cancers that erode vessels, and the use of anticoagulant medications. Direct pressure, elevation and tourniquets are used for external wounds, while internal bleeding often requires imaging, surgery or [[Blood transfusion|transfusion]].

=== KEY: Blizzard_ccf381
HEADWORD: Blizzard
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jet stream
ENTRY:
Severe snowstorm with strong sustained winds and low visibility, typically lasting at least three hours. In the United States, the National Weather Service defines a blizzard as a storm with sustained winds or frequent gusts of at least 56 km/h (35 mph) and blowing snow that reduces visibility to 400 m (0.25 mi) or less. Environment Canada uses a threshold of 40 km/h (25 mph), persisting for four hours, or six hours north of the arctic tree line. A ground blizzard involves snow already on the ground being blown by wind, with no precipitation falling at the time. The term was first recorded for weather in 1829 and became common in the press during the harsh winter of 1880–81. Blizzards usually form when the [[Jet stream|jet stream]] dips far to the south, allowing cold, dry polar air to clash with warm, humid air from the south.

=== KEY: Blood_747f99
HEADWORD: Blood
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hemoglobin | Immune system | Lung
ENTRY:
Specialized connective tissue and body fluid of the circulatory system in humans and other vertebrates, delivering nutrients and oxygen to cells and carrying metabolic waste away from them.

Blood is composed of blood cells suspended in plasma. Plasma makes up about 55% of blood, is straw-yellow, and is 92% water by volume, with proteins, glucose, mineral ions, and hormones making up the rest. By volume, red blood cells account for about 45% of whole blood and white cells about 0.7%. In humans, blood accounts for about 7% of body weight, with an average adult volume of roughly 5 liters (11 US pt).

The most abundant cells are red blood cells (erythrocytes), numbering 4.7 to 6.1 million per microliter in men and 4.2 to 5.4 million in women. They contain [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]], which binds oxygen reversibly and transports it. White blood cells (leukocytes), numbering 4,000–11,000 per microliter, are part of the [[Immune system|immune system]], destroying aberrant cells and attacking pathogens. Platelets (thrombocytes), numbering 200,000–500,000 per microliter, take part in clotting.

Blood is pumped through the vessels by the heart. Arterial blood carries oxygen from inhaled air to the tissues, and venous blood carries carbon dioxide to the [[Lung|lungs]] to be exhaled. Blood is bright red when its hemoglobin is oxygenated and dark red when deoxygenated. Its other functions include removing waste such as urea and lactic acid, detecting foreign material through antibodies, clotting after vessel injury, transporting hormones, and regulating core body temperature.

Human blood is classified into the ABO groups (A, B, AB, O) and Rh factor (positive or negative), which matter for safe transfusions.

=== KEY: Blood_pressure_398b09
HEADWORD: Blood pressure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circulatory system | Endocrine system | Hypertension | Kidney | Nervous system | Stroke
ENTRY:
Pressure exerted by circulating blood on the walls of blood vessels. Most of this pressure results from the heart pumping blood through the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]]. Unqualified, the term refers to pressure in a brachial artery, where it is most commonly measured. It is expressed as systolic pressure over diastolic pressure in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). A normal adult resting reading is under 120/80 mmHg, according to the American Heart Association and American College of Cardiology. Globally, the average has changed little since 1975, at approximately 127/79 mmHg in men and 122/77 mmHg in women. Baroreceptors regulate pressure in the short term by acting through the [[Nervous system|nervous system]] and [[Endocrine system|endocrine system]]. Consistently high pressure, called [[Hypertension|hypertension]], raises the risk of [[Stroke|stroke]], heart disease, and [[Kidney|kidney]] failure.

=== KEY: Blood_sugar_level_027886

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood plasma | Diabetes | Glucose | Glycogen | Hypoglycemia | Insulin | Liver | Pancreas
ENTRY:
Concentration of [[Glucose|glucose]] in the blood, also called glycemia. The body tightly regulates blood glucose as part of metabolic homeostasis. For a 70 kg (154 lb) human, approximately four grams of dissolved glucose is maintained in the [[Blood plasma|blood plasma]] at all times. Glucose not circulating is stored as [[Glycogen|glycogen]] in the [[Liver|liver]] and skeletal muscle. [[Insulin|Insulin]], a hormone produced in the [[Pancreas|pancreas]], primarily regulates the uptake of glucose into cells. The human brain consumes approximately 60% of blood glucose in fasting, sedentary individuals. Abnormally high levels, called hyperglycemia, characterize [[Diabetes|diabetes mellitus]], while low levels are called [[Hypoglycemia|hypoglycemia]].

Intravenous transfer of blood products into a human or other animal. The Bluetooth Special Interest Group, with more than 35,000 member companies, oversees the specification and manages qualification. As of 2021, 4.7 billion Bluetooth [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuit]] chips shipped annually.

=== KEY: Board_game_5ad19f
HEADWORD: Board game
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chess | Dice | Mancala | Mesopotamia | Monopoly (game) | Neolithic
ENTRY:
Tabletop game played with small pieces placed and moved in particular ways on a specially designed patterned board, sometimes with other components such as [[Dice|dice]].

Card games not using a standard deck, and games using neither cards nor a board, are often loosely included under the labels table and board games or tabletop games.

Board games have been found at archaeological sites in many cultures. As of 2024, 14 sites reported 51 game boards from the [[Neolithic]] period, dating from the early 7000s BC to the mid-6000s BC. Senet, from ancient Egypt, has been argued to be the oldest known board game, with possible board fragments c. 3100 BC; its rules were never written down. The Royal Game of Ur originated around 2500 BC in [[Mesopotamia]], and its rules survive on a cuneiform tablet written by a Babylonian astronomer in c. 177 BC–c. 176 BC. [[Mancala]] is a broad family of games of uncertain age found across Africa, the Middle East, and southern Asia.

The 1880s–1920s, called the Golden Age by American art historian Margaret Hofer, saw mass production make games cheaper and more available. [[Monopoly (game)|Monopoly]] (1935) was the most popular, with 500 million games played as of 1999. *The Settlers of Catan* (1995) is often credited with popularizing German-style games outside Europe, and a Board Game Renaissance in the early 21st century included *Carcassonne* (2000) and *Ticket to Ride* (2004).

Games vary in their balance of skill and luck: [[Chess|chess]] depends entirely on player skill, while children's games such as *Candy Land* (1949) and snakes and ladders are decided purely by luck.

=== KEY: Bohr_model_9293d6
HEADWORD: Bohr model
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Electron | Ernest Rutherford | J. J. Thomson | Niels Bohr | Solar System
ENTRY:
Obsolete atomic model incorporating early quantum concepts, developed by [[Niels Bohr]] from 1911 to 1918. Building on [[Ernest Rutherford]]'s discovery of the [[Atomic nucleus|atomic nucleus]], it places a small, dense nucleus surrounded by orbiting [[Electron|electrons]], an arrangement analogous to the [[Solar System]] but held together by electrostatic attraction rather than gravity. Electron energies are quantized, and the model explained the Rydberg formula for hydrogen's spectral lines. It replaced the plum pudding model of [[J. J. Thomson]], and the quantum atomic model superseded it in the 1920s. Because of its simplicity and correct results for selected systems, it is still taught to introduce students to quantum mechanics and energy level diagrams.

=== KEY: Boiling_359316
HEADWORD: Boiling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boiling point | Condensation | Evaporation | Gas | Liquid | Steaming | Virus
ENTRY:
Rapid phase transition from [[Liquid|liquid]] to [[Gas|gas]], the reverse of [[Condensation|condensation]]. Boiling occurs when a liquid is heated to its [[Boiling point|boiling point]], where its vapor pressure equals the pressure of the surrounding atmosphere. Boiling and [[Evaporation|evaporation]] are the two main forms of vaporization. In nucleate boiling, bubbles form at discrete points on the heated surface, while in critical heat flux boiling a film of vapor forms on the surface, sharply reducing heat transfer. Water boils at 100 °C (212 °F) at sea level but at lower temperatures at high altitudes. Holding water at 100 °C for one minute inactivates most microorganisms and [[Virus|viruses]], and boiling is also used in cooking methods such as blanching, [[Steaming|steaming]], and poaching.

=== KEY: Boiling_point_4c7772
HEADWORD: Boiling point
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bar (unit) | Liquid | Sea level
ENTRY:
Characteristic temperature of a substance, at which the vapor pressure of its [[Liquid|liquid]] equals the surrounding pressure. Lower pressure lowers the boiling point, so water boils at 100 °C at [[Sea level|sea level]] under standard pressure but at 93.4 °C at 1,905 m altitude. The normal boiling point is the temperature at which vapor pressure equals one atmosphere, and IUPAC has defined the standard boiling point since 1982 as boiling under a pressure of one [[Bar (unit)|bar]]. Unlike evaporation, which occurs at the surface below the boiling point, boiling forms vapor bubbles throughout the liquid.

=== KEY: Bomb_cd3abf

Explosive weapon using the exothermic reaction of an [[Explosive|explosive]] material to release energy suddenly and violently. Detonations cause damage through ground- and air-transmitted mechanical stress, the impact of pressure-driven projectiles and pressure effects. Bombs have been used since the 11th century, starting in [[East Asia]], where cast-iron shells packed with [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]] date to 13th-century China. In 1221 a Jurchen army of the [[Jin dynasty (1115–1234)|Jin dynasty]] used explosive bombs against a Chinese Song city. The word derives from Latin *bombus*, from Greek *bombos*, meaning "booming" and "buzzing".

Military combat aircraft for air-to-ground attack with [[Bomb|bombs]], torpedoes or air-launched cruise missiles. Bombers are classed as strategic or tactical. Strategic bombing by long-range heavy bombers aims to diminish an enemy's capacity to wage war by destroying infrastructure or reducing industrial output.
