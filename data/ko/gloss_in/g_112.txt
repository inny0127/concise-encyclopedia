=== KEY: Rainforest_7e4781
HEADWORD: Rainforest
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deforestation | Equator | Photosynthesis | Temperate rainforest | Tropical rainforest
ENTRY:
Forest characterized by a closed, continuous tree canopy, moisture-dependent vegetation, epiphytes and lianas, and the absence of wildfire. Rainforests are generally classified as [[Tropical rainforest|tropical]] or [[Temperate rainforest|temperate]], though other types have been described. Estimates vary from 40% to 75% of all biotic species being indigenous to rainforests. Tropical rainforests have been called the "jewels of the Earth" and the "world's largest pharmacy," because over one quarter of natural medicines have been discovered there. They are typically found within 10 degrees of the [[Equator|equator]] and receive average annual rainfall of no less than 168 cm (66 in). Although they have been called the "Earth's lungs," rainforests contribute little net oxygen to the atmosphere through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. Rainforests are disappearing rapidly because of [[Deforestation|deforestation]], habitat loss and pollution of the atmosphere.

Ninth month of the [[Islamic calendar]], observed by Muslims as a month of [[Fasting|fasting]]. Believers also devote the month to communal prayer, reflection, study of the [[Quran]] and charity. The Quran is believed to have been revealed to the prophet [[Muhammad]] during Ramadan, and the observance is one of the [[Five Pillars of Islam|five pillars of Islam]]. Spectroscopic technique typically used to determine vibrational modes of [[Molecule|molecules]]. Named after the physicist [[C. V. Raman]], it relies on inelastic scattering of photons, known as Raman scattering, usually from a laser. [[Pharaoh]] of [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]], second ruler of the Twentieth Dynasty. He is considered the last pharaoh of the New Kingdom to wield substantial power. === KEY: Ramesses_II_f23b70
HEADWORD: Ramesses II
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Cairo | Canaan | Hittites | Nubia | Ozymandias | Pharaoh | Valley of the Kings
ENTRY:
Third pharaoh of Egypt's Nineteenth Dynasty (r. 1279–1213 BC), known as Ramesses the Great. He is often regarded as the most powerful and celebrated [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]] of the New Kingdom. His 66-year reign was the longest recorded of any pharaoh, possibly alongside Pepi II.

Ramesses was not born a prince; his father, Seti I, made him prince regent at about age 14. He ascended the throne at 25 and first focused on building cities, temples, and monuments. He founded Pi-Ramesses in the Nile Delta, made it the new capital, and used it as the main staging point for campaigns in Syria. Greek sources called him [[Ozymandias]], from the first part of his regnal name, Usermaatre Setepenre.

He conducted no fewer than 15 military campaigns, all victories except the Battle of Kadesh against the [[Hittites]], generally considered a stalemate, though his inscriptions presented it as a triumph. He reasserted Egyptian control over [[Canaan]] and Phoenicia and led expeditions into [[Nubia]]. The Egyptian-Hittite peace treaty of 1258 BC is the earliest surviving international accord.

He celebrated thirteen or fourteen Sed festivals, more than any other pharaoh, and was one of the few worshipped as a deity during his lifetime. Estimates of his age at death vary, but 90 or 91 is considered most likely. He was buried in tomb KV7 in the [[Valley of the Kings]]; his body was later moved to the Royal Cache, where archaeologists discovered it in 1881. His mummy is displayed in the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization in [[Cairo]]. Scholars dispute his popular identification as the pharaoh of the Exodus.

ENTRY:
Measurable function modeling a quantity or object that depends on random events. Despite the name, the mathematical definition refers neither to randomness nor to variability, but to a [[Function (mathematics)|function]]. === KEY: Randomized_controlled_trial_2a80c1

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Form of [[Experiment|experiment]] that evaluates the efficacy or safety of an intervention by randomly allocating participants to comparison groups. One group receives the intervention under study, while others receive an alternative treatment, a [[Placebo|placebo]] or standard care. Randomized controlled trials are widely considered among the highest-quality sources of evidence in evidence-based medicine, because random allocation reduces selection bias and the influence of confounding factors, though they have been criticized for failing to reduce bias in some cases. A well-blinded trial is considered the gold standard for clinical trials. The first reported clinical trial, by James Lind in 1747, tested treatments for [[Scurvy]]. The modern form emerged with a 1948 British Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin for pulmonary [[Tuberculosis|tuberculosis]], which used sealed envelopes for random allocation.

Section of a river with a steep gradient, increased [[Velocity|velocity]] and [[Turbulence|turbulence]]. Rapids lie between a run, a smoothly flowing stretch of stream, and a cascade. The river bed becomes shallower, with some [[Rock (geology)|rocks]] exposed, and splashing water mixes in air bubbles, producing whitewater. Number that can be expressed as the quotient or [[Fraction|fraction]] p/q of two [[Integer|integers]], with a nonzero denominator q. Every integer is rational. The rationals are closed under [[Addition|addition]], [[Subtraction|subtraction]], [[Multiplication|multiplication]] and [[Division (mathematics)|division]] by a nonzero rational, and form a field under these operations. === KEY: Real_number_28dfb5
HEADWORD: Real number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculus | Imaginary number | Irrational number | Isomorphism | Pi | Rational number | Real analysis | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Number that can be used to measure a continuous one-dimensional quantity such as a length, duration, or temperature. Here "continuous" means that pairs of values can have arbitrarily small differences. Every real number can be almost uniquely represented by an infinite decimal expansion. The set of real numbers is usually written R in bold or ℝ. The real numbers are fundamental in [[Calculus|calculus]]. The adjective "real" was used in the 17th century by [[René Descartes]] to distinguish these numbers from [[Imaginary number|imaginary numbers]], such as the square roots of negative numbers.

The real numbers include the [[Rational number|rational numbers]], such as the integer −5 and the fraction 4/3. Those that are not rational are [[Irrational number|irrational]]. Real numbers that are roots of polynomials with rational coefficients are algebraic, a class containing all rationals and irrationals such as √2 = 1.414.... Others, such as [[Pi|π]] = 3.1415..., are transcendental. Real numbers can be pictured as the points of a line, the number line, on which the integers are equally spaced.

A suitable formal definition was a major achievement of 19th-century mathematics and is the foundation of [[Real analysis|real analysis]]. One modern axiomatic definition is that the real numbers form the unique (up to [[Isomorphism|isomorphism]]) Dedekind-complete ordered field. Other common definitions use equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences of rational numbers, Dedekind cuts, or infinite decimal representations; all are equivalent.

As an ordered field, the reals obey the rules of elementary arithmetic: addition and multiplication are commutative and associative, multiplication distributes over addition, and a total order is compatible with both operations. Completeness means that every nonempty set of reals bounded above has a least upper bound, a property the rationals lack.

=== KEY: Realism_arts_be576b
HEADWORD: Realism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Caravaggio | Gustave Courbet | Impressionism | Jan van Eyck | Leonardo da Vinci | Perspective (graphical) | Renaissance | Romanticism
ENTRY:
Attempt in the arts to represent subject matter truthfully, without artificiality, exaggeration, or speculative or supernatural elements. The term is often used interchangeably with naturalism, though the two are not necessarily synonymous. In Western visual art, naturalism seeks to depict objects with the least possible distortion and is tied to the development of linear [[Perspective (graphical)|perspective]] and illusionism in [[Renaissance]] Europe. It became especially marked in the 15th-century Early Netherlandish painting of Robert Campin and [[Jan van Eyck]].

Realism as a specific movement originated in France after the Revolution of 1848. Motivated by renewed interest in the commoner and the rise of leftist politics, painters such as [[Gustave Courbet]] took up the mundane, ugly, or sordid and rejected [[Romanticism]], which then dominated French art and literature. Courbet's large *Burial at Ornans* (1849–50) scandalized the Salon by treating a provincial funeral on the scale reserved for elevated themes. Realism departed from the idealization of academic art and paved the way for later movements such as [[Impressionism]].

"Naturalism" was erected in 19th-century Europe as a breakaway sub-movement that avoided politics and social issues and claimed a quasi-scientific basis. The critic Jules-Antoine Castagnary originated the term in 1863, and Émile Zola adopted it for the novel. Realism spread to literature and to other arts, including the opera style *verismo*.

Renaissance theorists debated the balance between observing nature and idealizing it: [[Leonardo da Vinci]] championed the pure study of nature, while Leon Battista Alberti stressed the typical and Michelangelo the most beautiful. In the 17th century, the followers of [[Caravaggio]] used "naturalist" as a self-description in opposition to the classical idealism of the Carracci.

=== KEY: Reality_bea223
HEADWORD: Reality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Existence | Latin | Mahatma Gandhi | Metaphysics | Santa Claus
ENTRY:
Totality of existence, or what all existing entities have in common. It is often contrasted with appearance, opposing what is with what seems to be, though the accuracy and fundamentality of this contrast are disputed. Proposed criteria for distinguishing real from unreal entities include causal powers, mind-independence, and being non-illusory. The concept overlaps with being, [[Existence|existence]], world, actuality, and truth, and is central to [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]].

Philosophers debate what belongs to reality and how it is organized. Proposed constituents include particulars and universals, concrete and abstract objects, facts, events, and elementary particles. Questions of structure concern parts and wholes, fundamental and non-fundamental entities.

Realism holds that reality does not depend on human knowledge or consciousness. Anti-realism holds that certain aspects are shaped by mental processes, subjective perspectives, or social conventions. The debate extends to universals, abstract objects, values, and the unobservable entities posited by scientific theories.

The causal criterion, discussed in ancient Greek philosophy as the Eleatic principle, states that to be real is to produce changes and be affected by them. On this view [[Mahatma Gandhi]] was real because he mobilized millions, whereas [[Santa Claus]] is unreal because he cannot act upon the world. The mind-independence criterion holds that real things, such as a planet or a virus, exist whatever people believe, while a fictional character like Gandalf depends on thought.

The word comes from the [[Latin]] *realitas*, from *res*, "thing"; its earliest known English use dates to the early 1500s. As a countable noun, "a reality" denotes a coherent framework, such as the social reality of a culture or the virtual reality of a video game.

=== KEY: Reason_fffcb4
HEADWORD: Reason
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Deductive reasoning | Inductive reasoning | Logic | Philosophy | Plato | Self-determination | Western philosophy
ENTRY:
Capacity to apply logic consciously by drawing valid conclusions from new or existing information, with the aim of seeking truth. Reason is associated with activities considered characteristic of humans, including [[Philosophy|philosophy]], religion, science, language, and mathematics, and is generally considered a distinguishing human ability. The term is sometimes used for rationality, which is more about the application of reason. The field of [[Logic|logic]] studies how formal reasoning produces valid arguments and true conclusions; reasoning may be subdivided into [[Deductive reasoning|deductive]], [[Inductive reasoning|inductive]], and abductive forms. [[Aristotle]] distinguished logical discursive reasoning (reason proper) from intuitive reasoning, which may tend toward the personal and subjectively opaque. In mathematics, intuition is often necessary for arriving at a formal proof.

The English word comes from French *raison*, derived from Latin *ratio*, which translated the Greek *logos*, also the root of "logic". The proposal that reason gives humanity a special position in nature has been argued to be a defining characteristic of [[Western philosophy]] and later Western science, beginning in classical Greece. [[Plato]] described reason as the natural monarch that should rule over the other parts of the soul, such as spiritedness and the passions. Reason is also closely identified with the ability to change goals, beliefs, traditions, and institutions, and so with freedom and [[Self-determination|self-determination]].

Philosophers distinguish theoretical reason, which seeks knowledge of the world, from practical reason, which deliberates on ends and means to guide action. Kant argued that pure reason, when it oversteps into metaphysics, yields antinomies. Psychologists and cognitive scientists study which cognitive and neural processes are engaged in reasoning and how culture affects the inferences people draw, while automated reasoning studies whether reasoning can be modeled computationally.

The Compromise of 1877 awarded the disputed 1876 election to [[Rutherford B. Hayes]] in exchange for removing federal troops from the South. Critics cite violence and corruption, while supporters credit it with restoring the Union.

=== KEY: Recorder_musical_instrument_3a037d
HEADWORD: Recorder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: George Frideric Handel | Johann Sebastian Bach | Musical instrument | Romantic music
ENTRY:
Family of woodwind [[Musical instrument|instruments]] and the most prominent duct flute in the Western classical tradition. Recorders have a thumb hole and seven finger holes, and are made in soprano, alto, tenor, and bass sizes. Traditionally made of wood or ivory, modern professional instruments are often boxwood, while student instruments are commonly molded plastic. The recorder is first documented in Europe in the Middle Ages and was popular in the Renaissance and Baroque periods, but was little used in the Classical and [[Romantic music|Romantic]] periods. It was revived in the 20th century by the historically informed performance movement and became a popular educational instrument. Composers who wrote for it include Monteverdi, Purcell, [[George Frideric Handel|Handel]], and [[Johann Sebastian Bach|Bach]]. Its clear, sweet sound has historically been associated with birds and shepherds. Its English name, used since the 14th century, has no equivalent in Middle French.

=== KEY: Recreation_b72cc5
HEADWORD: Recreation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Latin | Leisure | Play (activity) | Universal Declaration of Human Rights
ENTRY:
Activity of [[Leisure|leisure]] done for enjoyment, amusement, or pleasure, often regarded as "fun." The need for recreation is an essential element of human biology and psychology. The English word, first used in the late 14th century, derives from [[Latin]] *re* ("again") and *creare* ("to create"). Leisure, the time free from physical and social needs, is a prerequisite of recreation, and it is a human right under the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]. Recreation is difficult to separate from [[Play (activity)|play]], which is usually the term for children's activity, and from work, since playing an instrument may be a profession for one musician and a recreation for another. Recreation has health benefits, and therapeutic recreation specialists work in rehabilitation, psychiatric care, and the care of the elderly. Recreational physical activity helps reduce obesity and the risk of osteoporosis and some cancers.

=== KEY: Recreational_drug_use_48ee1f
HEADWORD: Recreational drug use
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcohol (drug) | Caffeine | Cannabis (drug) | Consciousness | European Union | Nicotine | Organized crime
ENTRY:
Use of psychoactive drugs to induce an altered state of [[Consciousness|consciousness]], usually for pleasure or another casual purpose. Recreational drugs are commonly divided into depressants, which slow the central nervous system; stimulants, which speed it up; and hallucinogens, which induce perceptual distortions. Common examples include [[Caffeine|caffeine]] in coffee, tea, and chocolate, [[Alcohol (drug)|alcohol]] in beer and wine, and [[Nicotine|nicotine]] in tobacco. Substances controlled by law vary by country but often include [[Cannabis (drug)|cannabis]], cocaine, opioids, and MDMA. As of 2015, about 5% of people aged 15 to 65 (158 million to 351 million) had used controlled drugs at least once. Drug use is generally tolerated socially, though addiction is stigmatized. Experts apply a biopsychosocial model, as no single cause has been identified. Since the early 2000s the [[European Union]] has pursued drug policies to prevent use and to counter [[Organized crime|organized crime]] and the illegal drug trade.

=== KEY: Recursion_12fa46
HEADWORD: Recursion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer science | Fibonacci sequence | Mathematics | Natural number
ENTRY:
Process in which a definition or procedure depends on a simpler or earlier version of itself. It is most common in [[Mathematics|mathematics]] and [[Computer science|computer science]], where a function is applied within its own definition. A recursive definition has a base case and a recursive step that reduces all cases toward it. The [[Fibonacci sequence]], with F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1, and F(n) = F(n − 1) + F(n − 2) for n > 1, is a classic example. The Peano axioms define the [[Natural number|natural numbers]] recursively. Recursive procedures must skip self-reference in some cases to avoid endless loops.

=== KEY: Recycling_61c440
HEADWORD: Recycling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air pollution | Greenhouse gas | Printed circuit board | Tire | Waste | Water pollution
ENTRY:
Process of converting [[Waste|waste]] materials into new materials and objects, often including the recovery of energy. As the third step of the reduce, reuse, and recycle waste hierarchy, recycling saves raw materials, lowers [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]] emissions, and avoids [[Air pollution|air pollution]] from incineration and [[Water pollution|water pollution]] from landfilling. Recyclable materials include glass, paper, cardboard, metal, plastic, [[Tire|tires]], textiles, batteries, and electronics, and composting food waste is also recycling. Metal cans can be remanufactured repeatedly without losing purity, while many other materials are turned into different products. Components of value or hazard, such as lead from car batteries, gold from [[Printed circuit board|printed circuit boards]], and mercury from thermometers, are salvaged from complex products. Paper recycling was first recorded in 1031, when Japanese shops sold repulped paper. Plastic recycling rates are low, around 9% globally, and critics argue that many programs are not economically viable without subsidies.
