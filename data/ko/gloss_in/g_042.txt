Founding emperor of the [[Sui dynasty]] of China (r. 581–604). Personal name Yang Jian. As a Buddhist he encouraged the spread of [[Buddhism]] through the state. Seventh emperor of the [[Han dynasty]] of China (r. 141–87 BC). Personal name Liu Che. === KEY: Empire_f1737b
HEADWORD: Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Empire | Byzantine Empire | Delian League
ENTRY:
Group of territories controlled by a monarch or supreme central authority, usually created by conquest. Within an empire, different populations may have different sets of rights and may be governed differently. Scholars typically distinguish empires from nation-states by the hierarchy of rights and prestige among their peoples. Territorial empires, such as the Macedonian and [[Byzantine Empire|Byzantine]] empires, extend outward from an original frontier. The word derives from the Roman concept of *imperium*, the right to command. Empires have taken many forms throughout history.

19th-century [[Constitutional monarchy|constitutional monarchy]] in South America (1822–1889), covering modern [[Brazil]] and, until 1828, [[Uruguay]]. ENTRY:
Japanese nation state (1868–1947), also known as Imperial Japan. It began with the [[Meiji Restoration]] on January 3, 1868, and under the slogan "Enrich the Country, Strengthen the Armed Forces" underwent large-scale industrialization and militarization. Economic turmoil in the 1920s, including the [[Great Depression]], fed militarism and nationalism, and Japan joined the [[Axis powers]] alongside Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. === KEY: Empiricism_39b708
HEADWORD: Empiricism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hume | George Berkeley | John Locke | Rationalism
ENTRY:
Epistemological view of knowledge as derived primarily from sensory experience and empirical evidence. It contrasts with [[Rationalism]], which holds that knowledge may be derived from reason independently of the senses, and with innatism, which holds that some ideas are present at birth. Empiricists argue that empirical evidence is more reliable than reasoning alone, because human reasoning is prone to cognitive biases and limitations. Historically, empiricism has been associated with the "blank slate" concept (*tabula rasa*), and its classical proponents include [[John Locke]], [[George Berkeley]] and [[David Hume]]. In the philosophy of science, empiricism underpins the scientific method: hypotheses and theories must be tested against observations of the natural world rather than resting solely on a priori reasoning, intuition or revelation.

=== KEY: Employment_a3a5ef
HEADWORD: Employment
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Relationship between an employer and an employee regulating paid labor services, usually based on a [[Contract|contract]]. Employees are paid [[Wage|wages]] by the hour, by piecework or by annual salary, depending on the type of work, sector conditions and the parties' bargaining power. === KEY: Empty_set_de99f0
HEADWORD: Empty set
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axiom | Cardinality | Intersection (set theory) | Subset | Union (set theory)
ENTRY:
Unique mathematical set containing no elements, with [[Cardinality|cardinality]] zero. Its existence is either an [[Axiom|axiom]] of some set theories or can be deduced in others, and by the principle of extensionality there is only one such set. The symbol ∅ was introduced by the Bourbaki group in 1939. The empty set is a [[Subset|subset]] of every set, the [[Union (set theory)|union]] of any set with it is that set, and its [[Intersection (set theory)|intersection]] with any set is empty. Because zero is the identity element for addition, the sum of the elements of the empty set is taken to be zero, and by the same logic its product is one. Some texts call it the "null set", a term that in measure theory denotes a set of measure zero that need not be empty.

=== KEY: Emu_631732
HEADWORD: Emu
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australia | Common ostrich
ENTRY:
Large flightless bird (*Dromaius novaehollandiae*) endemic to [[Australia]]. It is the tallest native bird there and the only living member of its genus, and it is the third-tallest living bird after the [[Common ostrich|common ostrich]] and the Somali ostrich. === KEY: Emulsion_eb670a
HEADWORD: Emulsion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Colloid | Gelatin | Milk | Mixture | Photographic film
ENTRY:
[[Mixture|Mixture]] of two or more normally immiscible liquids, one dispersed as droplets in the other. Emulsions are a type of [[Colloid|colloid]], and the term narrowly refers to systems in which both phases are liquids. Oil and water can form an oil-in-water emulsion, in which oil is dispersed in water, or a water-in-oil emulsion; examples include vinaigrettes, homogenized [[Milk|milk]] and some cutting fluids. === KEY: Enceladus_642979
HEADWORD: Enceladus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Methane | Saturn | Sodium chloride | Solar System | Voyager 1 | Voyager 2
ENTRY:
Icy moon of [[Saturn]], the sixth-largest moon of its planet and about 500 km in diameter. Its surface is covered by fresh snow, making it one of the most reflective bodies in the [[Solar System]]; noon temperatures reach only −198 °C. William Herschel discovered it on August 28, 1789. Little was learned until [[Voyager 1]] and [[Voyager 2]] flew past in 1980 and 1981, and the Cassini spacecraft began close flybys in 2005. Cassini found water-rich plumes erupting from the south polar region, with more than 100 geysers identified. The plumes supply most of Saturn's E ring and carry [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]] crystals and ice particles. In 2014, NASA reported evidence for a large south polar subsurface ocean of liquid water, about 10 km thick. Research suggests that its hydrothermal environment could be habitable to microorganisms similar to some on Earth, and that [[Methane|methane]] in the plumes could be produced by such organisms.

=== KEY: Encephalitis_e9a998
HEADWORD: Encephalitis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aphasia | Autoimmune disease | Herpes | Medical imaging | Rabies | Vaccine
ENTRY:
Inflammation of the brain, with symptoms including reduced or altered consciousness, [[Aphasia|aphasia]], headache, fever, confusion and a stiff neck. Complications may include seizures, hallucinations, memory problems and hearing difficulties. Causes include viruses such as [[Herpes|herpes simplex]] and [[Rabies]], bacteria, fungi and parasites, as well as [[Autoimmune disease|autoimmune diseases]] and certain medications. === KEY: Enclosure_e7113d
HEADWORD: Enclosure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black Death | Essex | Inflation | Kent | Yorkshire
ENTRY:
In England, appropriation of common land, ending commoners' traditional rights of access and use. Enclosure took three forms: owners creating closes from common fields, groups of proprietors (usually small farmers or squires) enclosing whole parishes, and enclosure by acts of Parliament. Its stated justification was to improve agricultural efficiency, though there were other motives, such as raising the value of the land. Historians regard enclosure riots as "the pre-eminent form" of social protest from the 1530s to 1640s. After the [[Black Death]] in the middle of the 14th century, population fell and surviving laborers' wages rose, translating into [[Inflation|inflation]]. Some historians see the resulting labor shortage as contributing to the abandonment of land and the decline of the feudal system, while others argue that the plague only sped up an existing process. Parts of south-east England, notably sections of [[Essex]] and [[Kent]], retained small enclosed fields, while the open-field system lay in a broad band from [[Yorkshire]] and Lincolnshire diagonally across England.

=== KEY: Encryption_d7f261
HEADWORD: Encryption
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Kindi | Caesar cipher | Enigma machine | Internet | Thomas Jefferson | Transport Layer Security | World War II
ENTRY:
Cryptographic process converting plaintext into ciphertext, decodable only by authorized parties. Encryption does not itself prevent interference but denies intelligible content to an interceptor. Modern schemes usually rely on a pseudo-random key generated by an algorithm; with a well-designed scheme, decryption without the key should be computationally infeasible. Early forms were used in military messaging, including the [[Caesar cipher|Caesar cipher]], which shifted each letter a fixed number of positions. By c. 800, the Arab mathematician [[Al-Kindi|al-Kindi]] had developed frequency analysis to break such ciphers. Around c. 1790, [[Thomas Jefferson]] theorized a wheel cipher for military correspondence that was never built. In [[World War II]], Germany used the [[Enigma machine]] for military communications. Modern systems use public-key and symmetric-key methods, such as [[Transport Layer Security]], which protects communication over the [[Internet]].

General-knowledge English-language encyclopedia, first published in Edinburgh in 1768–71. Its second edition ran to 10 volumes, and by the fourth edition (1801–1810) it had grown to 20. Its size, measured in total word count, has remained roughly steady at about 40 million words since the early 20th century. General encyclopedia published in France from 1751 to 1772, central to the Enlightenment. Edited by [[Denis Diderot]] and, until 1759, by [[Jean Le Rond d'Alembert]], it drew on many contributors known as the *Encyclopédistes*. Reference work summarizing knowledge, general or specialized, usually arranged in articles. === KEY: Endangered_language_9f9068
HEADWORD: Endangered language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dialect | Globalization | Language death
ENTRY:
Language at risk of disappearing through loss or shift of its speakers. A language with no fluent speakers left is considered extinct, though a [[Language death|dead]] language may still be studied through recordings or writings. Language shift usually occurs when speakers adopt a language of greater social, economic or wider use, a process accelerated by [[Globalization|globalization]], mass migration, linguistic imperialism and neocolonialism. Between 6,000 and 7,000 languages are spoken today, and some linguists estimate that 50–90% of them will be severely endangered or dead by 2100. Most languages are spoken by fewer than 10,000 people. Revitalization projects, education and literacy programs in minority languages, and documentation of little-known languages aim to slow the loss. Regional [[Dialect|dialects]] of widely spoken languages, such as varieties of English on the American east coast, are also becoming endangered.

=== KEY: Endangered_species_249589

ENTRY:
Species with a very high risk of extinction in the near future, worldwide or in a particular region. Species usually become endangered through [[Habitat|habitat]] loss and loss of genetic variation. Habitat is lost through agricultural, housing and industrial development, and through [[Climate change|climate change]] and [[Invasive species|invasive species]]. === KEY: Endocrine_system_86d042

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circulatory system | Nervous system | Pancreas | Pituitary gland | Testosterone | Thyroid
ENTRY:
Hormone-producing glands releasing their products directly into the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]]. The endocrine system is a messenger system of hormone feedback loops that regulate distant organs. In humans the major endocrine glands are the [[Thyroid|thyroid]], parathyroid, [[Pituitary gland|pituitary]], pineal and adrenal glands, and the testes or ovaries; the hypothalamus, [[Pancreas|pancreas]] and thymus also have endocrine functions. === KEY: Endoplasmic_reticulum_84dbd7
HEADWORD: Endoplasmic reticulum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Liver | Protein | Ribosome | Sperm | Steroid
ENTRY:
Membrane-enclosed organelle of eukaryotic cells, forming a network of sacs and tubules that processes [[Protein|proteins]]. The rough ER (RER) is studded with [[Ribosome|ribosomes]], the sites of protein synthesis, and is especially prominent in [[Liver|liver]] cells. The smooth ER (SER) lacks ribosomes and carries out lipid synthesis, production of [[Steroid|steroid]] hormones and detoxification. Charles Garnier observed it by light microscopy in 1897, coining the term ergastoplasm, and its lacy membranes were first seen by electron microscopy in 1945.

Closed drainage basin retaining its water, with no outflow to rivers or oceans. === KEY: Endoscopy_f9bc40
HEADWORD: Endoscopy

ENTRY:
Medical procedure for examining the interior of a hollow organ or body cavity with an endoscope. Unlike many other [[Medical imaging|medical imaging]] techniques, endoscopes are inserted directly into the organ. The procedure may be performed by a doctor or a surgeon, and the patient may be fully conscious or anesthetized. Most often the term refers to examination of the upper [[Gastrointestinal tract|gastrointestinal tract]], known as esophagogastroduodenoscopy. === KEY: Energy_5cc28f
HEADWORD: Energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Aristotle | Conservation of energy | James Prescott Joule | Joule | Kinetic energy | Noether's theorem | Potential energy
ENTRY:
Quantitative property transferred to a body or physical system, recognizable in the capacity to do work and in the form of heat and light. The word derives from the Ancient Greek *enérgeia*, 'activity'. Energy is a conserved quantity: the law of [[Conservation of energy|conservation of energy]] states that the total energy of an isolated system remains constant, although it can change form. The SI unit is the [[Joule|joule]] (J).

Forms include [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] of moving objects, [[Potential energy|potential energy]] stored by position in a field, elastic energy in solids, chemical energy associated with reactions, radiant energy carried by electromagnetic radiation, internal energy within a thermodynamic system, and rest energy associated with rest mass. These categories are not mutually exclusive. Kinetic and potential energy together suffice to describe all forms, and particular combinations are named separately, as when their sum within a system is called mechanical energy. All living organisms take in and release energy, and Earth's climate and ecosystem processes are driven primarily by radiant energy from the Sun.

The concept developed over centuries. [[Aristotle]] used *energeia* in the 4th century BC as a qualitative notion broad enough to include happiness and pleasure. In the late 17th century Gottfried Leibniz proposed *vis viva*, the product of mass and velocity squared, and believed it conserved; kinetic energy differs from it only by a factor of two. In the early 18th century Émilie du Châtelet proposed conservation of energy in the marginalia of her French translation of Newton's *Principia Mathematica*. In 1807 Thomas Young was possibly the first to use "energy" in its modern sense; Gustave-Gaspard Coriolis described kinetic energy in 1829, and William Rankine coined "potential energy" in 1853. In 1845 [[James Prescott Joule]] discovered the link between mechanical work and heat, and the theory of conservation of energy was formalized largely by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) as thermodynamics. Rudolf Clausius gave entropy its mathematical formulation. According to [[Noether's theorem]], conservation of energy follows from the fact that the laws of physics do not change over time. [[Albert Einstein]]'s 1905 theory of special relativity showed that rest mass corresponds to an equivalent amount of rest energy, so that conservation of energy and conservation of mass are one law. Niels Bohr proposed quantized energy levels in 1913, and Erwin Schrödinger and Werner Heisenberg advanced the modern quantum theory of them in 1926.

Work is force times distance, and the joule is the energy expended in applying a force of one newton through a distance of one meter. Power, energy per unit time, is measured in watts, one joule per second. A kilowatt-hour equals 3.6 million joules. Other units include the erg, the foot-pound, the electronvolt, the food calorie, the kilocalorie, and the BTU. Work and energy depend on the frame of reference.

=== KEY: Engine_7bfa30
HEADWORD: Engine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric motor | Industrial Revolution | Internal combustion engine | Jet engine | Steam engine
ENTRY:
Machine designed to convert one or more forms of energy into mechanical energy. Sources include potential energy (as in hydroelectric power), heat, chemical energy, electric potential, and nuclear energy. Many of these processes produce heat as an intermediate form, so heat engines have special importance. Mechanical energy is central to transportation and to industrial processes such as cutting, grinding, crushing, and mixing.

Heat engines convert heat into work through thermodynamic processes. The [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engine]] is perhaps the most common: combustion of a fuel pressurizes gases in the chamber, which expand and drive a piston that turns a crankshaft. A reaction engine such as a [[Jet engine|jet engine]] instead produces thrust by expelling reaction mass, in accordance with Newton's third law. [[Electric motor|Electric motors]] convert electrical energy into motion, pneumatic motors use compressed air, and clockwork motors use elastic energy. In living systems, molecular motors such as myosins in muscles use chemical energy. Airbreathing engines use atmospheric gas in the fuel reaction; rockets and deeply submerged submarines must carry an oxidizer or obtain heat by nuclear means.

All chemically fueled heat engines emit exhaust gases. Only engines burning pure hydrogen with pure oxygen are strictly zero-emission, in practice some rocket engines. Burning a hydrocarbon emits CO₂, a greenhouse gas.

The word derives from Old French *engin*, from Latin *ingenium*. Siege weapons such as catapults and battering rams were called siege engines, and "gin", as in cotton gin, is short for engine. Most devices of the [[Industrial Revolution]] were called engines, though the original [[Steam engine|steam engines]], such as Thomas Savery's, were pumps. "Motor" was first used to distinguish the internal combustion engine from the steam engine; in standard English the terms are interchangeable.

=== KEY: Engineering_ab0268
HEADWORD: Engineering
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antikythera mechanism | Archimedes | Chemical engineering | Civil engineering | Electrical engineering | Imhotep | Industrial Revolution | Mechanical engineering
ENTRY:
Practice of systematically applying natural science and mathematics to design and improve systems, devices, or processes that solve problems under constraints. It is typically motivated by human needs and has produced bridges, engines, smartphones, pacemakers, the internet, spacecraft, and washing machines. Engineering balances competing demands such as safety, performance, aesthetics, cost, and time, within fundamental limits such as the laws of physics.

The traditional disciplines are [[Civil engineering|civil]], [[Mechanical engineering|mechanical]], [[Electrical engineering|electrical]], and [[Chemical engineering|chemical]], and the academic field encompasses many more specialized subfields. Modern practice follows the engineering design process, with testing and simulation used before production, and computer software now assists much of the product lifecycle from design to manufacturing. When a deployed product fails, forensic engineering is used to determine what went wrong. Large projects are normally funded by companies, investors, or governments, so engineers also need knowledge of economics and business practice. Engineers typically follow a code of ethics that favors honesty and integrity and is dedicated to public safety and welfare. A definition by the American Engineers' Council for Professional Development, predecessor of ABET, describes it as the creative application of scientific principles to design or develop structures, machines, apparatus, or manufacturing processes, with regard to function, economics, and safety to life and property.

The word derives from *engineer*, which dates to the 14th century, when it meant a builder or operator of a siege engine, a "constructor of military engines". Military engineering corps, such as the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, preserve that usage. The term civil engineering arose to distinguish the design of non-military works such as bridges and buildings.

Engineering has existed since ancient times, beginning with the six classic simple machines. The wedge and inclined plane were known in prehistoric times. The wheel and axle was invented in Mesopotamia during the 5th millennium BC, and the lever appeared in the Near East around 5,000 years ago. The shadoof, the first crane, appeared in Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC. Pulleys date to the early 2nd millennium BC, and the screw, the last simple machine invented, to the Neo-Assyrian period (911–609 BC). The Egyptian pyramids were built using the inclined plane, wedge, and lever. The earliest civil engineer known by name is [[Imhotep]], an official of the pharaoh Djoser, who probably designed the Step Pyramid at Saqqara around 2630–2611 BC. Water wheels and watermills appeared in the Persian Empire by the early 4th century BC. Greek engineering included the [[Antikythera mechanism]], an early mechanical analog computer, and the inventions of [[Archimedes]], and the Greeks developed artillery around the 4th century BC. In Europe, the scientific and [[Industrial Revolution|industrial revolutions]] made engineering a scientific profession, and the steam engine, far more powerful than animals, brought mechanical propulsion to ships and railways. Later advances extended engineering to electrical, chemical, and aerospace needs and to new materials.

=== KEY: English_language_3e1aed

West Germanic language of the Indo-European family that emerged in early medieval England and has become a global [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]]. It is named after the Angles, one of the [[Germanic peoples]] who migrated to Britain after the end of Roman rule.

=== KEY: Enthalpy_1910d3
HEADWORD: Enthalpy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bar (unit) | Calorie | Heat | Internal energy | Joule
ENTRY:
Thermodynamic quantity equal to the sum of a system's [[Internal energy|internal energy]] and the product of its pressure and volume. Enthalpy is a state function, depending only on the final configuration of internal energy, pressure and volume rather than on the path taken. A change in enthalpy is the preferred expression for measurements at constant pressure, which Earth's atmosphere conveniently provides. When no matter is transferred and no work is done, the enthalpy change at constant pressure equals the energy exchanged as [[Heat|heat]]. The SI unit is the [[Joule|joule]]; the [[Calorie|calorie]] and the British thermal unit are historical units. The standard enthalpy of reaction is the enthalpy change when reactants in their standard states change to products in their standard states, usually at 1 [[Bar (unit)|bar]] and 298 K. Enthalpy change is positive for endothermic processes and negative for exothermic ones. The word derives from the Greek *enthalpein*, meaning to heat.

=== KEY: Entropy_15fc39
HEADWORD: Entropy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boltzmann constant | Cosmology | Economics | Heat | Information theory | Ludwig Boltzmann | Second law of thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Thermodynamic state variable that quantifies the distribution of accessible microstates in a system. Entropy is central to the [[Second law of thermodynamics|second law of thermodynamics]], which states that the entropy of an isolated system left to spontaneous evolution cannot decrease, so isolated systems evolve toward equilibrium, where entropy is highest. Rudolf Clausius defined it in 1865 as the quotient of an infinitesimal amount of [[Heat|heat]] to the instantaneous temperature. [[Ludwig Boltzmann|Ludwig Boltzmann]] explained entropy as a measure of the number of possible microscopic arrangements of atoms and molecules that comply with a system's macroscopic condition, in a simple logarithmic law with the [[Boltzmann constant|Boltzmann constant]]. The concept has applications in [[Cosmology|cosmology]], [[Economics|economics]] and [[Information theory|information theory]].

=== KEY: Epistemology_b4d386
HEADWORD: Epistemology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Belief | Empiricism | Philosophy | Plato | Rationalism | Truth
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Philosophy|philosophy]] that examines the nature, origin, and limits of knowledge. Also called the theory of knowledge, it explores propositional knowledge about facts, practical knowledge in the form of skills, and knowledge by acquaintance. The word, from Greek *episteme* (knowledge) and *logos* (study), was coined only in the 19th century.

Epistemologists study [[Belief|belief]], [[Truth|truth]], and justification, and investigate sources of justification such as perception, introspection, memory, reason, and testimony. Epistemology is normative, concerned with standards for evaluating beliefs, whereas psychology describes the beliefs people actually hold. Skepticism questions the human ability to attain knowledge, and fallibilism holds that knowledge is never certain. [[Empiricism|Empiricists]] hold that all knowledge comes from sense experience, while [[Rationalism|rationalists]] believe some knowledge does not depend on it. Coherentists argue that a belief is justified if consistent with other beliefs; foundationalists maintain that basic beliefs are justified independently of other beliefs. Internalists and externalists debate whether justification depends solely on mental states.

Separate branches address scientific, mathematical, moral, and religious knowledge; naturalized, formal, social, and historical epistemology take distinct approaches.

Reflection on knowledge appears in ancient Greek, Indian, and Chinese philosophy, and [[Plato]] explored it in the *Theaetetus*. Reason and faith were central in the medieval period, and the modern era was marked by empiricism and rationalism. A long-standing analysis defined knowledge as justified true belief. Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper presented counterexamples, now called Gettier cases, where a justified true belief fails to be knowledge because of luck.

=== KEY: Equality_mathematics_abb647

=== KEY: Equation_aa8a10
HEADWORD: Equation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circle | Gaussian elimination | Polynomial | Quadratic equation | Variable (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Mathematical formula expressing the equality of two expressions by connecting them with the equals sign (=). In English, any well-formed formula of two expressions related by an equals sign counts as an equation.

Solving an equation means determining which values of the variables, called unknowns, make the equality true; those values are its solutions. An identity is true for all values of the [[Variable (mathematics)|variables]], and a conditional equation only for particular values. The most common type is the [[Polynomial|polynomial]] (algebraic) equation, as in Ax² + Bx + C − y = 0. Terms other than the unknowns are called constants, coefficients, or parameters. Unknowns are usually denoted by letters at the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) and parameters by letters at the beginning (a, b, c), as in the general [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equation]] ax² + bx + c = 0. The equation x² + y² = R² is, for R = 2, that of a [[Circle|circle]] of radius 2 about the origin.

An equation is analogous to a balanced weighing scale: whatever is done to one side must be done to the other. Two equations are equivalent if they have the same solutions. Adding or subtracting the same quantity on both sides, and multiplying or dividing both sides by a nonzero quantity, produce equivalent equations. Applying a function to both sides keeps the original solutions but may add extraneous ones: squaring x = 1 gives x² = 1, which introduces x = −1. Such transformations underlie most elementary solving methods and some less elementary ones, such as [[Gaussian elimination]].

The "=" symbol was invented in 1557 by Robert Recorde, who considered that nothing could be more equal than parallel straight lines of the same length.

=== KEY: Equation_solving_839cb4
HEADWORD: Equation solving
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Newton's method | Quadratic equation
ENTRY:
Process of finding the values of unknown variables that make an equation true. A solution is an assignment of values to the unknowns that makes the equality hold, and the set of all solutions is the solution set. An equation may be solved numerically, admitting only numbers, or symbolically, using expressions. For example, x + y = 2x − 1 is solved for x by x = y + 1. Depending on the context, solving may mean finding any solution, all solutions, or one satisfying a further condition. Babylonians used geometric techniques for [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equations]] as early as 2000 BC. In 1824 Niels Henrik Abel proved that no general algebraic formula exists for quintic equations using radicals. Higher-degree equations are often approximated with methods such as [[Newton's method|Newton's method]].

=== KEY: Equations_of_motion_615409
HEADWORD: Equations of motion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Differential equation | Euclidean space
ENTRY:
Set of equations describing the motion of a physical system as a function of time. Its variables are usually spatial coordinates and time, and in classical mechanics the functions are defined in [[Euclidean space|Euclidean space]], while in relativity they are defined in curved spaces. If the dynamics of a system is known, its equations of motion are the solutions of the [[Differential equation|differential equations]] describing that dynamics. For constant acceleration, kinematic equations relate final velocity to initial velocity, acceleration and time: v = v₀ + at. === KEY: Equinox_e130d9
HEADWORD: Equinox
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Equator | Latin | Leap year | Refraction | Solstice | Sun
ENTRY:
Semi-annual astronomical moment with the [[Sun]] directly above the [[Equator|equator]].
Equinoxes occur twice a year, around March 20 and September 23. An equinox is the moment when the plane of Earth's equator passes through the geometric center of the Sun's disk and Earth's rotation axis is perpendicular to the Sun-Earth line. The name comes from [[Latin]] *aequinoctium*, from *aequa* (equal) and *nox* (night). Day and night are nearly but not exactly equal in length, because of the Sun's angular size, atmospheric [[Refraction|refraction]] and changing day length. Equinoxes result from Earth's [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]] of 23.5 degrees. === KEY: Equisetum_0719bc

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Genus of vascular plants known as horsetails.
Equisetum is the only living genus in the family Equisetaceae, whose members reproduce by [[Spore|spores]] rather than seeds. The oldest remains of modern species appear in the Early Jurassic. The plants have hollow, jointed, ridged stems, and their leaves are reduced to sheaths at the nodes. Most temperate species die back in winter, while most tropical species are evergreen. ENTRY:
Set of legal principles supplementing, but distinct from, the [[Common law|common law]].
Equity developed in the English Court of Chancery, which created equitable doctrines distinct from the rest of English common law. These covered much of English private law, including [[Property|property]], [[Contract|contract]], [[Tort|tort]] and restitution. They produced equitable principles such as good faith, interests such as those under [[Trust (law)|trusts]], and remedies such as [[Injunction|injunctions]]. Medieval Lord Chancellors gave relief where the rigid rules of the common law courts seemed inequitable or unconscionable. The Judicature Acts of 1873–1875 abolished the split court system in England while retaining equity as a distinct but complementary set of rules. The tradition of flexible adjudication traces to [[Aristotle]]'s concept of *epieikeia* and to Roman *aequitas*. In [[Civil law (legal system)|civil law]] systems equity was integrated into legal rules, but in common law systems it became an independent body of law.

=== KEY: Equivalence_principle_06d13c
HEADWORD: Equivalence principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Galileo Galilei | General relativity | Isaac Newton | Mass | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Hypothesis that inertial and gravitational [[Mass|mass]] are equivalent.
The weak form, known for centuries, holds that masses of any composition in free fall follow the same trajectories and land at identical times. [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] found that gravitation is independent of the amount of mass accelerated. [[Isaac Newton|Newton]] compared the periods of pendulums of different materials, found them identical, and inferred that gravitational and inertial mass are the same. In 1907 [[Albert Einstein|Einstein]] introduced an extended form requiring that [[Special relativity|special relativity]] also hold in free fall and that the weak form be valid everywhere. This form was a critical input for [[General relativity|general relativity]]. The strong form extends the principle to stellar objects. In 1911 Einstein used it to predict that clocks run at different rates in a gravitational potential and that light bends in a gravitational field. The MICROSCOPE satellite mission (2016–2018) tested for composition-dependent violations of the weak form to 10⁻¹⁵ precision.

=== KEY: Equivalence_relation_7e6883
HEADWORD: Equivalence relation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equality (mathematics) | Integer | Triangle
ENTRY:
Mathematical relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
An equivalence relation generalizes [[Equality (mathematics)|equality]]. It partitions a set into disjoint equivalence classes, and two elements are equivalent if and only if they belong to the same class. Reflexivity means every element is equivalent to itself. Symmetry means that if *a* is equivalent to *b*, then *b* is equivalent to *a*. Transitivity means that if *a* is equivalent to *b* and *b* to *c*, then *a* is equivalent to *c*. Examples include numerical equality, similarity and congruence of [[Triangle|triangles]], and congruence modulo *n* on the [[Integer|integers]]. The relation ≥ is reflexive and transitive but not symmetric. "Approximately equal to" is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive, so it is not an equivalence relation.

=== KEY: Erasmus_3b744b
HEADWORD: Erasmus
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Humanism | John Calvin | Martin Luther | New Testament | Reformation | Renaissance | Rotterdam
ENTRY:
Dutch [[Humanism|humanist]], theologian and philosopher.
Known as Erasmus of [[Rotterdam]], he was one of the most influential scholars of the Northern [[Renaissance]]. As a Catholic priest he applied humanist methods to texts and prepared new Latin and Greek editions of the [[New Testament]] and of the Church Fathers. His annotations influenced both the Protestant [[Reformation|Reformation]] and the Catholic Reformation. His works include *The Praise of Folly* (1511) and *On Free Will* (1524), which was written against [[Martin Luther]]'s views on predestination. He remained in the [[Catholic Church]] and sought reform from within. He promoted synergism, a traditional doctrine that [[John Calvin|Calvin]] and Luther rejected in favor of monergism, and his middle position angered partisans on both sides.

=== KEY: Eratosthenes_eefd34
HEADWORD: Eratosthenes
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Athens | Axial tilt | Chronology | Geography | Library of Alexandria | Prime number | Sieve of Eratosthenes | Troy
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek polymath, geographer and chief librarian of the [[Library of Alexandria]] from around 240 BC.
Born in Cyrene and educated in [[Athens]], he is best remembered for the first known calculation of Earth's circumference. He observed that the Sun stood directly overhead at Syene on the summer solstice, while a shadow at Alexandria formed a 7.2-degree angle, and he reasoned that the circumference was about 250,000 stadia. He also calculated Earth's [[Axial tilt|axial tilt]] and coined the terms [[Geography|geography]] and geographer. He founded scientific [[Chronology|chronology]], dating the sack of [[Troy]] to 1184 BC. In mathematics he devised the [[Sieve of Eratosthenes]] for identifying [[Prime number|prime numbers]]. Devotees nicknamed him *Pentathlos*, but according to the 10th-century encyclopedia *Suda*, critics called him *Beta*, or second, because he always came second.

=== KEY: Erbil_85bdcf
HEADWORD: Erbil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Assyria | Iraq | Kurdistan Region | Mesopotamia | Ottoman Empire | Ur | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
Capital and most populated city of the [[Kurdistan Region]] in [[Iraq]].
Erbil is the region's cultural, economic, industrial and medical hub, with a population exceeding 1.5 million including surrounding districts. It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, with settlement possibly dating to the 5th millennium BC. Its ancient Citadel, on a mound, was inscribed as a [[World Heritage Site]] in July 2014. The earliest historical reference, from the Third Dynasty of [[Ur]], records King Shulgi mentioning a city called Urbilum. The city came under [[Assyria|Assyrian]] control in the late 2nd millennium BC and was later part of successive empires, including the [[Achaemenid Empire]], Seleucid, Parthian and Sasanian. In the Middle Ages it was ruled by the Seljuk and [[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] empires. Its archaeological museum holds a large collection of pre-Islamic art, particularly from [[Mesopotamia]].

=== KEY: Erbium_7c32b8
HEADWORD: Erbium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic number | Chemical element | Dentistry | Dermatology | Lanthanide | Laser | Optical fiber | Rare-earth element
ENTRY:
[[Lanthanide]] [[Chemical element|chemical element]] with symbol Er and [[Atomic number|atomic number]] 68.
When isolated, erbium is a silvery-white solid metal, but in nature it is always combined with other elements. Carl Gustav Mosander discovered it in 1843 in yttrium from the Ytterby mine near Stockholm, Sweden, which gave the element its name. It is a [[Rare-earth element|rare-earth element]]. Its principal uses depend on the optical fluorescence of its pink Er³⁺ ions. Erbium-doped glass and crystals amplify signals in [[Optical fiber|optical fiber]] networks, where the 1550 nm wavelength is especially important because standard single-mode fibers have minimal loss there. In medicine, the ion's 2940 nm emission is strongly absorbed by water, making erbium [[Laser|lasers]] useful in [[Dermatology|dermatology]] and [[Dentistry|dentistry]] because their effect is superficial.

=== KEY: Erectile_dysfunction_bf0b5b
HEADWORD: Erectile dysfunction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cardiovascular disease | Depression (mood) | Diabetes | Obesity | Sildenafil
ENTRY:
Male sexual dysfunction involving the recurring inability to achieve or maintain an erection.
Also called impotence, it is the most common sexual problem in males and can cause psychological distress. Notable predictors include aging, [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular disease]], [[Diabetes|diabetes]], high blood pressure, [[Obesity|obesity]], smoking and [[Depression (mood)|depression]]. It is reported in 18% of males aged 50 to 59 years and 37% of males aged 70 to 75. Treatment often uses PDE5 inhibitors such as [[Sildenafil]], which dilate blood vessels to increase blood flow into the penis.
