PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer science | Function (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Mathematical notation that describes the approximate size of a [[Function (mathematics)|function]] on a domain. The O stands for *Ordnung*, meaning order of approximation. Paul Bachmann proposed the notation in 1894, and Edmund Landau extended it in 1909; it is part of the wider Bachmann–Landau family of notations. Big O gives an upper bound on how a function grows as its variable becomes large. In [[Computer science|computer science]] it classifies algorithms by how their run time or space requirements grow with input size.

=== KEY: Bilateria_7e3a72
HEADWORD: Bilateria
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anus | Cambrian | Clade | Cnidaria | Echinoderm | Ediacaran | Mouth | Sponge
ENTRY:
Large [[Clade|clade]] of animals characterized by bilateral symmetry during embryonic development. Their body plans are arranged around a longitudinal axis with a front (head) and rear (tail) end, and a left and right side. Nearly all bilaterians are bilaterally symmetrical as adults; the exception is [[Echinoderm|echinoderms]], which have pentaradial symmetry as adults. Most have complete digestive tracts with a separate [[Mouth|mouth]] and [[Anus|anus]], and sense organs and nerve ganglia concentrated at the front end of the body, a feature called cephalization.

Bilaterians form one of five main animal lineages, the others being [[Sponge|sponges]], [[Cnidaria]], Ctenophora and Placozoa. They diversified rapidly in the late [[Ediacaran]] and the [[Cambrian]] and include over 98% of known animal species. Traditionally they are divided into deuterostomes and protostomes, depending on whether the blastopore becomes the anus or the mouth. The phylum Xenacoelomorpha, erected in 2011, does not clearly fit either group.

=== KEY: Binary_number_1f0e56
HEADWORD: Binary number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bit | Computer | I Ching | Logic gate | Rational number
ENTRY:
Number expressed in the base-2 numeral system, which uses only the symbols 0 and 1. Each digit is called a [[Bit|bit]]. Because binary is simple to implement in digital circuits using [[Logic gate|logic gates]], almost all modern [[Computer|computers]] use it. Binary-like systems appeared in ancient cultures: Egyptian Horus-Eye fractions were a binary system for fractional quantities of grain and liquids, and the Chinese [[I Ching]] used hexagrams analogous to three- and six-bit binary numerals. The modern binary system was first studied in Europe by Thomas Harriot in the 15th to 17th centuries, and Gottfried Leibniz is credited with its invention. A binary number may also be a [[Rational number|rational number]] with a finite binary representation, the quotient of an integer by a power of two.

=== KEY: Binary_star_a2a8eb
HEADWORD: Binary star
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astrometry | Nova | Spectroscopy | Supernova | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Pair of stars gravitationally bound to, and orbiting, each other. Binary stars are among the most important objects in astrophysics because they allow direct measurement of stellar masses and tests of theories of stellar evolution. Stars that appear single to the naked eye but are resolved by a telescope are called visual binaries. Others are detected by [[Spectroscopy|spectroscopy]] or [[Astrometry|astrometry]], and eclipsing binaries have orbits aligned with the line of sight, so their stars eclipse each other. Close binaries can exchange mass, and binaries are progenitors of [[Nova|novae]] and type Ia [[Supernova|supernovae]]. The term was first used by [[William Herschel]] in 1802. The first orbit of a binary star was computed in 1827 by Félix Savary, for Xi Ursae Majoris. The Washington Double Star Catalog lists over 100,000 double stars, but orbits are known for only a few thousand of them.

=== KEY: Binding_energy_31996c
HEADWORD: Binding energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Nuclear physics
ENTRY:
Smallest amount of energy needed to remove a particle from a system of particles, or to disassemble the system into its individual parts. The term is mainly used in condensed matter physics, atomic physics and chemistry, while [[Nuclear physics|nuclear physics]] uses separation energy. A bound system typically has lower energy than its unbound constituents, and a decrease in total energy ΔE comes with a decrease in mass Δm, where Δmc² = ΔE. In a nucleus, binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56, at about 8.8 MeV, and falls for heavier elements, so fusion of light nuclei and fission of heavy nuclei both release energy. For an [[Atom|atom]], binding energy is the energy needed to remove an electron; in hydrogen it is 13.6 eV.

=== KEY: Binoculars_93f3f2
HEADWORD: Binoculars
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eye | Lens | Parallax | Prism (optics)
ENTRY:
Optical instrument made of two identical refracting telescopes mounted side by side and pointed in the same direction, giving magnified vision for both [[Eye|eyes]]. Unlike a monocular telescope, binoculars produce a three-dimensional image, because each eyepiece presents a slightly different view and [[Parallax|parallax]] lets the visual cortex generate an impression of depth. Most are held with both hands, though sizes range from small opera glasses to large pedestal-mounted tower viewers. Early binoculars used Galilean optics, with a convex objective and a concave eyepiece [[Lens|lens]]; this gave an erect image but a narrow field of view and limited magnification. Keplerian optics achieved higher magnification but inverted the image, which had to be corrected with erecting lenses or [[Prism (optics)|prisms]]. Ignazio Porro patented a prism erecting system in 1854, and in 1894 the Carl Zeiss company introduced improved "modern" Porro prism binoculars, whose well-separated, offset objectives gave a better sensation of depth.

=== KEY: Binomial_coefficient_1049c8

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Number of ways to choose an unordered subset of k elements from a set of n elements. === KEY: Binomial_distribution_a4f4e4
HEADWORD: Binomial distribution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bernoulli distribution | Binomial coefficient | Expected value | Jacob Bernoulli | Normal distribution | Probability distribution | Statistics
ENTRY:
Discrete [[Probability distribution|probability distribution]] of the number of successes in a sequence of n independent yes–no experiments, each with success probability p. The probability of exactly k successes is [[Binomial coefficient|C(n, k)]]pᵏ(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ, and the [[Expected value|expected value]] is np. A single trial is a [[Bernoulli distribution|Bernoulli distribution]]. The distribution is frequently used to model successes in a sample drawn with replacement from a population, and it remains a good approximation for sampling without replacement when the population is much larger than the sample. It is the basis of the binomial test of [[Statistics|statistical]] significance. [[Jacob Bernoulli]] formalized it in his posthumous 1713 work *Ars Conjectandi*, and Abraham de Moivre developed its [[Normal distribution|normal approximation]] in 1733.

=== KEY: Binomial_nomenclature_49aa8c
HEADWORD: Binomial nomenclature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Linnaeus | Genus | Latin | Species
ENTRY:
Formal system of naming [[Species|species]] with a two-part name in [[Latin]] form, such as *Homo sapiens* for modern humans. The first part, the generic name, identifies the [[Genus|genus]]; the second, the specific epithet, distinguishes the species within it. [[Carl Linnaeus]] formally introduced the system in *Species Plantarum* (1753), though Gaspard Bauhin had used many genus names later adopted by Linnaeus as early as 1622. Binomial names were designed to be succinct, relatively stable, verifiable and understood internationally, unlike common names, which differ in every language. The International Code of Zoological Nomenclature governs animals, and the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants governs plants and fungi. In modern usage the genus name is capitalized and both parts are italicized.

=== KEY: Binomial_theorem_89d445
HEADWORD: Binomial theorem
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Formula for the algebraic expansion of powers of a binomial. === KEY: Biochemistry_053d79
HEADWORD: Biochemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biotechnology | Chemistry | Justus von Liebig | Metabolism | Molecular biology | Rosalind Franklin
ENTRY:
Branch of science that studies the chemical processes within and relating to living organisms. A sub-discipline of both [[Chemistry|chemistry]] and biology, it may be divided into structural biology, enzymology, and [[Metabolism|metabolism]], and is closely related to [[Molecular biology|molecular biology]]. Much of it concerns the structures, functions, and interactions of biological macromolecules (proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and lipids) and the reactions of small molecules and ions.

Its findings are applied mainly in medicine, nutrition, and agriculture. Biochemists investigate the causes and cures of diseases, nutritional deficiencies, and soils and fertilizers. Combined with engineering approaches, biochemical methods underlie [[Biotechnology|biotechnology]].

Scholars disagree on when biochemistry began. Some point to Anselme Payen's discovery of the first enzyme, diastase, in 1833; others to Eduard Buchner's 1897 demonstration of alcoholic fermentation in cell-free extracts, or to the 1842 work *Animal Chemistry* by [[Justus von Liebig]], which presented a chemical theory of metabolism. Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 synthesis of urea was regarded by some as the overthrow of vitalism, the belief in a vital principle distinct from non-living matter, though others reject that view. Vinzenz Kletzinsky printed a *Compendium der Biochemie* in Vienna in 1858, and Carl Neuberg is often cited as having coined the word in 1903.

From the mid-20th century, techniques such as chromatography, X-ray diffraction, and NMR spectroscopy allowed detailed analysis of pathways such as glycolysis and the Krebs cycle. In the 1950s James Watson, Francis Crick, [[Rosalind Franklin]], and Maurice Wilkins were instrumental in solving the structure of DNA, and in 1958 George Beadle and Edward Tatum received the Nobel Prize for showing that one gene produces one enzyme.

=== KEY: Biodiversity_935c18
HEADWORD: Biodiversity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian explosion | Holocene extinction | Permian–Triassic extinction event
ENTRY:
Variability of life on Earth, measurable at the levels of genetic, species, ecosystem, and phylogenetic diversity. Biologists often define it as the totality of genes, species, and ecosystems of a region.

Diversity is unevenly distributed and is greater in the tropics, owing to warm climate and high primary productivity near the equator. Tropical forests cover less than one-fifth of Earth's land area yet contain about 50% of the world's species. Estimates by Mora et al. (2011) were approximately 8.7 million terrestrial species and 2.2 million oceanic species; other estimates include 10–30 million insects, of which about 0.9 million are known.

Over geological time, biodiversity has risen and fallen. The Phanerozoic, spanning the last 540 million years, saw rapid growth through the [[Cambrian explosion]], when most multicellular phyla first appeared. Five major mass extinctions and several minor ones have caused sudden drops. The [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]], 251 million years ago, was the worst, and vertebrate recovery took 30 million years.

Human activities are driving an ongoing loss often called the [[Holocene extinction]], or sixth mass extinction. Habitat destruction for farming is a key cause, and climate change also plays a role. A 2007 estimate held that up to 30% of all species could be extinct by 2050. The Living Planet Report 2014 claimed that vertebrate populations were on average about half the size they were 40 years earlier. Other analyses question whether present extinction rates exceed background levels once under-detection biases are considered.

=== KEY: Biofuel_c08926
HEADWORD: Biofuel

ENTRY:
Fuel produced from biomass over a short time span, rather than by the very slow natural processes that form [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]]. Mostly used for transportation, biofuels are regarded as a [[Renewable energy|renewable energy]] source. The two most common types are [[Ethanol|bioethanol]], an alcohol made by fermentation mostly from sugar and starch crops such as [[Maize|maize]] and [[Sugarcane|sugarcane]], and biodiesel, such as that made from canola or soybeans. === KEY: Biography_9100c7

=== KEY: Biological_pigment_15e731

=== KEY: Biological_sex_696dfd
HEADWORD: Biological sex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chromosome | Mammal | Meiosis | Reptile | Sexual reproduction
ENTRY:
Trait of a sexually reproducing organism defined by its production of gametes of one of two sizes or shapes, male or female. By convention, organisms producing smaller gametes (sperm) are male and those producing larger gametes (egg cells) are female; an organism producing both is a hermaphrodite, and some hermaphrodites can self-fertilize. Fungi and most unicellular organisms lack differing gamete sizes and are said to have mating types instead.

[[Sexual reproduction]], found only in eukaryotes, combines gametes from two parents. A diploid organism produces haploid gametes by [[Meiosis|meiosis]], which includes genetic recombination through chromosomal crossover, and fertilization restores the diploid state in a zygote carrying half its genetic material from each parent. Gametes may look alike (isogamy) or differ in size (anisogamy); the difference is greatest in oogamy, where a small motile gamete fuses with a much larger non-motile one.

In non-hermaphroditic species, sex is fixed by one of several determination systems. Most [[Mammal|mammals]] have the XY system, in which the male usually carries an X and a Y [[Chromosome|chromosome]] and the female two X chromosomes. Birds use the ZW system, and some insects the XO system. In environmental systems, such as temperature-dependent determination in [[Reptile|reptiles]] and crustaceans, conditions set sex.

In animals, sperm are small cells propelled by a single flagellum, while egg cells are large, immobile, and rich in nutrients. Aquatic animals often fertilize externally; most land animals use internal fertilization to prevent gametes drying up.

The sexes of a species may look alike (sexual monomorphism) or differ (sexual dimorphism), as in most birds and mammals, where sex is usually identified by observing sexual characteristics. Sexual selection, or mate choice, can accelerate the evolution of differences between the sexes.

=== KEY: Biological_warfare_da8873
HEADWORD: Biological warfare
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthrax | Chemical weapon | Cold War | Plague (disease) | Smallpox | Soviet Union | Unit 731 | World War II
ENTRY:
Use of living organisms or their toxins, such as bacteria, viruses, insects or fungi, to kill, harm or incapacitate humans, animals or plants as an act of war. Offensive use in international armed conflicts is a war crime under the 1925 Geneva Protocol, and the 1972 Biological Weapons Convention bans the development, production, stockpiling and use of such weapons while permitting defensive research. Biological warfare is distinct from [[Chemical weapon|chemical]], nuclear and radiological warfare, and some agents, such as [[Smallpox|smallpox]] and pneumonic [[Plague (disease)|plague]], can spread from person to person. Japan's Imperial Army, through [[Unit 731]], tested plague, [[Anthrax|anthrax]] and cholera on Chinese civilians and prisoners during [[World War II]], resulting in tens of thousands of deaths. After World War II, the [[Soviet Union]] and the United States developed offensive programs amid [[Cold War]] tensions.

=== KEY: Biology_9470dd
HEADWORD: Biology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Biochemistry | Carl Linnaeus | Charles Darwin | DNA | Evolution | Gregor Mendel
ENTRY:
Scientific study of life and living organisms. A broad natural science, it seeks to explain the structure, function, growth, origin, evolution, and distribution of life. Five themes are central: the cell as the basic unit of life; genes and heredity as the basis of inheritance; [[Evolution|evolution]] as the driver of biological diversity; energy transformation for sustaining life processes; and homeostasis, the maintenance of internal stability. Biology examines life at levels from molecules and cells to organisms, populations, and ecosystems. Its subdisciplines include molecular biology, physiology, ecology, evolutionary biology, developmental biology, and systematics, using observation, experimentation, and mathematical modeling. Modern biology rests on the theory of evolution by natural selection, first articulated by [[Charles Darwin]], and on the molecular understanding of genes encoded in [[DNA]]. Its applications span medicine, agriculture, biotechnology, and environmental science. Biologists classify organisms, from single-celled archaea and bacteria to multicellular plants, fungi, and animals, by shared characteristics and evolutionary relationships.

The name comes from Greek *bios*, "life," and *logia*, "study of." The compound appears in the title of a 1766 work by Michael Christoph Hanow, and its modern sense was introduced independently by Thomas Beddoes (1799), Karl Friedrich Burdach (1800), Gottfried Reinhold Treviranus (1802), and Jean-Baptiste Lamarck (1802).

Roots of the science, which then included medicine, lie in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, c. 3000–1200 BC, and shaped Greek natural philosophy. [[Aristotle]] explored biological causation and the diversity of life, and his successor Theophrastus began the scientific study of plants. Anton van Leeuwenhoek's improvement of the microscope revealed spermatozoa, bacteria, and microscopic life. In 1838 Schleiden and Schwann promoted the idea that the cell is the basic unit of organisms; Robert Remak and Rudolf Virchow established that all cells come from other cells, and by the 1860s cell theory was accepted. [[Carl Linnaeus]] published a basic taxonomy in 1735 and introduced scientific names for all his species in the 1750s. Lamarck presented the first coherent theory of evolution; Darwin forged a more successful one based on natural selection, and Alfred Russel Wallace independently reached the same conclusions.

[[Gregor Mendel]]'s work of 1865 outlined the principles of inheritance, though its significance was not realized until the early 20th century, when the modern synthesis reconciled Darwinian evolution with classical genetics. Experiments by Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase in the 1940s and early 1950s pointed to DNA as the carrier of genes, and James Watson and Francis Crick described its double-helical structure in 1953, opening the era of molecular genetics. Har Gobind Khorana, Robert W. Holley, and Marshall Warren Nirenberg cracked the genetic code, and the Human Genome Project was launched in 1990.

Major fields include [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], the study of chemical processes in living organisms; molecular biology, centered on nucleic acids and proteins; cell biology; and genetics, the study of inheritance, whose chromosome theory was supported by Thomas Morgan's fruit fly experiments. The 1953 Miller–Urey experiment showed that organic compounds could form abiotically under conditions mimicking early Earth, suggesting a route for abiogenesis.

=== KEY: Biome_c25aed
HEADWORD: Biome
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Desert | Ecosystem | Forest | Grassland | Taiga | Tundra
ENTRY:
Distinct geographical region with a specific climate, vegetation and animal life, consisting of a biological community that has formed in response to its physical environment and regional climate. The International Biological Program (1964–74) projects popularized the concept. In 1935 Arthur Tansley added climatic and soil aspects to the idea, calling it [[Ecosystem|ecosystem]]. Because the change from one biome to another is gradual, boundaries must be drawn arbitrarily. Classification schemes differ: Leslie Holdridge (1947) used temperature and rainfall to define 30 "humidity provinces", and Robert Whittaker used precipitation and temperature in a simpler scheme. Principal biome types include [[Tundra|tundra]], [[Taiga|taiga]], deciduous [[Forest|forest]], [[Grassland|grasslands]], [[Desert|desert]] and tropical forest.

=== KEY: Biomechanics_0b9895
HEADWORD: Biomechanics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Animal locomotion | Blood | Cell (biology) | Friction | Navier–Stokes equations | Organelle
ENTRY:
Branch of biophysics that applies the methods of mechanics to the structure, function and motion of biological systems. It operates at every level, from whole organisms to organs, [[Cell (biology)|cells]], [[Organelle|cell organelles]] and even proteins. Biofluid mechanics studies gas and liquid flows in or around organisms, such as [[Blood|blood]] flow, which can be modeled by the [[Navier–Stokes equations]]. Biotribology studies [[Friction|friction]], wear and lubrication in biological systems, especially human joints such as hips and knees. Comparative biomechanics applies the field to non-human organisms, most often for [[Animal locomotion|animal locomotion]], while computational biomechanics uses tools such as the finite element method. In medicine, the field informs orthopedic implant design and gait analysis for rehabilitation.

Application of [[Engineering|engineering]] principles and design concepts to medicine and biology for healthcare purposes, such as diagnosis and [[Therapy|therapy]]. === KEY: Biomolecule_a55704
HEADWORD: Biomolecule
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adenosine triphosphate | Carbohydrate | DNA | Hormone | Lipid | Nucleic acid | Protein | Vitamin
ENTRY:
Molecule produced by a living organism and essential to one or more biological processes. Biomolecules include large [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]], [[Protein|proteins]], [[Lipid|lipids]] and [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]], as well as small molecules such as [[Vitamin|vitamins]] and [[Hormone|hormones]]. Most are organic compounds; oxygen, carbon, hydrogen and nitrogen alone make up 96% of the human body's mass. === KEY: Biopsy_d8a0b9

Medical test that extracts cells or tissue from the body for examination to determine the presence or extent of disease. === KEY: Biosphere_26120d
HEADWORD: Biosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cryosphere | Ecology | Ecosystem | Lithosphere | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Worldwide sum of all [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]] on Earth, also called the ecosphere and described as the zone of life on Earth. It is virtually a closed system for matter but an open system for energy, as [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] captures solar energy at a rate of around 100 terawatts. The biosphere integrates all living beings with the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]], [[Cryosphere|cryosphere]], hydrosphere and atmosphere, and is thought to have evolved at least 3.5 billion years ago. Geologist Eduard Suess coined the term in 1875 for the place on Earth's surface where life dwells. Vladimir Vernadsky developed the concept in the 1920s and defined [[Ecology|ecology]] as the science of the biosphere. The total number of living cells on Earth is estimated at 10³⁰. Artificial biospheres such as Biosphere 2 are studied as closed, self-regulating systems.

=== KEY: Biotechnology_8a07f2
HEADWORD: Biotechnology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biochemistry | Genetic engineering | Insulin
ENTRY:
Multidisciplinary field integrating natural and engineering sciences to apply organisms and their parts to products and services. Károly Ereky first used the term in 1919 for producing goods from raw materials with the aid of living organisms. It harnesses bacteria, yeast, plants, and other biological systems to perform tasks or make valuable substances, drawing on [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], genetics, and microbiology.

The practice reaches back to the domestication of animals, the cultivation of plants, and improvement of crops and livestock by artificial selection and hybridization. Cultivation of plants may be viewed as the earliest biotechnological enterprise, with farmers since the Neolithic Revolution selecting and breeding the best-suited crops. Fermentation of beer, bread, wine, and cheese is another ancient form. Modern usage also covers [[Genetic engineering]], which inserts genes from one organism into another or edits genes to create or modify traits, and cell and tissue culture, which grows cells and tissues in the laboratory. Recombinant DNA technology was developed in the 1970s, and the first commercial recombinant human [[Insulin|insulin]] appeared in 1982. CRISPR-Cas9 has enabled genome editing since 2012.

Applications include drugs, vaccines, biofuels, genetically modified crops, and new materials. Medical biotechnology produces therapeutic proteins, agricultural biotechnology has engineered crops resistant to pests and herbicides, and industrial biotechnology employs microbes for biofuel production and waste remediation. Microorganisms are also used to clean contaminated sites.

Biotechnology raises ethical and societal questions concerning genetic modification, germline editing that could introduce heritable changes, and intellectual property rights. Regulation varies by jurisdiction.

=== KEY: Bird_ebe418
HEADWORD: Bird
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaeopteryx | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Dinosaur | Feather | Hummingbird | Passerine | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Warm-blooded [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] of the class Aves, characterized by [[Feather|feathers]], toothless beaked jaws, hard-shelled eggs, a high metabolic rate, a four-chambered heart, and a strong yet lightweight skeleton.

Birds live worldwide and range in size from the 5.5 cm (2.2 in) bee [[Hummingbird|hummingbird]] to the 2.8 m (9 ft 2 in) common ostrich. There are over 11,000 living species in 44 orders; more than half are [[Passerine|passerine]], or perching, birds. Nearly all have wings, which are modified forelimbs, although flight has been lost in some groups, including ratites and penguins. Their digestive and respiratory systems are uniquely adapted for flight. The study of birds is called ornithology.

Birds evolved from earlier theropods and are the only living [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]]; in the modern cladistic sense they are considered reptiles, and their closest living relatives are the crocodilians. They descend from primitive avialans such as [[Archaeopteryx]], which appeared in the Late Jurassic. Modern birds evolved in the Late Cretaceous and diversified dramatically around the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]] 66 million years ago.

Birds are social, communicating through visual signals, calls, and songs. Most species are socially monogamous, usually for one breeding season at a time. Eggs are usually laid in a nest and incubated by the parents, and most birds give extended parental care after hatching.

Birds supply eggs, meat, and feathers, and guano is harvested as fertilizer. About 120 to 130 species have become extinct through human activity since the 17th century, and about 1,200 are threatened. Birdwatching is an important part of ecotourism.

=== KEY: Birth_control_356c82
HEADWORD: Birth control
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Condom | Family planning | Sex education | Sexually transmitted infection
ENTRY:
Use of methods or devices to prevent pregnancy; also called contraception or fertility control.

Birth control has been used since ancient times, but effective and safe methods became available only in the 20th century; the combined oral contraceptive pill was approved for use in 1960. Planning, making available, and using it is called [[Family planning|family planning]]. Some cultures limit or discourage access because they consider it morally, religiously, or politically undesirable.

Methods include barrier methods, hormonal methods, intrauterine devices (IUDs), sterilization, and behavioral methods. The most effective are vasectomy, tubal ligation, IUDs, and implants, each with first-year failure rates of less than 1%. Hormonal pills, patches, vaginal rings, and injections follow; [[Condom|condoms]], diaphragms, and fertility awareness methods are less effective, and spermicides and withdrawal least. With typical use, first-year failure rates for hormonal pills, patches, and rings are 9%, owing to inconsistent use. Sterilization is not usually reversible; other methods are. Condoms also help prevent [[Sexually transmitted infection|sexually transmitted infections]]. Emergency birth control can prevent pregnancy if taken within 72 to 120 hours after unprotected sex.

All methods carry some potential adverse effects, but the risk is less than that of pregnancy. Hormonal methods are associated with a 2- to 4-fold increased risk of venous thromboembolism and with elevated breast cancer incidence during use.

Comprehensive [[Sex education|sex education]] and access to birth control reduce unintended pregnancies among teenagers. About 222 million women in developing countries who want to avoid pregnancy are not using a modern method. In developing countries, birth control has decreased pregnancy-related deaths by 40% (about 270,000 deaths prevented in 2008). Some commentators link high contraceptive use in developed nations to sustained sub-replacement fertility and aging populations.

=== KEY: Birth_e8329d

ENTRY:
Process of bearing or bringing forth offspring, also called parturition. In mammals, hormones cause the muscular walls of the [[Uterus|uterus]] to contract, expelling the [[Fetus|fetus]] when it is ready to feed and breathe. === KEY: Black_Death_45e6e9

ENTRY:
Plague pandemic that struck Europe, the Near East, and [[North Africa]] from 1346 to 1353.

The Black Death was one of the most fatal pandemics in human history. Estimates of the dead range from up to 50 million to 75 to 200 million worldwide, including around 30% to 60% of the European population. The disease was caused by the bacterium [[Yersinia pestis]], spread mainly by flea bites and also by contact with infected tissues or fluids and, in pneumonic cases, by respiratory droplets. Some researchers argue that human lice contributed significantly to the spread, challenging the rodent-centered account.

Its origin is disputed; one account places it in [[Central Asia]]. The plague reached Europe in 1347 on Genoese trading ships from the [[Crimea|Crimean]] port of Kaffa, spreading through Mediterranean ports including [[Sicily]] and [[Marseille]]. Contemporary writers called the event the pestilence or the great death. The name black death was not applied to this pandemic in English until the 1750s, first attested in 1755 as a translation of the Danish *den sorte død*.

The pandemic was the second great natural disaster to strike Europe in the late Middle Ages, after the Great Famine of 1315–1317. It disrupted feudal structures, raised wages for survivors, and prompted shifts in labor, religion, and medicine, including early quarantine measures. Further outbreaks followed, and the European population did not regain its 14th-century level until the 16th century.

=== KEY: Black_body_radiation_0b9e2a
HEADWORD: Black-body radiation
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Thermal electromagnetic radiation emitted by an idealized black body, an object that absorbs all radiation falling on it. A black body's emission is a continuous spectrum that depends only on its temperature, described by [[Planck's law]]. The term *black body* was introduced by [[Gustav Kirchhoff]] in 1860. At room temperature a black body radiates mostly in the [[Infrared|infrared]]; at about 500 °C it begins to glow dull red, and at higher temperatures it emits more orange, yellow, green and blue light. Planets and stars, including the [[Earth]] and [[Sun]], are not perfect black bodies, but black-body radiation is a good first approximation for the energy they emit. In 1900 [[Max Planck]] resolved the ultraviolet catastrophe by proposing that electromagnetic energy is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets, or quanta.

=== KEY: Black_hole_05b337
HEADWORD: Black hole
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Cygnus X-1 | Event horizon | General relativity | Hawking radiation | Sagittarius A* | Spacetime
ENTRY:
Astronomical body so compact that its gravity prevents anything, including light, from escaping.

[[Albert Einstein]]'s theory of [[General relativity|general relativity]], which describes gravitation as the curvature of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], predicts that any sufficiently compact mass will form a black hole. The boundary of no escape is the [[Event horizon|event horizon]]; crossing it traps an object but produces no locally detectable change. General relativity also predicts a central singularity, where the curvature of spacetime is infinite.

The idea of a body from which light could not escape was proposed in the late 18th century by John Michell, whose letter of 1784 considered a star of the same density but 500 times the radius of the Sun, and independently by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1796. The first widely accepted black hole was [[Cygnus X-1]], proposed through studies between 1971 and 1974.

Black holes typically form when massive stars collapse at the end of their life cycle, often in a supernova, and can grow by absorbing surrounding mass. Supermassive black holes of millions to billions of solar masses exist at the centers of most galaxies; the radio source [[Sagittarius A*]] in the Milky Way contains one of about 4.3 million solar masses.

Black holes are detected through their effects on nearby matter and light. Infalling plasma forms a glowing accretion disk, which in extreme cases powers a quasar. Merging black holes emit gravitational waves, and the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadows of M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime predicts that event horizons emit [[Hawking radiation]], causing very slow mass loss.

He blockaded the port of [[Charleston, South Carolina|Charles Town, South Carolina]] and ransomed its inhabitants. After accepting a royal pardon in North Carolina he soon returned to piracy, and he was killed by a force of sailors led by Lieutenant Robert Maynard at the battle of Ocracoke Inlet. He relied on his fearsome image rather than violence, and he became the inspiration for an archetypal pirate in fiction. The wreck of his flagship was discovered by divers in 1996 off North Carolina.

=== KEY: Blackberry_30a48b
HEADWORD: Blackberry

ENTRY:
Edible fruit produced by many species of *Rubus* in the [[Rosaceae|rose family]]. The fruit is an aggregate of small drupelets, so botanically it is not a [[Berry (botany)|berry]]. Blackberry plants are perennial, bearing biennial canes: in the first year a new stem, the primocane, produces leaves but no flowers, and in the second year it becomes a floricane that flowers and fruits. Over 375 species are recognized, many of them closely related apomictic microspecies. Annual volumes of 20,000 pounds (9,100 kg) per acre (0.40 ha) are possible. The Himalayan or Armenian blackberry, *Rubus armeniacus*, is considered a noxious weed and [[Invasive species|invasive species]] in many regions of the [[Pacific Northwest|Pacific Northwest]] of Canada and the United States. Blackberry leaves feed certain caterpillars, and mammals disperse the seeds when they eat the fruit. Unlike a [[Raspberry|raspberry]], the torus stays with a blackberry when it is picked.

Medium-sized [[Antelope|antelope]] native to India and Nepal. It is the sole living member of the genus *Antilope*, described by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758. It stands up to 74–84 cm (29–33 in) at the shoulder. Males have dark brown to black upper bodies and corkscrew horns 35–75 cm (14–30 in) long; females and juveniles are yellowish-fawn. Excessive hunting, [[Deforestation|deforestation]] and habitat destruction caused sharp declines in the 20th century, and small herds now largely survive in protected areas. Hunting is prohibited in India under the Wildlife Protection Act of 1972, and the species holds significance in [[Hinduism|Hinduism]].
