=== KEY: Vacuum_6cd4b7
HEADWORD: Vacuum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Atmospheric pressure | Lucretius | Outer space | Plato | Quantum field theory | Vacuum cleaner
ENTRY:
Space devoid of matter; in practice, a region with gaseous pressure much less than [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]]. Physicists often discuss ideal results in a perfect vacuum and use the term partial vacuum for an actual imperfect one, as in a laboratory or in space. The quality of a partial vacuum is how closely it approaches a perfect one: lower gas pressure means higher quality. A typical [[Vacuum cleaner|vacuum cleaner]] reduces air pressure by around 20%, whereas ultra-high vacuum chambers, common in chemistry, physics, and engineering, operate below one trillionth (10⁻¹²) of atmospheric pressure (100 nPa) and can reach around 100 particles/cm³. [[Outer space]] is an even higher-quality vacuum, with just a few hydrogen atoms per cubic meter on average in intergalactic space.

Ancient Greek philosophers debated the void in the context of atomism. [[Lucretius]] argued for its existence in the first century BC, and Hero of Alexandria tried unsuccessfully to create an artificial vacuum in the first century AD. Following [[Plato]], however, the idea of a featureless void met considerable skepticism, and [[Aristotle]] believed that no void could occur naturally. Empirical study began in the 17th century, when Evangelista Torricelli produced the first laboratory vacuum in 1643 by filling a tall glass container closed at one end with mercury and inverting it in a bowl. In [[Quantum field theory|quantum field theory]], the vacuum is not simply empty but the lowest-energy ground state of the quantum fields, in which virtual particles briefly emerge and annihilate, contributing to phenomena such as the Casimir effect and zero-point energy.

=== KEY: Vacuum_cleaner_530cdb
HEADWORD: Vacuum cleaner
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Birmingham | Carpet
ENTRY:
Device using suction, often with agitation, to remove dirt from [[Carpet|carpets]] and hard floors. Dirt is collected in a dust bag or plastic bin. Types include stick, handheld, upright, canister and robot vacuums, and specialized shop vacuums can clean both solids and liquids. The first manual models, using bellows, were developed in the 1860s, and the first motorized designs appeared at the turn of the 20th century. The first portable model for the home was built in 1905 by Walter Griffiths of [[Birmingham]], England. In the United Kingdom and Ireland the appliance is often called a hoover, a genericized trademark taken from the Hoover Company, one of the first and most influential makers of the device.

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coulomb's law | Electric charge | Electric field | Maxwell's equations | Speed of light
ENTRY:
Physical constant, the absolute dielectric permittivity of classical vacuum, denoted ε₀. Also called the permittivity of free space or the electric constant, it measures how dense an [[Electric field|electric field]] is permitted to form in response to [[Electric charge|electric charges]]. In [[Coulomb's law]], the constant fraction 1/(4πε₀) is approximately 9×10⁹ N⋅m²⋅C⁻². It also appears in [[Maxwell's equations]]. Its value follows from the defined [[Speed of light|speed of light]] c and the magnetic constant μ₀ through ε₀ = 1/(μ₀c²).

=== KEY: Vacuum_tube_5dea21
HEADWORD: Vacuum tube
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric guitar | Microwave oven | Radar | Radio | Semiconductor device | Television | Transistor
ENTRY:
Device that controls electric current in a high vacuum between electrodes, also called an electron tube or thermionic valve. The first and simplest type, the diode or Fleming valve, was invented in 1904 by John Ambrose Fleming and allowed current to flow in only one direction. Adding control grids produced triodes and tetrodes that could amplify as well as rectify signals. Vacuum tubes were central to [[Radio|radio]], [[Television|television]], [[Radar|radar]] and early computers in the first half of the 20th century. From the 1940s, the invention of [[Semiconductor device|semiconductor devices]] made solid-state electronics possible, and from the mid-1960s tubes began to be replaced by the [[Transistor|transistor]]. Cathode-ray tubes remained in displays until the early 21st century. Tubes persist in some applications, such as the magnetron in [[Microwave oven|microwave ovens]], and many audio enthusiasts prefer tube amplifiers, including [[Electric guitar|electric guitar]] players.

=== KEY: Vagus_nerve_ee0e67
HEADWORD: Vagus nerve
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Digestion | Epilepsy
ENTRY:
Tenth cranial nerve and main nerve of the parasympathetic nervous system. Its sensory and motor fibers link the brain to organs including the heart, lungs and [[Digestion|digestive tract]], regulating heart rate, breathing and digestion. It is the longest nerve of the autonomic nervous system, and most of its fibers are sensory, carrying information from the body's organs to the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]]. === KEY: Valence_bond_theory_b24fce
HEADWORD: Valence bond theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical bond | Gilbert N. Lewis | Linus Pauling | Molecular orbital theory | Schrödinger equation
ENTRY:
Quantum-mechanical theory of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonding]], one of two basic theories along with [[Molecular orbital theory|molecular orbital theory]]. It describes how the atomic orbitals of separate atoms combine to form individual bonds when a molecule forms, whereas molecular orbitals cover the whole molecule. In 1916 [[Gilbert N. Lewis]] proposed that a bond forms through two shared electrons. In 1927 Walter Heitler and Fritz London used the [[Schrödinger equation|Schrödinger wave equation]] to explain the covalent bond in the hydrogen molecule. [[Linus Pauling]] then developed the concepts of resonance (1928) and orbital hybridization (1930). Valence bond theory declined from the 1960s as molecular orbital methods suited large computer programs, but it revived from the 1980s as its computational problems were largely solved.

=== KEY: Valley_6c92ef

ENTRY:
Elongated low area, typically between hills or mountains, that usually contains a [[River|river]] or stream running along its length. Most valleys are formed by erosion over long periods, and river valleys are typically steep-sided and V-shaped in their upper reaches. Valleys carved by [[Glacier|glaciers]] have a U-shaped cross section. Rift valleys arise mainly from earth movements rather than erosion. === KEY: Value_added_tax_72e18c
HEADWORD: Value-added tax
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ivory Coast | OECD | Sales tax
ENTRY:
Consumption tax levied on the value added at each stage of production and distribution. Suppliers collect it from customers and remit it to the government, so it is an indirect tax, and exports are typically exempted. It is similar to a [[Sales tax|sales tax]], but under the invoice method each seller pays tax on sales and credits the tax paid on inputs, so only the difference is remitted. German industrialist Georg Wilhelm von Siemens proposed the concept in 1918. The modern form was first implemented in 1954 in France's [[Ivory Coast]] colony by Maurice Lauré of the French tax authority, and France adopted it in 1958. As of January 2025, 175 of the 193 UN member countries employed a VAT, including all [[OECD]] members except the United States.

=== KEY: Vampire_bat_5657f7
HEADWORD: Vampire bat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Rabies
ENTRY:
Subfamily of bats that feed exclusively on blood. Vampire bats (Desmodontinae) are leaf-nosed bats of the family Phyllostomidae, found in Central and South America. Three extant species, the common vampire bat (*Desmodus rotundus*), the hairy-legged vampire bat (*Diphylla ecaudata*) and the white-winged vampire bat (*Diaemus youngi*), are the only mammals that feed solely on blood, a diet called hematophagy. Their short muzzles, cutting front teeth and pit organs that detect warm blood vessels help them locate prey. Salivary anticoagulants such as draculin prevent clotting for up to several hours. Vampire bats are highly social, roosting in colonies of dozens to thousands and sharing food by regurgitation. They serve as vectors for [[Rabies|rabies]] and other pathogens, posing challenges to agriculture and wildlife, while their saliva compounds hold promise for anticoagulant therapies.

Zone of energetic charged particles, mostly from the [[Solar wind|solar wind]], trapped around [[Earth]] by its magnetic field. Earth has two main belts, extending about 640 to 58,000 km above the surface. Named after James Van Allen, who described them in 1958, the belts were confirmed that year by Explorer 1 and Explorer 3. === KEY: Van_der_Waals_force_bd8ac1

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Intermolecular force | Johannes Diderik van der Waals
ENTRY:
Distance-dependent [[Intermolecular force|intermolecular]] interaction between atoms or molecules, not arising from a chemical bond. Named after Dutch physicist [[Johannes Diderik van der Waals]], the force is comparatively weak and vanishes quickly at longer distances. It arises from transient shifts in electron density that induce charges in nearby particles, and at very short distances it becomes repulsive. The force is usually described as a combination of London dispersion forces, Debye forces and the Keesom force. It underlies many properties of organic compounds and molecular solids, including their solubility.

In 1831 Swedish chemist Nils Gabriel Sefström rediscovered it and named it after the Norse goddess Vanadís, for the many colorful compounds it forms. Henry Enfield Roscoe isolated the pure metal later in the 19th century. Vanadium occurs in about 65 minerals and fossil fuel deposits. Approximately 93% of it is used in steel alloys, such as high-speed tool steels. Vanadium pentoxide is an important catalyst in the production of [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]], and some ocean organisms use vanadium as an active center of enzymes.

=== KEY: Variable_mathematics_ceb100
HEADWORD: Variable
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclid's Elements | Function (mathematics) | Mathematical logic | Quadratic equation | Real number | René Descartes
ENTRY:
Symbol, typically a letter, that refers to an unspecified mathematical object. The variable is said to denote the object, and any valid candidate for the object is the value of the variable. The values a variable can take are usually of the same kind, often numbers, and may form a set such as the set of [[Real number|real numbers]]. The term was originally used chiefly for the argument of a [[Function (mathematics)|function]], whose value could be thought of as varying within the function's domain. A variable that stays fixed during the solution of a problem is often called a parameter, and one to be determined is an unknown: in the [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equation]] ax² + bx + c = 0, a, b, and c are parameters and x is the unknown. In [[Mathematical logic|mathematical logic]], a variable is a symbol that either represents an unspecified constant of the theory or is quantified over.

The earliest uses of an unknown quantity appear in the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (c. 1500 BC). In [[Euclid's Elements|*Euclid's Elements*]] (c. 300 BC), mathematics was described geometrically. Diophantus of Alexandria used a syncopated algebra in his *Arithmetica* (c. 200 AD), calling an unknown number ζ. At the end of the 16th century, François Viète introduced representing known and unknown numbers by letters, using consonants for known values and vowels for unknowns. In 1637, [[René Descartes]] introduced the convention of x, y, and z for unknowns and a, b, and c for knowns, which remains in common use.

=== KEY: Variable_star_6cff2d
HEADWORD: Variable star
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Sun | Supernova
ENTRY:
Star whose brightness, as seen from [[Earth]], changes over time. The variation may be intrinsic, when the star's luminosity itself changes as it swells and shrinks, or extrinsic, when an orbiting companion eclipses it. Changes range from under an hour to multiple years. Most stars oscillate to some degree; the [[Sun|Sun]] varies by about 0.1% over its 11-year solar cycle, while a [[Supernova|supernova]] can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. === KEY: Variance_700bc9
HEADWORD: Variance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Expected value | Standard deviation | Statistics
ENTRY:
Statistical measure of how far a set of numbers spreads from its average. For a random variable X, variance is the [[Expected value|expected value]] of the squared deviation from the mean, and the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] is its square root. Variance is written as σ² and is non-negative, with zero meaning that all values are identical. Its units are the square of the data's units, which is why the standard deviation is commonly reported. Its advantage is that it is easier to manipulate algebraically; the variance of a sum of uncorrelated random variables equals the sum of their variances. Population variance uses all observations, while sample variance estimates it from a subset. Variance is central to [[Statistics|statistics]], including descriptive statistics and hypothesis testing.

=== KEY: Vascular_cambium_ac93e3
HEADWORD: Vascular cambium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bark (botany) | Gymnosperm | Meristem | Oak | Pine | Plant hormone
ENTRY:
Main growth tissue in the stems and roots of many plants with secondary growth, notably dicots such as [[Oak|oak trees]] and [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperms]] such as [[Pine|pine trees]]. It produces secondary xylem inward, toward the pith, and secondary phloem outward, toward the [[Bark (botany)|bark]]; generally more xylem than phloem is produced. In woody plants it forms a continuous cylinder of unspecialized [[Meristem|meristem]] cells. Unlike xylem and phloem, it does not transport water, minerals or food. Its activity is regulated by signaling networks involving [[Plant hormone|hormones]] such as auxin, ethylene and cytokinins. === KEY: Vascular_tissue_0b6de4
HEADWORD: Vascular tissue
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aphid | Leaf | Tree | Vascular cambium
ENTRY:
Complex conducting tissue in vascular plants, consisting of xylem and phloem. These tissues transport water, minerals and nutrients throughout the plant, and their cells are long and slender, somewhat like pipes. Vascular tissue is arranged in strands called vascular bundles, which also contain supporting and protective cells. In stems and roots, xylem lies toward the interior and phloem toward the exterior. Between them is the [[Vascular cambium|vascular cambium]], a meristem that divides to form new xylem and phloem, increasing the girth of the plant. In [[Tree|trees]] and other woody plants, a separate cork cambium forms cork that protects the surface and reduces water loss. In [[Leaf|leaves]], xylem faces the upper surface and phloem the lower, which is why [[Aphid|aphids]] are typically found on the undersides of leaves.

=== KEY: Vedas_e9bcc1
HEADWORD: Vedas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hindu philosophy | Hinduism | Indra | Jainism | Mantra | Sanskrit | Upanishads | Vedic period
ENTRY:
Oldest scriptures of [[Hinduism]], a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India. Composed in Vedic [[Sanskrit]], they constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature. There are four Vedas: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, and the Atharvaveda. Each has four subdivisions: the Samhitas (mantras and benedictions), the Brahmanas (commentaries on and explanations of rituals, ceremonies, and sacrifices), the Aranyakas (texts on rituals, ceremonies, sacrifices, and symbolic sacrifices), and the [[Upanishads]] (texts discussing meditation, philosophy, and spiritual knowledge). Some scholars add a fifth category, the Upāsanās (worship).

The word *veda* means "knowledge". The Vedas are *śruti* ("what is heard"), distinguishing them from other religious texts, called *smṛti* ("what is remembered"). Hindus consider them *apauruṣeya*, "not of a man, superhuman" or authorless: revelations of sacred sounds and texts heard by ancient sages after intense meditation. They have been transmitted orally since the 2nd millennium BC with the help of elaborate mnemonic techniques. The [[Mantra|mantras]], the oldest part, are recited today for their phonology rather than their semantics and are regarded as "primordial rhythms of creation".

The Samhitas were composed between c. 1500 and 1200 BC (Rigveda books 2–9) and between 1200 and 900 BC for the other three, and contain invocations to deities such as [[Indra]]. The Brahmanas date from about 900 BC to about 700 BC. The older principal Upanishads were composed between 800 BC and the end of the [[Vedic period]] and are the foundation of Hindu philosophical thought.

Indian philosophical schools differ in their positions. The six "orthodox" (*āstika*) schools of [[Hindu philosophy]] acknowledge the primal authority of the Vedas, whereas *śramaṇa* traditions such as Charvaka, Ajivika, Buddhism, and [[Jainism]], which did not, are called "heterodox" (*nāstika*).

=== KEY: Vegetable_cb02fd
HEADWORD: Vegetable
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztecs | Broccoli | Fruit | Hunter-gatherer | Maize | Subsistence agriculture | Tomato | Vitamin
ENTRY:
Edible part of a plant consumed by humans or other animals as food. In its original and still common meaning, the term covers all edible plant matter, including flowers, fruits, stems, leaves, roots, and seeds. An alternative definition, applied somewhat arbitrarily and often by culinary and cultural tradition, may include savory [[Fruit|fruits]] such as [[Tomato|tomatoes]] and squash, flowers such as [[Broccoli|broccoli]], and seeds such as pulses, while excluding foods derived from plants that are fruits, flowers, nuts, and cereal grains. "Fruit" has a precise botanical meaning, a part developed from the ovary of a flowering plant, so eggplants, bell peppers, and tomatoes are botanically fruits. In *Nix v. Hedden* (1893), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously that a tomato is correctly identified, and taxed under the Tariff of 1883, as a vegetable, while acknowledging that botanically it is a fruit.

The word was first recorded in English in the early 15th century and was originally applied to all plants; the sense "plant grown for food" was not established until the 18th century.

Vegetables were originally collected from the wild by [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherers]] and entered cultivation in several parts of the world, probably between 10,000 BC and 7,000 BC. [[Subsistence agriculture]] continues today in Africa, Asia, South America, and elsewhere. The staple diet of the [[Aztecs]] was [[Maize|maize]], supplemented by cultivated tomatoes, avocados, beans, peppers, pumpkins, squashes, peanuts, and amaranth seeds. Today most vegetables are grown worldwide as climate permits, and China is the largest producer.

Vegetables can be eaten raw or cooked and are mostly low in fat and carbohydrates but high in [[Vitamin|vitamins]], minerals, and dietary fiber. Many nutritionists encourage eating plenty of fruit and vegetables, with five or more portions a day often recommended.

=== KEY: Vehicle_d6af9c

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Device for carrying people, cargo, or both. === KEY: Velocity_88156d
HEADWORD: Velocity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acceleration | Classical mechanics | Derivative | Integral | International System of Units | Kinematics | Special relativity | Speed
ENTRY:
Measurement of speed in a certain direction of motion. It is a fundamental concept in [[Kinematics|kinematics]], the branch of [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] that describes the motion of physical objects. Velocity is a vector quantity: both magnitude and direction are needed to define it. Its scalar magnitude is called [[Speed|speed]] and is measured in meters per second (m/s) in the [[International System of Units|International System of Units]] (SI). For example, "5 meters per second" is a scalar, whereas "5 meters per second east" is a vector. If speed, direction, or both change, the object is undergoing an [[Acceleration|acceleration]]. Constant velocity means motion in a straight line at a constant speed; a car circling at a constant 20 kilometers per hour has constant speed but not constant velocity.

The average velocity of an object over a period of time is its change in position, Δs, divided by the duration, Δt. The instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval approaches zero, that is, the [[Derivative|derivative]] of position with respect to time: v = ds/dt. In one dimension, the area under a graph of velocity against time is the displacement, so the [[Integral|integral]] of the velocity function is the displacement function. Average velocity is always less than or equal to average speed.

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time (a = dv/dt), and velocity is the integral of acceleration. For constant acceleration, v = u + at, where u is the velocity at t = 0, and v² = u² + 2(a·x), known as the Torricelli equation. In [[Special relativity|special relativity]], velocity remains a vector but is constrained by the speed of light as the universal maximum.

ENTRY:
Diagram showing all possible logical relations between a collection of sets. === KEY: Venom_071dbd

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Toxin produced by an animal and actively delivered to another animal through a [[Wound|wound]], usually by a bite, sting or other penetrative action. Venoms are injected through specialized structures such as fangs or stingers, a process called envenomation, and differ from [[Poison|poisons]], which are passively ingested, inhaled or absorbed through the skin. === KEY: Venus_0bdc50
HEADWORD: Venus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Gravity assist | Greenhouse effect | Mercury (planet) | Solar wind | Sulfuric acid | Sun | Volcano
ENTRY:
Second planet from the [[Sun]], similar in size and mass to Earth. Its diameter is 12,103.6 km and its mass is 81.5% of Earth's. Venus has no liquid water, and its atmosphere is far thicker and denser than that of any other rocky body in the Solar System. It is 96.5% [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], with most of the remaining 3.5% nitrogen, and a cloud layer of [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]] spans the whole planet. The resulting intense [[Greenhouse effect|greenhouse effect]] gives a mean surface temperature of 737 K (464 °C; 867 °F), the hottest of any planet in the Solar System, and a surface pressure 92 times that at Earth's sea level, which turns the lowest layer of the atmosphere into a supercritical fluid.

From Earth, Venus is visible as a star-like point of light brighter than any other natural point of light in the sky, as the "morning star" or "evening star". It has no moons and rotates very slowly in a retrograde direction; a Venusian day lasts 116.75 Earth days, and its year 224.7. Venus has a weak magnetosphere induced by the [[Solar wind|solar wind]] interacting with the atmosphere. Internal heat escapes through active [[Volcano|volcanism]] and periodic resurfacing; about 80% of the surface is covered by smooth volcanic plains. Potential biomarkers found in 2020 at cloud-layer altitudes, where conditions are the most Earth-like in the Solar System, spurred new research.

By 1613, the phases of Venus seen through telescopes were presented as decisive evidence for the heliocentric model. Venera 1 flew past the planet in 1961, achieving the first interplanetary spaceflight, and Mariner 2 returned the first data in 1962. Mariner 10 captured the first images during its 1974 [[Gravity assist|gravity assist]] en route to [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]], and Magellan mapped the surface in 1990–91.

ENTRY:
Toxic red [[Pigment|pigment]] made by grinding the mineral [[Cinnabar|cinnabar]], a form of mercury sulfide. Vermilion is synonymous with red-orange, but it is not one hue: particle size produces shades from bright orange-red to a duller reddish purple. The Chinese probably made a synthetic version as early as the 4th century BC, and the Persian alchemist [[Jabir ibn Hayyan|Jabir ibn Hayyan]] described the process in the 9th century. A 17th-century Dutch method is still used today with essentially the same process. The pigment tends to darken, and like most mercury compounds it is toxic.

Landlocked state in the [[New England|New England]] region of the northeastern United States, bordered by [[Quebec|Quebec]] to the north. Its estimated population of 644,663 makes it the second-least populous state, and its capital, Montpelier, is the least populous U.S. state capital. An independent Vermont Republic was established in 1777, and it abolished [[Slavery|slavery]] before any other U.S. state. Vermont was admitted to the Union in 1791 as the 14th state. Its economy ranks last among states and territories by GDP, but it has the highest proportion of renewable electricity generation, at 99.9%, and it produces approximately 50% of the nation's maple syrup.

=== KEY: Vertebrate_172014
HEADWORD: Vertebrate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blue whale | Brain | Cambrian explosion | Chordate | Hagfish | Lamprey | Spinal cord | Tetrapod
ENTRY:
Animal with a vertebral column and a cranium; also called a craniate. The vertebral column surrounds and protects the [[Spinal cord|spinal cord]], and the cranium protects the [[Brain|brain]]. Vertebrates form the subphylum Vertebrata, by far the largest ranked grouping in the phylum [[Chordate|Chordata]], yet they make up less than five percent of all described animal species. The two major living subgroups are the jawless Cyclostomi, which include [[Hagfish|hagfish]] and [[Lamprey|lampreys]], and the jawed Gnathostomata. Bony fish include the lobe-finned fish, which gave rise to the [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]], the four-limbed animals.

Vertebrates are distinguished from other animals, including other chordates, by a vertebral column and a skull made of cartilage or bone; a brain divided into 3 or more sections; a muscular heart with 2 or more chambers; an inner ear with semicircular canals; kidneys with glomeruli; a thyroid; and a liver. Most vertebrates are aquatic and breathe with gills; in jawed vertebrates, the first pair of gill arches evolved into the jaws. Tetrapods lost the gills and adapted the swim bladder into lungs. Size ranges from the frog *Brachycephalus pulex*, with a minimum adult snout–vent length of 6.45 millimeters, to the [[Blue whale|blue whale]], at up to 33 m and some 150 tonnes.

The first vertebrates appeared in the [[Cambrian explosion]] some 518 million years ago and were jawless. Jawed vertebrates evolved in the Ordovician or Silurian. The first tetrapods appeared towards the end of the Devonian, and the first land amphibians in the Carboniferous. Mammals and dinosaurs appeared in the Triassic, and birds arose from dinosaurs in the Jurassic. Populations of many species have declined steeply since 1970 because of land-use change, overexploitation of natural resources, climate change, pollution, and invasive species.

=== KEY: Vertigo_film_c4820b
HEADWORD: Vertigo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Hitchcock | James Stewart | Library of Congress | San Francisco
ENTRY:
1958 American psychological thriller film directed by [[Alfred Hitchcock|Alfred Hitchcock]]. Based on the 1954 novel *D'entre les morts* by Boileau-Narcejac, it stars [[James Stewart|James Stewart]] as a former San Francisco police detective who retired after developing an extreme fear of heights. He is hired to follow the wife of an acquaintance from college, and the investigation of her strange behavior draws him into a troubled romance. Much of the film was shot on location in [[San Francisco|San Francisco]], and it was the first film to use the dolly zoom, an in-camera effect that distorts perspective to convey disorientation, often called the *Vertigo* effect. It received mixed reviews on release but later came to be considered Hitchcock's masterpiece. In 1989 the [[Library of Congress|Library of Congress]] selected it for the United States National Film Registry, and in the 2012 *Sight & Sound* poll of critics it replaced *Citizen Kane* as the greatest film ever made, placing second in 2022.

=== KEY: Veterinary_medicine_1ecd5a
HEADWORD: Veterinary medicine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Animal | Ashoka | Horse | Livestock | Lyon | Medicine | Neolithic
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Medicine|medicine]] concerned with the prevention, diagnosis and treatment of disease and injury in non-human [[Animal|animals]], both domesticated and wild. It is practiced by veterinarians, supported by paraveterinary workers such as veterinary nurses and technicians. Veterinary science helps human health by monitoring and controlling zoonotic diseases, supports food safety and [[Livestock|livestock]] health, and keeps pets healthy. Veterinarians are ethically obliged to look after animal welfare. Veterinary procedures date to the [[Neolithic|Neolithic]] (3400–3000 BC), shown by a trepanned cow skull, and the first surviving record is the Egyptian Papyrus of Kahun from the Twelfth Dynasty. The Indian treatise Shalihotra Samhita dates from the time of [[Ashoka|Ashoka]], and early efforts to regulate care focused on [[Horse|horses]]. The first veterinary school was founded in [[Lyon|Lyon]], France, by Claude Bourgelat, and its model drew on human medicine, natural history and comparative anatomy.

=== KEY: Vichy_France_404d7d
HEADWORD: Vichy France
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antisemitism | Battle of France | Jews | Nazi Germany | Nazi concentration camps | Philippe Pétain | World War II
ENTRY:
French state headed by Marshal [[Philippe Pétain|Philippe Pétain]] from 1940 to 1944, during [[World War II|World War II]]. It was established after the German victory in the [[Battle of France|Battle of France]] and governed the unoccupied southern zone from Vichy while Germany occupied the north and west. It adopted a policy of collaboration with [[Nazi Germany|Nazi Germany]] and promoted [[Antisemitism|antisemitism]] and an authoritarian program called *Révolution nationale*. After Allied landings in North Africa in November 1942, Germany occupied the rest of France, and at least 72,500 [[Jews|Jews]] deported from Vichy France were killed in [[Nazi concentration camps|Nazi concentration camps]].

=== KEY: Vicia_faba_76bfa5
HEADWORD: Vicia faba
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Erosion | Fabaceae | Israel | Loam | Neolithic
ENTRY:
Species of vetch in the pea and bean family [[Fabaceae|Fabaceae]], commonly called the broad bean, fava bean or faba bean. It is widely cultivated for human food and as a cover crop that prevents [[Erosion|erosion]] and fixes nitrogen in the soil. Varieties with smaller, harder seeds, called field beans, are fed to livestock. Some people have favism, a hemolytic reaction to broad beans linked to a metabolic disorder known as G6PDD. Otherwise the beans can be eaten raw or cooked once the outer seed coat is removed. Charred remains from [[Neolithic|Neolithic]] sites in [[Israel|Israel]]'s Lower Galilee suggest domestication may have begun as early as 8250 BC. The plant is hardy and tolerates saline and clay soils, though it prefers rich [[Loam|loams]]. Its diploid genome contains 13 GB of DNA and a predicted 34,221 protein-coding genes.
