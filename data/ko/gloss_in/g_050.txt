=== KEY: Gain_electronics_245543

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amplifier | Antenna (radio) | Decibel
ENTRY:
Measure of a two-port circuit's ability to increase the power or amplitude of a signal. It is usually the ratio of output to input signal amplitude or power, often expressed in [[Decibel|decibels]] (dB). Gain above one is amplification, the defining property of an active device such as an [[Amplifier|amplifier]]; a passive circuit has gain below one. The term alone is ambiguous: audio and operational amplifiers usually mean voltage gain, and radio-frequency amplifiers usually mean power gain. Power gain in decibels is 10 log₁₀(Pout / Pin) dB, and voltage gain is 20 log₁₀(Vout / Vin) dB when input and output impedances are equal. In [[Antenna (radio)|antenna]] design, gain instead compares a directional antenna's radiation intensity with that of a lossless antenna.

=== KEY: Galaxiidae_1f2858
HEADWORD: Galaxiidae
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Family of mostly small freshwater [[Fish|fish]] of the Southern Hemisphere, comprising seven genera. Most species live in southern Australia or [[New Zealand]], but some are found in South Africa, southern [[South America]], Lord Howe Island, [[New Caledonia]], and the [[Falkland Islands]]. The common galaxias (*Galaxias maculatus*) is probably the most widely naturally distributed freshwater fish in the Southern Hemisphere. Galaxiids are coolwater species of temperate latitudes, and many live in cold, high-altitude rivers, streams, and lakes. Many have a partly marine life cycle, or are amphidromous: larvae hatch in rivers, drift to the sea, and return to fresh water as juveniles, unlike [[Salmon|salmon]], which return only to breed. === KEY: Galaxy_756fb2
HEADWORD: Galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andromeda Galaxy | Dark matter | Edwin Hubble | Local Group | Milky Way | Observable universe | Supermassive black hole | Virgo Supercluster
ENTRY:
System of stars, planetary systems, stellar remnants, interstellar gas, dust, and [[Dark matter|dark matter]] bound together by gravity. The word derives from the Greek *galaxias*, "milky", a reference to the [[Milky Way]], the galaxy that contains the Solar System. Galaxies average an estimated 100 million stars, ranging from dwarfs with fewer than a thousand stars to supergiants with one hundred trillion. Most of a typical galaxy's mass is dark matter, with only a few percent visible as stars and nebulae. [[Supermassive black hole|Supermassive black holes]] are a common feature at their centers.

Galaxies are categorized by their visual characteristics as elliptical, spiral, or irregular; the Milky Way is a spiral. An estimated 200 billion (2×10¹¹) to 2 trillion galaxies exist in the [[Observable universe|observable universe]]. Most are 1,000 to 100,000 parsecs in diameter (approximately 3,000 to 300,000 light-years) and are separated by millions of parsecs. The Milky Way has a diameter of at least 26,800 parsecs (87,400 light-years), and its nearest large neighbor, the [[Andromeda Galaxy]], is just over 750,000 parsecs (2.4 million light-years) away.

Most galaxies are gravitationally organized into groups, clusters, and superclusters. The Milky Way and Andromeda dominate the [[Local Group]], which is part of the [[Virgo Supercluster]]. At the largest scale these associations form sheets and filaments surrounded by immense voids. Galaxy formation is less well understood than star formation.

The Greek philosopher Democritus proposed that the Milky Way might consist of distant stars . Galaxies were first known telescopically as spiral nebulae and were called island universes once their distance beyond the Milky Way was recognized. [[Edwin Hubble]] kept to the term nebulae, and the nomenclature did not fully change until after his death in 1953.

=== KEY: Galaxy_formation_and_evolution_58bc88
HEADWORD: Galaxy formation and evolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andromeda Galaxy | Big Bang | Cosmology | Elliptical galaxy | Milky Way | Supermassive black hole
ENTRY:
Subfield of [[Cosmology|cosmology]] concerned with how galaxies formed and changed over time. It studies how a nearly uniform early universe became structured, and how the first galaxies formed. Galaxy formation is hypothesized to arise from structure-formation theories, beginning with tiny quantum fluctuations after the [[Big Bang]]. The simplest model in general agreement with observations is the Lambda-CDM model, in which clustering and merging let galaxies accumulate mass and so determine their shape. Galaxies fall into two broad groups: blue, star-forming galaxies resembling spirals, and red, non-star-forming galaxies resembling [[Elliptical galaxy|ellipticals]]. Most giant galaxies contain a [[Supermassive black hole|supermassive black hole]] whose mass is tied to that of the galaxy's bulge. The first stars and protogalaxies appeared about 300 million years after the Big Bang, according to observations by the James Webb Space Telescope. The [[Milky Way]] is projected to collide with the [[Andromeda Galaxy]] in about 4.5 billion years.

=== KEY: Galaxy_groups_and_clusters_f5d01e
HEADWORD: Galaxy groups and clusters
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dark matter | Galaxy | Local Group | Milky Way | Supercluster | X-ray
ENTRY:
Largest known gravitationally bound structures in the universe, formed by aggregations of [[Galaxy|galaxies]]. They make up the densest part of the large-scale structure of the universe. Clusters formed relatively recently, between 10 billion years ago and now, after the smallest structures collapsed first. Groups typically contain no more than 50 galaxies within 1 to 2 megaparsecs, with a mass of about 10¹³ solar masses, and they are the most common galaxy structures. The [[Milky Way]] lies in the [[Local Group]] of more than 54 galaxies. Clusters are larger and contain hot intracluster gas at 10⁷ to 10⁸ K, which emits [[X-ray|X-rays]]. In a typical cluster, galaxies make up about 5% of the mass and hot gas about 10%, with the remainder [[Dark matter|dark matter]]. The Bullet Cluster provides the strongest evidence for dark matter, although some researchers, such as Brownstein and Moffat, use modified gravity to explain cluster masses without it. Clusters are often associated with larger, non-gravitationally bound [[Supercluster|superclusters]].

=== KEY: Galaxy_morphological_classification_c0ad23
HEADWORD: Galaxy morphological classification
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edwin Hubble | Elliptical galaxy | Galaxy | Spiral galaxy
ENTRY:
System for grouping [[Galaxy|galaxies]] by visual appearance, shape, structure, and distribution of light. The best-known scheme is the Hubble sequence, devised by [[Edwin Hubble]] in 1926 and later expanded by Gérard de Vaucouleurs and Allan Sandage. Hubble divided galaxies into elliptical, spiral, and lenticular classes. [[Elliptical galaxy|Elliptical galaxies]] are smooth and featureless, rated by degree of ellipticity and denoted E. [[Spiral galaxy|Spiral galaxies]] have a flattened disk, spiral arms, and a central bulge, and are denoted S; about half also have a bar, denoted SB. Lenticular galaxies (S0) have a bulge and disk but no visible spiral structure and little ongoing star formation. The sequence is often drawn as a tuning fork, and it remains the most commonly used system in research and amateur astronomy. In 2019 citizen scientists working through Galaxy Zoo reported that the relationship between spiral arms and the nucleus may need reassessment. Gérard de Vaucouleurs's 1959 extension added rings and lenses to describe spirals more fully.

=== KEY: Galen_99572d
HEADWORD: Galen
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Andreas Vesalius | Circulatory system | Commodus | Dissection | Marcus Aurelius | Philosophy | Pig
ENTRY:
Greco-Roman physician, surgeon, and philosopher whose writings shaped medicine from antiquity into the Renaissance. Born in Pergamon (present-day Bergama, Turkey), he traveled widely to study medical theories before settling in Rome, where he became personal physician to several emperors, including [[Marcus Aurelius]] and [[Commodus]]. His anatomy and medicine were principally shaped by the theory of the four humors. His anatomical reports were based mainly on [[Dissection|dissection]] of Barbary apes, and later of [[Pig|pigs]], because human dissection was forbidden in the Roman Empire by his time. His views dominated Western medical science for more than 1,300 years. His theory of the [[Circulatory system|circulatory system]] went unchallenged until c. 1242, when Ibn al-Nafis described pulmonary circulation. [[Andreas Vesalius]] published human dissections in *De humani corporis fabrica* in 1543, and Galen's physiology was adapted to them. Galen considered himself both a physician and a [[Philosophy|philosopher]], as his treatise *That the Best Physician Is Also a Philosopher* shows.

=== KEY: Galilean_moons_519e79

Four largest [[Jupiter|moons of Jupiter]], in descending size order: Ganymede, Callisto, Io, and Europa. They are visible with binoculars and were discovered in 1610 by [[Galileo Galilei]] and, independently, Simon Marius. They were the first objects found to orbit a planet other than Earth. === KEY: Galileo_Galilei_dfe6c3
HEADWORD: Galileo Galilei
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Isaac Newton | Milky Way | Moons of Jupiter | Nicolaus Copernicus | Polymath | Strength of materials | Telescope
ENTRY:
Italian astronomer, physicist, engineer, and [[Polymath|polymath]]. Born in Pisa, then part of the Duchy of Florence, the son of the lutenist and music theorist Vincenzo Galilei, he enrolled in 1580 at the University of Pisa for a medical degree. Galileo has been called the father of observational astronomy, classical physics, the scientific method, and modern science.

He studied speed and velocity, gravity and free fall, inertia, and projectile motion, and described the properties of the pendulum. In 1609 he improved the refracting [[Telescope|telescope]], reaching magnifications up to 30 times. With it he observed the stars of the [[Milky Way]], the phases of Venus, the four largest [[Moons of Jupiter|satellites of Jupiter]], Saturn's rings, lunar craters, and sunspots. These findings, detailed in *Sidereus Nuncius* (1610), supported the heliocentric model of [[Nicolaus Copernicus|Copernicus]].

His championing of heliocentrism met opposition from within the [[Catholic Church]] and from some astronomers. The Roman Inquisition investigated the matter in 1615 and concluded that his opinions contradicted accepted Biblical interpretations. His *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) appeared to dispute and satirize Pope Urban VIII, alienating both the Pope and the Jesuits, who had strongly supported him. Tried by the Inquisition, he was found "vehemently suspect of heresy", forced to recant, and kept under house arrest for the rest of his life. During that time he wrote *Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences* (1638), primarily on kinematics and the [[Strength of materials|strength of materials]]. His emphasis on mathematics and experiment influenced later scientists, among them [[Isaac Newton]].

Small hollow [[Vertebrate|vertebrate]] organ where bile is stored and concentrated before release into the small intestine. === KEY: Galley_e19685
HEADWORD: Galley
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cannon | Early modern period | Greek fire | Middle Ages | Phoenicia | Piracy | Ship
ENTRY:
Type of [[Ship|ship]] optimized for propulsion by oars, used in the Mediterranean for warfare, trade, and [[Piracy|piracy]]. Galleys had long, slender hulls, shallow drafts, and low freeboard, and most also carried sails for favorable winds. The type developed in the Mediterranean during antiquity and survived in various forms until the early 19th century. The primary warships of the [[Phoenicia|Phoenician]], Greek, and Roman navies were galleys, and the type remained dominant until the start of the [[Early modern period|early modern period]]. Oars let galleys make progress where sailing ships were becalmed, and their large crews could defend against pirates, which made them among the safest passenger vessels for Christian pilgrims in the High and Late [[Middle Ages]]. Ancient galleys had 15 to 30 pairs of oars. Weapons included rams and catapults, then [[Greek fire]] in the Early Middle Ages and [[Cannon|cannons]] from the 15th century. Full-rigged ships with broadside armament eventually made galleys obsolete.

=== KEY: Gallic_Wars_529bdc
HEADWORD: Gallic Wars
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: English Channel | Gaul | Julius Caesar | Rhine | Roman Empire | Roman Republic | Vercingetorix
ENTRY:
Roman campaigns against the peoples of [[Gaul]], 58–50 BC. Led by [[Julius Caesar|Gaius Julius Caesar]], the wars were fought against Gallic, Germanic, and Brittonic tribes in present-day France, Belgium, and Switzerland, and extended to Britain and Germania. They began with the migration of the Helvetii in 58 BC. Caesar undertook expeditions across the [[Rhine]] and the [[English Channel]] in 55 BC and the following year. The Roman victory at the Battle of Alesia in 52 BC, after [[Vercingetorix]] led a revolt, crushed the Gallic coalition. Gaul became a Roman province only in 27 BC. === KEY: Galois_theory_379284
HEADWORD: Galois theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Field (mathematics) | Group theory | Nth root | Permutation | Polynomial | Évariste Galois
ENTRY:
Mathematical connection between [[Field (mathematics)|field theory]] and [[Group theory|group theory]]. [[Évariste Galois]] introduced it to study the roots of [[Polynomial|polynomials]], characterizing the equations solvable by radicals in terms of the [[Permutation|permutation]] group of their roots. An equation is solvable by radicals if its roots can be written using only integers, [[Nth root|nth roots]], and the four basic arithmetic operations. This generalizes the Abel–Ruffini theorem, which asserts that a general polynomial of degree at least five cannot be solved by radicals. The fundamental theorem of Galois theory reduces certain problems in field theory to group theory, which makes them simpler. The theory shows that doubling the cube and trisecting the angle cannot be done as originally stated, and it is needed to prove the complete list of constructible regular polygons. Galois's work was published by Joseph Liouville fourteen years after his death, and the theory took time to become popular among mathematicians.

=== KEY: Gambling_12fe6f
HEADWORD: Gambling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Casino | Elizabeth I | Insurance | Mesopotamia | Monaco | Poker
ENTRY:
Wagering of something of value ("the stakes") on a random event with the intent of winning something else of value, where instances of strategy are discounted. Gambling requires three elements: consideration (an amount wagered), risk (chance), and a prize. The outcome may be immediate, as with a roll of dice or a spin of a roulette wheel, or may take longer, as with wagers on a sports season. "Gaming" typically refers to gambling specifically permitted by law, though the distinction is not universally observed. The legal gambling market totaled an estimated $335 billion in 2009. By one estimate, roughly 46% of adults gamble in a year and about 2% develop problem gambling.

Gambling dates back at least to the Paleolithic period. In [[Mesopotamia]], the earliest six-sided dice date to about 3000 BC. By the early 20th century gambling was almost uniformly outlawed in the United States, which helped spur organized crime; laws relaxed in the late 20th century.

Many jurisdictions ban gambling or regulate it by licensing vendors, which generally produces gambling tourism and illegal gambling elsewhere. Legal gambling provides significant government revenue, as in [[Monaco]] and Macau, China. In many American states one must be over 21 to enter a casino but may buy a lottery ticket at 18. Contracts of [[Insurance|insurance]] resemble wagers but are distinguished in law because either party has an interest in the outcome beyond the financial terms.

=== KEY: Game_63d720
HEADWORD: Game
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Chess | Game theory | Go (game) | Gupta Empire | Ludwig Wittgenstein | Mesopotamia | Video game
ENTRY:
Activity defined by a challenge and a set of rules, typically done for enjoyment, competition, or development. Games may be played informally or in professional competitive settings before audiences. Popular formats include board games, card games, video games, and sports, which are distinguished from unstructured play by having rules. Scholars have long debated the essence of games, and no single definition has achieved universal acceptance.

[[Ludwig Wittgenstein]] argued in *Philosophical Investigations* that play, rules, and competition each fail to define games, and concluded that the term covers disparate activities bearing only family resemblances to one another. Bernard Suits, in *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia* (1978), defined playing a game as engaging in activity directed toward a specific state of affairs, using only means permitted by rules that prohibit more efficient in favor of less efficient means; he called the players' acceptance of unnecessary obstacles the lusory attitude. The French sociologist Roger Caillois, in *Les jeux et les hommes* (1961), held that a game is fun, separate in time and place, uncertain in outcome, non-productive, governed by rules, and fictitious. In *Homo Ludens* (1938), Johan Huizinga treated play as a primary force in cultural development.

Games are a universal feature of human culture and have served for entertainment, teaching, ritual, and socialization. The Royal Game of Ur, from [[Mesopotamia]], and Senet, played in [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]], are among the oldest known games, dating to at least 2600 BC; fragments of a Senet board date from about 3100 BC, and a board inscribed for Amenhotep III survives intact. Both were race games and precursors of backgammon. The board game [[Go (game)|Go]] has written accounts as early as the Spring and Autumn period in China, and mancala has Neolithic versions found in Jordan from about 6900 BC. [[Chess]] traces to the [[Gupta Empire]] of India around the 6th century, with chaturanga a likely ancestor; it spread through Persia as chatrang, entered Europe by way of Moorish Spain, and approached its modern form by the 15th century. Playing cards may date to Tang dynasty China (618–907) and reached Europe in the 14th century via the Mamluk Sultanate. Pachisi, played in the Mughal court, underlies Parcheesi and Ludo, and the Aztecs played a race game called patolli.

The 20th century brought mass-market tabletop games such as Monopoly, Scrabble, Clue, and Trivial Pursuit, and the emergence of [[Video game|video games]]; Pong, the first commercially successful arcade video game, appeared in 1972. The video game industry has grown to surpass film and music in annual revenue, and one estimate gives its revenue as $182.7 billion in 2024. Psychologists link play to cognitive development in children and to the absorbed concentration called flow in adults, and [[Game theory|game theory]] uses the structure of games to model decision making in economics and evolutionary biology.

Haploid stage in the life cycle of plants and algae. === KEY: Gamma_function_a0bb1f

Most common extension of the factorial function to [[Complex number|complex numbers]]. Denoted by Γ, it is defined for all complex numbers except non-positive integers, and satisfies Γ(n) = (n−1)! for every positive integer n. [[Daniel Bernoulli]] first studied it. === KEY: Gamma_ray_a2948d

ENTRY:
Penetrating form of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]]. Gamma rays arise from high-energy interactions, such as the radioactive decay of atomic nuclei or astronomical events like solar flares. The French chemist and physicist Paul Villard discovered gamma radiation in 1900 while studying radiation from radium. Trans-boundary river of South Asia, 2,525 km long, flowing through India and Bangladesh. === KEY: Gas_635459
HEADWORD: Gas
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Density | Ideal gas law | Intermolecular force | Noble gas | Paracelsus | Plasma (physics) | Robert Boyle | Viscosity
ENTRY:
State of matter with neither fixed volume nor fixed shape, a compressible form of fluid. A pure gas consists of individual atoms, as in a [[Noble gas|noble gas]] such as neon, or of molecules, such as oxygen (O₂) or carbon dioxide; pure gases can also be mixed, as in air. What distinguishes gases from liquids and solids is the vast separation of the individual particles, which makes some gases invisible. The gaseous state lies between the liquid and [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] states.

The only elements that are stable diatomic molecular gases at standard temperature and pressure are hydrogen (H₂), nitrogen (N₂), oxygen (O₂), fluorine (F₂), and chlorine (Cl₂). In the atmosphere, nitrogen makes up about 78% and oxygen about 21%.

The word was first used in the early 17th century by the Brabantian chemist Jan Baptist van Helmont, who identified carbon dioxide, the first known gas other than air. It appears to be a phonetic transcription of the Greek *chaos*, following alchemical usage first attested in the works of [[Paracelsus]].

Gases are described by four macroscopic properties: pressure, volume, number of particles, and temperature. [[Robert Boyle]] trapped gas in the closed end of a J-shaped tube with a column of mercury and found that as pressure increased the volume decreased, the product of the two remaining constant. Such observations by Boyle, Jacques Charles, John Dalton, Joseph Gay-Lussac, and Amedeo Avogadro led to the [[Ideal gas law|ideal gas law]]. Because the particles are widely separated, gases have weaker [[Intermolecular force|intermolecular forces]] than liquids or solids, low [[Density|density]] and [[Viscosity|viscosity]], and diffuse to distribute themselves evenly throughout any container. According to kinetic molecular theory, the particles move in continuous random motion and exert pressure on their container through elastic collisions.

=== KEY: Gastroenteritis_a6e855
HEADWORD: Gastroenteritis

ENTRY:
Inflammation of the [[Gastrointestinal tract|gastrointestinal tract]], including the stomach and intestine, also known as infectious diarrhea. Symptoms include diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, and may include fever and dehydration. Signs usually begin 12–72 hours after infection and typically last less than two weeks. Although it is unrelated to [[Influenza|influenza]], it is often called "stomach flu" in Canada and the United States.

The disease is usually caused by viruses, though bacteria, parasites, and fungi can also be responsible. In children, [[Rotavirus|rotavirus]] is the most common cause of severe disease; in adults, [[Norovirus|norovirus]] and *Campylobacter* are common causes. Norovirus accounts for about 90% of viral gastroenteritis outbreaks among adults in America, which occur where groups of people spend time close together, as on a [[Cruise ship|cruise ship]]. The disease spreads through improperly prepared food, contaminated water, or close contact with an infected person.

Dehydration is a common complication. Reactive arthritis follows about 1% of *Campylobacter* infections and Guillain–Barré syndrome 0.1%. Repeated infections, typical where sanitation is poor and malnutrition common, can cause stunted growth and long-term cognitive delays.

Treatment centers on fluids: oral rehydration solution for mild or moderate cases, and intravenous fluids for severe ones. Continued [[Breastfeeding|breastfeeding]] is recommended for breastfed infants and [[Zinc|zinc]] supplementation for children. [[Antibiotic|Antibiotics]] are generally not needed, except for young children with fever and bloody diarrhea. Prevention includes hand washing with soap, clean water, and proper disposal of human waste, and the rotavirus vaccine is recommended for children.

In 2021 there were 4.67 billion cases and 1.17 million deaths worldwide. Children and people in the developing world are affected most; in 2011 there were about 1.7 billion cases, causing about 700,000 deaths of children under five. Adults are less affected, partly because of acquired immunity.

=== KEY: Gauge_theory_e6729e

Physical [[Field (physics)|field theory]] whose Lagrangian is invariant under local transformations of a Lie group. The domesticated gayal, or mithun, originated partly from the wild gaur and is most common in the border regions of Northeast India, Bangladesh and Myanmar.

=== KEY: Gaussian_elimination_a67074
HEADWORD: Gaussian elimination
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Carl Friedrich Gauss | Determinant | Matrix (mathematics)
ENTRY:
[[Algorithm]] for solving systems of linear equations by row operations, named after [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]]. The three elementary row operations are swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero number, and adding a multiple of one row to another. These operations do not change the solution set. Forward elimination reduces the matrix to row echelon form, which shows whether a system has no solution, a unique solution or infinitely many, and back substitution then finds the solution. The method can also compute the rank of a [[Matrix (mathematics)|matrix]], its [[Determinant|determinant]] and the inverse of an invertible matrix. Reducing the matrix fully to reduced row echelon form is called Gauss–Jordan elimination. For an n × n system, the method has cubic time complexity, O(n³).

=== KEY: Gaussian_function_1ad810
HEADWORD: Gaussian function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Error function | Exponential function | Function (mathematics) | Normal distribution | Signal processing
ENTRY:
Bell-shaped [[Exponential function|exponential]] [[Function (mathematics)|function]] named after [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]]: f(x) = a·exp(−(x−b)²/2c²). The parameter a is the height of the peak, b its horizontal center, and c the width, which corresponds to the standard deviation. When normalized, a Gaussian is the probability density function of a [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]] with mean μ and variance σ². It is used in [[Signal processing|signal processing]] for Gaussian filters, in image processing for Gaussian blurs, and in solving heat and diffusion equations. Its indefinite integral is the [[Error function|error function]].

Province of [[South Africa]], the smallest by land area and the most populous, with 26% of the national population (pop., 2022: 16.1 million). Its name comes from the Sotho-Tswana word for gold, and it was formed in 1994 from part of the old Transvaal, initially as PWV until its renaming in 1995. Gauteng is the wealthiest province and generates about 34% of national [[Gross domestic product|gross domestic product]]. [[Johannesburg]], the provincial capital and largest city, is the financial hub, and [[Pretoria]] is South Africa's administrative capital. Politically, it is the most closely contested province between the [[African National Congress]] and the liberal Democratic Alliance.

=== KEY: Gavrilo_Princip_144a20
HEADWORD: Princip, Gavrilo
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Austria-Hungary | Sarajevo | Terrorism | Tuberculosis | World War I
ENTRY:
Bosnian Serb student and member of Young Bosnia, who assassinated Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir presumptive to the throne of [[Austria-Hungary]], and his wife Sophie in [[Sarajevo]] on 28 June 1914. The assassination set off the July Crisis, which led within a month to the outbreak of [[World War I]]. Princip was spared the death penalty because of his age and sentenced to twenty years in prison. He died of [[Tuberculosis|tuberculosis]], worsened by harsh prison conditions. His legacy is contested: many Serbs regard him as a hero who opposed colonial oppression, while Bosniaks and Croats frequently view him as a [[Terrorism|terrorist]].

=== KEY: Gaza_City_bf0aa6
HEADWORD: Gaza City
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crusader states | Gaza Strip | Gaza war | Mandatory Palestine | Ottoman Empire | Philistines | Six-Day War | World War I
ENTRY:
City in the [[Gaza Strip]], Palestine, and capital of Gaza Governorate. It was home to Palestine's only port. Its population of 590,481 in 2017 was mostly displaced or killed in the [[Gaza war]]. Inhabited since at least the 15th century BC, it was ruled by ancient Egyptians, the [[Philistines]], Romans and [[Crusader states|Crusaders]] before the [[Ottoman Empire]] incorporated it in the 16th century. British forces took it in [[World War I]], and it became part of [[Mandatory Palestine]] in 1922. Israel occupied it in the [[Six-Day War]] in 1967. Israeli airstrikes have largely destroyed it since October 2023.

=== KEY: Gaza_Strip_b9d2a1
HEADWORD: Gaza Strip
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Egypt | Hamas | Israel | Mediterranean Sea | Oslo Accords | Palestine | West Bank
ENTRY:
Smaller of the two Palestinian territories, with the [[West Bank]], that make up the State of [[Palestine]]. The coastal strip borders [[Egypt]], [[Israel]] and the [[Mediterranean Sea]], and covers 365 sq km. Its boundaries date from the 1949 Armistice Agreements, when Egypt controlled the area. Israel captured it in the 1967 Arab–Israeli War and withdrew its forces in 2005. The [[Oslo Accords]] created the Palestinian Authority in the mid-1990s, but [[Hamas]] took over governance in 2007 after armed conflict with Fatah. Israel and Egypt have blockaded it since 2007. In 2010 the strip had just over 2 million people, mostly Palestinian refugees and their descendants, among the most densely populated territories in the world. Since the Gaza war began in 2023, Israeli operations have caused large-scale loss of life and displacement; scholars and human-rights organizations describe them as genocide, a charge Israel denies.
