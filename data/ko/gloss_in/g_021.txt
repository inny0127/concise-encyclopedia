=== KEY: Caesar_cipher_cbf372
HEADWORD: Caesar cipher
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cryptography | Encryption | Julius Caesar
ENTRY:
One of the simplest and most widely known [[Encryption|encryption]] techniques in [[Cryptography|cryptography]], in which each letter is replaced by a letter a fixed number of positions along the alphabet. With a shift of three, A becomes D. The method is named after [[Julius Caesar]], who used it in his private correspondence. Shifts wrap around from Z to A, and the shift serves as the key. As a single-alphabet substitution, it is easily broken and offers essentially no communications security. Its ROT13 variant, a shift of 13, remains in use for mild obfuscation.

=== KEY: Caesium_137_3bc173

ENTRY:
Radioactive isotope of caesium that forms as one of the more common [[Nuclear fission|fission]] products of [[Uranium-235|uranium-235]] in nuclear reactors and weapons. Its [[Half-life|half-life]] is about 30.04 years, and it decays by beta emission to stable barium-137. Its gamma rays, at 0.6617 MeV, come from the metastable barium-137m. [[Chemical element]] with symbol Cs and atomic number 55, a soft, silvery-golden [[Alkali metal|alkali metal]] with a melting point of 28.5 °C. === KEY: Cahokia_2a67d0

ENTRY:
Archaeological site of a pre-Columbian city in southwestern Illinois, directly across the [[Mississippi River]] from present-day [[St. Louis]]. The city existed from c. 1050 to c. 1350 and was the largest and most influential urban settlement of the Mississippian culture, which developed societies across much of the central and southeastern United States from around 1000. === KEY: Cai_Lun_5af87f
HEADWORD: Cai Lun
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bamboo | Eunuch | Han dynasty | Luoyang | Paper
ENTRY:
Chinese [[Eunuch|eunuch]] court official of the [[Han dynasty|Eastern Han dynasty]], traditionally credited with improving [[Paper|papermaking]].

In AD 105 he substantially improved the process by using tree bark, hemp waste, old rags and fishnets. The new paper quickly displaced [[Bamboo]] and wooden slips for writing, and Cai received wealth and fame throughout the empire. Earlier paper had existed since the 3rd century BC, so his contribution is usually described as an innovation rather than an invention. He served at the imperial court in [[Luoyang]] and was made a marquis in AD 114. After his ally, Empress Dowager Deng, died, he was ordered to the Ministry of Justice over his role in the death of Consort Song. Expecting a death sentence, he took his own life. He is revered in ancestor worship and deified as the god of papermaking, and he is the only early figure among China's Four Great Inventions whose name is known.

City (pop. more than 9.8 million), capital and largest city of [[Egypt]], near the head of the [[Nile]] Delta.

Greater Cairo has over 22 million people and is the largest urban agglomeration in Africa, the Arab world, and the Middle East. The area has been inhabited for c. 6000 years, and the Giza pyramid complex and the ancient cities of Memphis and Heliopolis lie within the modern city. The predecessor city was Fustat. Landmarks include the 12th-century Citadel of Saladin. The city is Egypt's center of finance, commerce, academics, and the arts. It is home to [[Al-Azhar University]], Egypt's oldest university and one of the oldest in the world, and to the oldest and largest film and music industry in Africa and the Arab world. The [[Arab League]] has its headquarters there, and the World Health Organization and several other UN agencies maintain regional offices.

Cairo suffers from high levels of pollution and traffic. Its metro, opened in 1987, is the oldest in Africa and carries over 1 billion passenger rides a year. The International Monetary Fund ranked its economy first in Africa in 2025.

Reactive [[Alkaline earth metal|alkaline earth metal]], chemical element of atomic number 20 (symbol Ca). === KEY: Calculus_0349a5
HEADWORD: Calculus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Differential calculus | Fundamental theorem of calculus | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Integral | Isaac Newton | Limit (mathematics) | Mathematics
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Mathematics|mathematics]] that studies continuous change.

Originally called infinitesimal calculus, it has two major branches. [[Differential calculus]] studies instantaneous rates of change and the slopes of curves, through the derivative of a function; [[Integral|integral calculus]] studies the accumulation of quantities and the areas under or between curves. The two are related by the [[Fundamental theorem of calculus]], which shows that differentiation and integration are inverse processes. Calculus relies on the convergence of infinite sequences and series to a well-defined [[Limit (mathematics)|limit]]. It generalizes elementary geometry and algebra: instead of the slope of a straight line it can describe the changing slope of a complicated curve, and it can find the distance traveled by an object whose velocity varies. For f(x) = x², the derivative is f′(x) = 2x.

The method of exhaustion, used by Eudoxus and [[Archimedes]] to approximate areas and volumes, is counted among its origins. In the late 17th century [[Isaac Newton]] and [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] each independently formulated infinitesimal calculus. The infinitesimal approach fell out of favor in the 19th century because the notion was difficult to make precise; the epsilon-delta approach to limits replaced it and became the standard approach during the 20th century, though infinitesimals were revived by non-standard analysis and smooth infinitesimal analysis.

The Latin word *calculus* means 'pebble', recalling the counters used on Roman counting boards. The term is also used for other systems of computation, such as the calculus of variations and the lambda calculus.

Calculus is used throughout physics and engineering, including classical mechanics and electromagnetism, and in chemistry, biology, medicine, and economics, wherever variable quantities change with time.

=== KEY: Calculus_of_variations_254c43
HEADWORD: Calculus of variations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Isaac Newton | Johann Bernoulli | Joseph-Louis Lagrange | Leonhard Euler | Mathematical analysis
ENTRY:
Field of [[Mathematical analysis|mathematical analysis]] that uses variations, or small changes in functions, to find the maxima and minima of functionals. Solutions are often found with the Euler–Lagrange equation. A classic problem is the shortest curve between two points on a surface, called a geodesic. Fermat's principle, that light follows the path of shortest optical length, is a related problem. The field began with [[Isaac Newton]]'s minimal resistance problem, solved in 1685 and published in his *Principia* in 1687, and with the brachistochrone problem posed by [[Johann Bernoulli]] in 1696. [[Leonhard Euler]] first elaborated the subject from 1733, and [[Joseph-Louis Lagrange]] contributed greatly to its theory. Euler renamed the subject the calculus of variations in 1756.

=== KEY: Calendar_8ff922
HEADWORD: Calendar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze Age | Gregorian calendar | Islamic calendar | Julian calendar | Julius Caesar | Muhammad | Unix time
ENTRY:
System of organizing time by naming periods, typically days, weeks, months, and years. Periods are usually, though not necessarily, synchronized with the cycle of the Sun or the Moon.

The word comes from *kalendae*, the first day of the month in the Roman calendar, related to the Latin verb *calare*, 'to call out', for the 'calling' of the new moon when first seen. The first recorded physical calendars were the [[Bronze Age]] Egyptian and Sumerian calendars. Most pre-modern calendars were lunisolar, adding an intercalary month occasionally to stay aligned with the solar year.

[[Julius Caesar]] reformed the Roman calendar in 46 BC. His [[Julian calendar]] followed an algorithm of a leap day every four years rather than observation of the new moon. The [[Gregorian calendar]], introduced in 1582, corrected most of the remaining difference between the Julian calendar and the solar year, and became the international civil standard. The [[Islamic calendar]] rests on the prohibition of intercalation by [[Muhammad]], producing an observation-based lunar calendar that shifts relative to the seasons.

Calendars fall into three main types: lunar (the Islamic calendar), solar (the Persian calendar), and lunisolar (the traditional Chinese calendar, the Hindu calendar, and the Hebrew calendar). The simplest systems count time from a reference date, or epoch, as in [[Unix time]]; most others combine year, month, and day. Proposals for reform, such as the World Calendar and the International Fixed Calendar, have failed to gain traction because of the loss of continuity and the upheaval they would involve.

=== KEY: Calligraphy_d2e863
HEADWORD: Calligraphy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Book of Kells | Brush | Li Si | Pen | Qin Shi Huang | Shang dynasty | Wang Xizhi
ENTRY:
Fine handwriting as a visual art, in which lettering or characters are designed and executed with a [[Pen|pen]], [[Brush|ink brush]], or other writing instrument. The word comes from Ancient Greek *kalligraphía*, 'beautiful writing'. It is a prominent art in East Asia and the Islamic world, where its form is often influenced by the meaning of the text.

In China the oldest known characters, oracle bone script, were carved on ox scapulae and tortoise plastrons under the [[Shang dynasty]] for divination. Bronzeware and large seal scripts followed. Around 220 BC the emperor [[Qin Shi Huang]] imposed reforms including [[Li Si]]'s unification of characters into a set of 3300 standardized small seal characters; clerical script was also authorized under him. The traditional regular script (*kǎi shū*), largely finalized by Zhong You, remains in use today, and printing encouraged the stabilization of character shapes. Masters such as [[Wang Xizhi]] exemplified spontaneous expression in running and cursive scripts. Chinese calligraphy is called *shūfǎ*, Japanese *shodō*, and Korean *seoye*; each remains highly regarded. Traditional East Asian writing uses the Four Treasures of the Study: ink brush, inkstick, paper, and inkstone.

In the Islamic world calligraphy flourished under Ottoman patronage from the 16th century, with scripts such as naskh and nasta'liq used for copying the Qur'an, devotional panels, and diplomas, and in mosques and homes. Western calligraphy, rooted in Roman capitals, developed through the insular majuscule of illuminated manuscripts such as the [[Book of Kells]] (c. 800) and the humanist italic of the Renaissance.

Western tools include quills, dip pens, fountain pens, reed pens, and brushes, with water-based ink.

=== KEY: Camera_967d35

ENTRY:
Instrument for capturing and storing images and video, either digitally with an electronic image sensor or chemically on a light-sensitive material such as [[Photographic film|photographic film]]. Cameras have shaped the visual arts, media, entertainment, surveillance, and scientific research.

All cameras share a basic design: light enters an enclosed box through a converging lens, and the image is recorded on a light-sensitive medium. A shutter controls how long light enters, an aperture adjusts the amount of light, and a viewfinder shows the scene. Most cameras record visible light; specialized ones capture other parts of the spectrum, such as infrared. Types include single-lens reflex (SLR), large-format and medium-format, compact, rangefinder, motion picture, and digital cameras.

Dry plates and roll film led to smaller, cheaper cameras, epitomized by the original Kodak, first produced in 1888.

After World War I Germany led camera development, with the Leica and the Contax. In the second half of the 20th century Japanese manufacturers advanced the technology: the Ricohflex III arrived in 1952 and the Olympus AutoEye, the first 35mm SLR with automatic exposure, in 1960. Electronics became integral in the 1970s, in models such as Polaroid's SX-70 and Canon's AE-1. Digital camera sales surpassed film cameras in the United States by 2003, using sensors such as the [[Charge-coupled device|charge-coupled device]]. Cameras built into [[Smartphone|smartphones]] have since blurred the line between dedicated cameras and multifunctional devices, and computational photography uses algorithms to enhance image quality.

Country (pop. over 41 million) in [[North America]], with its capital at [[Ottawa]].

=== KEY: Canal_a2ebca
HEADWORD: Canal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Grand Canal (China) | Industrial Revolution | Lock (water navigation) | Mars | Panama Canal | Rail transport | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Artificial waterway built for navigation, irrigation, drainage, or water supply.

Canals originated in ancient Mesopotamia more than 6,000 years ago, chiefly for irrigation. Most navigation canals use a series of dams and [[Lock (water navigation)|locks]] that create slack-water levels. A navigation runs roughly parallel to the valley of an existing river and shares its drainage basin, whereas a true canal cuts across a drainage divide to connect two basins. This is harder to construct and needs an external water source, as in the [[Panama Canal]], the best-known example. Where the channel must change elevation, locks, lifts, or elevators raise and lower vessels.

Canals fall into two broad types: waterways carrying vessels, either linking lakes, rivers, and seas or forming city networks such as those of Venice, Amsterdam, and Bangkok; and aqueducts, which convey water for drinking, hydropower, and irrigation. The [[Roman Empire]]'s aqueducts were water supply canals.

Canals once held immense importance for commerce. Bulk raw materials such as coal and ores were difficult and costly to move without water transport, and canals, including Britain's Bridgewater Canal, helped fuel the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Replacement began in the United Kingdom in the 1840s, when [[Rail transport|railways]] began to supersede canal shipping. By the early 1880s many canals could not compete and were abandoned, and after World War I motor trucks and expanding road networks further reduced canal freight. Today most ship canals serve bulk cargo and large vessels. The longest existing canal is the [[Grand Canal (China)|Grand Canal]] of northern China, stretching 1,794 km from Beijing to Hangzhou.

The term was once also applied to linear features seen on [[Mars]], which modern imaging showed to be a misnomer.

=== KEY: Cancer_746354

Group of diseases involving uncontrolled cell growth, typically resulting in tumors with the potential to invade or spread to other parts of the body. Malignant tumors contrast with benign tumors, which do not spread. Over 100 types affect humans.

Cancer arises when genetic changes, usually many, disrupt normal cell regulation. About 33% of cancer deaths are attributed to [[Tobacco|tobacco]] and alcohol, obesity, a diet lacking fruit and vegetables, and lack of exercise. An estimated 20 million new cases were diagnosed in 2022, with 9.7 million deaths. The most common cancers in males are [[Lung cancer|lung]], prostate, colorectal, and stomach cancer; in females, breast, colorectal, lung, and cervical cancer. Global economic costs were estimated at US$1.16 trillion per year as of 2010.

ENTRY:
Ability of an object to store electric charge, expressed as the ratio of the change in charge to the change in electric potential. Two closely related notions are commonly recognized: self-capacitance, measured between an object and ground, and mutual capacitance, measured between two components, as in a [[Capacitor]].
