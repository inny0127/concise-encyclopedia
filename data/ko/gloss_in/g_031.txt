=== KEY: Coral_reef_ecf467
HEADWORD: Coral reef
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atoll | Calcium carbonate | Charles Darwin | Coral | Last Glacial Period
ENTRY:
Underwater ecosystem formed from colonies of reef-building [[Coral|corals]] held together by [[Calcium carbonate|calcium carbonate]]. Coral reefs are sometimes called the rainforests of the sea. Shallow reefs occupy less than 0.1% of the world's ocean area but provide a home for at least 25% of marine species. Most reefs grow best in warm, shallow, clear and agitated water. Most coral reefs formed after the [[Last Glacial Period]], when melting ice raised sea levels and flooded continental shelves. [[Charles Darwin]] proposed that a fringing reef around a subsiding volcanic island becomes a barrier reef and finally an [[Atoll|atoll]]. Shallow tropical reefs have declined by 50% since 1950, threatened by excess nutrients, rising ocean heat, acidification, overfishing and pollution.

=== KEY: Coral_reef_fish_8cfcb0
HEADWORD: Coral reef fish
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camouflage | Coral reef | Herbivore
ENTRY:
Marine fish found among or closely associated with [[Coral reef|coral reefs]]. Coral reefs occupy less than 1% of the surface area of the world's oceans but provide a home for 25% of marine fish species. The Indo-Pacific has an estimated 4,000–5,000 reef fish species, and the greater Caribbean has another 500–700. Most are ray-finned fishes, and many have spines that lock in place or are venomous. Some use [[Camouflage|cryptic coloration]] to avoid predators, while small fish hide in crevices or shoal. Surgeonfishes are [[Herbivore|herbivores]] that graze turf algae and also clean marine turtles. Habitat loss, pollution and overfishing threaten reef fish.

=== KEY: Coral_snake_99b9a8
HEADWORD: Coral snake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Argentina | Southern United States | United States
ENTRY:
Large group of elapid snakes, divided into Old World and New World coral snakes. The 83 New World species, in the genera *Micruroides* and *Micrurus*, range from the [[Southern United States|southern United States]] to [[Argentina]]. The 27 Old World species live in Asia and Africa. Most are secretive, burrowing snakes that spend much of their time beneath the ground or in leaf litter, and they feed mainly on smaller snakes and lizards. Their neurotoxic venom acts slowly, and bites are seldom lethal. As of 2018, only two confirmed fatalities had been documented in the [[United States]] in the preceding 100 years from the genus *Micrurus*. Coral snakes account for less than one percent of snake bites each year in the United States.

=== KEY: Coriolis_force_9523d9
HEADWORD: Coriolis force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artillery | Cyclone | Equator | Northern Hemisphere
ENTRY:
Pseudo-force acting on moving objects within a rotating frame of reference. Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis described it in an 1835 paper on the energy yield of machines with rotating parts, such as waterwheels. On Earth the force deflects moving air and water to the right of their direction in the [[Northern Hemisphere|Northern Hemisphere]] and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere. This shapes large-scale winds and ocean currents and causes the rotation of [[Cyclone|cyclones]]. The effect is greatest near the poles and vanishes at the [[Equator]]. It is imperceptible for everyday objects but matters for large-scale atmospheric motion and for [[Artillery|artillery]] and missile trajectories.

Subfamily of enveloped RNA [[Virus|viruses]] in the family Coronaviridae. Coronaviruses infect [[Mammal|mammals]] and birds. In humans, mild varieties cause some cases of the [[Common cold]], while more lethal ones cause SARS, MERS and [[COVID-19]]. === KEY: Corporation_ae63fe
HEADWORD: Corporation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Corpus Juris Civilis | Dutch East India Company | Hudson's Bay Company | Julius Caesar | Maurya Empire | Spice | Stock | Sweden
ENTRY:
Legal entity authorized by the state to act as a single entity and recognized in law as a legal person. Early corporations were established by charter, an act of a monarch or legislature; most jurisdictions now allow incorporation through registration. Corporations are usually divided by whether they can issue stock and whether they are formed to make a profit.

Shareholders own a registered corporation, and their liability is generally limited to their investment. Limited liability separates control from ownership: a passive shareholder is not personally liable for the corporation's contractual obligations or torts. Supporters credit it with encouraging investment and risk-taking, while critics argue that it can encourage moral hazard.

The word derives from Latin *corpus*, "body." Roman law recognized corporate entities as *universitas*, *corpus*, or *collegium*. After the Lex Julia under [[Julius Caesar]], *collegia* required the approval of the Senate or emperor; they could own property, make contracts, and sue and be sued. The concept was revived in the 11th–14th centuries with the recovery of Justinian's [[Corpus Juris Civilis]]. Early entities carrying on business included the *sreni* of the [[Maurya Empire]] in India. The alleged oldest commercial corporation, the Stora Kopparberg mining community in Falun, [[Sweden]], received a charter in 1347.

In the 17th century, chartered companies such as the [[Dutch East India Company]] and the [[Hudson's Bay Company]] led European colonial ventures. The Dutch company defeated Portuguese forces in the Moluccas to profit from European demand for [[Spice|spices]]; its investors held paper share certificates, traded them on the Amsterdam [[Stock|stock]] exchange, and were granted limited liability. Speculation in joint-stock companies also contributed to the 1720 South Sea Bubble.

=== KEY: Corpus_Juris_Civilis_67b992
HEADWORD: Corpus Juris Civilis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Civil law (legal system) | Common law | Constantinople | Justinian I
ENTRY:
Collection of Roman legal works, enacted 529–534 by order of [[Justinian I]]. It comprised the *Codex*, a compilation of imperial enactments; the *Digest* or *Pandects*, extracts from the writings of Roman jurists; and the *Institutes*, a student textbook. All three were given force of law and were intended as the sole source of law. Later imperial laws, the *Novellae Constitutiones*, are counted as a fourth part. The work was directed by Tribonian in [[Constantinople]] and composed almost entirely in Latin. Revised into Greek, it formed the basis of Byzantine law through the *Basilika* until the 15th century. Rediscovered in medieval Western Europe, it became the foundation of law in all [[Civil law (legal system)|civil law]] jurisdictions and influenced the canon law of the [[Catholic Church]]. Its influence on [[Common law]] systems was less marked.

=== KEY: Correlation_5f750c
HEADWORD: Correlation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cauchy–Schwarz inequality | Covariance | Francis Galton | Karl Pearson | Random variable | Standard deviation
ENTRY:
Statistical relationship between two [[Random variable|random variables]], usually a linear one. The most common measure is the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient, denoted ρ or r. It is the [[Covariance|covariance]] of two variables divided by the product of their [[Standard deviation|standard deviations]], ranging from −1 to +1. Pearson's coefficient is sensitive only to linear relationships, which may be present even when one variable is a nonlinear function of the other. [[Karl Pearson]] developed it from a similar idea by [[Francis Galton]]. Spearman's rank correlation is more robust and detects less structured relationships.

Correlation is not the same as dependence: independent variables are uncorrelated, but uncorrelated variables may still be dependent. A correlation alone does not indicate causation, yet correlations are useful for prediction: an electric utility, for example, produces less power on a mild day. The absolute value of Pearson's coefficient cannot exceed 1, a corollary of the [[Cauchy–Schwarz inequality]].

=== KEY: Corrosion_15631a
HEADWORD: Corrosion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diffusion | Iron | Metal | Oxygen | Paint | Rust | Stainless steel | Zinc
ENTRY:
Gradual deterioration of materials, usually [[Metal|metals]], by chemical or electrochemical reaction with their environment. In its most common form, a metal is oxidized by an oxidant such as [[Oxygen]] or hydrogen ions in aqueous solution, producing oxides or salts. [[Rust|Rusting]], the formation of red-orange [[Iron]] oxides, is the most familiar example. Corrosion can form pits or cracks or spread across a surface, and because it is a [[Diffusion|diffusion]]-controlled process it occurs on exposed surfaces. In galvanic corrosion, two different metals in electrical contact within an electrolyte cause the more active metal, the anode, to corrode faster. [[Zinc]] is often used as a sacrificial anode for steel structures. Corrosion costs approximately $2.5 trillion globally each year in maintenance and replacement. Prevention includes protective [[Paint]] and chromium alloying to form passive oxide layers, as in [[Stainless steel]].

=== KEY: Corruption_658a15
HEADWORD: Corruption
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fraud | Socrates | United Nations
ENTRY:
Dishonest or fraudulent conduct by those in positions of authority, for illicit personal gain. Corruption includes [[Fraud|fraud]], bribery, influence peddling and embezzlement, as well as some practices that are legal in many countries, such as lobbying. Political corruption occurs when an office-holder or other governmental employee acts in an official capacity for personal gain. Historically the term had a broader moral meaning: the ancient Greek philosopher [[Socrates]] was condemned to death partly for "corrupting the young." Corruption is perceived as most common in kleptocracies, oligarchies, narco-states and authoritarian states, but it also occurs in wealthy capitalist economies. Critics argue that global measures such as the Corruption Perceptions Index mostly survey executives about bribery and under-measure the legalized corruption of the rich. Strategies against corruption are grouped under anti-corruption, and the [[United Nations]] Sustainable Development Goal 16 aims to reduce it substantially.

=== KEY: Cosmic_distance_ladder_3c2119
HEADWORD: Cosmic distance ladder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cepheid variable | Large Magellanic Cloud | Parsec | Redshift | Supernova
ENTRY:
Succession of methods by which astronomers determine distances to celestial objects. Also called the extragalactic distance scale, the ladder is needed because no single method works at all ranges. Direct measurement works only within about 1,000 [[Parsec|parsecs]]. Each rung calibrates the next: [[Cepheid variable|Cepheid variables]] calibrate distances to the [[Large Magellanic Cloud]], and [[Supernova|Type Ia supernovae]] extend measurements to distant galaxies. Combining supernova distances with [[Redshift|redshift]] data gives Hubble constant values of 73–75 km/s/Mpc, while cosmic microwave background data give about 67 km/s/Mpc, a discrepancy known as the Hubble tension. Precision is about 1% at the nearest anchors and degrades to 5–10% at cosmological scales.

=== KEY: Cosmic_inflation_a30dd6
HEADWORD: Cosmic inflation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Field (physics) | Particle physics | Universe
ENTRY:
Theory of exponential expansion of space in the very early [[Universe|universe]]. This expansion supercooled the universe and ended when the energy of the driving [[Field (physics)|field]] condensed into hot, dense particles, a process called reheating. Inflation theory was developed in the late 1970s and early 1980s by, among others, Alexei Starobinsky, Alan Guth and Andrei Linde, who shared the 2014 Kavli Prize. Quantum fluctuations in the inflationary region, magnified to cosmic size, became the seeds of cosmic structure. Many physicists believe inflation explains why the universe is isotropic and flat and why no magnetic monopoles have been observed. Temperature variations measured by the COBE satellite in 1992 agree with its predictions, although the [[Particle physics|particle physics]] mechanism remains unknown and some scientists dissent.

=== KEY: Cosmic_microwave_background_3b886c
HEADWORD: Cosmic microwave background
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Dark matter | Microwave | Nobel Prize | Observable universe | Photon | Redshift
ENTRY:
[[Microwave]] radiation that fills the [[Observable universe|observable universe]], a relic of the [[Big Bang]]. Its energy density exceeds that of all the photons emitted by all the stars in history. American radio astronomers Arno Penzias and Robert Woodrow Wilson discovered it by accident, and it is key evidence for the Big Bang theory. As the universe expanded, protons and electrons combined into neutral atoms in the recombination epoch, and [[Photon|photons]] began to travel freely; their wavelengths have since stretched through [[Redshift|cosmological redshift]]. The background is very uniform, but sensitive detectors measure temperature variations of about one part in 100,000. Its power spectrum reveals the density of normal and [[Dark matter|dark matter]]. Satellites including COBE, WMAP and Planck mapped it, and COBE work earned John Mather and George Smoot the 2006 [[Nobel Prize|Nobel Prize]] in Physics.

High-energy [[Proton|proton]] or atomic nucleus moving at nearly the speed of light. Cosmic rays come from the Milky Way, from distant galaxies and from the Sun. Victor Hess discovered them in 1912 with balloon experiments that showed radiation increasing with altitude, and he received the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics. Protons make up about 89% of primary cosmic rays, with helium nuclei about 10%. On striking the atmosphere they produce showers of secondary particles, but most are deflected into space by [[Earth's magnetic field|Earth's magnetic field]] or the [[Heliosphere]]. Neutrinos and gamma rays from the [[Blazar|blazar]] TXS 0506+056 in 2018 suggest that active galactic nuclei also produce cosmic rays, and Fermi Space Telescope data have been interpreted as evidence that many originate from [[Supernova|supernova]] explosions.

=== KEY: Cosmological_constant_9431fc
HEADWORD: Cosmological constant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Dark energy | Edwin Hubble | General relativity | Quantum field theory | Vacuum
ENTRY:
Coefficient in the field equations of [[General relativity|general relativity]] representing the energy density of empty space. [[Albert Einstein]] introduced it in 1917 to counterbalance gravity and produce a static universe, then abandoned it after [[Edwin Hubble]] confirmed that the universe was expanding. Most physicists took it to be zero from the 1930s until 1998, when the discovery that expansion is accelerating suggested a positive value. Denoted Λ, it is the simplest explanation for [[Dark energy|dark energy]], which makes up about 68% of the universe's mass–energy density in the ΛCDM model. [[Quantum field theory|Quantum field theory]], however, predicts a [[Vacuum|vacuum]] energy exceeding the observed value by some 120 orders of magnitude, a discrepancy called the cosmological constant problem.

=== KEY: Cotton_db547a
HEADWORD: Cotton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cellulose | Cotton gin | Indus Valley Civilisation | Industrial Revolution | Malvaceae | Marco Polo | Peru
ENTRY:
Soft, fluffy staple fiber that grows in a protective boll around the seeds of plants of the genus *Gossypium*, in the mallow family [[Malvaceae]]. The fiber is almost pure [[Cellulose|cellulose]]. The plant is a shrub native to tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, Africa, and India; wild species are most diverse in Mexico. Cotton was domesticated independently in the Old and New Worlds. Spun into yarn or thread, it makes soft, breathable, durable cloth and is the most widely used natural fiber in clothing.

Four species are grown commercially: *Gossypium hirsutum* (upland cotton, 90% of world production), *G. barbadense* (extra-long staple, over 5%), *G. arboreum* (tree cotton, less than 2%), and *G. herbaceum* (Levant cotton, less than 2%).

The word derives ultimately from Hebrew *kuttōnĕṯ* and was the usual medieval Arabic word for cotton. [[Marco Polo]] described cotton grown in abundance in Khotan, today's Xinjiang.

Early evidence includes *G. barbadense* at Nanchoc, [[Peru]], dated to the 7th–6th millennia BC, and a cotton thread joining eight copper beads at Mehrgarh, Pakistan, dated to the sixth millennium BC. Fragments from the Bronze Age [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] date to the 3rd millennium BCE. In Mexico, *G. hirsutum* was domesticated between around 3400 and 2300 BC, and cotton sent to Aztec rulers as tribute amounted to about 53,000 tonnes annually.

The [[Cotton gin]], invented in 1793, lowered production costs, and cotton supplied the textile mills of the [[Industrial Revolution]]. World production is about 25 million tonnes, or 110 million bales, annually, on 2.5% of the world's arable land. India is the largest producer, and the United States has been the largest exporter for many years.

=== KEY: Cotton_gin_ff0b6c

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Machine that quickly separates [[Cotton|cotton]] fibers from their seeds, far faster than manual separation. Roller gins were used in the [[Indian subcontinent]] from around the 5th century. === KEY: Coulomb_s_law_b37809
HEADWORD: Coulomb's law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Daniel Bernoulli | Electromagnetism | Newton's law of universal gravitation
ENTRY:
Law of physics describing the electrostatic force between two electrically charged particles at rest. The force is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Like charges repel and unlike charges attract, along the line joining them. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb published the law in 1785, though earlier investigators including [[Daniel Bernoulli]] and [[Alessandro Volta]] had suspected an inverse-square dependence. Like [[Newton's law of universal gravitation|Newton's law of universal gravitation]], it is an inverse-square law, but electrostatic forces can attract or repel, while gravity always attracts. The law was essential to the development of [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]] and has been tested from 10⁻¹⁶ m to 10⁸ m.

=== KEY: Country_59716c

Political entity or its territory, a term applied to sovereign states, unrecognized states, constituent countries, and dependent territories. A country may consist of multiple nations, a structure called a multinational state. There is no universal agreement on the number of countries, because some entities are not recognized as sovereign by the UN system but are recognized by at least one member. *The Economist* wrote in 2010 that "any attempt to find a clear definition of a country soon runs into a thicket of exceptions and anomalies."

LINK TARGETS: Atom | Electron | Gilbert N. Lewis | Hydrogen | Ionic bonding | Irving Langmuir | Organic chemistry | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Chemical bond formed by the sharing of [[Electron|electron]] pairs between [[Atom|atoms]]. In a covalent bond the shared pairs, also called bonding pairs, give each atom the equivalent of a full valence shell, a stable electronic configuration. Covalent bonding is much more common than [[Ionic bonding|ionic bonding]] in [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]], where atoms are merely held together by electrostatic attraction. The term "covalence" was introduced by [[Irving Langmuir|Irving Langmuir]] in 1919, and [[Gilbert N. Lewis|Gilbert N. Lewis]] described shared electron pairs in 1916. In the molecule H₂, the two [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] atoms share two electrons. Walter Heitler and Fritz London gave the first [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanical]] explanation of a chemical bond, in molecular hydrogen, in 1927. Covalent character is greatest between atoms with similar electronegativities. Electrons shared among more than two atoms are called delocalized.

=== KEY: Covariance_c85e97
HEADWORD: Covariance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Correlation | Expected value | Random variable | Standard deviation | Variance
ENTRY:
Measure of the joint variability of two [[Random variable|random variables]]. Covariance is positive when the variables tend to show similar behavior and negative when they tend to show opposite behavior. Its magnitude reflects the geometric mean of the [[Variance|variances]] shared by the two variables, so a larger magnitude indicates stronger mutual dependence. Its units are those of X times those of Y, so changing the units changes its value proportionally, which makes strength hard to judge from covariance alone. The [[Correlation|correlation coefficient]] normalizes covariance to a value between −1 and 1 by dividing by the product of the [[Standard deviation|standard deviations]]. For jointly distributed variables X and Y, covariance is the [[Expected value|expected value]] of the product of their deviations from their means: cov(X,Y) = E[(X − E[X])(Y − E[Y])]. Covariance is zero for independent variables, though the converse does not hold.

=== KEY: Cretaceous_Paleogene_extinction_event_01662b
HEADWORD: Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chicxulub crater | Cretaceous | Dinosaur | Iridium | Paleogene | Photosynthesis | Plesiosaur | Pterosaur
ENTRY:
Mass extinction at the end of the [[Cretaceous]] period, around 65.5 million years ago. It eliminated roughly three-quarters (75%) of plant and animal species on Earth. The non-avian [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]], all [[Pterosaur|pterosaurs]], [[Plesiosaur|plesiosaurs]], mosasaurs, ammonites and rudists died out, as did most other tetrapods weighing more than 25 kg (55 lb), though some ectothermic species such as sea turtles survived. A thin worldwide layer of sediment marks the K–Pg boundary, with unusually high levels of [[Iridium|iridium]], a metal more common in asteroids than in Earth's crust. The leading explanation is an asteroid 10 to 15 km (6 to 9 mi) wide that formed the [[Chicxulub crater]] on the Yucatán Peninsula. Its impact caused a prolonged impact winter that halted [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] in plants and plankton. Mammals diversified in the following [[Paleogene]] Period, and the surviving dinosaurs radiated into modern birds.

Third and last period of the [[Mesozoic|Mesozoic Era]], lasting from about 143.1 to 66 million years ago. At around 77.1 million years, it is the ninth and longest geological period of the [[Phanerozoic]]. Its name derives from the Latin *creta*, meaning [[Chalk|chalk]], which is abundant in deposits from the latter half of the period. The climate was relatively warm, with high sea levels that created numerous shallow inland seas. Dinosaurs continued to dominate the land, and the earliest relatives of placentals and [[Marsupial|marsupials]] appeared, as did the earliest crown group birds toward the end. [[Flowering plant|Flowering plants]] appeared in the Early Cretaceous and became the dominant plants worldwide by the end of the period, as widespread gymnosperm groups declined. The period ended with the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event|Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction]], in which many groups, including non-avian dinosaurs, pterosaurs and large marine reptiles, died out.

The sport spread globally with the expansion of the [[British Empire]], and its earliest known definite reference is to play in southeast England in the mid-16th century. The International Cricket Council (ICC) has over 100 members, twelve of them full members who play Test matches. The sport is played mainly in [[India]], [[Pakistan]], Bangladesh, [[Sri Lanka]], [[Australia]], New Zealand, England and Wales, South Africa and the West Indies. Australia has won eight One Day International trophies, including six World Cups.

[[Orthoptera|Orthopteran]] insect of the family Gryllidae, related to bush crickets and, more distantly, to [[Grasshopper|grasshoppers]]. Crickets have cylindrical bodies, long antennae and enlarged hind legs for jumping. Males produce a loud, persistent chirp by rubbing their leathery forewings together, mostly at night. Crickets are distributed worldwide except at latitudes of 55° or higher, with the greatest diversity in the [[Tropics|tropics]]. The largest members of the family are the bull crickets, *Brachytrupes*, up to 5 cm (2 in) long. Crickets appear in literature, including *The Adventures of Pinocchio* and [[Charles Dickens|Charles Dickens]]'s *The Cricket on the Hearth*, and in poems by [[William Wordsworth]] and [[John Keats]]. They are kept as pets in countries from [[China]] to Europe and are eaten deep-fried in Southeast Asia.
