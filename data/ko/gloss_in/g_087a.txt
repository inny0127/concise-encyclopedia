=== KEY: Meditation_3ed75f
HEADWORD: Meditation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Christianity | Hinduism | Islam | Jainism | Judaism | Taoism | Upanishads
ENTRY:
Practice in which an individual uses a technique or combination of techniques to train attention and awareness and to detach from "discursive, ruminating thought," aiming at a mentally clear, emotionally calm, and stable state. Techniques are broadly classified as focused (concentrative) methods, which direct attention to an object such as the breath or a mantra, and open-monitoring methods, which include mindfulness and awareness of subjective experience in the present moment.

The earliest records of meditation (*dhyana*) are found in the [[Upanishads]], and it plays a salient role in [[Jainism]], [[Buddhism]], [[Hinduism]], and [[Taoism]]. Meditation-like techniques are also known in [[Judaism]], [[Christianity]], and [[Islam]] in the context of remembrance of, and prayer and devotion to, God. The English word comes from Latin *meditatio*, from *meditari*, "to think, contemplate, devise, ponder." In the Catholic tradition, *meditatio* as a formal, stepwise process dates to at least the 12th-century monk Guigo II.

Meditation is hard to define because it covers dissimilar practices in different traditions, and no definition of necessary and sufficient criteria has gained wide acceptance in the scientific community. A study by Bond and colleagues (2009), using a panel of 7 experts, identified three essential criteria: a defined technique, logic relaxation, and a self-induced state.

Asian techniques have spread to other cultures and are applied in non-spiritual contexts such as business and health. Jon Kabat-Zinn developed Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction in the late 1970s. Several meta-analyses of clinical studies of mindfulness have demonstrated mental and physical health benefits. Research, initially criticized for methodological weaknesses, now commonly employs randomized controlled trials and active control groups. Meditation can occasionally exacerbate anxiety or depression in vulnerable individuals.

=== KEY: Mediterranean_Sea_980a86
HEADWORD: Mediterranean Sea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Black Sea | Bosporus | Red Sea | Roman Empire | Strait of Gibraltar | Suez Canal
ENTRY:
Intercontinental sea between Europe, Asia, and Africa, almost completely enclosed by land. In the west it connects to the [[Atlantic Ocean]] through the [[Strait of Gibraltar]], only 14 km wide; in the northeast to the [[Black Sea]] through the [[Bosporus]]; and in the southeast to the [[Red Sea]] via the [[Suez Canal]].

The sea covers about 2,500,000 sq km, 0.7% of the global ocean surface, and includes fifteen marginal seas. Its average depth is 1,500 m, and the deepest recorded point is 5,109 ± 1 m in the Calypso Deep in the Ionian Sea. About 5.9 million years ago it was cut off from the Atlantic and was partly or completely desiccated over some 600,000 years during the Messinian salinity crisis, before the Zanclean flood refilled it about 5.3 million years ago.

Twenty countries border the sea or its marginal seas, from Spain, France, and Italy to Egypt, Libya, and Morocco; Cyprus and Malta are island countries within it. Sicily and Sardinia are its largest islands.

The Levant was among the first regions with permanent human habitation, as early as 12,000 BC, and Egypt, Greece, and the Fertile Crescent saw some of the earliest civilizations. The sea was an important route for trade, colonization, and conquest. The [[Roman Empire]], which called it *Mare Nostrum* ("Our Sea"), is the only state to have controlled all of its coasts. The name *Mare Mediterrāneum*, Latin for "in the middle of land," is first attested in the 6th century. Today the sea faces eutrophication, plastic pollution, and warming waters, and irregular crossings from North Africa to Europe have resulted in over 35,000 documented deaths since 2014.

=== KEY: Mediterranean_and_Middle_East_theatre_of_World_War_II_a7a098
HEADWORD: Mediterranean and Middle East theater of World War II
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eastern Front (World War II) | Erwin Rommel | Malta | Operation Torch
ENTRY:
Major theater of operations of World War II, covering the Mediterranean, North Africa, the Balkans and the Middle East. Combat began on June 10, 1940, when Italy declared war on the United Kingdom and France, and ended on May 2, 1945. Driven by Italian ambitions for a new Roman Empire, Italy invaded France, bombed [[Malta]] and invaded Greece in October 1940, which prompted German intervention in April 1941. In North Africa, [[Erwin Rommel]]'s forces made early gains, but Allied victories at El Alamein in late 1942 and in [[Operation Torch]] forced the Axis to surrender in Tunisia by May 1943. The Allies then invaded Italy. German forces diverted from the Western and [[Eastern Front (World War II)|Eastern Front]] suffered over two million losses. Italy lost around 177,000 men, Britain around 300,000 killed, wounded or captured, and the United States around 130,000.

=== KEY: Mediterranean_climate_139dff
HEADWORD: Mediterranean climate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: California | Chaparral | Chile | Köppen climate classification | Mediterranean Sea | South Africa
ENTRY:
Temperate climate type with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, found mainly on western coasts between about 30° and 45° north and south latitude. Also called a dry summer climate, it is designated Cs in the [[Köppen climate classification|Köppen system]]. It occurs around the [[Mediterranean Sea]] and in coastal [[California]], central [[Chile]], the Cape region of [[South Africa]] and southwestern Australia. Its vegetation includes [[Chaparral|chaparral]], fynbos and maquis, and the region has traditionally been suited to wheat, grapes and olives.

=== KEY: Medusa_68e064
HEADWORD: Medusa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Athena | Greek mythology | Herodotus | Hesiod
ENTRY:
Female monster in [[Greek mythology]], one of the three Gorgons. Medusa was usually described as having living snakes for hair, and anyone who looked at her was turned to stone. Unlike her immortal sisters Stheno and Euryale, she was mortal. The hero Perseus beheaded her with help from [[Athena]], using a mirrored shield to avoid her gaze, and gave the head to Athena, who placed it on her shield. The Gorgons were described by [[Hesiod]] as daughters of the sea gods Phorcys and Ceto, and [[Herodotus]] associated them with Libya. In classical antiquity the image of her head, the Gorgoneion, served as an evil-averting device. Medusa was pregnant by Poseidon when she was beheaded, and the winged horse Pegasus and the giant Chrysaor sprang from her body.

=== KEY: Meerkat_4cdd45
HEADWORD: Meerkat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mongoose | Southern Africa
ENTRY:
Small social [[Mongoose|mongoose]] of [[Southern Africa|southern Africa]], also called the suricate (*Suricata suricatta*). Meerkats live in packs of two to 30 individuals that occupy home ranges of about 5 sq km. Dominant animals usually breed, while subordinate members help care for the pups; litters of three to seven are born after a gestation of 60 to 70 days. Meerkats feed mainly on insects, with small birds, reptiles and plants also eaten, and they retreat to burrows when danger appears. The species is listed as Least Concern by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN).

=== KEY: Megabat_fd01db
HEADWORD: Megabat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Bat | Eurasia | Oceania | Public health
ENTRY:
Family of fruit-eating [[Bat|bats]] (Pteropodidae), also called flying foxes. The family contains about 197 species, as of 2018, and is found in tropical and subtropical areas of [[Eurasia]], [[Africa]] and [[Oceania]]. It likely evolved in Australasia. Megabats include the largest bats, with some species weighing up to 1.45 kg and having wingspans of up to 1.7 m, although nearly a third of species weigh less than 50 g. With few exceptions they cannot echolocate, relying instead on sight and smell to find mainly fruit and nectar. Most species bear one offspring at a time after a pregnancy of four to six months, and a quarter of species are threatened, mainly by habitat destruction and overhunting. As natural reservoirs of several viruses that affect humans, megabats are of interest to [[Public health|public health]] researchers.

=== KEY: Megafauna_43c82b
HEADWORD: Megafauna
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alfred Russel Wallace | Human | Pleistocene
ENTRY:
Large animals, commonly defined as those weighing about 45 kg or more. Megafauna suppress the growth of woody vegetation, reducing wildfire frequency, and help regulate the abundance of smaller animals. During the [[Pleistocene]], megafauna were diverse across the globe. From about 50,000 years ago, most large mammal species became extinct, including 80% of mammals over 1,000 kg, while small animals were largely unaffected. [[Human|Humans]] and climatic change are the most likely causes, though their relative importance is disputed. One of the earliest uses of the term appeared in [[Alfred Russel Wallace]]'s 1876 work on animal distribution.

=== KEY: Megalith_f909f1
HEADWORD: Megalith
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brittany | Bronze Age | Göbekli Tepe | Neolithic | Stonehenge | Sweden
ENTRY:
Large stone used, alone or with other stones, to construct a prehistoric structure or monument. The word was first used in 1849 by the British antiquarian Algernon Herbert in reference to [[Stonehenge]]; it derives from the Greek *mégas* ("great") and *líthos* ("stone"). More than 35,000 megalithic structures have been identified across Europe, from [[Sweden]] to the Mediterranean. Most were erected between the [[Neolithic]] and the [[Bronze Age]]. The oldest known ceremonial structures, at [[Göbekli Tepe]] in Turkey, date to c. 9000 BC. Common types include the menhir, a single upright stone; the dolmen, a stone table usually covered with earth; and alignments, such as the Carnac Stones in [[Brittany]], France, which consist of thousands of stones. Stone circles are called cromlechs in most languages.

=== KEY: Megalodon_34bd64
HEADWORD: Megalodon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cetacean | Great white shark | Sea turtle | Whale
ENTRY:
Extinct giant mackerel shark, *Otodus megalodon*, that lived from about 23 to 3.58 million years ago. Once classified with the [[Great white shark]], it is now placed in the family Otodontidae. Estimated maximum lengths range from 14.2 to 24.3 m, and its bite force is thought to have been 108,500 to 182,200 newtons. It probably preyed on large marine animals, including [[Whale|whales]], seals and [[Sea turtle|sea turtles]]. Its decline may have been caused by oceanic cooling, falling sea levels, reduced whale prey and competition with whale-eating [[Cetacean|cetaceans]].

=== KEY: Megalopolis_f68217
HEADWORD: Megalopolis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boston | Massachusetts | Metropolitan area | United States | Washington, D.C.
ENTRY:
Group of neighboring [[Metropolitan area|metropolitan areas]] perceived as one continuous urban region, also called a supercity or megaregion. Linked by common systems of transport, economy and ecology, a megalopolis is integrated enough that coordinating policy across it is valuable, although its constituent metropolises keep their own identities. The term comes from the Greek for "big city" and was first applied to the Arcadian city of Megalopolis, founded in the 4th century BC. In the late 1950s and early 1960s the political scientist Jean Gottmann applied it to the urbanized northeastern seaboard of the [[United States]], from [[Boston]], [[Massachusetts]], to [[Washington, D.C.]] Gottmann defined a megalopolis as having a population of about 25 million. In China the official term is "city cluster."

=== KEY: Megathrust_earthquake_cdf9e3
HEADWORD: Megathrust earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Oceanic trench | Thrust fault | Tsunami
ENTRY:
Type of very powerful earthquake occurring at convergent plate boundaries, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another. It results from slip along a [[Thrust fault|thrust fault]] at the contact between the two plates. Megathrust earthquakes are the planet's most powerful, with moment magnitudes that can exceed 9.0; every earthquake of magnitude 9.0 or greater since 1900 has been one. Because the faults often lie beneath [[Oceanic trench|oceanic trenches]], they can abruptly displace the seafloor and generate [[Tsunami|tsunamis]] that are often more destructive than the earthquake itself, including teletsunamis that cross ocean basins.

=== KEY: Megatsunami_7f7600
HEADWORD: Megatsunami
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1883 eruption of Krakatoa | Alaska
ENTRY:
Extremely large wave created by a sudden displacement of material into water, such as a landslide, meteor impact or volcanic eruption. Ordinary tsunamis, caused by seafloor displacement from earthquakes, rarely exceed 30 m in height, whereas megatsunamis can have initial wave heights in the hundreds of meters because the displacement splashes water upward and outward. Examples include the wave from the [[1883 eruption of Krakatoa|1883 eruption of Krakatoa]] and the 1958 Lituya Bay landslide in [[Alaska]], which produced a runup of 524.6 m. The term has no precise definition but is commonly used for waves over 100 m high.

State in [[Northeast India|northeastern India]], with Shillong as its capital (pop., 2014 est.: 3,211,474). Formed on January 21, 1972, from districts carved out of [[Assam]], it covers 22,429 sq km. === KEY: Mehmed_II_78686d
HEADWORD: Mehmed II

LINK TARGETS: Anatolia | Byzantine Empire | Constantinople | Istanbul | John Hunyadi | Ottoman Empire | Turkey
ENTRY:
[[Ottoman Empire|Ottoman]] sultan (r. 1444–1446, 1451–1481), known as Mehmed the Conqueror. He was sultan twice. During his first reign he defeated a crusade led by [[John Hunyadi]], which followed Hungarian violations of the Treaties of Edirne and Szeged. Restored to the throne in 1451, he strengthened the navy and at the age of 21 captured [[Constantinople]] in 1453, effectively ending the [[Byzantine Empire]]. He then claimed the title caesar of Rome, a claim recognized by the Patriarchate of Constantinople but rejected by most European monarchs. Mehmed reunified [[Anatolia]], campaigned as far west as Bosnia, implemented political and social reforms and patronized the arts and sciences. He is considered a hero in modern [[Turkey]], and [[Istanbul]]'s Fatih district and the Fatih Mosque are named after him.

=== KEY: Mehmed_IV_67b834
HEADWORD: Mehmed IV
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Battle of Vienna | John III Sobieski | Suleiman the Magnificent
ENTRY:
Ottoman sultan (r. 1648–1687), nicknamed Mehmed the Hunter for his love of hunting. He came to the throne at the age of six after his father, Sultan Ibrahim, was overthrown in a coup. His reign was the second longest in Ottoman history after that of [[Suleiman the Magnificent]]. Its first and last years brought defeat and instability, but during the Köprülü era, under Grand Vizier Köprülü Mehmed Pasha from 1656, the empire revived. Ottoman forces regained the Aegean islands and Crete from Venice during the Cretan War (1645–1669) and campaigned successfully against Transylvania and Poland. In 1683 the Ottomans suffered a catastrophic defeat at the [[Battle of Vienna]] by Polish-Lithuanian and imperial forces led by [[John III Sobieski]]. Soldiers overthrew Mehmed in 1687 during the War of the Holy League, and he retired to Edirne, where he died. Contemporaries called him a *gazi*, or holy warrior.

=== KEY: Meiji_Restoration_b6275c
HEADWORD: Meiji Restoration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boshin War | Emperor Meiji | Industrialisation | Japan | Tokugawa shogunate
ENTRY:
Political event of 1868 that restored imperial rule in [[Japan]] under [[Emperor Meiji]]. On January 3, 1868, the emperor declared that political power had been restored to the Imperial House, ending more than two centuries of rule by the [[Tokugawa shogunate]]. Its origins lay in economic and political difficulties under the shogunate and in pressure from foreign powers, which challenged the policy of sakoku; after the Perry Expedition, Japan was forced to open to the West under unequal treaties. Samurai from domains such as Satsuma and Chōshū overthrew the shogunal system, and the [[Boshin War]] followed. The new government abolished the domain system and eventually the samurai class, and Japan rapidly [[Industrialisation|industrialized]] and adopted Western ideas. The Meiji Constitution of 1889 remained in place until the Allied occupation after World War II.

=== KEY: Meiosis_ed87e3
HEADWORD: Meiosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chromosome | Down syndrome | Fertilisation
ENTRY:
Cell division that produces haploid gametes, such as sperm and egg cells, in sexually reproducing organisms. Meiosis consists of two rounds of division that produce four cells, each with one copy of each [[Chromosome|chromosome]]. Before division, DNA is replicated and homologous chromosomes pair and exchange genetic material by recombination, which creates new combinations of genes. Because two haploid gametes fuse at [[Fertilisation|fertilization]], alternating meiosis and fertilization sustain sexual reproduction in animals, plants and fungi. Errors that produce an abnormal number of chromosomes, known as aneuploidy, are the leading known cause of miscarriage and the most frequent genetic cause of developmental disabilities, including [[Down syndrome]].

=== KEY: Mekong_734409
HEADWORD: Mekong
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambodia | Laos | Myanmar | Southeast Asia | Thailand | Tibet | Tibetan Plateau | Vietnam
ENTRY:
Transboundary river of East and [[Southeast Asia|Southeast Asia]], the world's twelfth-longest river. It rises on the [[Tibetan Plateau]], where it is called the Lancang River in China, and flows through [[Myanmar]], [[Laos]], [[Thailand]], [[Cambodia]] and southern [[Vietnam]]. Its estimated length is 4,909 km and its drainage area is 795,000 sq km, and it discharges 475 km³ of water annually. The name Mae Nam Khong combines *mae nam* ("mother of water[s]"), used in Thai and Lao for large rivers, with *khong*, an archaic word for river. Rapids, waterfalls and extreme seasonal variations make navigation difficult, although the river remains a major trade route between [[Tibet]] and Southeast Asia. Dams built along the river since the 2000s have caused serious ecosystem problems, including exacerbated drought.
