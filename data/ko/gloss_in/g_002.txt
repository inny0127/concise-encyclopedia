=== KEY: Adenosine_triphosphate_5732ec
HEADWORD: Adenosine triphosphate
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Nucleoside triphosphate that supplies free energy in living cells. ATP provides about 58 kJ/mol (0.6 eV) to drive processes such as [[Muscle contraction|muscle contraction]], nerve impulse propagation and chemical synthesis. It is found in all known forms of life and is often called the "molecular unit of currency" for energy transfer. When consumed, it converts to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) or adenosine monophosphate (AMP), and it is regenerated by processes such as [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]]. It is also a precursor to [[DNA]] and [[RNA]]. An average adult recycles about 50 kilograms of ATP daily (about 100 [[Mole (unit)|moles]]). It was first isolated in 1929 by Karl Lohmann.

=== KEY: Adi_Shankara_8f66f8
HEADWORD: Adi Shankara
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bhagavad Gita | Brahman | Kerala | Mahayana | Shiva | Upanishads | Vijayanagara Empire | Vishnu
ENTRY:
Indian monk and philosopher, the foremost teacher of Advaita Vedanta. He wrote influential commentaries on the Brahma sutras, ten principal [[Upanishads]], and the [[Bhagavad Gita]], and the *Upadeśasāhasrī*; these are the works widely accepted as his own, though over 300 texts are attributed to him. Scholars generally place him in the first half of the 8th century, whereas Advaita monasteries date him to the 5th century BC.

Reliable information on his life is scant. Traditional accounts describe a child prodigy born into a Nambudiri Brahmin family at Kaladi in [[Kerala]], who mastered the Vedas young, won his mother's consent to become a monk, and became a disciple of Govinda Bhagavatpada on the Narmada River. He is said to have traveled across the Indian subcontinent, defeating rival schools in debate, and to have died at 32. Hagiographies of the 14th–17th centuries, written long after his death, portray him as founding four monasteries (*mathas*), organizing the Dashanami monastic order, and reconciling the worship of [[Shiva]], [[Vishnu]], and other deities as forms of one [[Brahman]]. Some scholars argue that the historical Shankara was relatively obscure and that his later fame grew when the Sringeri monastery received patronage from the [[Vijayanagara Empire]]. Heads of certain monasteries bear the title Shankaracharya.

His authentic works present a harmonizing reading of Hindu scripture centered on liberating knowledge that the individual self (*jīvātman*) is identical with ātman-Brahman, knowledge he held to arise from hearing the Upanishadic great sayings. He treated the Upanishads as the authoritative means of knowledge, beyond the ritual exegesis of Mīmāṃsā. His thought shows influences from [[Mahayana]] Buddhism despite his criticisms of it; some Vaishnava opponents called him a "crypto-Buddhist," a charge the Advaita tradition rejects.

=== KEY: Adolescence_dba40c
HEADWORD: Adolescence
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Estrogen | Heredity | Pituitary gland | Puberty | Testosterone | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Transitional stage of human physical and psychological development between puberty and adulthood. The term derives from Latin *adolescere*, "to mature." It is usually associated with the teenage years, but its physical, psychological, and cultural expressions may begin earlier or end later. Age is only a rough marker, and scholars have not agreed on a precise definition; some definitions run from 10 to as late as 30. The [[World Health Organization]] designates ages 10 to 19.

Biologically, adolescence begins with [[Puberty|puberty]], which averages 10–11 years of age for girls and 11–12 for boys. A surge of hormone production, triggered by the [[Pituitary gland|pituitary gland]], activates the gonads, which release [[Testosterone|testosterone]] in the testes and [[Estrogen|estrogen]] in the ovaries. Secondary sex characteristics appear, such as a deeper voice in boys and breast development in girls, along with rapid changes in height, weight, body composition, and the circulatory and respiratory systems. The landmarks are the first ejaculation in males (on average at 13) and menarche, the onset of menstruation, in females (typically around 13, give or take two or three years). Timing is influenced mainly by [[Heredity|heredity]], and also by diet, exercise, and body fat; malnutrition delays menarche.

Timing has psychological and social consequences. Early-maturing boys tend to be taller, stronger, and more confident but are more likely to engage in risky behavior, whereas late-maturing boys can suffer from poor body image. Early-maturing girls tend to be more self-conscious and are more likely to develop eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa. Physical growth and cognitive development can continue beyond the teens.

=== KEY: Adoption_985d38
HEADWORD: Adoption
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Code of Hammurabi | Common law | Inheritance | Massachusetts | Napoleonic Code | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Legal process of permanently assuming the parenting of a child in place of the original parents. Legal adoption transfers all rights and responsibilities, along with filiation, to the adoptive parents, and requires societal recognition through legal or religious sanction. The [[Code of Hammurabi]] detailed the rights of adopters and adoptees. In the [[Roman Empire]], adoption strengthened political ties between wealthy families, and many emperors were adopted sons. Medieval bloodline-based nobility largely rejected adoption; [[Common law|English common law]] did not permit it because it contradicted the customary rules of [[Inheritance|inheritance]]. France's [[Napoleonic Code]] made adoption difficult, requiring adopters to be over 50, sterile, at least 15 years older than the adoptee, and to have fostered the adoptee for at least six years. Modern systems, arising in the 20th century, are governed by comprehensive statutes. Regulated adoption in the United States began in [[Massachusetts|Massachusetts]] with the 1851 Adoption of Children Act. About 50,000 children were adopted from U.S. foster care in fiscal year 2023.

=== KEY: Adposition_727401

LINK TARGETS: Hindi | Latin | Noun | Turkic languages | Urdu
ENTRY:
Class of words expressing spatial or temporal relations or semantic roles. Adpositions express relations such as in, under, towards and ago, or roles such as of and for. An adposition typically combines with exactly one complement, usually a [[Noun|noun]] phrase, to form an adpositional phrase that modifies or completes another element of a clause. The word preposition comes from [[Latin]] prae- ("before") and ponere ("to put").

=== KEY: Adrenal_gland_53452b
HEADWORD: Adrenal gland
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrenaline | Blood pressure | Cortisol | Immune system | Kidney | Metabolism
ENTRY:
Endocrine gland located above each [[Kidney|kidney]]. Also called suprarenal glands, each has an outer cortex that produces steroid hormones and an inner medulla. The cortex has three zones. The zona glomerulosa produces mineralocorticoids such as aldosterone, which regulate [[Blood pressure|blood pressure]] and electrolyte balance. The zona fasciculata produces glucocorticoids, including [[Cortisol|cortisol]], which regulate [[Metabolism|metabolism]] and suppress the [[Immune system|immune system]]. The zona reticularis produces androgens. The medulla produces [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]] and other catecholamines, which trigger rapid responses in stress situations. Overproduction of cortisol causes Cushing's syndrome, while insufficient production is associated with Addison's disease. The glands measure approximately 5 cm in length, 3 cm in width and up to 1 cm in thickness, and their combined weight in an adult ranges from 7 to 10 grams.

=== KEY: Adrenaline_abe1a4
HEADWORD: Adrenaline
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrenal gland | Anxiety | Asthma | Blood sugar level | Cardiac arrest | Glaucoma | Heart | Neurotransmitter
ENTRY:
Hormone and medication, also known as epinephrine. Normally produced by the [[Adrenal gland|adrenal glands]] and a small number of neurons in the medulla oblongata, it plays an essential role in the fight-or-flight response, increasing blood flow to muscles, increasing [[Heart|heart]] output by acting on the SA node, dilating the pupils and raising [[Blood sugar level|blood sugar]]. Common side effects include shakiness, [[Anxiety|anxiety]] and sweating. The adrenal medulla contributes over 90% of circulating adrenaline, and a small number of neurons use it as a [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitter]].

=== KEY: Adult_5a600f
HEADWORD: Adult
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Firearm | Marriage | Puberty | Voting
ENTRY:
Fully grown animal; in humans, a person who has attained legal and social independence. Biologically, an adult is an organism that has reached sexual maturity and is capable of reproduction. Historically and across cultures, human adulthood was determined chiefly by the onset of [[Puberty|puberty]], and the passage from child to adult was often marked by a coming-of-age test or ceremony. A person may be physically mature by about age 15 or 16 yet not be an adult in law until a later age.

Legally, an adult has reached the age of majority, when parents lose their rights and responsibilities regarding the person. The typical age is 18, although it varies by country, and in some places is 20 or 21. The age of majority may be set separately from the minimum ages for contracts, [[Marriage|marriage]], [[Voting|voting]], employment, military service, [[Firearm|firearms]], driving, alcohol, smoking, gambling, and other activities. In the U.S., for example, one cannot vote or join the armed forces until 18, and many legal and financial responsibilities begin at 21. Most modern societies set legal adulthood by age alone, without requiring a demonstration of maturity.

Definitions of adulthood are often inconsistent. A person may be biologically adult and behave maturely but still be treated as a child under the law, while another may be legally an adult yet lack maturity and responsibility. Media claims since 2005 that the brain reaches full maturity at age 25 rest on a 2004–05 imaging study of participants no older than 21; later studies show prefrontal cortex maturation continuing past age 30. Whether any single age of full mental maturity exists remains debated.

=== KEY: Aeolian_processes_4e5151
HEADWORD: Aeolian processes
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Dune | Wind
ENTRY:
Erosion, transportation and deposition of sediment by [[Wind|wind]]. Named after Aeolus, the Greek god of the winds, these processes are important in arid environments with sparse vegetation, lack of soil moisture and abundant unconsolidated sediment, although water is a far more powerful eroding force. Deflation removes fine particles such as silt and clay, while abrasion by windborne sand forms ventifacts and yardangs. Particles smaller than 0.2 mm are carried in suspension and form loess; the thickest known deposit, 335 m, lies on [[China|China]]'s Loess Plateau. Common types include barchans and transverse dunes.

=== KEY: Aesthetics_0fe549
HEADWORD: Aesthetics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Dada | David Hume | Immanuel Kant | Plato
ENTRY:
Branch of philosophy that studies beauty, taste, and related phenomena. In a broad sense it includes the philosophy of art, which examines the nature of art, artistic creativity, the meanings of artworks, and audience appreciation. The relation between the two fields is disputed: some hold the philosophy of art to be a subfield of aesthetics, which also covers beauty in nature and everyday life, while others take it to be the broader discipline.

Aesthetic properties are features that shape an object's appeal, including general values such as beauty and ugliness and more specific ones such as grace and elegance. Philosophers debate whether these properties exist objectively or depend on observers' subjective experience. A common view holds that aesthetic experience involves disinterested pleasure, detached from practical concerns. Taste is a subjective sensitivity to aesthetic qualities, and differences in taste lead to disagreements about aesthetic judgments. Some definitions of art stress intrinsic aesthetic qualities, while others treat art as a socially constructed category. Twentieth-century movements such as [[Dada]] challenged traditional definitions by prizing idea over craftsmanship.

Aesthetic thought has roots in antiquity: [[Plato]] linked beauty to ideal forms, and [[Aristotle]] discussed imitation (*mimesis*) and catharsis. [[David Hume]] argued that taste is refined by custom and practice, and [[Immanuel Kant]] described aesthetic judgment as disinterested pleasure arising from the free play of imagination and understanding. Aesthetics emerged as a distinct field in the 18th century; the German philosopher Alexander Baumgarten coined the Latin term *aesthetica* in 1735, defining it as the study of sensibility. The word comes from Greek *aisthesis*, "sensation, perception." Comparative aesthetics analyzes Western, Indian, Chinese, Islamic, and African traditions, and other fields examine the psychology of aesthetic experience and its roles in ethics and religion.

=== KEY: Africa_f5cd26
HEADWORD: Africa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: African Union | Algeria | Ancient Egypt | Ethiopia | Great Zimbabwe | Liberia | Madagascar | Nigeria
ENTRY:
Continent, the world's second largest and second most populous after Asia, covering about 30.3 million sq km, including adjacent islands, or about 20% of Earth's land area. It straddles the equator and the prime meridian and is bounded by the Mediterranean Sea to the north, the Red Sea and Gulf of Suez to the northeast, the Indian Ocean to the southeast, and the Atlantic Ocean to the west. Its population is the youngest of any continent, with a median age of 19.7 in 2012 against a world median of 30.4, and is projected to exceed 3.8 billion by 2100.

The continent includes [[Madagascar]] and various archipelagos. It contains 54 fully recognized sovereign states, eight cities and islands belonging to non-African states, and two de facto independent states with limited or no recognition. [[Algeria]] is the largest country by area and [[Nigeria]] the largest by population. The [[African Union]], headquartered in Addis Ababa, coordinates cooperation among states. Africa is the least wealthy inhabited continent per capita, which scholars attribute to factors including geography, climate, corruption, colonialism, the Cold War, and neocolonialism, yet it also hosts many of the world's fastest-growing economies.

Africa has the largest number of megafauna species of any continent, having been least affected by the Pleistocene extinctions. It faces desertification, deforestation, water scarcity, and pollution, and the UN Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change has identified it as the continent most vulnerable to climate change.

Eastern Africa in particular is widely accepted as the origin of humans and the great apes. The earliest hominids are dated to about 7 million years ago, and *Homo sapiens* is believed to have originated there 350,000 to 260,000 years ago. Kingdoms and empires rose and fell, among them [[Ancient Egypt]], Makuria, the Fatimids, Mali, the Asante, Kongo, the Luba, [[Great Zimbabwe]], and the Zulu, although many societies were stateless. African societies have generally recorded history through oral tradition. Slave trades created diasporas, especially in the Americas. From the late 19th to the early 20th century most of the continent was conquered and colonized by European powers, except [[Ethiopia]] and [[Liberia]], and colonies were maintained for economic exploitation and extraction of resources. Most present states emerged through decolonization after World War II, founded the Organisation of African Unity in 1963 (the predecessor of the African Union), and retained their colonial borders.

The name derives from the Latin *Afri*, applied to people west of the Nile, probably a native Berber tribe. Under Rome, the province of Africa Proconsularis was created after the victory over Carthage in the Third Punic War in 146 BC. Several other etymologies have been proposed, among them Latin *aprica*, "sunny," and Phoenician *ʿafar*, "dust." Some scholars criticize the terms "North Africa" and "Sub-Saharan Africa" for their roots in colonial thought.

Diverse beliefs and practices of the peoples of [[Africa]], comprising many ethnic religions. Most can be described as animistic, with polytheistic and pantheistic aspects. The role of humanity is generally seen as harmonizing nature with the supernatural.

[[Islam]] and [[Christianity]] have largely displaced indigenous religions, but they are often adapted to African cultural contexts, and many people combine them with traditional practice; a 2010 Pew survey of 19 African countries found that many respondents held several beliefs derived from traditional or animist sources. The [[Yoruba religion]] and the Igbo religion Odinala are reported to be growing, and they are practiced in the Caribbean and in parts of Central and South America. === KEY: Afterlife_055ede
HEADWORD: Afterlife
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Heaven | Hell | Hinduism | Jainism | Plato | Pythagoras | Sikhism
ENTRY:
Postulated continued existence of an individual's consciousness or identity after the death of the physical body. The surviving essential aspect varies among belief systems: it may be a partial element or the entire soul or spirit, carrying personal identity. Major views derive from religion, esotericism, and metaphysics, and philosophers since antiquity have debated them, with dualists such as [[Plato]] arguing for an immortal soul and materialists questioning personal identity without a physical body.

In some views the continued existence occurs in a supernatural realm, such as paradise or the underworld, to which the dead go as determined by their god or gods according to actions and beliefs in life. In others the individual is reborn into this world, a process called reincarnation. Reincarnation is part of the *saṃsāra* and *karma* doctrine of cyclic existence in all the major Indian religions: [[Hinduism]], [[Buddhism]], [[Jainism]], and [[Sikhism]]. Belief in rebirth was also held by Greek figures such as [[Pythagoras]] and Plato, and is found in tribal societies in Australia, East Asia, Siberia, and South America. Most Abrahamic denominations reject reincarnation, but groups such as the Cathars, the Druze, and followers of Kabbalah hold it. Christianity and Islam teach a final judgment, with the righteous entering heaven (in Islam, *Jannah*) and others facing hell.

[[Heaven]] is commonly depicted as a higher, holy place where gods, angels, saints, or venerated ancestors live, accessible according to standards of goodness, piety, or faith. [[Hell]] is a place or state of punishment after death; religions with a linear divine history sometimes depict it as eternal, while those with a cyclic history usually treat it as an interval between incarnations. Believers cite theological arguments for divine justice and reports of near-death experiences; skeptics point to the lack of conclusive empirical evidence.

=== KEY: Age_of_Discovery_58e94b
HEADWORD: Age of Discovery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Azores | Canary Islands | Christopher Columbus | Columbian exchange | Globalization | Kerala | Magellan expedition | Vasco da Gama
ENTRY:
Period of European overseas exploration, c. 1418–c. 1620, also called the Age of Exploration, part of the early modern period and overlapping the Age of Sail. Seafarers from European countries explored, colonized, and conquered regions across the globe, connecting previously isolated parts of the world and laying the groundwork for [[Globalization|globalization]]. The opening of maritime routes to the East Indies and the colonization of the Americas by the Portuguese and Spaniards, later joined by the English, French, and Dutch, spurred international trade and created European colonial empires.

Portuguese exploration began with expeditions to the Macaronesian islands, including the [[Canary Islands]], Madeira, and the [[Azores]], continued along the coast of West Africa from 1434, and culminated in [[Vasco da Gama]]'s sea route to India in 1498, which established a Portuguese presence in [[Kerala]] and the Indian Ocean. Spain sponsored the voyages of [[Christopher Columbus]] (1492–1504), which began the colonization of the Americas, and the [[Magellan expedition]] (1519–22), which opened a route from the Atlantic to the Pacific and, under Juan Sebastián Elcano, completed the first circumnavigation of the globe. Spain also conquered Mexico (1519–21) and Peru (1532–33) and ran the Manila galleon trade route (1565–1815), which linked the Americas and Asia. Expeditions to Asia, Africa, and Oceania continued into the 19th century, followed by polar exploration in the 20th.

Exploration began the [[Columbian exchange]], the transfer of plants, animals, enslaved people, diseases, and culture between the Old World and the New World. Mapping the world reshaped European views, and the continents on European maps developed from abstract shapes into recognizable outlines. New diseases, especially among American Indians, caused rapid population declines in some regions, and the era saw widespread enslavement, exploitation, and military conquest of Indigenous peoples. The term "discovery" has been scrutinized by historians; J. H. Parry, calling the period the Age of Reconnaissance, stressed that it also expanded geographical knowledge and empirical science.

=== KEY: Age_of_Enlightenment_85e98b
HEADWORD: Enlightenment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Deism | Encyclopédie | French Revolution | Immanuel Kant | Isaac Newton | John Locke | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
European intellectual and cultural movement of the late 17th and 18th centuries, marked by an emphasis on reason, empirical evidence, and the scientific method. Also called the Age of Reason, it emerged in western and central Europe, reached its peak in the 18th century, and spread to the European colonies in the Americas and Oceania.

The movement grew out of the [[Scientific Revolution]], whose figures included Galileo, Francis Bacon, and [[Isaac Newton|Newton]]. Philosophical foundations were laid by Descartes, Hobbes, Spinoza, and [[John Locke|Locke]]. Dating varies: some historians begin the period with Descartes' *Discourse on the Method* (1637), others with Newton's *Principia Mathematica* (1687). Traditional European chronology runs from the death of Louis XIV in 1715 to the [[French Revolution]] in 1789, though many historians now extend it into the early 19th century.

Ideas circulated through scientific academies, literary salons, coffeehouses, Masonic lodges, and an expanding print culture. Among the best known publications was the [[Encyclopédie]] (1751–72, 35 volumes), compiled by Diderot, d'Alembert, and about 150 others. Other landmark works include Montesquieu's *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748), Rousseau's *The Social Contract* (1762), and Adam Smith's *The Wealth of Nations* (1776). [[Immanuel Kant|Kant]]'s essay *Answering the Question: What Is Enlightenment?* gave the movement its motto, *sapere aude* ("dare to know").

Central doctrines were individual liberty, representative government, the rule of law, and religious tolerance, in contrast to absolute monarchy and religious persecution. Some currents went further, arguing for [[Deism]] or atheism, secular states, and the suppression of religious orders, including the Jesuits. Critics, whom Isaiah Berlin later termed the "Counter-Enlightenment," defended traditional religious and political authority against rationalist critique,.

Its ideas weakened the authority of monarchies and established churches and helped prepare the political revolutions of the 18th and 19th centuries. Liberalism, socialism, and neoclassicism all trace their intellectual heritage to it.

=== KEY: Ageing_951075
HEADWORD: Aging
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atherosclerosis | Dementia | Jeanne Calment | Menopause | Natural selection | Stem cell
ENTRY:
Process of becoming older, in which an organism accumulates changes over time and its functions decline. The term refers mainly to humans, many other animals, and fungi; bacteria, perennial plants, and some simple animals are potentially biologically immortal, though evidence of aging has been reported in the bacterium *Escherichia coli* and in yeast.

In humans, aging comprises physical, psychological, and social change. Reaction time may slow while knowledge accumulates. Of roughly 150,000 people who die each day worldwide, about two-thirds die of age-related causes. Typical changes include falling female fertility after age 35, presbyopia by age 45–50, and [[Menopause|menopause]] between ages 44 and 58. Osteoarthritis incidence reaches 53% at ages 60–64 and [[Dementia|dementia]] affects about 3% of people aged 65–74, 19% of those aged 75–84, and nearly half of those over 85. [[Atherosclerosis]] is classed as an aging disease and leads to cardiovascular disease, the most common cause of death worldwide. Maximum human lifespan is suggested to be about 115 years; the oldest person recognized by the *Guinness Book of Records* was [[Jeanne Calment]], who reached 122.

Theories fall into two groups. Damage theories hold that accumulating harm, such as DNA oxidation, causes biological systems to fail; programmed theories hold that internal processes, such as epigenetic maintenance, inherently cause aging. Programmed aging is distinct from programmed cell death (apoptosis). Evolutionary accounts treat aging as a by-product of [[Natural selection|natural selection]] favoring early-life fitness. Researchers have cataloged twelve proposed hallmarks of aging, including [[Stem cell|stem cell]] exhaustion, and debate which are causes and which are correlates.

Normal human cells in culture die after about 50 divisions (the Hayflick limit, discovered 1961), whereas cancer cells and germ cells can be effectively immortal. In nematodes, flies, and mice, caloric restriction and genetic changes extend healthy lifespan; interventions in humans remain largely preclinical.

=== KEY: Agriculture_8e54e9
HEADWORD: Agriculture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aurochs | Civilization | Deforestation | Green Revolution | Irrigation | Maize | Mesoamerica
ENTRY:
Cultivation of plants and raising of animals to produce food, fibers, fuels, and raw materials. Broader definitions include forestry and aquaculture.

Agriculture was a key factor in the rise of sedentary human [[Civilization|civilization]], since farming of domesticated plants and animals created food surpluses that allowed people to live in cities. Humans gathered wild grains from at least 105,000 years ago, but deliberate planting began only about 11,500 years ago. Sheep, goats, pigs, and cattle were domesticated about 10,000 years ago. Crops were independently developed in at least 11 regions of the world. The word derives from Latin *agricultura*, from *ager* ("field") and *cultura* ("cultivation").

Regional beginnings differ. Rice was domesticated in southern China, cattle from the wild [[Aurochs|aurochs]] in what is now Turkey and Pakistan, and pigs from wild boar in Eurasia. In the Andes, the potato, beans, coca, llamas, alpacas, and guinea pigs were domesticated; in [[Mesoamerica]], the wild grass teosinte was bred into [[Maize|maize]] between 10,000 and 6,000 years ago; sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and sorghum in the Sahel. The horse was domesticated on the Eurasian Steppe around 3500 BC. The Sumerians, relying on the Tigris and Euphrates and a canal system for [[Irrigation|irrigation]], had plows by about 3000 BC, and Egyptian farming depended on the Nile's seasonal flooding. China had a nationwide granary system from the 5th century BC and iron-share heavy plows by the late 2nd century. The Aztecs built terraces and artificial islands called chinampas.

Major products are foods (cereals, vegetables, fruits, cooking oils, meat, milk, eggs), fibers, fuels, and raw materials such as rubber and timber. Annual output is about 11 billion tonnes of food, 32 million tonnes of natural fiber, and 4 billion m³ of wood; roughly 14% of food is lost before reaching retail. As of 2021, small farms of one hectare or less produced about a third of the world's food, while the 1% of farms larger than 50 hectares held more than 70% of farmland.

In the 20th century, industrial agriculture based on large monocultures came to dominate output. Plant breeding, fertilizers, pesticides, and mechanization sharply raised yields, and advocates credit these advances, often grouped as the [[Green Revolution]], with averting famine and supporting population growth. Critics and researchers also link intensive agriculture to [[Deforestation|deforestation]], depletion of aquifers, antibiotic resistance, pollution, biodiversity loss, and contributions to climate change, while agriculture is itself sensitive to soil degradation, desertification, and a changing climate. Genetically modified organisms are widely used, although some countries ban them, and the use of pesticides and animal-husbandry methods remains debated on environmental and animal-welfare grounds.

Sub-discipline of plant agriculture concerned with crop production and soil management, particularly for field crops under intensive farming. It applies biology, chemistry, economics, ecology, earth science and [[Genetics|genetics]]. Plant breeding has raised crop yields and improved the nutritional value of corn, soybeans and [[Wheat|wheat]]; triticale, a hybrid of rye and wheat, contains more usable protein than either parent. Agronomists analyze soils for [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]], [[Phosphorus|phosphorus]], [[Potassium|potassium]] and other nutrients, and develop methods such as contour plowing and no-till farming to prevent erosion. Agroecology, closely associated with sustainable agriculture, manages farming systems with an emphasis on ecological and environmental applications.

=== KEY: Ahmad_Shah_Durrani_05ef9a
HEADWORD: Ahmad Shah Durrani
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Delhi | Kandahar | Mughal Empire | Nader Shah
ENTRY:
Ruler who founded the Durrani Empire and is often regarded as the founder of modern Afghanistan. Born into the Abdali tribe of Pashtuns and a commander under [[Nader Shah]], he was crowned near [[Kandahar]] after Nader's assassination in 1747 led to the breakup of the Afsharid Empire. Over 25 years he fought over fifteen major campaigns across West Asia, Central Asia and South Asia. From 1748 he invaded the [[Mughal Empire]], sacked [[Delhi]] in 1757 and annihilated Maratha armies at the Third Battle of Panipat in 1761. His campaigns also brought massacres, pillaging and mass enslavement, and contributed to the decline of Muslim power in northern India. He is buried in Kandahar.

=== KEY: Ahmad_al_Mansur_c5ede6
HEADWORD: al-Mansur, Ahmad
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Elizabeth I | England | Marrakesh | Songhai Empire
ENTRY:
Sultan of Morocco from 1578 to 1603 and the sixth and most famous ruler of the Saadi dynasty. He succeeded his brother Abd al-Malik, who died fighting the Portuguese at Ksar el-Kebir in 1578, and used ransoms from captured Portuguese to fill the royal treasury. He commissioned the El Badi Palace in [[Marrakesh]], finished in 1593 or 1594, and sought gold from the trans-Saharan trade of the [[Songhai Empire]]. In 1600 he sent an envoy to Queen [[Elizabeth I]] of [[England]] to negotiate an alliance against Spain. He was described as a man of profound Islamic learning who loved books, calligraphy and mathematics.

=== KEY: Ahmad_ibn_Hanbal_40d6a6
HEADWORD: Ahmad ibn Hanbal
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Abbasid Caliphate | Hadith | Quran | Sunni Islam
ENTRY:
Arab jurist and founder of the Hanbali school of jurisprudence. Widely recognized as the scholar who memorized more [[Hadith|hadith]] than any other in Islamic history, he compiled *al-Musnad*, one of the largest hadith collections, which shaped the methods later used in *Sahih Bukhari* and *Sahih Muslim*. During the Mihna, a policy of the [[Abbasid Caliphate|Abbasid]] caliph al-Ma'mun that enforced the doctrine that the [[Quran]] was created, he refused to endorse that view and suffered physical persecution, a stance that made him a revered figure in traditional [[Sunni Islam|Sunni]] thought. His influence is debated: the Wahhabi reform movement has cited him as a principal influence, while some scholars argue that his own beliefs differ from Wahhabi doctrine.

=== KEY: Ahmad_ibn_Ibrahim_al_Ghazi_92b043

PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Ethiopia | Ethiopian Empire | Imam | Ottoman Empire
ENTRY:
[[Imam]] of the Adal Sultanate (1527–1543) who led its invasion and conquest of Abyssinia. Known in Amharic as Grañ and in Somali as Gurey, both meaning the left-handed, he won nearly all of his battles against the [[Ethiopian Empire|Ethiopians]] before 1541, including the Battle of Amba Sel, after which Emperor Dawit II was never again in a position to offer a pitched battle. The orientalist Frederick A. Edwards dubbed him the African Attila. He was defeated after a Portuguese intervention drew his war into a wider struggle between the Portuguese and the [[Ottoman Empire]]. His invasion remains part of the memory of both modern [[Ethiopia]] and Somalia.

=== KEY: Ahmadiyya_2c9975

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Islamic messianic movement founded in British India on March 23, 1889, by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad. Adherents, known as Ahmadi Muslims, believe Ahmad was the promised [[Messiah]] and Mahdi, who restored Islam to the precepts practiced by [[Muhammad]] and the early Muslim community. The movement is led by a caliph. Mainstream Muslims regard Ahmad's claim to prophethood as heretical, and Ahmadis have faced non-recognition and persecution in many parts of the world. A small group, the Lahore Ahmadiyya Movement, split from the community over Ahmad's prophetic status.

=== KEY: Ahmedabad_a6ed05

ENTRY:
Most populous city in the Indian state of [[Gujarat]], on the Sabarmati River. Its population was 5,570,585 at the 2011 census. Historically known as Karnavati, it was renamed after Ahmad Shah I of the Gujarat Sultanate, who transferred the capital there in 1411. The city is the second-largest producer of [[Cotton|cotton]] in India and, with [[Kanpur]], was known as the Manchester of India. Ahmedabad is an important economic, industrial and cultural hub, and [[Cricket|cricket]] is popular: the Narendra Modi Stadium at Motera seats 132,000 and is the largest stadium in the world. The city will host the 2030 Commonwealth Games. The historic city, or Old Ahmedabad, was declared a [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Site in 2017.

=== KEY: Ahmose_I_2f5f9a
HEADWORD: Ahmose I
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Canaan | Hathor | New Kingdom of Egypt | Nubia | Pharaoh
ENTRY:
[[Pharaoh]] of [[Ancient Egypt]] and founder of the Eighteenth Dynasty. His reign is usually dated to the mid-16th century BC, at the beginning of the Late Bronze Age. He completed the conquest and expulsion of the Hyksos, restored Theban rule over Lower and Upper Egypt, and reasserted Egyptian power in [[Nubia]] and [[Canaan]]. He reorganized the administration, reopened quarries, mines and trade routes, and began a building program that culminated in the last pyramid built by native Egyptian rulers. His mother, Ahhotep, served as regent during his minority. Manetho credited him with abolishing human sacrifice to [[Hathor]] at Heliopolis. His reign laid the foundations of the [[New Kingdom of Egypt|New Kingdom]], under which Egyptian power reached its peak.

=== KEY: Ahura_Mazda_e4624c
HEADWORD: Ahura Mazda
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Darius the Great | Zoroaster | Zoroastrianism
ENTRY:
Principal god and sky deity of [[Zoroastrianism]]. The words Ahura and Mazda literally mean “lord” and “wisdom”. In the Yasna, he is the first and most frequently invoked spirit. Some Zoroastrian historians hold that his worship predates [[Zoroaster]]. The first notable invocation of Ahura Mazda came in the Behistun Inscription of [[Darius the Great]] during the Achaemenid period (c. 550–330 BC). Until the reign of Artaxerxes II he was invoked alone in royal inscriptions; under Artaxerxes II he was grouped in a triad with Mithra and Anahita. Images of him appeared from the 5th century BC, but under the Sasanian dynasty an iconoclastic movement replaced them with stone-carved figures and then removed them entirely.
