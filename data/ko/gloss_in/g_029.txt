ENTRY:
Ancient Roman amphitheater in central Rome, Italy. Known as the Flavian Amphitheatre, it was begun under Emperor Vespasian in AD 72 and completed under his successor Titus in AD 80, with later modifications under [[Domitian]]. Built of travertine, [[Tuff|tuff]] and Roman concrete, it could hold an estimated 50,000 to 80,000 spectators. It hosted [[Gladiator|gladiatorial]] contests, animal hunts and executions, as well as re-enactments of famous battles and dramas based on [[Roman mythology]]. Earthquakes and stone robbers ruined much of it, and it was later reused as housing, workshops, a fortress and a quarry. It remains one of the best-known symbols of Imperial Rome and one of Rome's most popular tourist attractions. Each Good Friday, the [[Pope|pope]] leads a torchlit Catholic procession in the area around it.

=== KEY: Columbian_exchange_ce328d

ENTRY:
Widespread transfer of plants, animals and diseases between the Old and New Worlds, beginning in the late 15th century. === KEY: Columbidae_0371ea

ENTRY:
Bird family of doves and pigeons, the only family in the order Columbiformes. Members are stout-bodied, with small heads, short necks and slender bills. The family contains 52 genera and 353 species, and most species eat seeds and fruit. Doves are usually the smaller species and pigeons the larger, although the distinction is not scientific. The domestic pigeon descends from the wild [[Rock dove]]. Most species are monogamous, and parents feed their chicks crop milk, a nutrient-rich secretion from the esophagus. Of the family's species, 118 (34%) are at risk and 13 are extinct, including the [[Dodo]] and the [[Passenger pigeon]], which once flocked in the billions.

=== KEY: Coma_4e05a8
HEADWORD: Coma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brainstem | Cerebral cortex | Hypoglycemia | Pneumonia | Stroke | Vegetative state
ENTRY:
Prolonged state of deep unconsciousness with no response to pain, light or sound. A patient in a coma lacks a sleep–wake cycle, cannot initiate voluntary actions and does not respond normally to stimuli. Clinically, a coma is the consistent inability to follow a one-step command. Consciousness depends on the [[Cerebral cortex|cerebral cortex]] and the reticular activating system in the [[Brainstem|brainstem]]. Causes include trauma, [[Stroke|stroke]], metabolic disorders such as [[Hypoglycemia|hypoglycemia]], infections and drug overdoses; comas can also be medically induced, as during general anesthesia. The Glasgow Coma Scale scores consciousness from 3 (deepest coma) to 15 (fully alert). Complications include [[Pneumonia|pneumonia]] and blood clots, and prolonged coma beyond weeks raises the risk of death, [[Vegetative state|persistent vegetative state]] or long-term disability.

ENTRY:
Selection of distinct items from a set, in which order does not matter. Unlike a [[Permutation|permutation]], a combination is a subset of k distinct elements. === KEY: Comedy_e6b99f
HEADWORD: Comedy
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Genre of dramatic works intended to be humorous or amusing by inducing laughter.

Comedy originated in [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]], where [[Satire|political satire]] by comic poets influenced voters in Athenian democracy. Greek comedy typically pitted two groups, ages, genders or societies against each other in an amusing conflict. [[Aristotle]] defined it as an imitation of people worse than the average, and the word derives from *kōmōidía*, a compound of *kômos* (revel) and *ōidḗ* (singing, ode).

Other varieties include screwball, black and romantic comedy, and the comedy of manners, which satirizes upper-class society. In the Middle Ages the term expanded to include narrative poems with happy endings.

=== KEY: Comet_b9a850
HEADWORD: Comet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid | Astronomical unit | European Space Agency | Halley's Comet | Kuiper belt | Latin | Oort cloud
ENTRY:
Icy small Solar System body that warms and releases gas when passing close to the Sun. This outgassing produces an extended atmosphere, the coma, around the nucleus, and sometimes a tail of gas and dust blown out by solar radiation and the solar wind. Nuclei range from a few hundred meters to tens of kilometers across and are loose collections of ice, dust, and small rocky particles. The coma may be up to 15 times Earth's diameter, and the tail may stretch beyond one [[Astronomical unit|astronomical unit]].

Nuclei contain water ice, frozen carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, methane, and ammonia, along with organic compounds such as methanol and formaldehyde. The amino acid glycine was confirmed in comet dust recovered by NASA's Stardust mission in 2009. Nuclear surfaces have very low albedo: the Giotto probe found that the nucleus of [[Halley's Comet]] reflects about four percent of incident light, compared with seven percent for asphalt.

Comets usually have highly eccentric orbits, with periods from several years to potentially several million years. Short-period comets originate in the [[Kuiper belt]] or its scattered disc beyond Neptune; long-period comets are thought to come from the [[Oort cloud]].

Comets differ from [[Asteroid|asteroids]], which form no tail and are thought to have formed inside the orbit of Jupiter, although main-belt comets have blurred the distinction. As of November 2021, 4,584 comets were known. NASA's Deep Impact blasted a crater on Comet Tempel 1, and the [[European Space Agency]]'s Rosetta was the first to land a robotic spacecraft on a comet.

The word derives from [[Latin]] *comēta*, from Greek *κομήτης*, 'wearing long hair'.

=== KEY: Comics_265bb5
HEADWORD: Comics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Manga | Onomatopoeia | Osamu Tezuka | Superman | The Adventures of Tintin | Will Eisner
ENTRY:
Medium that expresses ideas with images, often combined with text, typically in a sequence of panels. Speech balloons, captions, and [[Onomatopoeia|onomatopoeia]] indicate dialogue, narration, and sound effects. Theorists and historians have no consensus on a single definition; some emphasize the combination of image and text, others sequentiality, mass reproduction, or recurring characters. Common forms include comic strips, gag cartoons, and comic books, and since the late 20th century graphic novels and webcomics.

The European, American, and Japanese traditions followed different paths. Europeans often trace their tradition to the Swiss Rodolphe Töpffer, whose cartoon strips date from as early as 1827. The medium became widely popular in the 1930s after strips and books such as [[The Adventures of Tintin]]. American comics emerged as a mass medium in the early 20th century through newspaper strips, with Richard F. Outcault's *The Yellow Kid* in the 1890s often cited as the origin; comic books followed in the 1930s, and the superhero genre became prominent after [[Superman]] debuted in 1938. Japanese comics, called [[Manga|manga]], trace their origins to as early as the 12th century; [[Osamu Tezuka]]'s prolific work helped modern manga flourish after 1945.

In the U.S., psychiatrist Fredric Wertham linked comics to juvenile delinquency in the 1950s. Senate hearings followed, and the industry adopted the self-regulating Comics Code Authority in 1954; its content limits are said to have delayed mature themes in U.S. comics. Graphic novels such as [[Will Eisner]]'s *A Contract with God* (1978) later broadened the medium. Comics had a lowbrow reputation for much of their history, but toward the end of the 20th century they gained respect as a serious art form among the public and academics.

=== KEY: Commerce_b9738d

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Organized system of activities and institutions that facilitate the large-scale exchange of goods, services and other value.

The [[Industrial Revolution]] and modern banking reshaped commerce in the 19th century, and standardized shipping containers in the mid-20th century spurred a surge in international trade.

Calendar notation equivalent to [[Anno Domini|Anno Domini (AD)]] and Before Christ (BC).

=== KEY: Common_cold_a573ef
HEADWORD: Common cold
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antibiotic | Coronavirus | Ibuprofen | Pneumonia | Vaccine | Zinc
ENTRY:
Viral infectious disease of the upper respiratory tract that primarily affects the mucosa of the nose, throat, sinuses, and larynx. Symptoms may appear as little as two days after exposure and include cough, runny nose, sneezing, nasal congestion, sore throat, headache, fatigue, and sometimes fever, which is uncommon in adults but common in infants and young children. People usually recover in seven to ten days, though some symptoms may last up to three weeks. Occasionally, those with other health problems develop [[Pneumonia|pneumonia]]. Influenza symptoms are similar but usually more severe.

Well over 200 virus strains are implicated. Rhinoviruses cause up to 40% of cases and have over 100 known serotypes; [[Coronavirus|coronaviruses]], adenoviruses and adenoviruses are also common. Transmission is by airborne droplets or by contact with nasal secretions or contaminated objects. Risk factors include attending child care facilities, poor sleep, and psychological stress. Symptoms are mostly due to the body's immune response rather than tissue destruction by the viruses.

There is no [[Vaccine|vaccine]], because of the rapid mutation and wide variation of the viruses, and no cure. Prevention rests on hand washing, not touching the eyes, nose, or mouth with unwashed hands, and avoiding sick people. Symptoms can be treated: [[Zinc|zinc]] may reduce their duration and severity if started shortly after onset, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as [[Ibuprofen|ibuprofen]] may help with pain. [[Antibiotic|Antibiotics]] should not be used, since colds are caused by viruses, and there is no good evidence that cough medicines shorten a cold.

The common cold is the most frequent infectious disease in humans. The average adult has two to three colds a year and the average child six to eight, most often in winter.

=== KEY: Communication_07a3dd
HEADWORD: Communication
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Body language | Claude Shannon | Latin | Noise | Sign language
ENTRY:
Transmission of information, in which a source conveys a message through a channel to a receiver. The precise definition is disputed. Scholars disagree about whether unintentional or failed transmissions count, whether deliberate deception counts, and whether communication only transmits meaning or also creates it. The term can also refer to the message itself or to the field of inquiry, communication studies. It derives from the [[Latin]] verb *communicare*, 'to share' or 'to make common'.

Definitions range from broad to narrow. Broad definitions include unconscious and non-human behavior, so that animals communicate within their species and flowers signal the location of nectar to bees through color and shape. Narrower ones restrict communication to conscious interaction among humans, focus on symbols and signs, or treat the communicator's intent as central. Philosopher Paul Grice identifies communication with actions that aim to make the recipient aware of the communicator's intention. Researchers of animal and plant communication tend to define it by observable responses and by benefit to the participants.

Models of communication are simplified, usually diagrammatic overviews of its main components and their interaction. Many include a source that uses a coding system to express a message, which is sent through a channel to a receiver who must decode it. [[Claude Shannon]] and Warren Weaver characterized communication as transmission involving a source, message, encoder, channel, decoder, and receiver. Models are commonly classed as linear transmission models, in which information flows in one direction; interaction models, which add a feedback loop; and transaction models. Transactional and constitutive views reject the pure transmission view, holding that communication also shapes participants' experience by creating meaning. [[Noise]] and other barriers can disrupt the process.

Human communication is commonly classified as verbal or non-verbal. Verbal communication uses messages in linguistic form, including spoken and written messages and [[Sign language|sign language]]. Non-verbal communication occurs without a linguistic system, through [[Body language|body language]], touch, and facial expressions. Communication may also be interpersonal, between distinct persons, or intrapersonal, with oneself. Communicative competence is the ability to formulate and understand messages.

Non-human communication includes animal and plant communication. Animals communicate in courtship and mating, parent–offspring relations, navigation, and self-defense. Chemical communication is especially important for relatively immobile plants; maple trees, for example, release volatile organic compounds into the air to warn other plants of a herbivore attack. Most communication occurs within a species because its purpose is usually cooperation. Interspecies communication occurs mainly in symbiotic relationships, and humans engage in it when interacting with pets and working animals.

How people exchange information has changed with communication technologies, among them writing systems, mass printing, radio and television, and the internet. These advances also created new forms, such as the exchange of data between computers.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Artificial satellite that relays radio signals between widely separated points on Earth. === KEY: Communism_868f00
HEADWORD: Communism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dissolution of the Soviet Union | Karl Marx | Latin
ENTRY:
Political and economic ideology whose goal is a communist society: a classless order based on common ownership of the means of production, distribution, and exchange, with products allocated according to need. Such a society would have no private property or social classes and, ultimately, no money or state. The word derives from French *communisme*, from [[Latin]] *communis*, 'common'.

Communists often seek voluntary self-governance but disagree on the means. A libertarian socialist approach stresses revolutionary spontaneity and workers' self-management; an authoritarian socialist, party-driven approach aims to establish a socialist state expected to wither away. These share the analysis that under capitalism the proletariat, who sell their labor power, and the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, stand in an exploitative relationship that only social revolution can resolve.

Modern communism grew out of the 19th-century European socialist movement. In 1848 [[Karl Marx]] and Friedrich Engels offered a new definition in *The Communist Manifesto*. Communist governments espousing Marxism–Leninism came to power first in the Soviet Union with the 1917 Russian Revolution, and after World War II in Eastern Europe, Asia, and elsewhere.

For much of the 20th century more than one-third of the world's population lived under Communist governments, characterized by one-party rule, state control of the economy, and suppression of opposition. Supporters and critics assess the record differently: some regimes achieved rapid industrialization or literacy gains, while critics point to repression, forced labor, and famines. Mass killings under communist regimes and their mortality figures remain controversial and debated.

After the [[Dissolution of the Soviet Union]] in 1991, many governments abolished Communist rule. Remaining Communist governments include China, Cuba, Laos, North Korea, and Vietnam.

=== KEY: Community_7d6198
HEADWORD: Community
PERSON: no

Social unit of people sharing one or more socially significant characteristics, such as place, norms, [[Culture|culture]], [[Religion|religion]], values, customs, or [[Identity (social science)|identity]]. Communities may share a sense of place within a geographical area, such as a [[Country|country]], village, town, or neighborhood, or may exist in virtual space through communication platforms. Durable good relations that extend beyond immediate genealogical ties also define a sense of community, which is important to people's identity and to roles in social institutions such as family, work, government, and society. Disciplines use the term differently. Early sociological studies treated communities as fringe groups at the behest of local power elites. The word usually carries a positive connotation, which politicians and advertisers exploit rhetorically.

=== KEY: Commutative_property_a66923
HEADWORD: Commutative property
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Property of a binary operation whose result is unchanged when its operands are swapped. Examples include 3 + 4 = 4 + 3 and 2 × 5 = 5 × 2. Addition and multiplication are commutative for [[Real number|real numbers]], [[Complex number|complex numbers]] and [[Integer|integers]], and in every [[Field (mathematics)|field]]. [[Ring (mathematics)|Ring]] with commutative multiplication. The study of such rings is called [[Commutative algebra]]. === KEY: Compact_space_60390e
HEADWORD: Compact space
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Continuous function | Euclidean space | Topological space
ENTRY:
Type of [[Topological space|topological space]] central to general topology and analysis. A space is compact if every open cover has a finite subcover, which lets it behave in many ways like a finite set. Every [[Continuous function|continuous]] real-valued function on a compact space attains a maximum and a minimum. For subsets of [[Euclidean space]], compactness is equivalent to being closed and bounded, by the Heine–Borel theorem. In metric spaces it has several equivalent formulations, including sequential compactness, but these can fail in more general spaces. The closed unit interval [0,1] is compact, while the open interval (0,1) is not. Maurice Fréchet introduced compactness in 1906; Pavel Alexandrov and Pavel Urysohn developed the open-cover definition now standard. Arbitrary products of compact spaces are compact, by Tychonoff's theorem.

=== KEY: Comparative_linguistics_147234

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ferdinand de Saussure | Historical linguistics | Indo-European languages | Morphology (linguistics) | Syntax
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Historical linguistics|historical linguistics]] concerned with comparing languages to establish their historical relatedness. Genetic relatedness implies a common origin, or proto-language. Comparative linguists construct language families and reconstruct proto-languages, prefixing an asterisk to any form not found in surviving texts. The fundamental technique compares phonological and [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphological]] systems, [[Syntax|syntax]] and the lexicon, using the comparative method, which developed through the 19th century. === KEY: Comparative_mythology_e7b791
HEADWORD: Comparative mythology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jupiter (god) | Myth | Zeus
ENTRY:
Comparison of [[Myth|myths]] from different cultures to identify shared themes and characteristics. Scholars have used it to trace the development of religions and cultures, to propose common origins for myths, and to support psychoanalytical theories. Anthropologist C. Scott Littleton defined it as the systematic comparison of myths and mythic themes from a wide variety of cultures. Particularists stress differences between myths, while comparativists stress similarities. Many 18th- and 19th-century scholars held that myths evolved from poetic descriptions of the sun's behavior, but modern scholars lean toward particularism. === KEY: Compass_83ca2d
HEADWORD: Compass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cardinal direction | Earth's magnetic field | Han dynasty | Navigation | Shen Kuo | Song dynasty | Thales of Miletus | Western Europe
ENTRY:
Instrument showing the [[Cardinal direction|cardinal directions]], used for [[Navigation|navigation]] and geographic orientation. It typically consists of a magnetized needle, or another element such as a compass card, that pivots to align itself with magnetic north. Compasses often show angles in degrees: north is 0°, east 90°, south 180°, and west 270°.

The magnetic compass works because the needle aligns itself with the horizontal component of [[Earth's magnetic field]], which exerts a torque on it, pulling its north end approximately toward Earth's North magnetic pole. The needle turns on a low-friction pivot, in better compasses a jewel bearing, and settles after a few seconds. Magnetic north differs from true north by an angle called magnetic declination, which varies with location and changes slowly over time, so maps usually give the local value. Modern compasses commonly seal the needle in a liquid-filled capsule, which damps oscillation.

One of the earliest known references to the magnetic properties of lodestone was made by the 6th-century BC Greek philosopher [[Thales of Miletus]]. The magnetic compass was first invented in China as a device for divination, as early as the [[Han dynasty]] (since c. 206 BC). The first compasses used for navigation appeared in China by 1088 during the [[Song dynasty]], as described by [[Shen Kuo]]; they used iron needles magnetized by striking them with a lodestone. The first recorded use in [[Western Europe]] occurred around 1190 and in the Islamic world in the 13th century. Dry compasses appeared around 1300, and the liquid-filled compass supplanted them in the early 20th century.

=== KEY: Competition_biology_abf876
HEADWORD: Competition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aphid | Ecosystem | Plant | Population (biology) | Red deer
ENTRY:
Interaction between organisms or species that share a limited resource, such as food, water or territory. Competition lowers the fitness of both participants, because each one's use of the resource reduces the amount left for the other. === KEY: Complex_number_130495
HEADWORD: Complex number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Cubic equation | Field (mathematics) | Fundamental theorem of algebra | Gerolamo Cardano | Imaginary unit | Leonhard Euler | Real number
ENTRY:
Number of the form a + bi, where a and b are [[Real number|real numbers]] and i is the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]], satisfying i² = −1. The number a is the real part and b the imaginary part. The set of complex numbers is denoted ℂ.

Complex numbers extend the real numbers and support addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division under the same associative, commutative, and distributive laws, which makes them a [[Field (mathematics)|field]]. They are added by adding real and imaginary parts separately, and multiplied using the distributive law and i² = −1: (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i. In electromagnetism and electrical engineering, j is often written instead of i, because i commonly denotes electric current.

Each complex number corresponds to a point (a, b) in the complex plane, or Argand diagram, with the real part on the horizontal axis. Addition then corresponds to translation of the plane, and multiplication to scaling and rotation. Complex numbers give solutions to all non-constant polynomial equations, a result known as the [[Fundamental theorem of algebra]].

Because the square of any nonzero real number is positive, square roots of negative numbers were historically considered impossible. In the 16th century such roots proved helpful in solving [[Cubic equation|cubic equations]]; [[Gerolamo Cardano]] encountered them while deriving formulas for these equations, though he regarded them with suspicion. They were named imaginary numbers in the 17th century. Acceptance grew through the work of [[Leonhard Euler]] and later [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]]. Complex numbers model waves and quantum states in physics and are used in analyzing alternating-current circuits, signal processing, and fluid dynamics.

=== KEY: Computability_theory_32df78
HEADWORD: Computability theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alan Turing | Algorithm | Computer science | Diophantine equation | Halting problem | Kurt Gödel | Mathematical logic | Turing machine
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Mathematical logic]] concerned with computable functions and Turing degrees. Also called recursion theory, it originated in the 1930s with work by [[Kurt Gödel]], Alonzo Church, Rózsa Péter, [[Alan Turing]], Stephen Kleene and Emil Post. Turing's 1936 model, the [[Turing machine]], became the main formalization of computability, and the Church–Turing thesis holds that any function computed by an [[Algorithm|algorithm]] is computable in this sense. In 1936 Church and Turing independently showed that the Entscheidungsproblem is not effectively decidable. The [[Halting problem]] is undecidable: no algorithm can decide for every program and input whether it halts. In 1970 Yuri Matiyasevich proved a theorem implying that [[Diophantine equation|Diophantine]] problems such as Hilbert's tenth problem have no effective solution. The field also studies computably enumerable sets and relative computability. Its results inform [[Computer science]] topics such as formal languages.

=== KEY: Computable_function_776227
HEADWORD: Computable function
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Computability theory | Computational complexity theory | Lambda calculus | Turing machine
ENTRY:
Function whose value an [[Algorithm|algorithm]] can compute for every argument. Computable functions are the basic objects of study in [[Computability theory]]. Because the notion of algorithm lacks a precise definition, formal definitions refer to a specific model of computation. The major models, including [[Turing machine]]s, register machines, [[Lambda calculus]] and general recursive functions, define exactly the same class of computable functions. The Church–Turing thesis, which is unprovable, asserts that no notion of computability computes more. Effective computability does not imply efficiency: some functions require running times that grow exponentially with input length. [[Computational complexity theory]] studies functions that can be computed efficiently.

=== KEY: Computational_complexity_theory_595a19
HEADWORD: Computational complexity theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computability theory | Millennium Prize Problems | P versus NP problem | Parallel computing | Theoretical computer science | Travelling salesman problem
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Theoretical computer science]] that classifies computational problems by the resources needed to solve them. Resources include time, storage, communication, circuit gates and the number of processors in [[Parallel computing]]. A problem is inherently difficult if its solution requires significant resources whatever algorithm is used. Complexity theory asks what can be solved with restricted resources, while [[Computability theory]] asks what can be solved at all. Analysis of algorithms studies the resources of one algorithm, whereas complexity theory considers all possible algorithms for a problem. Classes include P, NP and PSPACE. The [[P versus NP problem]], one of the [[Millennium Prize Problems]], asks whether every problem whose solution can be verified in polynomial time can also be solved in polynomial time. NP-complete problems, including the [[Travelling salesman problem|traveling salesman problem]], are as hard as the hardest problems in NP.

=== KEY: Computer_181900

Machine that can be programmed to carry out sequences of arithmetic or logical operations automatically. Modern digital electronic computers perform sets of operations known as programs, which enable a wide range of tasks. === KEY: Computer_science_284fcf
HEADWORD: Computer science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ada Lovelace | Alan Turing | Artificial intelligence | Charles Babbage | Cryptography
ENTRY:
Study of computation, information, and automation and their associated phenomena. It spans theoretical disciplines, such as algorithms, the theory of computation, and information theory, and applied disciplines, including the design and implementation of hardware and software. Its fundamental concern is determining what can and cannot be automated, and the Turing Award is generally recognized as its highest distinction.

Algorithms and data structures are central to the field. The theory of computation concerns abstract models of computation and the classes of problems they can solve. [[Cryptography]] and computer security study secure communication and the prevention of vulnerabilities. Human–computer interaction examines the interfaces between people and machines, and software engineering the principles of developing software. [[Artificial intelligence]] and machine learning aim to synthesize processes such as problem-solving, decision-making, and learning found in humans and animals.

The foundations predate the digital computer. Wilhelm Schickard built the first working mechanical calculator in 1623, and in 1673 Gottfried Leibniz demonstrated the Stepped Reckoner. [[Charles Babbage]] began designing the Difference Engine in 1822 and the programmable Analytical Engine in 1834, adopting punched cards derived from the Jacquard loom. In 1843 [[Ada Lovelace]] wrote, in notes to a translated article on the Analytical Engine, an algorithm to compute the Bernoulli numbers, considered the first published algorithm tailored for a computer. In 1936 [[Alan Turing]] introduced the universal Turing machine, formalizing the principles of computation. During the 1940s machines such as the Atanasoff–Berry computer and ENIAC led the word computer to refer to machines rather than people, and the field broadened to study computation in general.

=== KEY: Computer_security_e18c9b

Subdiscipline of information security concerned with protecting computer software, systems and networks. === KEY: Computer_simulation_5aabed
HEADWORD: Computer simulation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Manhattan Project | Monte Carlo method | Physics
ENTRY:
Running of a mathematical model on a computer to represent the behavior or outcome of a real-world or physical system. A model consists of the equations that capture a system's behavior, while the simulation is the running of a program that solves them, often approximately. Computer simulations are used in [[Physics|physics]], astrophysics, climatology, chemistry and biology, and in economics, psychology, social science, health care and engineering. Large-scale simulation first appeared in the [[Manhattan Project|Manhattan Project]] during World War II, when a [[Monte Carlo method|Monte Carlo algorithm]] modeled nuclear detonation. In 1997 a simulated desert battle modeled 66,239 tanks, trucks and other vehicles around Kuwait, using multiple supercomputers. Other examples include a 2.64-million-atom model of the ribosome in 2005 and a complete simulation of the life cycle of *Mycoplasma genitalium* in 2012.

=== KEY: Computer_worm_fd9272
HEADWORD: Computer worm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ARPANET | Malware
ENTRY:
Self-replicating [[Malware|malware]] program that spreads across computer networks. A worm copies itself to other computers, often by exploiting security failures, and each infected machine becomes a host that scans for new targets, so infection grows rapidly. Unlike viruses, worms almost always harm networks, even if only by consuming bandwidth. The first was a self-replicating version of Creeper, created in 1971 by Ray Tomlinson and Bob Thomas at BBN to spread across the [[ARPANET|ARPANET]]. The Morris worm of 1988 disrupted many of the computers then connected to the Internet.

=== KEY: Computing_28c028

ENTRY:
Goal-oriented activity that requires, benefits from or creates computing machinery. Computing includes the study of algorithmic processes and the development of hardware and software. === KEY: Conakry_8be2ff
HEADWORD: Conakry
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Port city (pop., 2025: 3,407,327), capital and largest city of [[Guinea|Guinea]]. Conakry was first settled on the small Tombo Island and later spread to the Kaloum Peninsula. It was founded after Britain ceded the island to France in 1887, and it became the capital of French Guinea in 1904. A railway to Kankan opened the interior for the export of groundnuts, and the deepwater port handles exports of bauxite. The city's population grew from 50,000 in 1958 to 600,000 in 1980 and to more than 3.4 million later. Conakry has a tropical monsoon climate, with a dry season dominated by the harmattan wind from December to April and a wet season in which rainfall averages more than 1,100 mm (43 in) in both July and August.

=== KEY: Concept_d99a60
HEADWORD: Concept
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Inference | Memory | Perception
ENTRY:
Fundamental unit of cognition that classifies entities and encodes shared features. Concepts make it possible to form and combine ideas, draw [[Inference|inferences]] and refer to external objects. They act as the meanings of words and play a central role in cognitive processes including [[Perception|perception]], [[Memory|memory]] and reasoning. The classical theory holds that concepts are definitions with fixed rules for the entities they apply to. Prototype theory holds that membership depends on similarity to a prototype, and exemplar theory holds that similarity derives from memories of concrete instances. Philosophers debate whether concepts are mental representations or abstract objects. Suggested mechanisms of concept learning include associative learning and hypothesis testing, while nativism and empiricism differ on whether concepts are inborn. Concepts are relevant to psychology, philosophy and linguistics.

=== KEY: Conceptual_art_230370
HEADWORD: Conceptual art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Contemporary art | Marcel Duchamp
ENTRY:
Art movement in which the concept behind a work takes priority over its material form. Also called conceptualism, it may be realized by anyone following a set of written instructions. Sol LeWitt defined it: "In conceptual art the idea or concept is the most important aspect of the work." [[Marcel Duchamp|Marcel Duchamp]]'s readymades are regarded as precursors, such as *Fountain* (1917), a standard urinal presented as art. Joseph Kosuth's 1969 essay "Art after Philosophy" argued that art exists conceptually, and the Art & Language collective, founded in 1968, published the journal *Art-Language*. In the United Kingdom, popular usage came to denote all [[Contemporary art|contemporary art]] that does not practice painting and sculpture. Critics argue that the movement conflates philosophy or rhetoric with art and diminishes the value of skill, while supporters credit it with broadening artistic discourse.

=== KEY: Concert_6c8730
HEADWORD: Concert
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Choir | Orchestra
ENTRY:
Live performance of music in front of an audience. A concert may be carried by a single musician, when it is often called a recital, or by an ensemble such as an [[Orchestra|orchestra]], [[Choir|choir]] or band. Concerts take place in settings from private houses and nightclubs to concert halls, arenas, stadiums, amphitheaters and parks. Before recorded music, concerts were the main opportunity to hear musicians play. The first known concert charging admission took place in 1672 at the London home of violinist John Banister. In 1678 Thomas Britton began weekly concerts in Clerkenwell, sold by yearly subscription at 10 shillings a year. In France, Anne Danican Philidor organized the Concerts Spirituels, held on religious holidays when the opera was closed. In the 1960s rock concerts became associated with countercultural fashion and attitudes.
