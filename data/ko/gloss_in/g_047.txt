=== KEY: Fishing_industry_dbc25f
HEADWORD: Fishing industry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aquaculture | Climate change | Pearl
ENTRY:
Economic branch that takes, cultures, processes, preserves, transports, markets or sells fish and fish products. Also called the seafood industry, its products include non-food items such as [[Pearl|pearls]]. The Food and Agriculture Organization includes recreational, subsistence and commercial fishing, along with related harvesting, processing and marketing. The livelihoods of over 500 million people in developing countries depend directly or indirectly on fisheries and [[Aquaculture|aquaculture]]. The world harvest stabilized around 85–95 million metric tons a year by 1986. In 2005, commercial wild fisheries captured 93.3 million metric tons, and fish farms produced 48.1 million metric tons. Stocks fished within biologically sustainable levels fell from 90% in 1974 to 62.3% in 2021. Overfishing, [[Climate change|climate change]] and biodiversity loss threaten the livelihoods and food security of a substantial part of the global population.

=== KEY: Fishing_vessel_8d2f2e
HEADWORD: Fishing vessel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Great Pyramid of Giza | Herring | Neolithic | Shrimp | Steel | Wood
ENTRY:
Boat or ship used to catch fish and other aquatic animals such as [[Shrimp|shrimp]] and krill at sea, on lakes or on rivers. Before the 1950s, fishing boats were little standardized, and traditional ones were built of [[Wood|wood]]. Fiberglass is increasingly used for vessels up to 25 meters, and [[Steel|steel]] for larger ones. The oldest boats found by archaeologists are dugout canoes from the [[Neolithic]] period, 7,000–9,000 years old. The Khufu ship, a 143-foot (44 m) vessel entombed at the foot of the [[Great Pyramid of Giza]] around 2,500 BC, shows the skill of Egyptian shipbuilders. Scandinavian builders incorporated iron into their designs and developed oars for propulsion, innovations that influenced fishing boat design long after the Viking period. In the 15th century, the Dutch developed a seagoing [[Herring|herring]] drifter that became a blueprint for European fishing vessels. Recreational fishing boats range from dinghies to superyachts.

=== KEY: Fission_biology_2e9928
HEADWORD: Fission
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Asexual reproduction | Bacteria | Cell (biology) | Mitochondria | Mitosis | Mutation
ENTRY:
Division of a single entity, usually a [[Cell (biology)|cell]], into two or more parts that each regenerate into a separate entity resembling the original. In binary fission, one entity produces two parts. It is a form of [[Asexual reproduction|asexual reproduction]] used by [[Archaea]] and [[Bacteria]] and by some organelles, such as [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]]. The single DNA molecule replicates, and each copy attaches to a different part of the cell membrane, so the daughter cells are genetically identical barring random [[Mutation|mutations]]. Unlike [[Mitosis|mitosis]], binary fission forms no spindle apparatus. In bacteria, FtsZ, homologous to β-tubulin, assembles into a Z ring that defines the division plane. *E. coli* cells typically divide about every 20 minutes at 37 °C. Multiple fission, by contrast, produces more than two parts.

=== KEY: Five_Dynasties_and_Ten_Kingdoms_period_1fb7c0
HEADWORD: Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Jin dynasty (1115–1234) | Liao dynasty | Song dynasty | Tang dynasty
ENTRY:
Era of political fragmentation in imperial [[China]], 907–979. Five dynastic states succeeded one another in the Central Plain, while more than a dozen concurrent states were established elsewhere, mainly in South China; the most powerful ten were known as the ten kingdoms. The era is traditionally dated from the fall of the [[Tang dynasty]] in 907 to the founding of the [[Song dynasty]] in 960. Song then spent 19 years subduing the remaining southern states, and the last Five Dynasties regime, Northern Han, fell to Song in 979. The [[Liao dynasty]] remained in the north until it was succeeded by the [[Jin dynasty (1115–1234)|Jin dynasty]]. Southern kingdoms were comparatively stable, which enabled trade, land reclamation and infrastructure projects that laid the groundwork for the Song economic boom.

=== KEY: Five_Pillars_of_Islam_b39a4e
HEADWORD: Five Pillars of Islam
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hadith | Hajj | Mecca | Muhammad | Quran | Ramadan | Sunni Islam
ENTRY:
Five obligatory acts of worship for all Muslims, forming the foundation of Islamic religious practice. They are the shahada (profession of faith), salah (prayer), zakat (almsgiving), sawm (fasting during [[Ramadan]]) and [[Hajj|hajj]] (pilgrimage to [[Mecca]]). The shahada affirms that there is no god but God and that [[Muhammad]] is the messenger of God. Each pillar is alluded to in the [[Quran]], and the [[Hadith]] gives further detail. The practices are particularly emphasized in [[Sunni Islam]], and Shia Muslims also recognize them as fundamental, although their classification may differ. Individual observance varies, and some communities, such as the Alevis, reject the pillars and follow a Four Doors system instead.

=== KEY: Fixed_wing_aircraft_77fea3
HEADWORD: Fixed-wing aircraft
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aircraft | Glider (aircraft) | Kite | Lift (force) | Wright Flyer | Wright brothers
ENTRY:
Heavier-than-air [[Aircraft|aircraft]] that generates [[Lift (force)|lift]] from fixed wings, distinct from rotary-wing aircraft and ornithopters. Gliding aircraft, including free-flying [[Glider (aircraft)|gliders]] and tethered [[Kite|kites]], use moving air to gain altitude, while powered airplanes gain forward thrust from an engine. Most are operated by a pilot, but some are remotely controlled or fully autonomous. Kites were used in China about 2,800 years ago, and paper kites were flying by 549 AD. In 1799, Sir George Cayley laid out the concept of the modern airplane as a fixed-wing machine with systems for lift, propulsion and control, and in 1853 he built a successful passenger-carrying glider. The [[Wright brothers]]' flights in 1903 with the [[Wright Flyer]] are recognized by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale as the first sustained and controlled heavier-than-air powered flight.

=== KEY: Fjord_2ac45f
HEADWORD: Fjord
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antarctica | Arctic | Glacier | Norway
ENTRY:
Long, relatively narrow sea inlet with steep sides or cliffs, in a valley created by a former [[Glacier|glacier]] and since flooded by the sea. Fjords are the glacial equivalent of drowned river valleys, known as rias. They occur on the coasts of [[Antarctica]], the [[Arctic]] and surrounding landmasses. A glacier forms a fjord by cutting a U-shaped valley, which the sea later floods. Most fjords are deeper than the adjacent sea; Sognefjord, in [[Norway]], reaches as much as 1,300 m (4,265 ft) below sea level. Many have a sill or shoal at the mouth, which can create extreme currents. Norway's coastline is estimated at 29,000 km (18,000 mi) including its nearly 1,200 fjords, but only 2,500 km (1,600 mi) when the fjords are excluded.

=== KEY: Flag_c58360
HEADWORD: Flag
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Heraldry | Monarchy | National flag | Nationalism | Zhou dynasty
ENTRY:
Piece of fabric, most often rectangular, with a distinctive design, flown from a pole for symbolic, signaling or decorative purposes. The study of flags is called vexillology, from the Latin *vexillum*, meaning flag or banner. The first flags aided military coordination on battlefields, and they later became a common tool for signaling and identification. The origin of the flag is unknown, but the oldest flag discovered is a bronze example from Shahdad, Iran, dating to c. 2400 BC. Cloth flags were almost certainly invented either in the Indian subcontinent or by the [[Zhou dynasty]] of ancient China. European flags developed independently, and the Roman cloth flag known as the *vexillum* is described as the root of the modern European flag tradition. In medieval Europe, flags developed in connection with warfare, Christianity, [[Heraldry|heraldry]] and [[Monarchy|monarchy]]. Modern [[National flag|national flags]] are associated with state formation, citizenship, revolution and [[Nationalism|nationalism]] after 1789.

=== KEY: Flagellate_bffbc8
HEADWORD: Flagellate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Flagellum | Protist
ENTRY:
Cell or organism with one or more whip-like appendages called [[Flagellum|flagella]]. The term describes a cell construction found in many prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and it does not imply a specific relationship among the organisms that bear flagella. Flagella are generally used for propulsion and sometimes to create currents that bring in food. In some flagellates they direct food into a cytostome, or mouth. Flagellates form a polyphyletic group of over 8,000 described species, many of them single-celled [[Protist|protists]]. Many parasites are flagellates in at least one life-cycle stage, including Naegleria, Trichomonas and Plasmodium. In aquatic ecosystems, flagellates are major consumers of bacteria and other protists.

=== KEY: Flagellum_8da1d1
HEADWORD: Flagellum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaea | Bacteria | Latin | Microorganism | Spore
ENTRY:
Hair-like cellular appendage that provides motility. Flagella protrude from certain plant and animal sperm cells, from [[Spore|fungal spores]] (zoospores) and from many [[Microorganism|microorganisms]]. The name is [[Latin]] for "whip" or "scourge," describing the lash-like swimming motion. Structure, protein composition and propulsion differ among the [[Bacteria]], [[Archaea]] and Eukaryota domains, but all provide motility. Bacterial flagella are helical filaments turned by rotary motors at their base; the archaeal version, the archaellum, is superficially similar but considered non-homologous. Eukaryotic flagella are membrane projections supported by the cytoskeleton that bend back and forth, with nine doublet microtubules surrounding two central singlets. A cell may have zero, one or many flagella. *Helicobacter pylori* uses its flagella to swim through the stomach to the mucous lining, and *Salmonella typhimurium* can sense wetness with them.

=== KEY: Flamenco_f62192
HEADWORD: Flamenco
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andalusia | Romani people | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Art form of song, guitar and dance from southern Spain. It developed within the *gitano* ([[Romani people|Romani]]) subculture of [[Andalusia]] and has also been present in Extremadura and Murcia. Its forms combine song, guitar, dance, *compás*, modal and tonal harmony, *falsetas*, handclaps and footwork. The identity of a *palo*, or musical form, depends on rhythmic cycle, mode, chord progression, stanzaic form and geographic origin. The oldest record dates to 1774, in José Cadalso's *Las Cartas Marruecas*. The term first referred to the music genre in an 1847 newspaper article, after it had long designated the Romani people themselves. On 16 November 2010, [[UNESCO]] declared flamenco one of the Masterpieces of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity.

=== KEY: Flamingo_1c55b6
HEADWORD: Flamingo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carotenoid
ENTRY:
Wading bird of the family Phoenicopteridae, the only living family in the order Phoenicopteriformes. Six living species occur in tropical and subtropical regions: four in the Americas, including the Caribbean, and two in Afro-Eurasia. Their pink plumage results from [[Carotenoid|carotenoids]] in their diet. Flamingos have long legs, sinuous necks and downward-curving bills, and they feed by sweeping their heads through water and filtering algae, crustaceans and small invertebrates with lamellae in their bills. They are highly gregarious, forming colonies called flamboyances that may hold hundreds of thousands of birds. Breeding pairs build conical mud nests, and the female lays a single egg. The lesser flamingo is the smallest species and often forms flocks of up to a million birds.

=== KEY: Flash_flood_af4ab8
HEADWORD: Flash flood
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Thunderstorm
ENTRY:
Rapid flooding of low-lying areas such as washes, rivers and dry lakes. They may follow heavy rain from severe [[Thunderstorm|thunderstorms]], hurricanes or tropical storms, or meltwater from ice and snow, and are distinguished from regular floods by fewer than six hours between rainfall and flooding. The collapse of a natural ice or debris dam, or of a man-made dam, can also trigger them. In the United States they cause more deaths in an average year than lightning, tornadoes or hurricanes. === KEY: Flat_Earth_6c65a9
HEADWORD: Flat Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Pythagoras
ENTRY:
Archaic, scientifically disproven conception of Earth as a plane or disk. Many ancient societies held a flat-Earth cosmography. The idea of a spherical Earth appeared with [[Pythagoras]] in the 6th century BC, and by about 330 BC [[Aristotle]] had provided strong empirical evidence for a sphere. === KEY: Flat_panel_display_0d09ec
HEADWORD: Flat-panel display
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: General Electric | Light-emitting diode | Liquid-crystal display | OLED
ENTRY:
Electronic display of text or images, much thinner than cathode-ray tubes. Usually less than 10 centimeters thick, flat-panel displays are lightweight and offer better linearity, higher resolution and contrast than typical consumer televisions of earlier eras. By 2016 they had almost completely replaced CRT displays. Most use [[Liquid-crystal display|LCD]] or [[Light-emitting diode|LED]] technologies, sometimes combined, and smartphones often use [[OLED]] panels. === KEY: Flatfish_b0240e
HEADWORD: Flatfish
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camouflage | Hydrothermal vent
ENTRY:
Ray-finned fishes that rest on one side of the seafloor. In adults both eyes lie on the upper side of the head, because one eye migrates across the skull during juvenile metamorphosis, a unique asymmetry among vertebrates. The upper surface is often pigmented for [[Camouflage|camouflage]], while the blind side is pale. Some families have eyes usually on the right side of the body, others on the left. Flatfishes live in oceans worldwide, with most species between 0 and 500 m deep and species diversity centered in the Indo-West Pacific. Many enter brackish or fresh water. The tonguefish *Symphurus thermophilus* congregates around sulfur at [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vents]]. Many species are important to fisheries, including halibut, flounder, sole and turbot.

=== KEY: Flatworm_869d0c
HEADWORD: Flatworm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cestoda | Earthworm | Snail
ENTRY:
Soft-bodied, unsegmented bilaterian invertebrate of the phylum Platyhelminthes. Lacking a body cavity and circulatory and respiratory organs, flatworms rely on flattened shapes that let oxygen and nutrients diffuse through the body. Their single digestive opening serves for both intake and waste removal, so food cannot be processed continuously. Most free-living species are predators in water or humid terrestrial habitats. The parasitic [[Cestoda|tapeworms]] and flukes have complex life cycles, while monogeneans are external parasites of aquatic animals. Since the mid-1980s, analyses have placed the Acoelomorpha as basal bilaterians, and over 20,000 species are known. A planarian introduced to control the giant African [[Snail|snail]] in the Philippines and the Maldives now threatens native snails and should not be used for biological control. New Zealand planarians prey on [[Earthworm|earthworms]] in northwestern Europe.

=== KEY: Flavius_Aetius_ac5d6f

ENTRY:
Roman general and statesman of the closing period of the [[Western Roman Empire]]. === KEY: Flax_c731a0
HEADWORD: Flax
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Annual flowering plant, *Linum usitatissimum*, grown for fiber and seed. Native from the eastern Mediterranean to India, it was first domesticated in the [[Fertile Crescent]] and is cultivated in temperate climates. Textiles made from its fibers are known as [[Linen]], traditionally used for bed sheets and table linen. Its seeds yield flaxseed or linseed and linseed oil. Cultivated plants grow to 1.2 meters tall and need little fertilizer or pesticide.

=== KEY: Flea_114508
HEADWORD: Flea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black Death | Yersinia pestis
ENTRY:
Wingless insect of the order Siphonaptera, an external parasite of mammals and birds. About 2,500 species feed on the blood of their hosts. Adults are 1.5 to 3.3 millimeters long, with laterally compressed bodies that move through fur or feathers and hind legs adapted for jumping. A flea can jump up to 18 centimeters vertically and 33 centimeters horizontally, second only to the froghopper among animals relative to body size. Each species usually specializes on one host species. The earliest known fleas lived in the Middle Jurassic. The oriental rat flea, *Xenopsylla cheopis*, transmits [[Yersinia pestis]], which causes bubonic plague; outbreaks included the Plague of Justinian, about 540, and the [[Black Death]], about 1350.

=== KEY: Flood_6593d0
HEADWORD: Flood
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1931 China floods | Climate change | Deforestation | Flash flood | Floodplain | Hurricane Katrina | Hydrology | Levee
ENTRY:
Overflow of water (rarely other fluids) that submerges land that is usually dry. Floods are of significant concern in agriculture, civil engineering, and public health, and are studied in [[Hydrology|hydrology]] and hydraulic engineering. Natural types include river flooding, groundwater flooding, coastal flooding, and urban flooding, sometimes known as [[Flash flood|flash flooding]]. Land may also be flooded intentionally for agricultural, military, or river-management purposes, as when paddy fields are prepared for rice.

Flooding may occur when a river, lake, sea, or ocean overtops or breaks [[Levee|levees]], or when rainwater accumulates on saturated ground, called an areal flood. Rivers flood when the flow rate exceeds the capacity of the channel, particularly at bends or meanders. Slow-rising floods most commonly occur in large rivers with large catchment areas and may result from sustained rainfall, rapid snowmelt, monsoons, or tropical cyclones. In the extremely flat Red River Valley of Minnesota, North Dakota, and Manitoba, where the river drops only 72 m over 890 km, a hybrid of river and areal flooding known locally as "overland flooding" can occur.

Human changes to the environment, such as [[Deforestation|deforestation]], removal of wetlands, changes in waterway course, and flood controls such as levees, often increase the intensity and frequency of flooding. [[Climate change]], through an intensified water cycle and sea level rise, is also cited as increasing flood risk, while natural variability in precipitation remains a major driver. Many people live and work near rivers because the land is flat and fertile and rivers provide travel and commerce, so floods often damage buildings on natural [[Floodplain|floodplains]].

Secondary impacts include a short-term increase in waterborne and vector-borne diseases and long-term displacement of residents. From 1990 to 2022, floods affected more than 3.2 billion people, caused 218,353 deaths, and caused more than 1.3 trillion USD in economic losses. Notable events include the [[1931 China floods]] and [[Hurricane Katrina]].

=== KEY: Flood_management_5599d2
HEADWORD: Flood management
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Methods for reducing or preventing the harmful effects of floodwaters. Structural flood control uses physical solutions such as [[Reservoir|reservoirs]], [[Levee|levees]], dredging and diversions. Non-structural methods include land-use planning, advanced warning systems, flood insurance and [[Zoning]] ordinances. === KEY: Florence_Nightingale_30e0fa
HEADWORD: Nightingale, Florence
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
English social reformer, statistician, and founder of modern nursing. Born in Florence, Italy, to a wealthy British family, she was named after her birthplace. At sixteen she experienced what she later described as the first of several calls from God to service, and she rejected the expected role of wife and mother despite her family's opposition. After a nine-year courtship she rejected the politician and poet Richard Monckton Milnes, convinced that marriage would interfere with her calling. In Rome in 1847 she met Sidney Herbert, a politician who became a lifelong friend and later facilitated her work in the Crimea.

Nightingale came to prominence as a manager and trainer of nurses during the [[Crimean War]], in which she organized care for wounded soldiers at [[Constantinople]]. She significantly reduced death rates by improving [[Hygiene|hygiene]] and living standards, and became an icon of Victorian culture as "The Lady with the Lamp," making ward rounds at night. Some commentators have asserted that the media exaggerated her wartime achievements, but critics agree on the importance of her later work in professionalizing nursing for women.

In 1860 she established her nursing school at St Thomas' Hospital in London, the first secular nursing school in the world, now part of King's College London. She wrote *Notes on Nursing* (1859). The Nightingale Pledge, taken by new nurses, and the Florence Nightingale Medal, the highest international distinction a nurse can achieve, are named in her honor, and International Nurses Day is celebrated on her birthday.

She was also an innovator in [[Statistics|statistics]], presenting analyses in graphical form. She is famous for the polar area diagram, or Nightingale rose diagram, equivalent to a modern circular histogram. Her social reforms included improving healthcare for all sections of British society, advocating better hunger relief in [[India]], and helping to abolish prostitution laws that were harsh for women.

=== KEY: Flower_338d1f
HEADWORD: Flower
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fertilisation | Flowering plant | Fruit | Pollen | Pollination | Seed
ENTRY:
Reproductive structure of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]], also called a blossom or bloom. A flower is typically built in four circular levels, or whorls, around the end of a stalk: the sepals (collectively the calyx), modified leaves that protect the flower; the petals, often designed to attract pollinators; the male stamens, where [[Pollen|pollen]] is presented; and the female gynoecia, where pollen is received. Flowers arranged in a group form an inflorescence. In botany, cones are regarded as the gymnosperm equivalent of flowers.

Pollen, which produces the male sex cells, is carried between the male and female parts of flowers in [[Pollination|pollination]]. This may occur between different plants (cross-pollination) or within one plant or flower (self-pollination), through animals such as birds and insects, or through wind and water. Plants favor cross-pollination because it joins sex cells from genetically distinct plants, increasing genetic diversity, and features that attract pollinators are among the most common adaptations.

After pollination, the sex cells fuse in [[Fertilisation|fertilization]], a key step in sexual reproduction. The resulting cell grows into a [[Seed|seed]] containing an embryo and usually a food reserve within a protective coat, while the female part of the flower forms into a [[Fruit|fruit]] that protects the seed and aids its dispersal.

Flowers are a key feature in plant classification and range in size from 0.1 mm to 1 meter (3.3 ft). Humans have used them for thousands of years for decoration, medicine, food, and perfumes, and they appear symbolically in art, literature, religious practice, ritual, and festivals. The word entered Middle English through Old French *flor* from Latin *flōs*; the spelling *flour* was common until the 17th century, when it came to mean ground grain.

Point at which light rays originating from a point on an object converge. A hyperbolic mirror can focus rays aimed at the focal point behind it toward the one in front, as in a Cassegrain telescope.

=== KEY: Fold_geology_caaa42

=== KEY: Folk_music_330d16
HEADWORD: Folk music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Béla Bartók | Folklore | Oral tradition
ENTRY:
Music of a people or community, traditionally passed on orally, and the later popular genre derived from it. Folk music has been defined in several ways: as music transmitted by [[Oral tradition|oral tradition]], music with unknown composers, music on traditional instruments, music about cultural or national identity, or music performed by custom over a long period. It has been contrasted with commercial and classical styles. The term originated in the 19th century, as an extension of [[Folklore|folklore]], a word coined in 1846 by the English antiquarian William Thoms. Observers find a precise definition elusive, since folk music cannot be clearly differentiated in purely musical terms. One widely used definition is simply "Folk music is what the people sing."

Early scholars, including Cecil Sharp and [[Béla Bartók]], saw it as the music of the country as distinct from that of the town, uninfluenced by art music and by commercial and printed song. Lloyd instead stressed economic class, associating it with a lower class.

Traditional folk music was learned by memory by largely illiterate people. It is culturally particular, tied to a region or culture, and often serves social cohesion among immigrant groups. It commemorates historical and personal events, marks holidays such as Christmas, Easter, and May Day, and may accompany weddings and funerals. Its songs have been performed by custom over several generations.

From the mid-20th century a new popular form, called contemporary folk music or folk revival music, evolved from the traditional kind. This second folk revival reached a zenith in the 1960s and includes fusion genres such as folk rock and folk metal. In U.S. English it shares its name, and often its performers and venues, with traditional folk music.

=== KEY: Folklore_7e3b03
HEADWORD: Folklore
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brothers Grimm | Cultural anthropology | Folk religion | Franz Boas | Halloween | Legend | Proverb
ENTRY:
Expressive culture shared by a particular group of people, culture, or subculture. Folklore includes oral traditions such as tales, [[Legend|legends]], [[Proverb|proverbs]], poems, and jokes; material culture, such as traditional building styles; and customary lore, covering actions based on folk beliefs, including [[Folk religion]], and the forms and rituals of festivals, weddings, folk dances, and initiation rites. Each of these, singly or in combination, is a folklore artifact or traditional cultural expression, and equally essential is its transmission from one region to another or one generation to the next. Folklore is not typically gained from formal schooling but is passed along informally, through verbal instruction or demonstration. Its academic study is called folklore studies or folkloristics.

The word, a compound of *folk* and *lore*, was coined in 1846 by the Englishman William Thoms to replace "popular antiquities" or "popular literature." *Lore* comes from Old English *lār*, "instruction." Thoms applied *folk* only to rural, frequently poor, and illiterate peasants. A more modern definition is a social group of two or more people with common traits who express shared identity through distinctive traditions, whether a nation or a single family. Folklorists describe the content as things people make with words (verbal lore), with their hands (material lore), and with their actions (customary lore). Folklore is not limited to what is old: new groups, such as surfers, motorcyclists, and computer programmers, create their own. Its artifacts are passed along as a rule anonymously and always in multiple variants. The folk group is community-based, in contrast to high culture, where a named artist's work is protected by copyright law.

Folklorists seek the significance of beliefs, customs, and objects for the group, since they would not be passed along without continued relevance. Meaning can shift: the [[Halloween]] celebration of the 21st century differs from the All Hallows' Eve of the Middle Ages, and the cleansing rituals of Orthodox Judaism, originally good public health in a land with little water, now signify identification as an Orthodox Jew for some people.

Folklore began to distinguish itself as a discipline during romantic nationalism in Europe. Johann Gottfried von Herder, in his writings of the 1770s, presented oral traditions as organic processes grounded in the locale. After the German states were invaded by Napoleonic France, Germans systematized recorded folk traditions and used them in nation building, an approach embraced by Finland, Estonia, and Hungary as they sought independence. Nineteenth-century European scholars focused on the oral folklore of rural peasants, regarded as survivals of the past; the best-known collection is *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* of the [[Brothers Grimm]] (first published 1812). American folklorists, led by [[Franz Boas]] and Ruth Benedict, studied Native American cultures and treated the totality of their customs and beliefs as folklore, aligning American folkloristics with [[Cultural anthropology|cultural anthropology]] and ethnology, while European folkloristics stayed with the humanities. The term *folkloristics* became widely used in the 1950s, and the American Folklife Preservation Act (Public Law 94-201) was passed in January 1976.

=== KEY: Food_0a38e7
HEADWORD: Food
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apex predator | Biofuel | Cooking | Food industry | Fossil fuel | Maize | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Any substance consumed by an organism for nutritional support. Food is usually of plant, animal, or fungal origin and contains essential nutrients such as carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, or minerals, which the organism's cells assimilate to provide energy, maintain life, and support growth. It is mainly composed of water, lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates; fat is the most energy-dense component. Inadequate or imbalanced intake leads to malnutrition, including undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and overnutrition-related conditions such as obesity.

In an ecosystem, food forms a web of interlocking chains with primary producers (algae, plants, bacteria, and protists that acquire energy from sunlight) at the bottom and [[Apex predator|apex predators]] at the top. Primary consumers are herbivores, and secondary consumers are the carnivores that eat them. Humans are omnivores, finding sustenance in vegetables, fruits, cooked meat, milk, eggs, mushrooms, and seaweed. Bacteria and other microorganisms form the lower rungs of the chain, and bacteria convert atmospheric nitrogen into ammonia, the precursor to proteins, nucleic acids, and most vitamins.

Through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], plants, algae, and certain bacteria use sunlight to transform water and carbon dioxide into oxygen and glucose, making it the primary source of energy and food for nearly all life on earth. The three main nutrients plants absorb from soil are nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

Cereal grain is a staple food that provides more food energy worldwide than any other type of crop. Corn ([[Maize|maize]]), wheat, and rice account for 87% of all grain production worldwide. Just over half of the world's crops are used to feed humans (55 percent), with 36 percent grown as animal feed and 9 percent for [[Biofuel|biofuels]]. Fungi and bacteria are used to prepare fermented foods such as bread, wine, cheese, and yogurt.

Human food is classified by content or by processing. Most systems include four basic groups: vegetables and fruit, cereals and bread, dairy, and meat; the Food and Agriculture Organization and World Health Organization use a system of eighteen or nineteen classifications. Humans generally use [[Cooking|cooking]] to prepare food. The industrial [[Food industry|food industry]] supplies most food energy through intensive agriculture and complex processing and distribution systems. Conventional agriculture relies heavily on [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]], and food and agricultural systems account for as much as 37% of total greenhouse gas emissions. The food system also bears on sustainability, biological diversity, economics, population growth, water supply, and food security.

=== KEY: Food_preservation_77baa0
HEADWORD: Food preservation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ionizing radiation | Louis Pasteur | Refrigeration
ENTRY:
Processes that make food more resistant to microorganism growth and slow the oxidation of fats. By slowing decomposition and rancidification, preservation reduces food waste, lowers production costs, and improves food security. Some traditional methods have a lower energy input and carbon footprint than modern ones, while some are known to create carcinogens.

Some techniques pre-date agriculture. Boiling kills microbes, which is why milk and water are often boiled. Burial preserves food through lack of light and oxygen, cool temperatures, or desiccants in the soil; in Odisha, India, rice is stored underground for three to six months during the dry season. In confit, meat is salted, cooked near 100 °C (212 °F) in fat, and stored immersed in it.

Canning involves cooking food, sealing it in sterilized cans or jars, and boiling the containers to kill or weaken remaining bacteria. It was invented by the French confectioner Nicolas Appert, and by 1806 the French Navy used it to preserve meat, fruit, vegetables, and even milk. It was not understood until 1864, when [[Louis Pasteur]] found the relationship between microorganisms, food spoilage, and illness. Poor manufacture or hygiene can allow contamination by *Clostridium botulinum*, whose toxin can cause severe illness or death and gives no gas or obvious taste, though cooking denatures it.

Later methods include [[Refrigeration|refrigeration]], which emerged with mechanical systems in the 19th century, and irradiation, which uses [[Ionizing radiation|ionizing radiation]] to disrupt microbial DNA. "Hurdle technology" combines several barriers, such as low temperature with preservatives. Nitrate additives have been linked in some cohort studies to cancer risk at excessive exposure, though other analyses affirm their safety within established limits.

=== KEY: Force_9eb6b7
HEADWORD: Force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Classical mechanics | Galileo Galilei | Isaac Newton | Newton (unit) | Standard Model | Torque
ENTRY:
In physics, an action that can change an object's velocity or shape, resist other forces, or cause pressure changes in a fluid. Because both magnitude and direction matter, it is a vector quantity, usually symbolized F. Its SI unit is the [[Newton (unit)|newton]] (N), the force needed to accelerate a mass of one kilogram at one meter per second squared.

Force is central to [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]], where it underlies all three of Newton's laws of motion. The second law relates net force to mass and acceleration (F = ma). The rotational version of force is [[Torque|torque]].

[[Aristotle]] and Archimedes retained fundamental errors, including the belief that a force is required to maintain motion, even at constant velocity. These were not corrected until the 17th century work of [[Galileo Galilei]], who showed that bodies are accelerated by gravity independently of their mass and argued that objects retain their velocity unless acted on by a force such as friction. [[Isaac Newton]] then formulated laws of motion, published in 1687, that were not improved for over two hundred years.

In modern physics, fundamental interactions are the ultimate origin of force, and the [[Standard Model]] holds that exchanged particles called gauge bosons emit and absorb forces. Only four main interactions are known, in order of decreasing strength: strong, electromagnetic, weak, and gravitational. Observations in the 1970s and 1980s confirmed that the weak and electromagnetic forces are expressions of a more fundamental electroweak interaction.

Ecosystem characterized by a dense community of trees. More than 800 definitions of forest are used around the world. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations defines it as land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and a canopy cover of more than 10 percent, excluding land predominantly under agricultural or urban use.

By that definition, forests covered 4.14 billion hectares, approximately 31 percent of the world's land area, in 2025. They are the largest terrestrial [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]] of Earth by area, and 45 percent of forest land is in the tropics. Forests account for 75% of the gross primary production of the biosphere and contain 80% of Earth's plant biomass. They form distinct [[Biome|biomes]]: boreal forests in subarctic climates, tropical moist and dry forests around the equator, and temperate forests at middle latitudes. In the [[Amazon rainforest]], trees release water from their leaves in anticipation of seasonal rains, so the wet season begins two to three months earlier than the climate would otherwise allow.

[[Deforestation]] occurs mostly in tropical forests, and the vast majority is driven by the production of four commodities: wood, beef, soy, and palm oil. Over the past 2,000 years, forest cover in Europe fell from 80% to 34%, and large areas have also been cleared in China and the eastern United States, where only 0.1% of land was left undisturbed. Almost half of Earth's forest area (49 percent) is relatively intact, while 9 percent lies in fragments with little or no connectivity.

Science and craft of creating, managing, planting, using, conserving and repairing [[Forest|forests]] and woodlands for human and environmental benefit. Modern forestry pursues multiple-use management, including timber, fuelwood, wildlife [[Habitat|habitat]], water quality, recreation, biodiversity, erosion control and preserving forests as sinks for [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]]. Silviculture is the commercial practice of forest management, primarily for timber production. In Germany, forests cover nearly a third of the land area, and forestry supports more than a million jobs and about €181 billion of value a year. Worldwide, an estimated 880 million people spend part of their time collecting firewood or producing [[Charcoal|charcoal]], many of them women. Genetic diversity in planting stock is needed to maintain forest vitality and resilience to pests and [[Disease|diseases]].

Manufacturing process that shapes [[Metal|metal]] using localized compressive forces from a [[Hammer|hammer]] or die. Forging is classified by temperature as cold, warm or hot. Hot forging, above the metal's recrystallization temperature, can be done more quickly and precisely, while cold forging typically causes work hardening. Forged parts range in weight from less than a kilogram to hundreds of metric tons. Smiths have made kitchenware, hand tools, edged weapons, [[Cymbal|cymbals]] and [[Jewellery|jewelry]] for millennia. Since the [[Industrial Revolution]], forged components have been used wherever high strength is required, and forging is now a major worldwide industry.
