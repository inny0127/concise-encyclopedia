=== KEY: School_955cd8
HEADWORD: School
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Education | Latin | Middle Ages | University
ENTRY:
Institution designed to provide learning environments for the teaching of students, usually under the direction of teachers. Most countries have formal [[Education|education]] systems, sometimes compulsory, in which students progress through government or private schools. These generally include primary school for young children and secondary school for teenagers; higher education is commonly taught at a [[University|university]] or college. Private schools may be religious, such as the gurukula (Hindu), madrasa (Arabic), hawza (Shi'i Muslim), and yeshiva (Jewish). Homeschooling and distance education take place outside the school building.

The word derives from Greek *scholē*, originally "leisure." Formal schools have existed at least since ancient Greece, Rome, India, and China. In Western Europe cathedral schools were founded in the early Middle Ages to teach future clergy and administrators; the oldest still operating include The King's School, Canterbury (established AD 597). Universities emerged in the 12th century. During the [[Middle Ages]] and much of the early modern period, the main purpose of schools was to teach [[Latin]], giving rise to the term "grammar school." The curriculum gradually broadened to include vernacular literacy and technical, artistic, and scientific subjects. In the Islamic world, mosques at first combined worship and learning, but by the 9th century the madrasa, independent of any mosque, had appeared. Denmark-Norway introduced obligatory attendance in 1739–41.

Proponents regard schools as effective means of education. Critics argue that they fail to prepare students for adult life, prescribe what students do and when, and, through grades and homework, inhibit natural curiosity and creativity. Compulsory attendance now lasts from 9 to 13 years in nearly all countries, yet approximately 250 million children remain out of school.

=== KEY: Schr_dinger_equation_a4388a
HEADWORD: Schrödinger equation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dirac equation | Erwin Schrödinger | Louis de Broglie | Paul Dirac | Richard Feynman | Special relativity | Wave function | Werner Heisenberg
ENTRY:
Partial differential equation governing the [[Wave function|wave function]] of a non-relativistic quantum-mechanical system. Austrian physicist [[Erwin Schrödinger]] postulated it in 1925 and published it in 1926. It is the quantum counterpart of Newton's second law in classical mechanics, giving the evolution over time of the wave function of an isolated system. Schrödinger built on [[Louis de Broglie]]'s proposal that all matter has an associated matter wave, and the equation predicted the bound states of the atom in agreement with experiment. Because it contains a first derivative in time and a second in space, it is non-relativistic. [[Paul Dirac]] later combined [[Special relativity|special relativity]] and quantum mechanics in the [[Dirac equation]]. Other formulations include matrix mechanics, introduced by [[Werner Heisenberg]], and the path integral formulation, developed chiefly by [[Richard Feynman]].

=== KEY: Schr_dinger_s_cat_424d8b
HEADWORD: Schrödinger's cat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Erwin Schrödinger | Niels Bohr | Quantum superposition | Werner Heisenberg
ENTRY:
Thought experiment in quantum mechanics devised by physicist [[Erwin Schrödinger]] in 1935, in discussion with [[Albert Einstein]]. A cat in a sealed box, linked to a random subatomic event, may be considered simultaneously alive and dead while unobserved. Schrödinger intended it to criticize the views of [[Niels Bohr]] and [[Werner Heisenberg]], which he saw as leading to absurd conclusions. It is often used to discuss the measurement problem, which asks when a [[Quantum superposition|superposition]] ends and reality resolves into one outcome. Superposition has been observed in very small objects, but a cat would pose considerable technical difficulties.

=== KEY: Schwarzschild_radius_e31828
HEADWORD: Schwarzschild radius
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Einstein field equations | Gravitational constant | Speed of light
ENTRY:
Parameter in the Schwarzschild solution to [[Einstein field equations|Einstein's field equations]] that corresponds to the radius of a black hole's event horizon, given by rₛ = 2GM/c², where G is the [[Gravitational constant|gravitational constant]], M the mass and c the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. German astronomer Karl Schwarzschild calculated the solution in 1916. Every mass has a Schwarzschild radius, which is proportional to the mass. The Sun's is about 3.0 km (1.9 mi), Earth's about 9 mm (0.35 in) and the Moon's about 0.1 mm (0.004 in). An object whose radius is smaller than its Schwarzschild radius is a [[Black hole|black hole]].

=== KEY: Science_35bf12
HEADWORD: Science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Islamic Golden Age | Natural philosophy | Natural science | Nobel Prize | Scientific Revolution | Scientific method | Social science | Thales of Miletus
ENTRY:
Systematic discipline that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations about nature and society. It is driven by the [[Scientific method|scientific method]], an empirical cycle that typically involves making observations, producing hypotheses, testing them with evidence, and drawing conclusions. Science encompasses both this process and the body of knowledge it produces, which the scientific community continuously challenges, validates, and organizes.

Modern science is typically divided into two major branches: the [[Natural science|natural sciences]], which study the physical world, and the [[Social science|social sciences]], which study individuals and societies. Logic, mathematics, and theoretical computer science, sometimes called the formal sciences, are typically regarded as separate because they rely on deductive reasoning rather than the scientific method. Applied sciences, such as engineering and medicine, use scientific knowledge for practical purposes.

The word has been used in English since the 14th century in the sense of "the state of knowing," borrowed from Anglo-Norman and ultimately from Latin *scientia*, 'knowledge, awareness, understanding.' Scientists were once called "natural philosophers"; in 1834 William Whewell introduced the term *scientist*, crediting it to "some ingenious gentleman," possibly himself.

Science has no single origin; scientific thinking emerged gradually over tens of thousands of years. Direct evidence becomes clearer with the writing systems of the Bronze Age civilizations of Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BC), whose contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and medicine later entered Greek and medieval science. The Egyptians developed a non-positional decimal numbering system, used geometry, and devised a calendar. The Mesopotamians used knowledge of natural chemicals to manufacture pottery, glass, soap, and metals.

In classical antiquity there was no real analogue of the modern scientist; educated, usually upper-class men investigated nature as time allowed. The Milesian school, founded by [[Thales of Miletus]], first attempted to explain natural phenomena without relying on the supernatural. Leucippus and his student Democritus developed the theory of atoms, and Hippocrates established the tradition of systematic medical science. Greek [[Natural philosophy|natural philosophy]] was later advanced during the [[Islamic Golden Age]], which, with the Golden Age of India, contributed the Hindu–Arabic numeral system.

The recovery of Greek works and Islamic inquiries in Western Europe during the Renaissance revived natural philosophy, which the [[Scientific Revolution]], beginning in the 16th century, transformed. In the 19th century the institutional and professional features of science took shape, and "natural philosophy" gave way to "natural science." Contemporary research is often collaborative, carried out in universities, research institutions, government agencies, and companies, though major advances are also credited to individuals and recognized through awards such as the [[Nobel Prize]]. The replication crisis, in which results in fields such as psychology and biomedicine have repeatedly failed to reproduce, has prompted calls for preregistration and open data.

=== KEY: Science_fiction_0e1852
HEADWORD: Science fiction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cyberpunk | H. G. Wells | Jules Verne | Mary Shelley | Space exploration | Technology
ENTRY:
Genre of speculative fiction that imagines advanced and futuristic changes in [[Technology|technology]], scientific knowledge or biological systems. Its common elements have grown from [[Space exploration|space exploration]], extraterrestrial life and time travel to parallel universes, dystopian societies, information technology and transhumanism. Subgenres include hard science fiction, which emphasizes scientific accuracy, space opera, and [[Cyberpunk|cyberpunk]], which explores the interface between technology and society. Precedents are claimed from antiquity, but the modern genre arose in the 19th and early 20th centuries. [[Mary Shelley]]'s *Frankenstein*, written in 1818, is often credited as the first true science fiction novel. [[Jules Verne]] and [[H. G. Wells]] were pivotal figures. The Golden Age of the 20th century added space operas, dystopian literature and pulp magazines. The genre has also influenced film and television, and it can criticize present-day society while inspiring a sense of wonder.

=== KEY: Scientific_Revolution_70305a
HEADWORD: Scientific Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andreas Vesalius | Francis Bacon | Galileo Galilei | Isaac Newton | Nicolaus Copernicus | Tycho Brahe
ENTRY:
Emergence of modern science in Europe during the 16th and 17th centuries. It was an irreversible break with the natural philosophy that preceded it. The "New Science" departed from Greek conceptions and traditions, was more mechanistic in its worldview and more integrated with mathematics, and focused on acquiring and interpreting new evidence.

The period is frequently said to have begun in 1543 with the printing of *De humani corporis fabrica* by [[Andreas Vesalius]] and *De Revolutionibus* by [[Nicolaus Copernicus]]; other historians propose 1572, when [[Tycho Brahe]] observed a supernova. It is said to have culminated in 1687 with [[Isaac Newton]]'s *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion, Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* (1665), the founding of the Royal Society of London (1660) and the French Academy of Sciences (1666), and the launch of *Philosophical Transactions* (1665), the first scientific journal, belong to the same period.

Much of the change in attitude is credited to [[Galileo Galilei]], whose telescopic observations provided persuasive evidence for heliocentrism and who developed the science of motion, and to [[Francis Bacon]], whose announcement of scientific progress inspired societies such as the Royal Society.

The revolution built on earlier learning: translations from Greek and Arabic into Latin, begun in the 10th century and accelerating in the 12th and 13th, together with the medieval university, supplied its foundation. Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, and Newton all studied at universities founded in the High Middle Ages.

The term was popularized by the historian Herbert Butterfield in *Origins of Modern Science*. David Wootton calls the period the most important transformation in human history since the Neolithic Revolution.

=== KEY: Scientific_law_727bad
HEADWORD: Scientific law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hooke's law | Newton's law of universal gravitation | Ohm's law | Theory
ENTRY:
Statement based on repeated experiments or observations that describes or predicts a range of natural phenomena. Laws are developed from data, are often expressed mathematically, and are directly or indirectly based on empirical evidence. Unlike a [[Theory|scientific theory]], a law does not posit a mechanism; it summarizes results within a certain range of application. [[Ohm's law]] applies only to linear networks, [[Newton's law of universal gravitation|Newton's law of gravitation]] only in weak gravitational fields, and [[Hooke's law]] only to strain below the elastic limit. Many laws take equation form, such as the law of conservation of energy, written ΔE = 0. Laws may be contradicted, restricted or extended by future observations. They differ from hypotheses, which have not been verified to the same degree.

=== KEY: Scientific_method_fb3b59
HEADWORD: Scientific method
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Empiricism | Experiment | Francis Bacon | Hypothesis | Ibn al-Haytham | René Descartes | Scientific Revolution
ENTRY:
Empirical method of acquiring knowledge through careful observation, skepticism, hypothesis testing, and experimental validation. Developed from ancient and medieval practices, it acknowledges that cognitive assumptions can distort the interpretation of observations. It has characterized science since at least the 17th century.

Inquiry typically involves forming a testable [[Hypothesis|hypothesis]] through inductive reasoning, predicting its consequences, testing it through [[Experiment|experiments]] and statistical analysis, and adjusting or discarding it according to the results. A hypothesis must be falsifiable: some possible outcome must conflict with its predictions. Although often presented as a fixed sequence of steps, the method is better described as a set of general principles; not all steps occur in every inquiry or in the same order, and chance has played a role in some discoveries.

Early expressions of [[Empiricism|empiricism]] appear with the ancient Stoics, Aristotle, Epicurus, [[Ibn al-Haytham|Alhazen]], Avicenna, Al-Biruni, Roger Bacon, and William of Ockham. During the [[Scientific Revolution]], [[Francis Bacon]] and Robert Hooke furthered empiricism, [[René Descartes]] described a rationalist approach, and inductivism rose to prominence through Isaac Newton and his followers. The term "scientific method" emerged in the 19th century, as institutional development of science and words such as "scientist" and "pseudoscience" drew boundaries between science and non-science.

By the 1960s and 1970s, philosophers of science such as Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend had questioned its universality, favoring a view of science as a heterogeneous and local practice. In *Against Method* (1975), Feyerabend argued against any universal rules of science; Karl Popper and others disagreed. Lee Smolin's 2013 essay "There Is No Scientific Method" and Daniel Thurs's 2015 chapter, which called it a myth or at best an idealization, show that the debate continues.

=== KEY: Scientific_modelling_20a8c7
HEADWORD: Scientific modeling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstraction | John von Neumann
ENTRY:
Activity of producing models of empirical objects, phenomena and physical processes, to make part of the world easier to understand, quantify, visualize or simulate. It requires selecting the relevant features of a real situation and building a model that replicates them. Conceptual, operational, mathematical, computational and graphical models serve different purposes. Models are typically used when direct experimental measurement is impossible or impractical, although direct measurement under controlled conditions is more reliable. [[John von Neumann]] wrote that the sciences mainly make models, justified if they are expected to describe phenomena from a reasonably wide area. A model is task-driven, leaving out details unimportant to the task and [[Abstraction|abstracting]] others. Scientific debate often concerns which model is better for a given task, such as seasonal climate forecasting.

=== KEY: Scientific_notation_81aa2e
HEADWORD: Scientific notation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculator
ENTRY:
Way of expressing numbers too large or too small to write conveniently in decimal form, as a coefficient multiplied by a power of ten. In normalized form, the coefficient is usually between 1 and 10 in absolute value and the exponent is an integer, so 350 is written as 3.5 × 10². It is also called scientific form, or standard form in the United Kingdom. Scientists, mathematicians and engineers use it widely, partly because it simplifies arithmetic and comparison of magnitudes. Engineering notation restricts the exponent to multiples of three to match SI prefixes, so 12.5 × 10⁻⁹ m can be written as 12.5 nm. [[Calculator|Calculators]] and computer programs typically display it in E notation, and decimal floating point is closely related.

=== KEY: Scientific_theory_a7e813
HEADWORD: Scientific theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fact | Plate tectonics | Scientific law | Scientific method
ENTRY:
Explanation of an aspect of the natural world that has been repeatedly tested and corroborated in accordance with the [[Scientific method|scientific method]]. A theory connects and explains multiple observations, and it is expected to make predictions that further observations could confirm or refute. A [[Fact|fact]] is a single observation, while a theory differs from a [[Scientific law|law]], which describes a relationship without explaining it. In everyday speech a theory can mean an unsubstantiated guess, but in science it usually refers to a well-tested explanation. A theory's strength depends on the diversity of phenomena it explains and its simplicity, and it may be modified or rejected if it cannot fit new findings. Some, such as evolution, heliocentric theory and [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], are so well established that they are unlikely to change fundamentally. Hypotheses are said to be supported rather than proven.

=== KEY: Scipio_Africanus_c998c4

ENTRY:
Roman general and statesman, one of the main architects of Rome's victory over Carthage in the Second Punic War. Born into a patrician family, he survived the Roman defeat at [[Battle of Cannae|Cannae]] in 216 BC. In 210 BC, although he had never held consular office, he was elected to command Rome's forces in [[Iberian Peninsula|Hispania]], where he won at Ilipa in 206 BC. === KEY: Scramble_for_Africa_ec24c2
HEADWORD: Scramble for Africa
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cold War | Ethiopia | Leopold II of Belgium | Liberia | New Imperialism | Otto von Bismarck | Quinine | Second Industrial Revolution
ENTRY:
European invasion, conquest, and colonization of most of Africa in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Seven Western European powers (Belgium, France, Germany, Italy, Portugal, Spain, and the United Kingdom) contended for territory, driven by the [[Second Industrial Revolution]] and the era of "[[New Imperialism]]". The period saw a shift from "informal imperialism", meaning military influence and economic dominance, to direct rule.

As late as the 1870s Europeans controlled approximately 10% of the continent, all of it near the coasts; the main holdings were Angola and Mozambique (Portugal), the Cape Colony (United Kingdom), and Algeria (France). Steamships, railways, and telegraphs aided expansion, and [[Quinine|quinine]] made much of the tropics more accessible. By 1914 only [[Ethiopia]], later occupied by Italy in 1936, and [[Liberia]], which had strong connections with the United States, remained outside European control.

Motives were economic, strategic, and political. Africa offered open markets during the Long Depression (1873–1896) and raw materials such as ivory, rubber, palm oil, cocoa, diamonds, tea, and tin. Britain wanted stopover ports on the route to India. John A. Hobson tied the era to shrinking continental markets, while William Easterly disputes a close link between capitalism and imperialism.

The Berlin Conference of 1884–1885, convened by [[Otto von Bismarck]] after claims in the Congo Basin by [[Leopold II of Belgium]], regulated colonization, requiring "effective occupation", and excluded African representatives. Most colonies gained independence during the [[Cold War]]; in 1964 the Organisation of African Unity chose to keep colonial borders, fearing civil wars and regional instability.

=== KEY: Screw_5fd1a1
HEADWORD: Screw
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Externally threaded [[Fastener|fastener]] tightened or loosened by torque applied to its head. Most screws hold objects together, and the head usually has a slot or other feature that takes a tool such as a screwdriver. Most screws have a right-hand thread. Left-hand threads are used where counterclockwise torque would loosen a right-hand screw, as on the left pedal of a bicycle. Mechanism that converts rotational motion and torque into linear motion and force, one of the six classical [[Simple machine|simple machines]]. === KEY: Sculpture_817e89
HEADWORD: Sculpture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Michelangelo | Middle Ages | Renaissance | Statue of Unity
ENTRY:
Branch of the visual arts that operates in three dimensions. Durable sculptural processes originally used carving, the removal of material, and modeling, the addition of material such as clay, in stone, metal, ceramics, wood, and other materials. Since Modernism there has been almost complete freedom of materials and process.

A basic distinction separates sculpture "in the round", which is free-standing, from relief, which is at least partly attached to a background surface. Relief is the usual medium for large figure groups, narrative subjects, and architectural sculpture. The term covers small works such as coins, medals, and figurines (normally no more than 46 cm tall) as well as colossal statues; the largest on record, the Indian [[Statue of Unity]], is 182 m tall.

Sculpture in stone survives far more often than work in perishable materials such as wood, and often forms the majority of surviving works, apart from pottery, of ancient cultures. Sculpture has been central to religious devotion in many cultures, and until recent centuries large sculptures were usually expressions of religion or politics. Public sculpture meant to impress with a ruler's power goes back at least to the Great Sphinx of some 4,500 years ago.

The Western tradition began in [[Ancient Greece]]. During the [[Middle Ages]], Gothic sculpture represented the agonies and passions of the Christian faith. The [[Renaissance]] revived classical models, producing works such as [[Michelangelo]]'s *David*. Modernist sculpture moved away from traditional processes and from depiction of the human body, toward constructed sculpture and found objects presented as finished works.

=== KEY: Sea_d2379e
HEADWORD: Sea
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mediterranean Sea | Ocean | Oceanography | Sodium chloride | Tide | Tsunami
ENTRY:
Large body of salt water. The term has two meanings: the interconnected body of seawater spanning most of Earth, also called the [[Ocean|ocean]], and a named body of water, usually a marginal sea such as the [[Mediterranean Sea]] or a large, nearly landlocked body. There is no clear distinction between seas and oceans, though seas are generally smaller and often enclosed by land.

Earth's 1,335,000,000 km³ of sea contain about 97.2 percent of its known water and cover approximately 71 percent of its surface. Another 2.15% of Earth's water is frozen. Salinity is lower near the surface and river mouths and higher in the depths, but the relative proportions of dissolved salts vary little. The most abundant is [[Sodium chloride|sodium chloride]].

The sea moderates Earth's climate and plays important roles in the water, carbon, and nitrogen cycles. Deep-sea currents, together called the global conveyor belt, carry cold water from near the poles to every ocean. [[Tide|Tides]], the generally twice-daily rise and fall of sea level, are caused by Earth's rotation and the gravitational effects of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun. Submarine earthquakes, volcanoes, huge landslides, and large meteorite impacts can cause destructive [[Tsunami|tsunamis]].

Bacteria, protists, algae, plants, fungi, and animals live in habitats from the sunlit surface to the cold, dark abyssal zone and from polar ice to tropical coral reefs. Life may have started in the sea. Humans draw food, mainly fish, from the sea and use it for trade, travel, and mineral extraction. Its scientific study is [[Oceanography|oceanography]].

=== KEY: Sea_ice_2cd6b2
HEADWORD: Sea ice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albedo | Arctic Ocean | Carbon dioxide | Climate change | Iceberg | Microorganism | Southern Ocean
ENTRY:
Frozen seawater that floats on the ocean's surface, distinct from [[Iceberg|icebergs]], which are chunks of ice shelves or glaciers. Sea ice covers about 7% of the Earth's surface and about 12% of the world's oceans, much of it in the polar ice packs of the [[Arctic Ocean]] and the [[Southern Ocean]]. Brine channels within the ice support [[Microorganism|microorganisms]] that form the base of food webs. Its white surface reflects sunlight back into space, a process known as the [[Albedo|albedo]] effect, and it limits the exchange of heat and [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] between the sea and the atmosphere. Satellite records show a marked decline in Arctic sea ice extent and thickness, a trend linked to [[Climate change|climate change]].

=== KEY: Sea_snake_f7d00f

Common name for two groups of venomous elapid snakes: the true sea snakes (subfamily Hydrophiinae), about 62 species that spend their lives in the ocean, and the sea kraits (genus *Laticauda*), about 8 semi-aquatic species that return to land to breed. Spiny, globular [[Echinoderm|echinoderm]] forming the class Echinoidea. Approximately 950 species live on the seabed in every ocean, from the intertidal zone to the deep sea. === KEY: Seabird_8ddce5

ENTRY:
[[Bird|Bird]] adapted to life in the marine environment, including [[Albatross|albatrosses]], [[Penguin|penguins]] and [[Gull|gulls]]. Seabirds show convergent evolution, as similar environmental problems and feeding niches have produced similar adaptations. The first seabirds evolved in the [[Cretaceous]] period, and modern families emerged in the [[Paleogene]]. They generally live longer, breed later and have fewer young than other birds, but invest heavily in them. Most species nest in colonies ranging from a few dozen birds to millions. Many make long annual migrations, some circumnavigating the Earth. Supraorbital salt glands excrete the salt taken in with seawater. Longline fishing bycatch is the main cause of mortality for over 100 species, and oil spills, climate change and offshore development also threaten them. Conservation includes wildlife refuges and changes to fishing methods.

=== KEY: Seamount_c89eb5

PERSON: no

ENTRY:

=== KEY: Season_6c7a44
HEADWORD: Season
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Axial tilt | Dry season | Equinox | Gregorian calendar | Monsoon | Nile | Northern Hemisphere
ENTRY:
Division of the year based on changes in weather, ecology, and hours of daylight in a given region. On Earth, seasons result from the [[Axial tilt|tilt]] of the planet's rotational axis, about 23.4° from its orbital plane, together with the axis's approximately constant direction toward Polaris through the year. From around March 20 to around September 22 the Northern Hemisphere tips toward the Sun, with the maximum on about June 21; the Southern Hemisphere's maximum is around December 21. The two instants when the Sun is directly overhead at the Equator are the [[Equinox|equinoxes]]. The low angle of the Sun in winter spreads radiation over a larger area, and with shorter days this accounts for most seasonal climate variation. Because of seasonal lag, June, July, and August are the warmest months in the [[Northern Hemisphere]].

Distance from the Sun is not the cause: Earth reaches perihelion in January and aphelion in July, and eccentricity produces a 7% variation in sunlight received.

Definitions vary by culture and region. In temperate and sub-polar regions four seasons based on the [[Gregorian calendar]], spring, summer, autumn, and winter, are generally recognized. Ecologists often use a six-season model not tied to fixed dates: prevernal, vernal, estival, serotinal, autumnal, and hibernal. Many tropical regions have two seasons, a rainy or [[Monsoon|monsoon]] season and a [[Dry season|dry season]]. The ancient Egyptians recognized flood, growth, and low-water seasons, based on the annual flooding of the [[Nile]]; in India, six seasons, or *Ritu*, have been recognized from ancient times for agriculture and trade.

Seasons hold special significance for agrarian societies, and their change is often attended by ritual.

=== KEY: Second_law_of_thermodynamics_ae632b

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Physical law based on empirical observation that heat always flows spontaneously from hotter to colder regions. It establishes [[Entropy|entropy]] as a physical property of a thermodynamic system, and the entropy of an isolated system left to spontaneous evolution cannot decrease. The law forbids what the [[First law of thermodynamics|first law]] would allow, such as a broken cup reassembling and jumping back onto a table. HEADWORD: Sect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cult | Heresy | Max Weber
ENTRY:
Subgroup of a religious, political or philosophical belief system, typically an offshoot of a larger organization. From Latin *secta*, "a way" or "road," the term originally referred to religious groups that had separated from a main body. It now applies to any group that diverges to follow distinct beliefs and practices, often from a perception of [[Heresy|heresy]]. [[Max Weber|Max Weber]] and Ernst Troeltsch were among the first to define sects within the church-sect typology, as voluntary associations whose membership comes from personal acceptance of doctrine rather than birth. Sects usually are more radical and ethically strict than mainstream churches. Roy Wallis described sects by "epistemological authoritarianism," whereas [[Cult|cults]] are marked by "epistemological individualism."

=== KEY: Secularism_dcc4e7
HEADWORD: Secularism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agnosticism | Anti-clericalism | Atheism | Rule of law
ENTRY:
Principle of conducting human affairs on naturalistic considerations, uninvolved with religion. It is most commonly understood as the separation of religion from civil affairs and the state, and may be broadened to minimizing the role of religion in any public sphere. The term was coined in 1851 in Britain; "secular", like "religion", is a Western concept not universal across cultures, languages, or history. Secularism may encompass [[Anti-clericalism|anti-clericalism]], [[Atheism|atheism]], [[Agnosticism|agnosticism]], or neutrality on religion. It is concerned with minimizing religion's role rather than disproving it, and may be hostile, neutral, or hospitable toward religion.

Distinct traditions exist, including the French, Turkish, American, and Indian models. Scholars distinguish a strict form, associated with French *laïcité*, in which the state is officially distanced from all religions and non-religious convictions; a "humanistic" form, which seeks policy based on evidence and human welfare; and a "liberal" or "pillarized" form, common in German-speaking and Benelux states, in which governments may fund religions so long as they treat all convictions equally. In Indian political discourse, "pseudo-secularism" is a pejorative term for states said to favor one religion while claiming impartiality.

Principles associated with political secularism include legal equality regardless of belief, separation of church and state, supremacy of the [[Rule of law|rule of law]] over canon law, freedom of thought, and religious tolerance. Supporters argue variously that it is crucial to modernization or the only guarantor of free religious exercise. Critics charge that it carries bias against religion, citing state atheism under communist regimes, and that strict neutrality can clash with cultural religious practice.

=== KEY: Security_2fae32
HEADWORD: Security
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aircraft carrier | Armour | Firearm | Handgun | Radar
ENTRY:
Protection from, or resilience against, potential harm or unwanted coercion. Beneficiaries of security may be persons, social groups, objects, institutions or ecosystems. The term also describes the absence of harm, the presence of an essential good (food security), secrecy (a secure telephone line) and containment (a secure cell). Security is both a feeling and a state of reality, so a person may feel secure without being safe, or insecure despite being safe. Protective measures include locks, fences, walls, [[Armour|armor]] and antivirus software, along with warning systems such as alarms and [[Radar|radar]]. Coercive capabilities include [[Aircraft carrier|aircraft carriers]], [[Handgun|handguns]] and [[Firearm|firearms]]. Approaches are contested: some argue that security depends on protective and coercive power, others on equitable relationships and meeting fundamental needs.
