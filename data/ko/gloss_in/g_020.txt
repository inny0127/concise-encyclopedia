=== KEY: Bryophyte_3c675e
HEADWORD: Bryophyte
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Group of non-[[Vascular tissue|vascular]] land plants comprising [[Liverwort|liverworts]], [[Hornwort|hornworts]] and [[Moss|mosses]]. Bryophytes are small and prefer moist habitats, though some species survive drier conditions. They produce enclosed reproductive structures but no flowers or [[Seed|seeds]]. They reproduce sexually by [[Spore|spores]] and asexually by fragmentation or gemmae. Their life cycles are dominated by the haploid [[Gametophyte|gametophyte]], on which the diploid [[Sporophyte|sporophyte]] remains dependent. Because they do not rely on roots, they can grow where vascular plants cannot. The name comes from Ancient Greek *brúon* ('tree moss, liverwort') and *phutón* ('plant'). Sperm are flagellated and must swim to archegonia, which may be on a different plant.

=== KEY: Bryozoa_e818a9

ENTRY:
Phylum of simple aquatic [[Invertebrate|invertebrates]], nearly all living in sedentary colonies and also called moss animals. Each individual, or zooid, is typically about 0.5 millimeters (1⁄64 in) long and feeds with a lophophore, a "crown" of tentacles used for filter feeding. Colonies take forms including fans, bushes and sheets. The earliest mineralized skeletons date from the early [[Cambrian]]. Larvae undergo a radical [[Metamorphosis|metamorphosis]] after settling. The invasive species *Membranipora membranacea* has reduced [[Kelp|kelp]] forests off the coasts of the U.S.

ENTRY:
Form of plague caused by the bacterium *Yersinia pestis* and usually spread by infected [[Flea|fleas]]. Symptoms appear one to seven days after exposure and include fever, headache, vomiting and swollen, painful lymph nodes called buboes. Without treatment, plague kills 30% to 90% of those infected, typically within 10 days. === KEY: Buddhism_3db247
HEADWORD: Buddhism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Four Noble Truths | Karma | Mahayana | Nirvana | Silk Road | The Buddha | Theravada | Vajrayana
ENTRY:
Indian religion and philosophy based on teachings attributed to [[The Buddha|the Buddha]], a wandering ascetic and religious teacher of the 6th or 5th century BC. The world's fourth-largest religion, it has an estimated 320 million to 540 million followers, about 4.1%–6.7% of the world population. It originated in the eastern Gangetic plain as a śramaṇa movement, spread through much of Asia via the [[Silk Road]], and reached the West in the 19th century.

According to tradition, the Buddha taught a path leading to awakening and liberation from dukkha (suffering, or unsatisfactoriness) by attaining [[Nirvana|nirvana]], the extinguishing of the passions. He described this path as a Middle Way between extreme asceticism and sensory indulgence. Widely observed teachings include the [[Four Noble Truths]], the Noble Eightfold Path, dependent origination, [[Karma|karma]], and the three marks of existence. Accounts of his life are inconsistent; tradition holds that he was born in Lumbini, achieved enlightenment at Bodh Gaya at age 35, taught for 45 years, and died about age 80 at Kushinagar.

The canon is vast, with textual collections in Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan, and Chinese. Scholars generally recognize two major extant branches. [[Theravada]] (School of the Elders) emphasizes attaining nirvana to end the cycle of death and rebirth and is followed mainly in Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and Sri Lanka. [[Mahayana]] (Great Vehicle) emphasizes the bodhisattva ideal of working for the liberation of all sentient beings; it is the largest branch, predominant in East Asia, and includes Chan, Zen, Pure Land, and Nichiren. [[Vajrayana]], which incorporates esoteric tantric techniques, is practiced in Tibet and Mongolia and may be viewed as a separate branch or as part of Mahayana.

=== KEY: Buddhist_canons_325651
HEADWORD: Buddhist canons

LINK TARGETS: East Asia | Nepal | Pali | Sanskrit | The Buddha | Theravada
ENTRY:
Collections of Buddhist sacred scriptures, some called *Tipiṭaka* in [[Pali|Pali]] or *Tripiṭaka* in [[Sanskrit|Sanskrit]], meaning three baskets. In ancient India, several canons were organized into three divisions: *Vinaya* (monastic rules), *Sutra* (teachings of [[The Buddha|the Buddha]]) and *Abhidharma* (systematic scholastic works). Many early Indian schools had canons with four or five divisions instead. The [[Theravada|Theravada]] canon of Southeast Asia is written in Pali. The Chinese Buddhist Canon, known in [[East Asia|East Asian]] Buddhism as the Great Storage of Scriptures, is written in Buddhist Chinese, and the Tibetan Buddhist Canon is written in Classical Tibetan. The Sanskrit canon of [[Nepal]] preserves many Sanskrit manuscripts and has been important for modern scholarship.

=== KEY: Buddhist_philosophy_5ade77
HEADWORD: Buddhist philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cosmology | Epistemology | Ethics | Karma | Logic | Metaphysics | The Buddha
ENTRY:
Philosophical system developed by Buddhist schools in ancient India and later across Asia. Buddhist philosophers have examined [[Cosmology|cosmology]], [[Ethics|ethics]], [[Epistemology|epistemology]], [[Logic|logic]], [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]], ontology, phenomenology and the philosophy of mind. Early texts describe the [[The Buddha|Buddha]] as declining to answer some metaphysical questions because they were irrelevant to liberation, although doctrines such as dependent arising, [[Karma|karma]] and rebirth carry metaphysical implications. A recurrent theme is the search for a Middle Way between extreme views.

=== KEY: Building_c39b56
HEADWORD: Building
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apartment | Architecture | Barn | Factory | Hotel | House | Skyscraper | Warehouse
ENTRY:
Enclosed structure with a roof and walls, usually standing permanently in one place, such as a house or factory. Buildings vary in size, shape, and function and have adapted over history to available materials, weather, land prices, ground conditions, specific uses, prestige, and aesthetics. They serve needs including shelter from weather, security, living space, privacy, and storage. The word structure is used more broadly, including formations without walls, such as fences. Interest in sustainable planning has made green buildings an intentional part of the design of many new ones.

There is clear evidence of homebuilding from around 18,000 BC, and buildings became common during the Neolithic period. Common materials include brick, concrete, stone, and combinations of them. In the 19th and 20th centuries steel framing and reinforced concrete allowed unprecedented heights, as in the [[Skyscraper|skyscraper]]. The distinction between low-rise and high-rise is debated, but generally three stories or less is low-rise.

Residential buildings include single-family [[House|houses]], duplexes, and [[Apartment|apartment]] buildings, as well as institutions such as nursing homes, orphanages, and prisons. Yurts and motorhomes are considered dwellings but not buildings. Commercial buildings house at least one business and no residents, such as stores, restaurants, and [[Hotel|hotels]]. Industrial buildings, such as [[Factory|factories]] and [[Warehouse|warehouses]], are those in which heavy industry takes place. Agricultural buildings are outbuildings on farms, such as [[Barn|barns]]. Some buildings mix uses, commonly commercial and residential, and a group of interrelated buildings may be called a complex.

Designing, constructing, and operating a building is a collective effort of developers, financiers, local planning authorities, construction managers, and licensed [[Architecture|architects]] and engineers.

Any of over 250 species of hairy, social [[Bee|bees]] in the genus *Bombus*. Bumblebees are found mainly in the [[Northern Hemisphere]], with a few lowland tropical species in South America. Most form colonies headed by a single queen. Colonies are smaller than those of [[Honey bee|honey bees]], growing to as few as 50 individuals. Their decline in Europe, North America and Asia is a concern, caused by habitat loss, the mechanization of agriculture and [[Pesticide|pesticides]].

=== KEY: Buoyancy_3b6629

ENTRY:
Upward force exerted by a fluid, opposing the weight of a partly or fully immersed object. [[Pressure]] in a fluid increases with depth, so the pressure at the bottom of a submerged object exceeds that at its top, producing a net upward force. [[Archimedes' principle]] states that the force equals the weight of the fluid displaced by the object. === KEY: Business_d6e6cb

ENTRY:
Organization or activity engaged in producing or buying and selling goods and services, usually for [[Profit (economics)|profit]]. Individuals who work neither for a government agency nor for a mission-driven nonprofit are almost always employed in the private sector, by businesses whose primary goal is to generate profit through the creation and capture of economic value above cost.

Forms of ownership vary by jurisdiction. A sole proprietorship is owned by one person, who has unlimited liability for the business's obligations and is personally taxed on all its income. Marketing scholar Philip Kotler has similarly said a business exists primarily to serve. Businesses have driven technological advance and raised living standards since the Industrial Revolution, though critics point to monopolistic behavior and environmental impacts, which prompt regulation.

=== KEY: Byzantine_Empire_257e52
HEADWORD: Byzantine Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constantinople | Corpus Juris Civilis | Diocletian | Hagia Sophia | Iconoclasm | Justinian I | Ottoman Empire | Rashidun Caliphate
ENTRY:
Eastern continuation of the Roman Empire, centered on [[Constantinople]] during late antiquity and the Middle Ages (330–1453). Its citizens called it the Roman Empire and themselves Romans; the term Byzantine Empire was coined only after its demise, and some modern historians believe it should not be used because it was originally prejudicial and inaccurate.

[[Diocletian]] (r. 284–305) divided the Roman Empire into eastern and western halves. Constantine I (r. 324–337) legalized Christianity and founded Constantinople as the eastern capital. Theodosius I (r. 379–395) made Christianity the state religion, and Greek gradually replaced Latin for official use. The empire survived the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century AD and adopted a defensive strategy, passing through recurring cycles of decline and recovery.

It reached its greatest extent under [[Justinian I]] (r. 527–565), who briefly reconquered much of Italy and the western Mediterranean coast; his reign produced the [[Corpus Juris Civilis]] and the rebuilding of [[Hagia Sophia]]. In the 630s and 640s Arab conquests permanently took Syria and Egypt, which passed to the [[Rashidun Caliphate]], and Africa was lost in 698. The [[Iconoclasm|Iconoclastic controversy]] (726–843) divided the empire over the veneration of icons. Under the Macedonian dynasty the empire expanded again, with a two-century renaissance.

The Fourth Crusade's sack of Constantinople in 1204 fragmented the empire into Greek rump states and Latin realms. Constantinople was recovered in 1261, but the reconstituted empire held only regional power. The [[Ottoman Empire|Ottomans]] annexed its remaining territories in wars of the 14th and 15th centuries, and the fall of Constantinople to Mehmed II in 1453 ended the empire.

HEADWORD: CMYK color model
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Subtractive color model used in color printing, with four [[Ink|inks]]: [[Cyan|cyan]], [[Magenta|magenta]], [[Yellow|yellow]] and black, the key plate. Inks reduce the light reflected from a white substrate such as paper, and black results from combining them. Black ink improves the precision of outlines and text, reduces ink consumption and drying time, and produces more consistent dark tones. Halftoning varies the size and spacing of dots to produce intermediate tones; 20% magenta coverage, for example, produces a pink tone. Unlike [[RGB color model|RGB]] displays, which emit light, CMYK has a more limited gamut, and conversion between the two requires color management profiles because no universal formula exists. CMYK printing was first implemented in the 1890s for color newspaper illustrations and comic strips.
