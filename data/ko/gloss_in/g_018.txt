=== KEY: Bone_d790f0
HEADWORD: Bone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cartilage | Connective tissue | Hearing
ENTRY:
Rigid organ forming part of the skeleton of most vertebrate animals.

Bones protect the organs of the body, produce red and white blood cells, store minerals, help regulate acid–base homeostasis, provide structure and support, and enable mobility and [[Hearing|hearing]]. In the human body about 300 bones are present at birth; many fuse during development, leaving 206 separate bones in the adult, not counting numerous small sesamoid bones. The largest is the femur, or thigh bone, and the smallest is the stapes in the middle ear.

Bone tissue is a mineralized [[Connective tissue|connective tissue]]. Its organic component is mainly ossein, a form of collagen, and its inorganic component is bone mineral, made of various salts. Osteoblasts form bone, osteocytes mineralize it, and osteoclasts resorb it. Bone comprises dense cortical (compact) bone and porous cancellous (spongy) bone, and bones may also contain marrow, nerves, blood vessels, and [[Cartilage|cartilage]].

Five types are found in the human body: long, short, flat, irregular, and sesamoid. Long bones, such as most limb bones, have a shaft and a rounded head at each end. Sesamoid bones, such as the patella, are embedded in tendons and increase the leverage of muscles.

Bone has a high compressive strength of about 170 MPa, a poorer tensile strength of 104–121 MPa, and a very low shear strength of 51.6 MPa. It is essentially brittle but has significant elasticity, contributed chiefly by collagen. Bones also act as a reservoir of calcium and phosphate, house bone marrow, and, through osteocalcin, function as an endocrine organ.

=== KEY: Bone_fracture_23160b
HEADWORD: Bone fracture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amputation | Bone | Calcium | Crystal | Infection | Osteoporosis | Tobacco
ENTRY:
Partial or complete break in the continuity of a [[Bone|bone]]. Fractures may result from high-force impact or stress, or from minimal trauma when conditions such as [[Osteoporosis|osteoporosis]], osteopenia or bone cancer weaken the bone; the latter are called pathologic fractures. An open fracture breaks through the skin, which raises the risk of [[Infection|infection]]. Bone tissue has no pain receptors, but the surrounding periosteum does. === KEY: Book_2b1f94
HEADWORD: Book
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Braille | Censorship | Cuneiform | Fiction | Johannes Gutenberg | Maya script | Mesopotamia | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Written work of substantial length, typically printed pages bound together and protected by a cover.

Books are broadly classified into [[Fiction|fiction]], containing invented or imaginary narratives, and non-fiction, containing content intended as factual truth. Google Books estimated in 2010 that roughly 130 million unique books had been published. The word comes from the Old English *bōc*, possibly from a Germanic root meaning beech.

There is no consensus on a basic definition. [[UNESCO]] defined a book in 1964, for national statistics, as a non-periodical printed publication of at least 49 pages, exclusive of the cover pages, published in the country and made available to the public. Historian James Raven proposed a broader definition, a portable, durable, replicable, and legible means of recording and disseminating information.

Proto-writing was independently developed into full writing in at least three cases: Sumerian [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] in [[Mesopotamia]], written Chinese, and the [[Maya script]] in Mesoamerica. The earliest surviving texts widely accepted as writing are cuneiform clay tablets, created c. 3200 BC during the Uruk period in modern Iraq. The codex, a bound book of separate leaves, arose in the Roman period around the 1st to 4th centuries AD and, unlike a scroll, allowed random access to content. The invention of movable-type printing by [[Johannes Gutenberg]] c. 1440 accelerated production and lowered costs.

Publishing, now usually undertaken by commercial companies, has been reshaped by ebooks and audiobooks and by accessible formats such as [[Braille|braille]] and large print. Books are sold in stores and online and borrowed from libraries. Their reception has had social consequences, including [[Censorship|censorship]] and, in periods of ideological conflict, destruction.

=== KEY: Boolean_algebra_bb3eec
HEADWORD: Boolean algebra
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Claude Shannon | Elementary algebra | George Boole | Logic gate | Propositional logic | Set theory
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics and logic using the truth values true and false as variables. Unlike [[Elementary algebra|elementary algebra]], it uses the logical operators conjunction (∧), disjunction (∨) and negation (¬). [[George Boole]] introduced it in *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847), and it was perfected in the late 19th century by Jevons, Schröder, Huntington and others. In the 1930s [[Claude Shannon]] applied its rules to switching circuits, analyzing and designing them with [[Logic gate|logic gates]]. The algebra is now fundamental to digital electronics and is provided for in all modern programming languages. It is also used in [[Set theory|set theory]] and statistics, and it describes classical [[Propositional logic|propositional logic]].

=== KEY: Boolean_satisfiability_problem_113511

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Problem of determining whether a Boolean formula can be made true. It asks whether some assignment of TRUE or FALSE to the formula's variables makes the formula evaluate to TRUE. SAT was the first problem proven to be NP-complete. No known algorithm solves every instance in polynomial time, and resolving whether one exists would settle the [[P versus NP problem]]. Heuristic solvers nonetheless handle instances with tens of thousands of variables, enough for many practical uses in [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]], [[Circuit design|circuit design]] and automatic theorem proving.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
State of matter formed by cooling a dilute gas of [[Boson|bosons]] to temperatures close to [[Absolute zero|absolute zero]]. === KEY: Botany_1c80f3
HEADWORD: Botany
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Carl Linnaeus
ENTRY:
Branch of biology that studies the growth, reproduction, and evolution of plants; also called plant science.

Plants can be studied at levels from biochemistry, molecular biology, and genetics to ecology, pathology, anatomy, morphology, and taxonomy. They include approximately 410,000 species of land plants and some 391,000 species of vascular plants, of which approximately 369,000 are flowering plants and approximately 20,000 are bryophytes. The term comes from the Ancient Greek *botanē*, meaning pasture, herbs, or fodder.

Botany originated in prehistoric herbalism, the identification of edible, poisonous, and medicinal plants. Theophrastus (c. 371–287 BC), a student of [[Aristotle]] and called the Father of Botany, wrote *Enquiry into Plants* and *On the Causes of Plants*, the most important contributions to the subject until the Middle Ages. Medieval physic gardens were forerunners of university botanical gardens; the Padua garden of 1545 is usually considered the first still in its original location. Cataloguing garden collections led in 1753 to the binomial nomenclature of [[Carl Linnaeus]], still used to name all biological species.

In the last two decades of the 20th century, botanists used molecular genetic analysis, including DNA sequences, to classify plants more accurately. Dominant 21st-century themes are molecular genetics and epigenetics, which study the control of gene expression during differentiation of plant cells and tissues.

Botanical research supplies staple foods, timber, oil, rubber, fiber, and drugs, such as aspirin derived from willow bark, and serves horticulture, agriculture, forestry, plant breeding, and the maintenance of biodiversity. An estimated 45% of known plant species face extinction risks (as of 2023).

=== KEY: Brain_71bb21
HEADWORD: Brain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Central nervous system | Cognition | Neuron | Spinal cord
ENTRY:
Organ forming the center of the nervous system in all vertebrate and most invertebrate animals, consisting of nervous tissue and typically located in the head.

The brain receives information from the sensory nervous system, processes it (thought, [[Cognition|cognition]], and intelligence), and coordinates motor control and the endocrine system. It acts on the body by generating patterns of muscle activity and by driving the secretion of hormones, allowing rapid and coordinated responses to the environment. Simple responses such as reflexes can be mediated by the spinal cord, but sophisticated control of behavior requires a centralized brain.

Vertebrate brains develop from the dorsal nerve cord as an enlargement at the end of the neural tube and can be embryonically divided into forebrain, midbrain, and hindbrain. Together, the brain and [[Spinal cord|spinal cord]] constitute the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]]. In humans, the main regions are the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem; the cerebrum has two hemispheres and four lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital.

Brains are composed primarily of [[Neuron|neurons]] and glial cells. Glial cells provide structural and metabolic support, insulation, and guidance of development. Neurons send signals to specific target cells, sometimes over long distances, by means of axons, which are usually myelinated and carry rapid electrical pulses called action potentials. The human cerebral cortex contains approximately 14–16 billion neurons and the cerebellum an estimated 55–70 billion; another account gives about 86 billion neurons in total. The human brain weighs about 1.4 kg (3 lb) and consumes roughly 20% of the body's oxygen and energy while making up about 2% of body mass. Its neuroplasticity enables learning and recovery from injury.

=== KEY: Brain_death_829bc3

ENTRY:
Permanent, irreversible and complete loss of brain function. It differs from [[Vegetative state|persistent vegetative state]], in which some autonomic functions remain. Brain death is used as an indicator of legal death in many jurisdictions, though it is defined inconsistently. === KEY: Brain_injury_3e2d5b

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Concussion | Infection | Physical therapy | Psychotherapy | Stroke
ENTRY:
Destruction or degeneration of brain cells, also called brain damage or neurotrauma. It may result from external trauma, such as accidents or falls, or from internal causes such as [[Stroke|strokes]] or [[Infection|infections]]. Traumatic brain injury (TBI), caused by external physical trauma to the head, is the most common type. Primary injuries occur at the moment of trauma, while secondary injuries develop afterward from physiological responses such as hypoxia and swelling. Symptoms can include headaches, confusion and fatigue, and severe injuries may cause loss of consciousness and long-term cognitive impairment. Treatments include medication, [[Physical therapy|physical therapy]], [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] and surgery.
