=== KEY: Resistor_858963
HEADWORD: Resistor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric current | Integrated circuit | Ohm | Ohm's law | Voltage | Watt
ENTRY:
Passive two-terminal electronic component that provides electrical resistance in a circuit. Resistors reduce [[Electric current|current]] flow, adjust signal levels, divide [[Voltage|voltages]], bias active elements and terminate transmission lines. High-power types dissipate many [[Watt|watts]] as heat in motor controls, power distribution systems or test loads for generators. Variable resistors serve as volume controls or lamp dimmers, or as sensors of heat, light, humidity, force or chemical activity. Resistors are ubiquitous in electronic equipment and are also implemented within [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]]. Their function is set by resistance, measured in [[Ohm|ohms]], and commercial resistors span more than nine orders of magnitude. Under [[Ohm's law]], the voltage across a resistor is proportional to the current through it, with its resistance as the constant of proportionality.

=== KEY: Resonance_685328
HEADWORD: Resonance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amplitude | Frequency | Galileo Galilei | Latin
ENTRY:
Phenomenon in which an oscillating system absorbs energy at its [[Frequency|resonant frequency]] and responds with maximum amplitude. It occurs when an external force or vibration matches that frequency, and the system then vibrates with greater [[Amplitude|amplitude]] than at other frequencies. This can be useful, as in musical instruments or radio receivers, but it can also cause excessive vibration or structural failure. Resonance occurs in mechanical, electrical, acoustic and electromagnetic systems, as well as in nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and electron spin resonance (ESR). Resonant systems can generate a specific frequency, as in musical instruments, or select one from a complex vibration, as in filters. The term comes from [[Latin]] *resonantia*, "echo", and originated in acoustics, where [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] discussed sympathetic resonance in musical instruments in *Dialogues Concerning Two New Sciences*.

=== KEY: Respiration_physiology_59326c
HEADWORD: Respiration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blood | Breathing | Capillary | Carbon dioxide | Cellular respiration
ENTRY:
Biological process of transporting oxygen from the environment to body tissues and removing carbon dioxide, using a respiratory system. Gas exchange in the lungs occurs through [[Breathing|ventilation]] (commonly called breathing), the movement of air in and out of the lungs, and perfusion, the pulmonary circulation of [[Blood|blood]] through the [[Capillary|pulmonary capillaries]]. Inhalation is usually active, while exhalation is usually passive, though speaking, singing, coughing and swimming involve active exhalation. Each breath of about 350 mL does not fill the alveoli with fresh air; it is mixed with about 2.5 L of functional residual capacity, the gas that remains in the lungs after each exhalation. Mechanisms keep this gas composition constant as it equilibrates with the blood. Physiological respiration differs from [[Cellular respiration|cellular respiration]], the metabolic process by which cells obtain energy from nutrients in the form of ATP and NADPH. Cellular respiration produces [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and water as waste products.

=== KEY: Respiratory_failure_e392e4
HEADWORD: Respiratory failure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide
ENTRY:
Condition in which inadequate gas exchange leaves arterial oxygen, [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], or both outside normal levels. A drop in blood oxygen is called hypoxemia, and a rise in arterial carbon dioxide is called hypercapnia. Respiratory failure is classified as Type 1 or Type 2, depending on whether carbon dioxide is high, and it can be acute or chronic. Clinical trials usually define it by increased respiratory rate, abnormal blood gases, or evidence of increased work of breathing. It can cause an altered state of consciousness because of ischemia in the brain. Typical reference values are PaO₂ above 80 mmHg (11 kPa) and PaCO₂ below 45 mmHg (6.0 kPa).

=== KEY: Respiratory_system_5110ef
HEADWORD: Respiratory system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amphibian | Bird | Fish | Gill | Insect | Lung | Mammal | Stoma
ENTRY:
Biological system of organs and structures used for gas exchange in animals and plants. In land animals the respiratory surface is internalized as the lining of the [[Lung|lungs]], where gas exchange occurs in millions of small air sacs with a rich blood supply: alveoli in [[Mammal|mammals]] and reptiles, atria in [[Bird|birds]]. Airways link these sacs to the environment. The trachea branches in the middle of the chest into two main bronchi, which divide into progressively narrower secondary and tertiary bronchi and then into bronchioles (parabronchi in birds). Air is pumped in by breathing, which involves the muscles of respiration; inhalation at rest results mainly from contraction of the diaphragm.

In humans and other mammals the tract is divided into an upper part (nose, nasal cavities, sinuses, pharynx, and the part of the larynx above the vocal folds) and a lower part (the lower larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli). The branching lower airways, called the respiratory tree, have about 23 generations in the adult human and about 13 in the mouse. Generations 0–16 serve only as conduits; gas exchange takes place in approximately generations 17–23. Because alveoli are dead ends, about 150 ml of dead space stays filled with air after exhalation.

In most [[Fish|fish]] and many other aquatic animals the system consists of [[Gill|gills]], whose thin filaments and lamellae expose a very large surface of vascularized tissue to water. [[Insect|Insects]] have very simple respiratory anatomy, and in [[Amphibian|amphibians]] the skin also plays a vital role in gas exchange. Plants also have respiratory systems, built around [[Stoma|stomata]], but the direction of gas exchange can be opposite to that in animals.

ENTRY:
Concept of coming back to life after death. It differs from [[Reincarnation|reincarnation]], which involves returning in a different body. Egyptian and Canaanite religions had cults of dying-and-rising gods such as [[Osiris]] and Baal. In the [[Hebrew Bible]], resurrection is rare, confined to a few prophetic miracles and to Daniel. === KEY: Retail_053e0b
HEADWORD: Retail
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Goods | Market (economics)
ENTRY:
Sale of [[Goods|goods]] and services to consumers, as distinct from wholesaling, which is sale to business or institutional customers. A retailer buys goods in large quantities from manufacturers, directly or through a wholesaler, and sells them in smaller quantities to consumers for a profit, making retailers the final link in the supply chain from producers to consumers. Retail [[Market (economics)|markets]] and shops date back to antiquity; some of the earliest retailers were itinerant peddlers, and over the centuries shops grew from little more than "rude booths" to modern shopping malls.

Retailing often occurs in stores or service establishments but may also take place through direct selling, such as vending machines, door-to-door sales, or electronic channels. The term also applies to service providers that sell to consumers, including retail banking, tourism, insurance, private healthcare, and private education. In some jurisdictions, legal definitions of retail specify that at least 80 percent of sales must be to end-users.

Retail operations center on obtaining goods in needed quantities and placing them where customers will buy them, so purchasing and supply management are core to strategy. Day-to-day decisions are often described through the retail marketing mix of six "Ps": product, place, promotion, price, personnel, and presentation. Pricing tactics include discounts, everyday low pricing, high–low pricing, loss leaders, bundling, and psychological pricing. One cited estimate is that in 2012 about 70% of United States retail workers were part-time. Many retailers have moved to multichannel models combining physical and online sales and have added services such as buy online and pick up in store, while some firms have tested cashierless store formats. Support services may include credit, delivery, and advisory services.

=== KEY: Revolution_ad09b2
HEADWORD: Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Revolution | Democracy | Fascism | French Revolution | Haitian Revolution | Iranian Revolution | Mexican Revolution | Socialism
ENTRY:
Rapid, fundamental transformation of a society's class, state, ethnic or religious structures. According to the sociologist Jack Goldstone, revolutions share a core of efforts to change the political regime through a competing vision of a just order, notable mass mobilization, and noninstitutionalized action such as demonstrations, strikes or violence. Some begin with peasant uprisings or guerrilla warfare on a country's periphery; others with urban insurrection aimed at seizing the capital. They may be inspired by ideologies such as nationalism, republicanism, [[Democracy|democracy]], liberalism, [[Fascism|fascism]] or [[Socialism|socialism]]. A regime may become vulnerable after military defeat, economic chaos, or persistent repression and corruption. Revolutions typically provoke counter-revolutions, and autocracies that take power during them tend to be durable. Notable examples include the [[American Revolution]] (1765–1783), the [[French Revolution]] (1789–1799), the [[Haitian Revolution]] (1791–1804), the [[Mexican Revolution]] (1910–1920) and the [[Iranian Revolution]] of 1979.

=== KEY: Revolutions_of_1848_23c675
HEADWORD: Revolutions of 1848
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Absolute monarchy | Austrian Empire | Denmark | Feudalism | Nationalism | Netherlands | Serfdom | Sicily
ENTRY:
Series of revolutions across Europe between January 1848 and October 1849, also known as the springtime of the peoples. More than fifty countries were affected, though revolutionaries coordinated little. They generally opposed conservative systems such as [[Absolute monarchy|absolute monarchy]] and [[Feudalism|feudalism]], and sought nation states founded on constitutionalism and popular sovereignty. The wave began in [[Sicily]] in January 1848 and spread after the revolution in France in February. Causes included dissatisfaction with political leadership, demands for democracy and a free press, working-class demands for economic rights, and rising [[Nationalism|nationalism]]. The European potato failure caused mass starvation, migration and unrest. Many revolutions were quickly suppressed, with tens of thousands killed and many more exiled. Still, reforms included the abolition of [[Serfdom|serfdom]] in [[Austrian Empire|Austria]] and Hungary, the end of absolute monarchy in [[Denmark]], and representative democracy in the [[Netherlands]].

=== KEY: Reynolds_number_d824fa
HEADWORD: Reynolds number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fluid dynamics | Turbulence
ENTRY:
Characteristic number in [[Fluid dynamics|fluid dynamics]] that measures the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. It helps predict flow patterns: low Reynolds numbers favor smooth, laminar (sheet-like) flow, while high values tend toward [[Turbulence|turbulence]], in which eddy currents churn the flow and, in liquids, increase the chances of cavitation. The number predicts the transition from laminar to turbulent flow and is used to scale experiments, such as between a model aircraft in a wind tunnel and its full-size version. Pipe flows are typically classified as laminar below Re = 2,300, transitional between 2,300 and 4,000, and turbulent above 4,000, though thresholds vary with geometry and disturbances. George Stokes introduced the concept in 1851. Arnold Sommerfeld named the number in 1908 after Osborne Reynolds, who popularized its use in 1883.

Rare, silvery-gray, heavy transition metal (symbol Re, atomic number 75) of group 7 in the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. Rhenium is one of the rarest elements in the Earth's crust. It has one of the highest melting and boiling points of any element and resembles manganese and technetium chemically. It is mainly obtained as a by-product of refining [[Molybdenum|molybdenum]] and [[Copper|copper]] ores. Nickel-based superalloys containing up to 6% rhenium are used in the combustion chambers and turbine blades of [[Jet engine|jet engines]], the element's largest single use. Masataka Ogawa announced an element in 1908 that he had mistakenly identified as element 43, but the element was rediscovered in 1925 by Walter Noddack, Ida Noddack and Otto Berg.

Art of persuasion, one of the three ancient arts of discourse (the *trivium*) of classical antiquity, along with grammar and [[Logic|logic]]. As an academic discipline in the humanities, rhetoric studies the techniques that speakers and writers use to inform, persuade and motivate audiences. [[Aristotle]] defined it as "the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion" and identified three audience appeals: *logos*, *pathos* and *ethos*. The five canons of rhetoric, first codified in classical Rome, are *inventio*, *dispositio*, *elocutio*, *memoria* and *pronuntiatio*. From ancient Greece to the late 19th century, rhetoric was central to Western and Islamic education. [[Plato]] criticized the [[Sophist|Sophists]] for using it to deceive rather than to discover truth, and in *Gorgias* he described it as a form of flattery, a view some scholars contest. Modern scholars study rhetoric across the sciences, fine art, journalism and digital media.

=== KEY: Rhyme_5861b9

Repetition of similar sounds, usually the same [[Phoneme|phonemes]], in the final stressed syllables of two or more words. === KEY: Rhythm_9e8218
HEADWORD: Rhythm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dance | Façade | Gamelan | Hip-hop | Music
ENTRY:
Regulated succession of strong and weak elements, or of opposite or different conditions, in time. The word comes from Greek *rhythmos*, "any regular recurring motion, symmetry." It most commonly refers to such movement of beats in [[Music|music]], but also applies to poetry, mechanical operations, and biological processes, and to cyclical natural phenomena with periodicities from microseconds to many years. The *Oxford English Dictionary* defines rhythm in verse as the measured flow of words or phrases, forming patterns of sound determined by the relation of long and short or stressed and unstressed syllables.

Rhythm is related to, and distinguished from, pulse, meter, and beats. Most music, dance, and oral poetry maintain an underlying metric level, the pulse or beat, a series of identical periodic stimuli to which listeners entrain as they tap a foot or [[Dance|dance]].

In the performing arts, rhythm is the timing of events on a human scale: musical sounds and silences, the steps of a dance, or the meter of speech and poetry. In [[Hip-hop|hip-hop]], the rhythmic delivery of lyrics is one of the most important elements of the style. Rhythm may also be visual, as "timed movement through space"; architects speak of the rhythm of a building in the spacing of windows, columns, and other elements of the [[Façade|façade]]. Percussion, whose fast-transient sounds define a beat clearly, favors cultures that develop polyrhythm and polymeter, as in the cross-rhythms of Sub-Saharan Africa and the interlocking kotekan rhythms of the [[Gamelan|gamelan]].

=== KEY: Ribosome_500adc

ENTRY:
Ribonucleoprotein particle found in most cells. It synthesizes [[Protein|proteins]] through [[Translation (biology)|translation]] of genetic information carried in messenger RNA (mRNA). During translation, the ribosome decodes successive codons in an mRNA molecule and, with the aid of transfer RNA (tRNA), links [[Amino acid|amino acids]] into a polypeptide chain. === KEY: Rice_5ced19

ENTRY:
[[Cereal]] grain, the staple food of over half of the world's population, particularly in [[Africa]] and [[Asia]]. Rice is the seed of the grass species *Oryza sativa* (Asian rice) or, less commonly, *Oryza glaberrima* (African rice). Asian rice was domesticated approximately 13,500 to 8,200 years ago in what is now [[China]]; African rice was domesticated independently in West Africa about 3,000 years ago. In 2023, 800 million tons were produced, third after [[Sugarcane|sugarcane]] and [[Maize|maize]], but only some 8% is traded internationally. China, India, and Indonesia are the largest consumers.

The plant can grow to over 1 m tall, or 5 m in deep water, and takes about six months from seed to harvest. Grain is planted directly in the field, or seedlings are transplanted, as is done by hand for most rice in Asia. Yields are reduced by pests including insects, rodents, and birds, by weeds, and by diseases such as rice blast.

Milling removes the outer layers, giving products from brown rice to white rice. Rice contains no gluten and provides protein but not all essential amino acids; cooked white rice contains 29% carbohydrate and 2% protein, with some manganese. Starch composition determines texture. Long-grain rice from the Indica cultivar is dry and fluffy and includes aromatic varieties such as basmati and jasmine, while short-grain Japonica rice is sticky and used in sushi and mochi. Black rice, colored by [[Anthocyanin|anthocyanins]], was reserved for the royal family in ancient China. Golden rice is a variety [[Genetic engineering|genetically engineered]] to contain vitamin A. Rice production is estimated to have caused over 1% of global greenhouse gas emissions in 2022.

=== KEY: Riemann_hypothesis_867439
HEADWORD: Riemann hypothesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bernhard Riemann | David Hilbert | Millennium Prize Problems | Number theory | Prime number | Pure mathematics | Riemann zeta function
ENTRY:
Conjecture about the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. The hypothesis states that all nontrivial zeros of the [[Riemann zeta function]] lie on the critical line, where the real part is 1/2. The zeta function has trivial zeros at the negative even integers, so the conjecture concerns the other zeros. [[Bernhard Riemann]] proposed it in his 1859 paper "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude." It is of great interest in [[Number theory|number theory]] because it implies results about the distribution of [[Prime number|prime numbers]]. Many consider it the most important unsolved problem in [[Pure mathematics|pure mathematics]]. It is one of the [[Millennium Prize Problems]], which carry a US$1 million prize. It forms part of the eighth of [[David Hilbert]]'s 23 unsolved problems. Numerical checks confirm it for the first 10¹³ nontrivial zeros, but no proof is known.

=== KEY: Riemann_surface_c78c80
HEADWORD: Riemann surface
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bernhard Riemann | Möbius strip | Sphere | Torus
ENTRY:
Connected one-dimensional complex manifold. Riemann surfaces, first studied by and named after [[Bernhard Riemann]], can be thought of as deformed versions of the complex plane: locally they resemble patches of the plane, but their global shape can differ. A [[Sphere|sphere]], a [[Torus|torus]] and several sheets glued together are examples. They include graphs of multivalued functions such as log(z), which become single-valued on the surface. A two-dimensional real surface can be turned into a Riemann surface if and only if it is orientable and metrizable. The sphere and torus admit such structures, but the [[Möbius strip]] and the Klein bottle do not. Every compact Riemann surface is a complex algebraic curve, by Chow's theorem and the Riemann–Roch theorem.

=== KEY: Riemannian_geometry_6b068c
HEADWORD: Riemannian geometry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bernhard Riemann | General relativity
ENTRY:
Branch of differential geometry that studies Riemannian manifolds. It studies smooth manifolds equipped with a Riemannian metric, a smoothly varying inner product on each tangent space, which defines length, angle, surface area and volume locally. Distances are measured along curves, as an insect crawling over a surface would measure them. The field originated with the vision of [[Bernhard Riemann]], expressed in his inaugural lecture "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry." It generalizes the differential geometry of surfaces to higher dimensions. Its development enabled the formulation of [[General relativity|Einstein's general theory of relativity]], and it made a profound impact on group theory, representation theory, analysis and topology.

=== KEY: Ring_of_Fire_72223d
HEADWORD: Ring of Fire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mount St. Helens | Oceanic trench | Stratovolcano | Volcano
ENTRY:
Tectonic belt of earthquakes and volcanoes surrounding most of the Pacific Ocean. It is about 40,000 km (25,000 mi) long and up to about 500 km (310 mi) wide. The belt contains between 750 and 915 active or dormant [[Volcano|volcanoes]], around two-thirds of the world total, and about 90% of the world's earthquakes, including most of its largest, occur within it. It was created by the subduction of several tectonic plates at convergent boundaries, forming [[Oceanic trench|oceanic trenches]], volcanic arcs and back-arc basins. More than 350 of its volcanoes have been active in historical times, and the four largest volcanic eruptions in the Holocene epoch all occurred there. Most of Earth's active volcanoes with summits above sea level lie within it, many of them [[Stratovolcano|stratovolcanoes]] such as [[Mount St. Helens]]. Its inclusion of areas such as the Antarctic Peninsula and western Indonesia is disputed.
