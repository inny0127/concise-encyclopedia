Landlocked country in [[Central Africa|Central Africa]] (pop., 2024: 5,357,744). It is bordered by [[Chad|Chad]], [[Sudan|Sudan]], [[South Sudan|South Sudan]], the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the Republic of the Congo and [[Cameroon|Cameroon]], and covers about 620,000 sq km. Its capital and largest city is [[Bangui|Bangui]], on the southern border with the Democratic Republic of the Congo. The territory was a French colony known as Ubangi-Shari before independence in 1960. Autocratic rulers followed, including Jean-Bédel Bokassa, who renamed the country the Central African Empire and ruled as monarch from 1976 to 1979. The Central African Bush War began in 2004, and civil war resumed in 2012 despite peace treaties in 2007 and 2011. Sango is the national language and, with French, co-official. Most of the land is [[Savanna|Sudano-Guinean savanna]], with equatorial forest in the south.

=== KEY: Central_America_ec70c3
HEADWORD: Central America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christopher Columbus | Costa Rica | El Salvador | Guatemala | Honduras | New Spain | Nicaragua | Panama
ENTRY:
Subregion of North America, an isthmus bordering Mexico to the north, Colombia to the southeast, the Caribbean to the east, and the Pacific Ocean to the southwest. It is usually defined as seven countries: Belize, [[Costa Rica]], [[El Salvador]], [[Guatemala]], [[Honduras]], [[Nicaragua]], and [[Panama]]; in specialized contexts Mexico is also included. The Mesoamerican biodiversity hotspot extends from southern Mexico to southeastern Panama. Active faults and the Central America Volcanic Arc make seismic activity high.

The region includes parts of two cultural areas: the Mesoamerican, covering Guatemala, Belize, El Salvador, and western Honduras and Nicaragua, and the Isthmo-Colombian, covering Panama, Costa Rica, and eastern Honduras and Nicaragua. Hundreds of indigenous groups lived there before the voyages of [[Christopher Columbus]], notably the Maya in the north. Spain began colonization from 1502 and began conquering the Maya kingdoms in 1523. From 1609 to 1821 most territories, except what became Belize and Panama, were governed from Mexico City as the Captaincy General of Guatemala within [[New Spain]].

Independence from Spain was proclaimed on September 15, 1821. Some provinces were annexed to the First Mexican Empire, but in 1823 they seceded to form the Federal Republic of Central America. Costa Rica, Guatemala, Honduras, and Nicaragua became independent countries in 1838, El Salvador in 1841, Panama in 1903, and Belize in 1981.

Economies depend heavily on agriculture, particularly coffee, bananas, and sugar, as well as tourism, manufacturing, and remittances from migrants in the United States. The Panama Canal opened in 1914. The region continues to face poverty, violence, corruption, and natural disasters.

=== KEY: Central_Asia_96542e
HEADWORD: Central Asia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bactria | Russian Empire | Silk Road | Sogdia | Soviet Union | Steppe | Tian Shan | Uyghurs
ENTRY:
Region of Asia comprising Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan and most of Kazakhstan. Its arid [[Steppe|steppes]], mountain ranges such as the [[Tian Shan|Tian Shan]], and deserts including the Karakum and Kyzylkum supported nomadic pastoralism alongside irrigated agriculture in river valleys. Iranian peoples such as the [[Bactria|Bactrians]], [[Sogdia|Sogdians]] and Scythians inhabited the region predominantly before and in the early Islamic era. Turkic migration made it the homeland of the Kazakhs, Kyrgyz, Turkmens, [[Uyghurs|Uyghurs]] and Uzbeks, and Turkic languages largely replaced Iranian ones, except in Tajikistan. The [[Silk Road|Silk Road]] trade routes crossed the region. From the mid-19th century until near the end of the 20th century, Central Asia was part of the [[Russian Empire|Russian Empire]] and then the [[Soviet Union|Soviet Union]]. The five countries became independent after the Soviet dissolution in 1991.

=== KEY: Central_Park_28b9cf
HEADWORD: Central Park
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Frederick Law Olmsted | Manhattan | New York City
ENTRY:
Urban park in [[Manhattan|Manhattan]], [[New York City|New York City]], between the Upper West Side and Upper East Side neighborhoods. It was the first landscaped park in the United States. At 843 acres (341 ha), it is the sixth-largest park in the city and the most visited urban park in the United States, with an estimated 42 million visitors annually as of 2016. The park is owned by the New York City Department of Parks and Recreation, but since 1998 the non-profit Central Park Conservancy has managed it under a public–private partnership. [[Frederick Law Olmsted|Frederick Law Olmsted]] and Calvert Vaux won an 1858 design competition with their "Greensward Plan." Construction began in 1857, and existing settlements, including Seneca Village, were seized by eminent domain and razed. The park was completed in 1876, and Robert Moses began a clean-up program in the 1930s after a period of decline.

=== KEY: Central_business_district_75c44e
HEADWORD: Central business district
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chicago | Honolulu | Moscow | Vancouver
ENTRY:
Commercial and business center of a city, often described in larger cities as a financial district. The district often coincides with the city center or downtown, although many cities have one located away from the traditional center. It is highly accessible and has a large variety and concentration of specialized goods and services. In the 21st century, urbanization has produced megacities with several such districts spread across the urban area.

=== KEY: Central_heating_e00cd8
HEADWORD: Central heating
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning
ENTRY:
Heating system that supplies warmth to many spaces in a building from one main source of heat. Most central heating systems use a furnace that converts fuel or electricity to heat, although some use a heat pump that transfers heat from outdoors. Heat is circulated by fans forcing warm air through ducts, by low-pressure steam sent to radiators, or by pumps moving hot water through radiators. Primary energy sources include coal or wood, oil, kerosene, natural gas and electricity. Compared with fireplaces and wood stoves, central heating gives more uniform temperature control, usually with automatic control of the furnace. Where ducts carry air, central air conditioning can be added to form a [[Heating, ventilation, and air conditioning|heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC)]] system. In 1793 British engineer William Strutt developed a hot-air system that used metal ducts to distribute furnace-heated air.

=== KEY: Central_limit_theorem_782909
HEADWORD: Central limit theorem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binomial distribution | Normal distribution | Probability theory
ENTRY:
Theorem in [[Probability theory|probability theory]] stating that, under appropriate conditions, the distribution of a normalized sample mean converges to a [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]]. This holds even when the original variables are not normally distributed, so methods developed for normal distributions apply to many other problems. Earlier versions date back to 1811, but in its modern form it was only precisely stated in the 1920s. The common form requires independent and identically distributed random variables, a requirement that can be weakened under certain conditions. The earliest version, that the normal distribution may approximate the [[Binomial distribution|binomial distribution]], is the de Moivre–Laplace theorem. In statistics it justifies the use of z-tests, t-tests and confidence intervals without assuming population normality.

=== KEY: Central_nervous_system_6dd645
HEADWORD: Central nervous system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Cephalopod | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Part of the nervous system consisting primarily of the brain and spinal cord. In vertebrates both are enclosed in meninges, which form a barrier to chemicals dissolved in the blood and protect the brain from most neurotoxins commonly found in food. The brain sits in the skull and the spinal cord in the spinal canal within the vertebrae, ending roughly level with the first or second lumbar vertebra. White matter consists of axons and oligodendrocytes, while gray matter consists of neurons and unmyelinated fibers. Microglia, a specialized form of macrophage, are involved in the brain's immune function. Only [[Arthropod|arthropods]], [[Cephalopod|cephalopods]] and [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]] have a true brain. The retina is also technically part of the CNS.

=== KEY: Central_processing_unit_af8440
HEADWORD: Central processing unit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer program | Integrated circuit | Intel | John von Neumann
ENTRY:
Primary processor of a computer, whose electronic circuitry executes the instructions of a [[Computer program|computer program]] by performing arithmetic, logic, control and input/output operations. Its main parts are the arithmetic–logic unit (ALU), which performs arithmetic and logic operations; processor registers, which supply operands to the ALU and store its results; and a control unit that directs the fetching, decoding and execution of instructions. Most modern CPUs are implemented on [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]], and chips containing several processor cores are called multi-core processors. The term central processing unit has been in use since as early as 1955. In 1945 [[John von Neumann]] outlined the stored-program design in *First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC*, in which programs are held in memory rather than wired in. In 1971 the [[Intel]] 4004 became the first commercially available single-chip microprocessor, capable of 92,000 instructions per second with 2,300 transistors.

=== KEY: Centrifugal_force_ef815c
HEADWORD: Centrifugal force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Centrifuge | Christiaan Huygens | Isaac Newton
ENTRY:
Fictitious (inertial) force that appears to act on all objects when viewed in a rotating frame of reference. It is directed perpendicularly away from the axis of rotation, with magnitude F = mω²ρ for an object of mass m at distance ρ from the axis, where ω is the angular velocity. The concept simplifies analysis of rotating devices such as [[Centrifuge|centrifuges]] and banked curves. The Neo-Latin term *vi centrifuga* appears in notes by [[Christiaan Huygens]] from 1659, and [[Isaac Newton]] developed the idea in *Principia* in 1687.

=== KEY: Centrifuge_d4fbf7
HEADWORD: Centrifuge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Centrifugal force | Dairy | Isotope | Washing machine
ENTRY:
Device that uses [[Centrifugal force|centrifugal force]] to subject a specimen to a specified constant force, most often to separate components of a fluid. Spinning fluid at high speed in a container separates components by density, driving denser substances and particles outward, or separates liquids from solids. In a laboratory sample tube, denser particles settle to the bottom while lighter substances rise to the top. Industrial centrifuges sediment suspended solids or separate immiscible liquids, and the cream separator in a [[Dairy|dairy]] is an example. Ultracentrifuges can separate fine particles down to the nano-scale and molecules of different masses. Large centrifuges simulate high-gravity environments, such as high-G training for test pilots. Medium-sized machines in [[Washing machine|washing machines]] and some swimming pools draw water out of fabrics. Gas centrifuges separate [[Isotope|isotopes]], such as enriching nuclear fuel for fissile isotopes. In 1864 Antonin Prandtl proposed a dairy centrifuge to separate cream from milk.

=== KEY: Centriole_817eb8
HEADWORD: Centriole
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eukaryote
ENTRY:
Cylindrical organelle, composed mainly of the protein tubulin, found in most [[Eukaryote|eukaryotic]] cells. Centrioles are typically made of nine sets of short microtubule triplets arranged in a cylinder, although crabs and *Drosophila melanogaster* embryos have nine doublets. A bound pair of centrioles, surrounded by pericentriolar material, forms a centrosome, the cell's primary microtubule-organizing center. Centrioles are absent from conifers, flowering plants and most fungi. They take part in forming cilia during interphase and in organizing the aster and mitotic spindle during cell division, although cells whose centrioles were removed by laser ablation can still progress through G1. Sperm centrioles are needed for sperm motility and for embryonic development. In humans the oocyte is acentriolar, so the sperm donates two centrioles that form the first centrosome in the zygote.

=== KEY: Centripetal_force_3bbc43
HEADWORD: Centripetal force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Centrifugal force | Christiaan Huygens | Isaac Newton
ENTRY:
Force that makes a body follow a curved path, always directed orthogonal to the body's motion toward the center of curvature. [[Isaac Newton]] coined the term from the Latin *centrum* (center) and *petere* (to seek). In Newtonian mechanics, gravity provides the centripetal force that causes astronomical orbits. For uniform circular motion at tangential speed v on a path of radius r, the force has magnitude Fc = mv²/r and points toward the center of the circle. [[Christiaan Huygens]] derived this mathematical description in 1659. The force can be a pull, such as rope tension for an object swung in a horizontal plane, or a push, such as a wall's normal reaction in a wall of death. It is distinct from the fictitious outward [[Centrifugal force|centrifugal force]] seen in a rotating frame.

=== KEY: Cephalopod_fb8019
HEADWORD: Cephalopod
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Colossal squid | Ordovician
ENTRY:
Member of the molluscan class *Cephalopoda*, such as a squid, octopus, cuttlefish or nautilus. These exclusively marine animals have bilateral symmetry, a prominent head, and arms or tentacles modified from the molluscan foot. Coleoids, which include octopuses, squids and cuttlefish, have an internal shell or none, while nautiluses keep an external shell. Cephalopods were dominant in the [[Ordovician]] period. About 800 living species are known, ranging from the 10 mm (0.3 in) *Idiosepius thailandicus* to the [[Colossal squid|colossal squid]], at 700 kilograms (1,500 lb) the largest extant invertebrate. They are widely regarded as the most intelligent invertebrates.

=== KEY: Cepheid_variable_1c8e3f
HEADWORD: Cepheid variable
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andromeda Galaxy | Cosmic distance ladder | Edwin Hubble | Milky Way
ENTRY:
Type of variable star that pulsates radially, varying in both diameter and temperature. Its brightness changes with a stable period, typically 1–100 days. A strong direct relationship exists between a Cepheid's luminosity and its pulsation period, so measuring the period gives the star's true luminosity and therefore its distance. This makes Cepheids benchmarks for the [[Cosmic distance ladder|cosmic distance ladder]]. Henrietta Swan Leavitt discovered the relationship in 1908 while studying thousands of variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds. In 1924 [[Edwin Hubble]] used Cepheids in the [[Andromeda Galaxy]] to show that it was not part of the [[Milky Way]]. Cepheids change brightness through the κ-mechanism, in which the main gas, thought to be helium, cycles between singly and doubly ionized states.

=== KEY: Ceramic_81fd1f
HEADWORD: Ceramic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | Piezoelectricity | Semiconductor | Superconductivity
ENTRY:
Inorganic, nonmetallic solid made by shaping and then firing a material such as clay at high temperature. Ceramics are hard, brittle, heat-resistant and corrosion-resistant. The earliest human-made ceramics were fired clay bricks; pots, vessels and figurines were hardened by sintering, and glazing later produced smooth, colored surfaces with less porosity. Ceramics now include domestic, industrial and building products, as well as advanced engineering materials such as [[Semiconductor|semiconductors]]. Ceramics are strong in compression but weak in shearing and tension, and most are good thermal and electrical insulators, though some are [[Piezoelectricity|piezoelectric]] or [[Superconductivity|superconductive]]. They can withstand temperatures from 1,000 °C to 1,600 °C (1,800 °F to 3,000 °F). The word derives from the [[Ancient Greek]] *keramikos*, meaning of or for pottery. Many experts do not consider glass, which is amorphous, to be a ceramic.

=== KEY: Ceramic_art_fdcb18
HEADWORD: Ceramic art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arts and Crafts movement | China | Japan | Korea | Porcelain
ENTRY:
Art made from ceramic materials, including clay, in forms such as pottery, tiles, figurines, sculptures and tableware. Some ceramic products are considered fine art, while others are decorative, industrial or applied art. Modern ceramic art in Britain and the United States took its inspiration in the early 20th century from the [[Arts and Crafts movement]], which emphasized traditional non-industrial production, faithfulness to the material and an absence of the excessive decoration typical of the Victorian era. Ceramic art has a long history in most developed cultures; the [[China|Chinese]], Cretan, Greek, Persian, Mayan, [[Japan|Japanese]] and [[Korea|Korean]] cultures are especially noted for ceramics. Its elements are the shape of the object, its decoration by painting or carving, and the glazing found on most ceramics. Earthenware is not fired to vitrification and remains permeable to water, while [[Porcelain]] is made by heating materials, generally including kaolin, to between 1,200 and 1,400 °C (2,200 and 2,600 °F).

=== KEY: Cereal_f33012
HEADWORD: Cereal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fertile Crescent | Green Revolution | Maize | Neolithic | Poaceae | Rice | Temperate climate | Wheat
ENTRY:
Grass of the family [[Poaceae]] cultivated for its edible grain. Cereals are the world's largest crops and staple foods. They include [[Rice|rice]], [[Wheat|wheat]], rye, oats, barley, millet, and [[Maize|maize]] (U.S.: corn). Edible grains of other plant families, such as amaranth, buckwheat, and quinoa, are called pseudocereals. The term derives from Latin *cerealis*, 'of [the goddess] Ceres', the Roman deity of grain crops and fertility.

Botanically, a cereal grain is a caryopsis, a fruit in which the seed coat is fused with the pericarp. Most cereals are annuals harvested once after sowing; rice, usually treated as an annual, can survive as a perennial. Cool-season cereals such as barley, oats, rye, and wheat suit a [[Temperate climate|temperate climate]], while millet and sorghum prefer a tropical one.

Charred wild wheat and barley 19,000 years old have been found at Ohalo II in Israel. Cereals were domesticated in the [[Neolithic]] around 8,000 years ago: wheat and barley in the [[Fertile Crescent]], rice and some millets in East Asia, and sorghum and other millets in Sudan. Maize was domesticated by Indigenous peoples in Mexico about 9,000 years ago.

Between 1964 and 2023 cereal production increased by 213%, mostly through higher yields, as harvested area rose by only 10%. This followed the [[Green Revolution]], which relied on mechanized tilling, monoculture, nitrogen fertilizers, and new seed strains. Cereals are eaten as whole grains or ground to flour for bread, porridge, and other foods, and their starch can be fermented into beer. Cereal farming has a substantial environmental impact; no-till farming and intercropping can mitigate it.

=== KEY: Cerebellum_3c2128
HEADWORD: Cerebellum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cerebrum | Vertebrate
ENTRY:
Major feature of the hindbrain of all [[Vertebrate|vertebrates]], located beneath the [[Cerebrum|cerebrum]]. Its name means little brain in Latin. Although usually smaller than the cerebrum, it may be as large as or larger than it in some animals, such as mormyrid fishes. In humans the cerebellum contributes to motor coordination, precision and accurate timing; it does not initiate movement but fine-tunes motor activity using input from the spinal cord and other parts of the brain. It also contributes to attention, language and the regulation of fear and pleasure responses, although its movement-related functions are the most solidly established. Its cortex is a thin layer folded like an accordion, and almost all of its output passes through four deep cerebellar nuclei. The cerebellum contains approximately 80% of the brain's neurons despite occupying only 10% of total brain volume. Cerebellar damage produces disorders in fine movement, equilibrium, posture and motor learning.

=== KEY: Cerebral_cortex_c3af58
HEADWORD: Cerebral cortex
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cerebrum | Mammal
ENTRY:
Outer layer of neural tissue of the [[Cerebrum|cerebrum]] in humans and other [[Mammal|mammals]]. It is the largest site of neural integration in the central nervous system and plays a key role in attention, perception, awareness, thought, memory, language and consciousness. The six-layered neocortex makes up about 90% of the cortex, with allocortex making up the remainder. Its folds, called *gyri* (ridges) and *sulci* (grooves), increase surface area within the cranium, and about two-thirds of the cortical surface is buried in the sulci. The four main lobes are the frontal, parietal, occipital and temporal lobes, with the insular cortex often included as the insular lobe. There are between 14 and 16 billion neurons in the human cerebral cortex. The motor cortex lies in the precentral gyrus, and the visual cortex in the occipital lobe.

=== KEY: Cerebral_palsy_4a7cc3
HEADWORD: Cerebral palsy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Botulinum toxin | Physical therapy | Twin
ENTRY:
Group of movement disorders that appear in early childhood. It is caused by abnormal development or damage to the parts of the brain that control movement, balance and posture, most often during pregnancy. Symptoms include poor coordination, stiff or weak muscles and tremors, and there may be problems with sensation, vision, hearing, speech, seizures and thinking. Symptoms may become more noticeable over the first years of life, although underlying problems do not worsen over time. Risk factors include preterm birth, being a [[Twin|twin]], certain infections, exposure to methylmercury during pregnancy, a difficult delivery and head trauma in early childhood. Subtypes are spastic, ataxic and dyskinetic. There is no known cure; treatment includes [[Physical therapy|physical therapy]], occupational and speech therapy, and medications such as diazepam, baclofen and [[Botulinum toxin|botulinum toxin]]. It is the most common movement disorder in children, occurring in about 2.1 per 1,000 live births.
