=== KEY: Standard_deviation_9f6063
HEADWORD: Standard deviation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Average | Normal distribution | Square root | Standard error | Variance
ENTRY:
Measure of the variation of a set of values about their arithmetic [[Average|average]], used in statistics. A low standard deviation means the values cluster near the average; a high one means they are spread over a wider range. It is most often represented by the Greek letter σ (sigma) and equals the [[Square root|square root]] of the [[Variance|variance]], so it is expressed in the same unit as the data. For a [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]], about 68.3% of values fall within one standard deviation of the average, 95.4% within two and 99.7% within three. It can also be used to calculate the [[Standard error|standard error]] for a finite sample and to determine statistical significance.

=== KEY: Standard_error_5c8480
HEADWORD: Standard error
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Confidence interval | Regression analysis
ENTRY:
Standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic, equal to the square root of the variance of an estimator. The standard error of the mean equals the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. Because the population standard deviation is seldom known, it is usually estimated by substituting the sample standard deviation. The standard error is often used in calculating [[Confidence interval|confidence intervals]] and also appears in [[Regression analysis|regression analysis]]. Small samples tend to underestimate the population standard deviation: with n = 2 the underestimate is about 25%, and with n = 6 only 5%. Reducing the error by a factor of two requires four times as many observations.

=== KEY: Standardization_91693e
HEADWORD: Standardization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economics | Industrial Revolution | Interchangeable parts | Mohenjo-daro
ENTRY:
Process of developing and implementing technical standards based on consensus among firms, users, interest groups, standards organizations and governments. Standardization can maximize compatibility, interoperability, safety, repeatability, efficiency and quality, and can normalize formerly custom processes. In [[Economics|economics]], it addresses coordination problems in which all parties gain only by making mutually consistent decisions; divergent national standards impose costs on consumers and can act as a non-tariff trade barrier. Standard weights and measures were developed in the Indus Valley, where towns such as [[Mohenjo-daro|Mohenjo-daro]] used uniform units of length. The [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]] made standards highly important for machine tools and [[Interchangeable parts|interchangeable parts]]; in 1800 Henry Maudslay's screw-cutting lathe allowed screw-thread sizes to be standardized for the first time.

=== KEY: Star_26f93e
HEADWORD: Star
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Hipparchus | Milky Way | Neutron star | Nuclear fusion | Sun | Supernova | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Luminous spheroid of plasma held together by its own gravity. The nearest star to Earth is the [[Sun]]. Only about 4,000 stars are visible to the naked eye, all within the [[Milky Way]], and the observable universe is estimated to contain 10²² to 10²⁴ stars. Astronomers group prominent stars into constellations and asterisms and compile catalogs with standardized designations.

A star forms when a gaseous nebula of mostly hydrogen and helium collapses under gravity, and its total mass largely determines its evolution and fate. Protostar cores reach about 15 million kelvin, igniting hydrogen fusion and beginning the main sequence, the longest stage of a star's life. A star shines for most of its active life through [[Nuclear fusion|thermonuclear fusion]] of hydrogen into helium. Low- to intermediate-mass stars (up to about 8 solar masses) expand into red giants and leave a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]]. Massive stars end as [[Supernova|supernovae]], leaving a [[Neutron star|neutron star]] or a [[Black hole|black hole]]. Stellar nucleosynthesis creates almost all naturally occurring elements heavier than lithium, and mass loss and supernovae return them to the interstellar medium to be recycled into new stars. Stars are classified by spectral type (O, B, A, F, G, K, M), from surface temperatures over 30,000 K to about 3,000 K.

Stars have served civilizations for religious practice, divination, navigation, and calendars. The oldest accurately dated star chart is Egyptian, from 1534 BC, and Babylonian astronomers compiled the earliest known catalogs in the late 2nd millennium BC. The catalog of [[Hipparchus]] (2nd century BC) listed 1,020 stars. Chinese astronomers were first to record a supernova, in 185 AD, and SN 1006 was the brightest stellar event in recorded history.

=== KEY: Star_cluster_b5f907
HEADWORD: Star cluster
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hyades (star cluster) | Infrared | Milky Way | Molecular cloud | Pleiades
ENTRY:
Group of stars that share an origin, formed at roughly the same time and held together by self-gravitation. Globular clusters are tight groups of ten thousand to millions of old stars that remain gravitationally bound for billions of years. Open clusters are looser groups, generally containing fewer than a few hundred members, that are young because [[Molecular cloud|giant molecular clouds]] disrupt them over time. Once no longer bound, they become stellar associations, or moving groups. Star clusters visible to the naked eye in the [[Milky Way|Milky Way]] include the [[Pleiades|Pleiades]] and [[Hyades (star cluster)|Hyades]] open clusters and the globular cluster 47 Tucanae. Clusters obscured in visible light but seen in [[Infrared|infrared]] light are classified as infrared clusters.

=== KEY: Star_formation_e3dd1e
HEADWORD: Star formation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binary star | Interstellar medium | Molecular cloud | Solar mass | Star cluster
ENTRY:
Process by which dense regions within [[Molecular cloud|molecular clouds]] in interstellar space collapse to form stars, sometimes called stellar nurseries. As a branch of astronomy, it studies the [[Interstellar medium|interstellar medium]] and giant molecular clouds as precursors, and protostars and young stellar objects as products. Most stars form in groups called [[Star cluster|star clusters]] or stellar associations, and star formation theory must account for [[Binary star|binary stars]] and the initial mass function. Filaments in molecular clouds fragment into gravitationally bound cores, most of which evolve into stars. Giant molecular clouds, typically with densities of 100 particles per cm³, can have diameters of 100 light-years and masses of up to 6 million [[Solar mass|solar masses]]; the coldest clouds tend to form low-mass stars.

Small number of stars that orbit each other, bound by gravitational attraction. A system of two stars is a [[Binary star|binary star]]; systems of three or four are called ternary or quaternary, and systems with four or more components are rare. Star systems are smaller than open [[Star cluster|star clusters]], which typically have 100 to 1,000 stars.

Galaxy undergoing an exceptionally high rate of star formation, compared with its long-term average or with most other galaxies. === KEY: State_of_matter_d6c219
HEADWORD: State of matter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bose–Einstein condensate | Degenerate matter | Ferromagnetism | Gas | Ice | Liquid | Plasma (physics) | Solid
ENTRY:
Distinct form in which matter can exist, differing in how component particles are arranged and behave collectively. Four states are observable in everyday life: [[Solid|solid]], [[Liquid|liquid]], [[Gas|gas]], and [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]]. In a solid, particles are tightly packed and can only vibrate, giving a definite shape and volume. In a liquid, particles stay close but move past one another, so volume is fixed while shape follows the container. In a gas, particles move freely, filling the container. Plasma resembles a gas but contains ions and free electrons that move independently and respond to electric and magnetic fields; a gas becomes plasma through a large voltage difference or very high temperatures.

Changes between states include melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation (solid to gas), and deposition (gas to solid). A liquid's volume is usually greater than that of the corresponding solid, the best-known exception being water, H₂O. A supercritical fluid, above both critical temperature and pressure, has gas-like properties but high density; supercritical carbon dioxide extracts caffeine from coffee.

The term phase is sometimes used as a synonym, but one state can include several phases. Iron has a body-centered cubic structure below 912 °C (1,674 °F) and a face-centered cubic structure between 912 and 1,394 °C (2,541 °F). [[Ice]] has fifteen known solid phases.

Many further states exist. Liquid crystals show properties of both solids and liquids, and magnetic states such as [[Ferromagnetism|ferromagnetism]] depend on the alignment of spins rather than atomic positions. Extreme conditions produce [[Bose–Einstein condensate|Bose–Einstein condensates]] and fermionic condensates (extreme cold), [[Degenerate matter|neutron-degenerate matter]] (extreme density), and quark–gluon plasma (extremely high energy).

=== KEY: State_polity_cc58a5
HEADWORD: State
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Federation | Government | Haudenosaunee | Louis XIV | Max Weber | Nation | Sovereign state | Unitary state
ENTRY:
Political entity that rules and governs a society and population within a definite territory. Governments act as the apparatus or instrument of states, and the term is often used as a metonym for them. A state is widely defined by its legitimate monopoly on the use of force. It may be a [[Unitary state|unitary state]] or a [[Federation|federal]] union; in the latter, the term "state" is sometimes applied to the federated polities, which may hold some attributes of a sovereign state but lack the same capacity to act internationally. A [[Sovereign state|sovereign state]] has sovereignty, though any group's claim to statehood is limited in practice by recognition from other states. Satellite states hold de facto sovereignty but are often indirectly controlled by another state.

For most of prehistory people lived in stateless societies, and the earliest states arose about 5,500 years ago. Societies became more stratified and developed institutions leading to centralized governments, gaining state capacity alongside the growth of cities, often spurred by insecurity and territorial competition. States have rested on varied justifications, such as divine right and the social contract. Today the modern nation state is the predominant form. Charles Tilly saw states in the archaeological record as of 6000 BC, appearing in Europe around 990 and becoming prominent after 1490.

The word derives from Latin *status*, "condition, circumstances", and entered Middle English c. 1200. Machiavelli's early 16th-century works helped popularize its near-modern sense. The expression "L'État, c'est moi", attributed to [[Louis XIV]] but probably apocryphal, is recorded in the late 18th century.

There is no academic consensus on the definition. The most common is that of [[Max Weber]], who described the state as a compulsory political organization with a centralized government that claims a monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a territory (1919). Walter Scheidel identifies common elements in centralized institutions that impose rules by force over a territorially bounded population, a distinction between rulers and ruled, and some autonomy and stability. The 1933 Montevideo Convention lists a permanent population, defined territory, government, and capacity for foreign relations. Contractarian views hold that states form because cooperation benefits everyone; predatory views stress the mismatch between the interests of rulers and people, and Tilly likened states to organized extortion that sells protection from itself.

A state is distinct from a [[Nation|nation]], defined by identity and cultural or historical factors; from a [[Government|government]], which is authorized to act on the state's behalf; and from society. Stateless societies, such as the [[Haudenosaunee]] Confederacy, can exist. The degree of governance determines whether a state is considered failed. Controversies concern the extent of state intervention in individual liberties.

=== KEY: State_religion_1bcbd4

Religion or creed endorsed by a sovereign state. A state with an official religion, also called a confessional state, is not necessarily a [[Theocracy|theocracy]]. Official religions have existed in almost all cultures throughout human history, reaching into the Ancient Near East and prehistory. They generally enjoy more rights and fewer restrictions than other faiths. === KEY: Static_electricity_79ea30
HEADWORD: Static electricity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric current
ENTRY:
Imbalance of electric charges within or on the surface of a material. The charge remains until it moves away as an [[Electric current|electric current]] or by electrical discharge. The word "static" distinguishes it from current electricity, in which charge flows through a conductor. Electrons may move between two materials on contact or sliding, leaving one positively charged and the other negatively charged; this triboelectric effect is the main cause of everyday static. A static shock is an electrostatic discharge, the neutralization of a charge when it reaches a conductor or an oppositely charged region.

=== KEY: Statics_4a8111
HEADWORD: Statics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Classical mechanics | Force | Torque
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] concerned with [[Force|forces]] and [[Torque|torques]] acting on systems in equilibrium. Statics analyzes systems that do not accelerate: they are either at rest or their center of mass moves at constant velocity. Zero acceleration means the total force is zero, and the summation of moments, which equals the moment of inertia times angular acceleration, is also zero. Together, these two "conditions for equilibrium" can be used to solve for unknown quantities acting on a system. A force is either a push or a pull, with magnitude, direction and point of application. The moment of a force, also called torque, equals the force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the axis to its line of action. [[Archimedes]] did pioneering work in statics.

=== KEY: Statistical_hypothesis_test_d3b746
HEADWORD: Statistical hypothesis test
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: P-value
ENTRY:
Method of statistical inference for deciding whether data provide sufficient evidence to reject a particular hypothesis. The test typically computes a test statistic, then decides by comparing it with a critical value or by evaluating the [[P-value|p-value]] computed from it. The null hypothesis (H₀), usually a statement of "no effect" or "no difference," is tested against the alternative hypothesis (H₁). If the p-value is less than or equal to the significance level (α), the null hypothesis is rejected, but the test does not conclude that it is false. Conclusions are never certain. A type I error rejects a true null hypothesis, with probability α, while a type II error accepts it when the alternative is true, with probability β. The power of a test, 1−β, measures its ability to detect a true alternative. Roughly 100 specialized statistical tests are in use.

=== KEY: Statistical_mechanics_e68823
HEADWORD: Statistical mechanics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: James Clerk Maxwell | Josiah Willard Gibbs | Ludwig Boltzmann | Physics | Thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Physics|physics]] that applies statistical methods and probability theory to large assemblies of microscopic entities. Also called statistical physics or statistical thermodynamics, it aims to clarify the properties of matter in aggregate, in terms of physical laws governing atomic motion. It arose from classical [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]], explaining properties such as temperature, pressure and heat capacity in terms of microscopic parameters described by probability distributions. Its applications extend to chemical reactions, neuroscience, computer science, information theory and sociology. Non-equilibrium statistical mechanics models the speed of irreversible processes driven by imbalances, such as flows of particles and heat. The field is generally credited to [[Ludwig Boltzmann]], [[James Clerk Maxwell]] and [[Josiah Willard Gibbs]], and Gibbs coined the name in 1884.

=== KEY: Statistical_population_1bbc88
HEADWORD: Statistical population
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Expected value | Law of large numbers | Milky Way | Probability distribution | Sampling (statistics)
ENTRY:
Set of similar items of interest for some question or experiment. A statistical population can be a group of existing objects, such as all stars within the [[Milky Way]], or a hypothetical, potentially infinite group, such as all possible hands in a game of poker. In statistical inference, the population is modeled by a [[Probability distribution|probability distribution]] with unknown parameters. By analyzing a [[Sampling (statistics)|sample]], these parameters can be estimated using appropriate sample statistics. The population mean is the arithmetic mean of a numerical property across the entire population, or the [[Expected value|expected value]] when the property is a random variable. Not every distribution has a well-defined mean; the Cauchy distribution is an example. The [[Law of large numbers|law of large numbers]] states that the larger the sample, the more likely its mean is to be close to the population mean.

=== KEY: Statistics_c33e40
HEADWORD: Statistics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Census | Karl Pearson | Observational study | Probability theory | Ronald Fisher | Sampling (statistics) | Standard deviation | Statistical population
ENTRY:
Discipline concerned with the collection, organization, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data. A study usually begins with a [[Statistical population|statistical population]] or a statistical model, such as all people living in a country or every atom in a crystal. Statistics covers every aspect of data, including the design of surveys and experiments, and is closely related to mathematics, physics, chemistry, and geography.

When [[Census|census]] data covering every member of a population cannot be collected, statisticians use experiment designs and survey samples. Random [[Sampling (statistics)|sampling]] allows conclusions to be extended from the sample to the whole population. An experimental study measures a system, manipulates it, and measures again, whereas an [[Observational study|observational study]] involves no manipulation.

Two main methods are used. Descriptive statistics summarize a sample with indexes such as the mean or [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]], describing central tendency and dispersion. Inferential statistics draw conclusions from data subject to random variation, using the framework of [[Probability theory|probability theory]]. Probability deduces results for samples from a given theory, while statistics induces statements about a population from a data set. A standard procedure proposes a hypothesis against a null hypothesis of no relationship and applies tests to the data. Two kinds of error are recognized: Type I (rejecting a true null hypothesis, a false positive) and Type II (failing to reject a false one, a false negative). Measurement is also prone to random error (noise), systematic error (bias), and blunders, and missing data or censoring can bias estimates. In the Mathematics Subject Classification, statistics is class 62-XX.

The English word comes from German *Statistik*, originally "description of a state, a country", ultimately from Latin *status*. Gottfried Achenwall coined the German term; it entered English in 1770 for the study of political arrangements and gained its modern meaning in the 1790s in the works of John Sinclair. Statistics emerged in the 17th century as "political arithmetic", with John Graunt analyzing demographic data in London. [[Karl Pearson]] advanced the study of correlation and [[Ronald Fisher]] experimental design and significance testing in the 20th century. Applications now span public health, business, government policy, and the analysis of large datasets, where concerns include bias in algorithms.

=== KEY: Statute_00df5e

=== KEY: Statutory_interpretation_d22534

=== KEY: Steam_engine_5e1f28
HEADWORD: Steam engine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric motor | Hero of Alexandria | Industrial Revolution | Internal combustion engine | James Watt | Steam locomotive | Steam turbine
ENTRY:
Heat engine that performs mechanical work using steam as its working fluid. Steam pressure pushes a piston back and forth inside a cylinder, and a connecting rod and crank convert this force into rotation. They are external combustion engines. The term normally means reciprocating engines, though some authorities also apply it to the [[Steam turbine|steam turbine]] and to devices such as the aeolipile of [[Hero of Alexandria]], known in the first century AD. It can also mean a whole plant, such as a [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]].

In 1606 Jerónimo de Ayanz y Beaumont patented the first steam-powered water pump for draining mines. Thomas Savery built the first commercially used steam device, a pump, in 1698. The first commercially successful engine able to transmit continuous power to a machine was Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine, developed around 1712. In 1764 [[James Watt]] greatly improved fuel efficiency by removing spent steam to a separate vessel for condensation.

By the 19th century stationary steam engines powered the factories of the [[Industrial Revolution]], paddle steamers replaced sailing ships, and steam locomotives ran on the railways. Reciprocating piston engines were the dominant source of power until the early 20th century. A Rankine cycle efficiency of 91% and a combined thermal efficiency of 31% were demonstrated and published in 1921 and 1928.

Advances in the [[Electric motor|electric motor]] and the [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engine]] gradually replaced steam engines in commercial use, and steam turbines supplanted them in power generation. Large reciprocating piston steam engines are still manufactured in Germany.

=== KEY: Steel_466811
HEADWORD: Steel
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Alloy of iron and carbon with improved mechanical properties compared with pure iron. Its high strength and low raw material cost make it one of the most commonly manufactured materials. It is used in construction, bridges, tools, ships, trains, cars, machines, appliances, and weapons.

Steel is an [[Alloy|alloy]] of [[Iron|iron]] and carbon, and often other elements, with a carbon content up to 2.14% by weight. Plain iron–carbon alloys above about 2.1% carbon are [[Cast iron|cast iron]], which is not malleable even when hot but casts well. [[Wrought iron]], now largely obsolete, contains little carbon but large amounts of slag. Alloy steels contain deliberately added elements such as manganese, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium, and tungsten. American production centered on Pittsburgh, Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, and Cleveland, Ohio until the late 20th century.

More than 1.6 billion tons are produced annually, and [[China]] produced 54% of the world's steel in 2023. The industry contributes 8% of global emissions, but steel is one of the most recycled materials, with a global recycling rate of over 60%.

=== KEY: Stellar_classification_d64fee

Scheme for grouping stars by the features of their spectra. Light from a star is split by a [[Prism (optics)|prism]] or [[Diffraction grating|diffraction grating]] into a spectrum whose lines show which chemical elements or [[Molecule|molecules]] are present; their strength mainly reflects the temperature of the photosphere. Most stars are classified by the Morgan–Keenan (MK) system, which uses the letters O, B, A, F, G, K and M from hottest to coolest, each subdivided by a digit from 0 (hottest) to 9 (coolest). === KEY: Stellar_evolution_f58241

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black hole | Main sequence | Neutron star | Red giant | Sun | Supernova | White dwarf
ENTRY:
Process by which a star changes over the course of time. Depending on mass, a star's lifetime ranges from a few million years for the most massive to trillions of years for the least massive. Stars form from collapsing clouds of gas and dust, settle into a [[Main sequence|main-sequence]] state, and fuse hydrogen into helium in their cores. Stars like the [[Sun]] later swell into [[Red giant|red giants]], and when their fuel is exhausted their cores become [[White dwarf|white dwarfs]] while the outer layers are expelled as a planetary nebula. === KEY: Stellar_magnetic_field_26abe1

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Plasma (physics) | Sun
ENTRY:
Magnetic field generated by the motion of conductive [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]] inside a star. Convection moves this plasma, and a localized field rises until it reaches the photosphere, creating starspots on the surface and related coronal loops. According to solar dynamo theory, the convective zone acts as a dynamo: circulation destroys the primordial field and generates a dipolar field, which differential rotation winds into toroidal flux ropes that emerge at the surface. === KEY: Stellar_structure_0a6e07
HEADWORD: Stellar structure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Convection | Hydrostatic equilibrium | Sun
ENTRY:
Model describing the internal structure of a star and predicting its luminosity, color and future evolution. Different classes and ages of stars have different internal structures, reflecting their composition and energy transport mechanisms. Heat moves outward mainly by [[Convection|convection]] and radiative transfer. Stars of 0.3–1.5 solar masses, including the [[Sun]], fuse hydrogen mainly through proton–proton chains, so their cores are radiative and their outer envelopes convective. In stars above about 1.5 solar masses the CNO cycle dominates, which makes the core convective and the envelope radiative. The lowest-mass main-sequence stars convect throughout. The simplest commonly used model assumes a steady, spherically symmetric star described by four first-order differential equations, including the condition of [[Hydrostatic equilibrium|hydrostatic equilibrium]].

=== KEY: Stem_cell_46076e
HEADWORD: Stem cell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cell (biology) | Embryo | Mammal
ENTRY:
Unspecialized [[Cell (biology)|cell]] that can change into various specialized cell types and proliferate. Stem cells can produce more of the same stem cell indefinitely, distinguishing them from progenitor cells, which cannot divide indefinitely. === KEY: Stereotype_8e15ee
HEADWORD: Stereotype
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Prejudice | Racism | Social psychology
ENTRY:
Generalized belief about a category of people, studied in [[Social psychology|social psychology]]. Stereotypes concern a group's personality, preferences, appearance or abilities, and they simplify information processing by letting people rely on stored knowledge rather than new input. They are often faulty and resistant to new information. Research on their accuracy has yielded mixed results: studies of national-origin and astrological-sign stereotypes found them inaccurate, while studies of gender stereotypes found them more likely to reflect reality. Explicit stereotypes are conscious beliefs that people knowingly use to judge others, whereas implicit stereotypes are automatic, involuntary associations between a social group and a domain or attribute. Stereotypes are regarded as the most cognitive component of intergroup attitudes, often occurring without conscious awareness; [[Prejudice|prejudice]] is the affective component, and discrimination the behavioral one. Stereotypes, prejudice, [[Racism|racism]], and discrimination are related but distinct concepts.

=== KEY: Steroid_6e1e1d

ENTRY:
[[Organic compound|Organic compound]] with four fused rings, designated A, B, C and D. Examples include the [[Lipid|lipid]] [[Cholesterol|cholesterol]], the sex hormones estradiol and [[Testosterone|testosterone]], anabolic steroids, and the anti-inflammatory corticosteroid dexamethasone.
