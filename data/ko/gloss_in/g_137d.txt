HEADWORD: Titans
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlas (mythology) | Gaia | Greek mythology | Hesiod | Homer | Prometheus | Zeus
ENTRY:
Pre-Olympian deities of [[Greek mythology]]. In the *Theogony* of [[Hesiod]], the Titans were the twelve children of the primordial deities Uranus (Sky) and [[Gaia]] (Earth): six male and six female. Cronus had seized power from Uranus and ruled with his fellow Titans until his defeat. [[Zeus]] and the other Olympians, children of Cronus and Rhea, overthrew them in a ten-year war, the Titanomachy. The defeated Titans were banished and imprisoned in Tartarus, although some, such as Oceanus and Helios, remained free. Other children of the Titans, including [[Prometheus]], [[Atlas (mythology)|Atlas]] and Leto, are sometimes also called Titans. [[Homer]]'s *Iliad* hints at a tradition in which Oceanus and Tethys were the parents of the Titans.

=== KEY: Titian_3af3fc
HEADWORD: Titian
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Renaissance | Venice
ENTRY:
Italian [[Renaissance|Renaissance]] painter. Born Tiziano Vecellio in Pieve di Cadore near Belluno, he was among the most versatile Italian painters, equally adept with portraits, landscape backgrounds, and mythological and religious subjects. Along with Giorgione, he is considered a founder of the Venetian school of painting. He worked for patrons in [[Venice]], then for the princes of northern Italy, the Habsburgs and the papacy. His use of color influenced painters of the late Renaissance and later Western artists. His late works, with loose brushwork and subtle tone, are considered remarkable and original, and his unfinished Pietà, intended for his tomb, was among his last works.

=== KEY: Tiwanaku_b104c7
HEADWORD: Tiwanaku
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bolivia | Ceramic | La Paz | Lake Titicaca | Wari culture
ENTRY:
Pre-Columbian archaeological site in western [[Bolivia]]. Located near [[Lake Titicaca]], about 70 kilometers from [[La Paz]], it is one of the largest sites in South America. Its surface remains, covering about 4 sq km, include decorated [[Ceramic|ceramics]], monumental structures and megalithic blocks. It was conservatively estimated to have held 10,000 to 20,000 people in AD 800. Spanish conquistador Pedro Cieza de León first recorded it in 1549. Radiocarbon dating places its founding around AD 110, and the city grew significantly from about AD 375 to 700 on a complex agropastoral economy supported by trade. Its Southern Andean iconographic style is shared with the [[Wari culture]]. The society appears to have collapsed around AD 1000, and drought likely played a role. Claims of an age of 11,000 to 17,000 years, made by Arthur Posnansky from 1910 to 1945, are now rejected.

=== KEY: To_Kill_a_Mockingbird_688adc
HEADWORD: To Kill a Mockingbird
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Academy Awards | Alabama | Gregory Peck | Pulitzer Prize | United States
ENTRY:
1960 Southern Gothic novel by Harper Lee. Loosely based on events near Lee's hometown of Monroeville, [[Alabama]], in 1936, it is narrated by Scout Finch as her lawyer father, Atticus Finch, defends a Black man falsely accused of raping a white woman. Its themes include racial injustice and the loss of innocence, and it won the [[Pulitzer Prize]] a year after publication. Atticus Finch has served as a moral hero for many readers and a model of integrity for lawyers. The 1962 film adaptation, starring [[Gregory Peck]], won three [[Academy Awards]]. Widely read in [[United States]] high schools and middle schools, the novel has nonetheless faced campaigns for removal from classrooms over its racial epithets. It remained Lee's only published book until *Go Set a Watchman*, an earlier draft, appeared in 2015.

=== KEY: To_the_Lighthouse_90a014
HEADWORD: To the Lighthouse
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: James Joyce | Marcel Proust | Time (magazine) | Virginia Woolf
ENTRY:
1927 novel by [[Virginia Woolf]]. Set on the Isle of Skye in Scotland, it follows the Ramsay family's visits between 1910 and 1920 to their summer house. Extending the modernism of [[Marcel Proust]] and [[James Joyce]], it has little dialogue and almost no direct action, with the plot secondary to philosophical introspection. It is cited as a key example of multiple focalization and is told in three parts: The Window, Time Passes and The Lighthouse. Its themes include loss, subjectivity, the nature of art and the problem of perception. The Modern Library ranked it No. 15 among the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century in 1998, and *[[Time (magazine)|Time]]* included it in its 2005 list of the 100 best English-language novels since 1923.

=== KEY: Tobacco_cfb993
HEADWORD: Tobacco
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cigarette | Heart | Jamestown | Lung | Nicotine | Plant | Slavery | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Product prepared from the cured leaves of [[Plant|plants]] of the genus *Nicotiana*. The chief commercial crop is *Nicotiana tabacum*. Dried leaves are used mainly for smoking in [[Cigarette|cigarettes]], cigars, pipes and hookahs, and also as snuff and chewing tobacco. Tobacco contains the addictive stimulant alkaloid [[Nicotine|nicotine]], making it among the most widely used recreational drugs. Its use causes or raises the risk of many deadly diseases, especially those of the [[Heart|heart]], liver and [[Lung|lungs]], and of many cancers. In 2008 the [[World Health Organization]] named tobacco use the world's single greatest preventable cause of death. The word derives from Spanish *tabaco*, probably from the Taíno language of the Caribbean. Tobacco was used ceremonially in the Americas for thousands of years, with some cultivation in Mexico dating to 1400–1000 BCE. After European contact it spread worldwide, and the English colony of [[Jamestown]] used it as currency and an export crop. Its demand was closely tied to [[Slavery]] in the Caribbean.

=== KEY: Tobacco_mosaic_virus_ceee2e
HEADWORD: Tobacco mosaic virus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bacteria | Brussels | Rosalind Franklin | The New York Times | Tobacco | Virus
ENTRY:
RNA [[Virus|virus]] that infects many plants, especially [[Tobacco|tobacco]]. It causes mottled, mosaic-like discoloration of the leaves and was the first virus discovered. Adolf Mayer described the disease in 1886. In 1892 Dmitri Ivanovsky showed that its infectious agent passed through filters fine enough to trap [[Bacteria|bacteria]]. In 1898 Martinus Beijerinck showed that it reproduces in host cells and adopted the term virus. Wendell Meredith Stanley crystallized it in 1935, news that appeared on the front page of *[[The New York Times]]*. Stanley received one-fourth of the 1946 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, although some of his conclusions were later shown to be incorrect. [[Rosalind Franklin]] built a model of the virus for the 1958 World's Fair in [[Brussels]]. Its rod-shaped capsid is made of 2,130 coat-protein molecules around a single 6,400-base RNA strand.

=== KEY: Tobacco_smoking_4fd9ae
HEADWORD: Tobacco smoking
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Addiction | Adolescence | Dopamine | Eurasia | Mesoamerica | South America | Tobacco | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Practice of burning [[Tobacco|tobacco]] and absorbing the smoke into the body. Smoking is the most common way to consume tobacco. The practice is believed to have begun as early as 5000–3000 BC in [[Mesoamerica]] and [[South America]], and it reached [[Eurasia]] in the 17th century through European colonists. Nicotine, the addictive psychostimulant in smoke, is absorbed through the lungs or the oral mucosa, and [[Dopamine|dopamine]] is released, leading to [[Addiction|addiction]]. German scientists linked smoking to lung cancer in the late 1920s, and British researchers demonstrated a clear relationship with cancer in 1950. Consumption in the developed world has peaked or declined since 1965, but it continues to climb in the developing world. The [[World Health Organization]] attributes 8 million deaths a year to tobacco smoking. Many smokers begin in [[Adolescence|adolescence]], often influenced by cigarette advertising, parents, siblings and friends.

=== KEY: Tocharians_3744a7
HEADWORD: Tocharians
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bactria | Han dynasty | Indo-European languages | Silk Road | Tang dynasty | Uyghurs | Xinjiang | Xiongnu
ENTRY:
Historical [[Indo-European languages|Indo-European]]-speaking people of the Tarim Basin, in present-day [[Xinjiang]], China. They spoke the Tocharian languages, known from about 7,600 documents from the 6th and 7th centuries. Early 20th-century scholars linked their speakers to the Tókharoi of [[Bactria]], an identification now considered erroneous. Agricultural communities appeared in the northern Tarim oases around 2000 BC and developed into city-states by the 2nd century BC, the largest being Kucha. These served as way stations on the [[Silk Road]] along the northern edge of the Taklamakan Desert. The basin was then ruled by the [[Xiongnu]], the [[Han dynasty]], the Tibetan Empire and the [[Tang dynasty]]. From the 8th century AD the Turkic-speaking [[Uyghurs]] settled in the region. The Tocharian languages are believed to have become extinct in the 9th century. Their origins are uncertain, with proposed links to the Andronovo and Afanasievo cultures.

=== KEY: Tofu_0f691b
HEADWORD: Tofu
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | China | Han dynasty | Japan | Song dynasty | Soy milk | Soybean
ENTRY:
Soy-based food made by pressing the curds of coagulated [[Soy milk|soy milk]] into solid white blocks. Tofu originated in [[China]] and has been eaten for over 2,000 years. It is a traditional component of East and Southeast Asian cuisines, and Western cooking commonly uses it as a meat substitute. Blocks range from silken and soft to firm and extra firm. Tofu making was first recorded in the [[Han dynasty]], and legend credits Prince Liu An (179–122 BC) with its invention. It did not become popular in China until the [[Song dynasty]] (960–1279). It spread with [[Buddhism|Buddhism]], which made it an important protein source in East Asian vegetarian diets, and tofu first appears in [[Japan|Japanese]] records in AD 965. Tofu is low in calories but rich in protein. [[Soybean|Soybean]] cultivation needs little land (1.3 m² per 1,000 kcal) and emits little greenhouse gas per gram of protein (1.6 kg CO₂ per 100 g of protein).
