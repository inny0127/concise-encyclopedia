=== KEY: Special_effect_39dd24
HEADWORD: Special effect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amusement park | Film | Georges Méliès | Mary, Queen of Scots | Scale model | Television | Theatre | Video game
ENTRY:
Illusions or visual tricks used in the [[Theatre|theater]], [[Film|film]], [[Television]], [[Video game|video game]], [[Amusement park|amusement park]] and simulator industries to simulate fictional events. Special effects are traditionally divided into mechanical effects, also called practical effects, and optical effects, also called photographic effects. Mechanical effects, such as animatronics, pyrotechnics and [[Scale model|scale models]], are usually accomplished during live-action shooting. Optical effects use multiple exposure, mattes or an optical printer. In 1895 Alfred Clark created what is commonly accepted as the first motion picture special effect, replacing an actor with a dummy during a re-enactment of the beheading of [[Mary, Queen of Scots]]. [[Georges Méliès]] later made more than 500 short films between 1896 and 1914, developing techniques such as multiple exposures, time-lapse photography and dissolves. Since the 1990s, computer-generated imagery (CGI) has come to the forefront, and many optical and mechanical techniques have been superseded by CGI.

=== KEY: Special_relativity_408a01
HEADWORD: Special relativity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Galileo Galilei | General relativity | Mass–energy equivalence | Speed of light | Time dilation
ENTRY:
Scientific theory of the relationship between space and time, presented by [[Albert Einstein]] in his 1905 paper "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies." It rests on two postulates: that the laws of physics are identical in all inertial frames of reference, a principle first formulated by [[Galileo Galilei]], and that the [[Speed of light|speed of light]] in vacuum is the same for all observers, regardless of the motion of the light source or observer. It replaces the idea of a single time flowing equally everywhere with the concept that time flows differently for each independent object. Consequences include [[Time dilation|time dilation]], length contraction and the relativity of simultaneity. Combined with other laws of physics, it predicts the [[Mass–energy equivalence|equivalence of mass and energy]], expressed as E = mc², where c is the speed of light in vacuum. The theory was extended to include gravity in 1916, in [[General relativity|general relativity]].

=== KEY: Species_e1520b
HEADWORD: Species
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Biodiversity | Charles Darwin | Genus | Hybrid (biology) | Natural selection | Taxonomic rank
ENTRY:
Basic unit of biological classification, a [[Taxonomic rank|taxonomic rank]], and a unit of [[Biodiversity|biodiversity]]. The best-known definition, the biological species concept, describes a species as the largest group of organisms whose appropriate sexes or mating types can produce fertile offspring, usually by sexual reproduction. Ernst Mayr formalized it in 1942, emphasizing reproductive isolation. Every species receives a two-part binomial name: the [[Genus|genus]] followed by the specific epithet, as in *Boa constrictor*. The most recent rigorous estimate of the number of eukaryote species is 8 to 8.7 million, of which about 14% had been described by 2011.

No definition is fully satisfactory. Species concepts also rest on karyotype, DNA sequence, morphology, behavior, or ecological niche; scholars have counted between about 24 and 26 distinct concepts. The morphological concept is useful for fossils but can mislead through convergent evolution. The phylogenetic concept defines species as the smallest monophyletic groups, and the ecological concept, proposed by Leigh Van Valen in 1976, views a species as a lineage adapted to a particular niche. Boundaries blur through [[Hybrid (biology)|hybridization]] and ring species, and among asexual organisms each clonal lineage is potentially a microspecies. Viruses are treated as quasispecies. Despite this "species problem," the concept remains a useful tool for scientists and conservationists, and legal protections such as the Endangered Species Act depend on it.

From [[Aristotle]] until the 18th century, species and higher taxa were seen as fixed categories in a hierarchy, the great chain of being. In the 19th century biologists recognized that species evolve. [[Charles Darwin]]'s 1859 book *On the Origin of Species* explained how they arise by [[Natural selection|natural selection]], and 20th-century genetics and population ecology extended that understanding.

Heat that must be added to one unit of mass of a substance to raise its temperature by one unit. Its SI unit is joule per kelvin per kilogram (J⋅kg⁻¹⋅K⁻¹). It varies with [[Temperature|temperature]], and it is technically undefined during a [[Phase transition|phase transition]] such as melting or boiling.

=== KEY: Spectrometer_8a587a
HEADWORD: Spectrometer
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Chemistry | Diffraction grating | Ion | Metal | Particle physics | Prism (optics)
ENTRY:
Scientific instrument used to separate and measure the spectral components of a physical phenomenon. In visible light, a spectrometer separates white light into narrow bands of color, called a spectrum, using a [[Prism (optics)|prism]] or a [[Diffraction grating|diffraction grating]]. A mass spectrometer measures the spectrum of the masses of atoms or molecules in a gas, identifying the amount and type of chemicals in a sample by their mass-to-charge ratio and the abundance of gas-phase [[Ion|ions]]. Spectrometers are used in [[Astronomy|astronomy]] to analyze the chemical composition of stars and planets, and in [[Chemistry|chemistry]] and [[Particle physics|particle physics]] to separate particles, atoms and molecules by their mass, momentum or energy. Optical emission spectrometers evaluate [[Metal|metals]] to determine their chemical composition with very high accuracy.

=== KEY: Spectroscopy_27d6bf
HEADWORD: Spectroscopy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Chemistry | Isaac Newton | James Clerk Maxwell | Materials science | Physics | Prism (optics) | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Field of study that measures and interprets electromagnetic spectra as they interact with matter. It is a fundamental exploratory tool in [[Astronomy|astronomy]], [[Chemistry|chemistry]], [[Materials science|materials science]] and [[Physics|physics]], allowing the composition and structure of matter to be investigated from the atomic scale to astronomical distances. Spectroscopy began when [[Isaac Newton]] split light with a [[Prism (optics)|prism]], and [[James Clerk Maxwell]] later extended the field to the entire electromagnetic spectrum. Spectroscopic studies were central to the development of [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], as early atomic models, including the Bohr model and the Schrödinger equation, reproduced the spectral lines of hydrogen. Current applications include biomedical spectroscopy in tissue analysis and medical imaging.

=== KEY: Speech_04a839
HEADWORD: Speech
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aphasia | Consonant | Diglossia | Language | Lev Vygotsky | Phonetics | Speech act | Vowel
ENTRY:
Use of the human voice as a medium for [[Language|language]]. Spoken language combines [[Vowel|vowel]] and [[Consonant|consonant]] sounds into units of meaning such as words. Speakers perform many intentional [[Speech act|speech acts]], including informing, declaring, asking, persuading, and directing, varying enunciation, intonation, loudness, and tempo to convey meaning. Speech also unintentionally reveals traits such as age, origin, and education. Some psychologists, such as [[Lev Vygotsky]], hold that silent inner speech helps organize cognition.

Normal speech is pulmonic: pressure from the lungs sets the glottis in the larynx vibrating, and the vocal tract and mouth shape the sound into vowels and consonants. Production is an unconscious, multistep process in which the speaker selects words from the lexicon, organizes them through syntax, retrieves their phonetic properties, and articulates them. Articulatory [[Phonetics|phonetics]] classifies sounds by place and manner of articulation. Speech errors are common, especially in children, and are used to build models of language production and acquisition. Children's over-regularizing of the English past tense ("singed" for "sang") suggests that regular forms are acquired first. Errors in [[Aphasia|aphasia]] have helped map components of speech onto the brain, including Broca's area and Wernicke's area.

Speech is studied by linguistics, cognitive science, communication studies, psychology, computer science, speech pathology, otolaryngology, and acoustics. It differs from written language in vocabulary, syntax, and phonetics, a situation called [[Diglossia|diglossia]].

The evolutionary origin of speech is debated. Although animals communicate by vocalization, and trained apes such as Washoe and Kanzi can use simple sign language, no animal's vocalizations are articulated phonemically and syntactically. No monkey or ape uses its tongue for sound production. The vocal tract does not fossilize, and indirect fossil evidence of changes to it has proved inconclusive.

=== KEY: Speech_act_4e7ff4
HEADWORD: Speech act
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Linguistics | Philosophy of language
ENTRY:
Utterance that not only presents information but also performs an action, as in the phrase "I resign," where saying the words enacts the resignation. The contemporary term goes back to J. L. Austin's work on performative utterances and his distinction among locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary acts. Acts commonly classed as speech acts include apologizing, promising, ordering, requesting, complaining, warning, inviting, refusing and congratulating. Kent Bach argued that almost any speech act is the performance of several acts at once, distinguished by the speaker's intention. The theory draws on Wittgenstein's view that meaning lies in use, and it is studied in [[Philosophy of language|philosophy of language]] and [[Linguistics|linguistics]].

=== KEY: Speech_synthesis_6eea02
HEADWORD: Speech synthesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bell Labs | Database | Operating system | Phoneme | Unix | Visual impairment
ENTRY:
Artificial production of human speech, carried out by a speech synthesizer in software or hardware. A text-to-speech (TTS) system converts ordinary text into speech in two stages: a front-end that turns symbols such as numbers and abbreviations into written-out words and assigns [[Phoneme|phonetic]] transcriptions, and a back-end that converts the result into sound. Synthesized speech may be made by concatenating recorded speech units stored in a [[Database|database]], or by modeling the vocal tract. Intelligible text-to-speech lets people with [[Visual impairment|visual impairments]] or reading disabilities hear written words. === KEY: Speed_44877c
HEADWORD: Speed
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Galileo Galilei | Knot (unit) | Metre | Special relativity | Speed of light | Velocity
ENTRY:
Magnitude of [[Velocity|velocity]], the rate at which the distance traveled by an object changes with time. Average speed is the total distance divided by the time interval, while instantaneous speed is the limit of average speed as the interval approaches zero. Speed has the dimensions of distance divided by time. Its SI unit is the [[Metre|meter]] per second (m/s), and the kilometer per hour, mile per hour and [[Knot (unit)|knot]] are also used. [[Galileo Galilei]] is usually credited with first measuring speed as the distance covered per unit of time. According to [[Special relativity|special relativity]], the fastest possible speed at which energy or information can travel is the [[Speed of light|speed of light]] in vacuum, c = 299,792,458 meters per second, and matter cannot reach it.

=== KEY: Speed_of_light_0f6c66
HEADWORD: Speed of light
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Electromagnetic radiation | Gravitational wave | James Clerk Maxwell | Mass–energy equivalence | Metre | Refractive index | Special relativity
ENTRY:
Speed at which electromagnetic waves travel; in a vacuum, a universal physical constant denoted *c*, exactly 299,792,458 m/s. The value is exact because, by international agreement, the [[Metre|meter]] is defined as the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458 of a second. Before 1983 the meter was defined separately and the speed of light was measured experimentally.

All [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]], massless particles, and field perturbations such as [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]] travel at *c* in a vacuum, and the speed is the same for all observers regardless of the motion of the source. It is the upper limit for the speed of information, matter, or energy. Particles with mass can approach *c* but never reach it. Light travels more slowly in materials. The ratio of *c* to its speed in a medium is the [[Refractive index|refractive index]], about 1.5 for glass, so light in glass travels at roughly 200,000 km/s. Apparent faster-than-light recession of distant galaxies arises from the expansion of the universe, since only local velocity is uniquely defined.

Ole Rømer first showed that light is not instantaneous, by studying delays in the eclipses of Jupiter's moon Io. In an 1865 paper [[James Clerk Maxwell]] proposed that light is an electromagnetic wave traveling at *c*. [[Albert Einstein]] postulated that *c* is constant in every inertial frame and independent of the source's motion; from this he derived [[Special relativity|special relativity]], in which *c* links space and time and appears in the [[Mass–energy equivalence|mass–energy equivalence]], E = mc². The origin of the symbol *c* is unclear; it may stand for "constant" or for the Latin *celeritas* ("swiftness").

=== KEY: Speed_of_sound_e2e4d9
HEADWORD: Speed of sound
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diamond | Iron | Isaac Newton | Mach number | Pierre-Simon Laplace | Water
ENTRY:
Distance a sound wave travels per unit of time through an elastic medium. At 20 °C (68 °F) it is about 343 m/s in air, and it depends strongly on temperature and on the medium. Sound travels most slowly in gases, faster in liquids and fastest in solids: about 1481 m/s in fresh [[Water|water]] at 20 °C, 5120 m/s in [[Iron|iron]] and 12,000 m/s in [[Diamond|diamond]]. The ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound in the same medium is its [[Mach number]], and objects faster than the speed of sound travel at supersonic speeds. [[Isaac Newton]]'s 1687 calculation of the speed of sound in air was too low by about 15%, a discrepancy that [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]] later explained by treating sound waves as adiabatic.

=== KEY: Sperm_87f982

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Male reproductive cell, or gamete, in the anisogamous forms of sexual reproduction, in which the male cell is smaller than the female. Sperm contribute approximately half of the nuclear genetic information to the diploid offspring, excluding in most cases mitochondrial DNA. === KEY: Sphagnum_39ff75
HEADWORD: Sphagnum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bog | Calcium | Gametophyte | Hydrogen | Magnesium | Peat
ENTRY:
Genus of approximately 380 species of mosses, also known as bog moss or sphagnum moss. Its plants can hold 16 to 26 times their dry weight in water. As it spreads into drier conditions it forms larger [[Bog|bogs]], both raised and blanket, and the peat accumulations provide habitat for sedges, orchids and carnivorous plants. === KEY: Sphere_b7095f
HEADWORD: Sphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomy | Circle | Geography | Mathematics | Three-dimensional space
ENTRY:
Surface of points all lying at the same distance from a fixed center point. In solid geometry, a sphere is the set of points in [[Three-dimensional space|three-dimensional space]] that are all the same distance, the radius, from a given point. It is the three-dimensional analogue of a [[Circle]]. Spheres are fundamental in [[Mathematics]], and they occur in nature and industry: soap bubbles take a spherical shape in equilibrium, and pressure vessels and most curved mirrors and lenses are based on spheres. The Earth is often approximated as a sphere in [[Geography]], and the celestial sphere is central to [[Astronomy]]. Spheres roll smoothly in any direction, so most sports balls, toys and ball bearings are spherical. Mathematicians distinguish the sphere, a two-dimensional closed surface, from the solid ball it encloses.

=== KEY: Spherical_coordinate_system_222f15
HEADWORD: Spherical coordinate system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Radian | Sphere
ENTRY:
System that locates a point in three-dimensional space by a distance and two angles. The coordinates are the radial distance r from a fixed origin, the polar angle θ between the radial line and a given polar axis, and the azimuthal angle φ, the rotation of the radial line around that axis. Once the radius is fixed, the three coordinates provide a coordinate system on a [[Sphere|sphere]]. Naming order and symbols differ among disciplines, and mathematics texts often swap the two angles. Mathematical systems normally measure angles in [[Radian|radians]] rather than degrees.

=== KEY: Spherical_harmonics_be0844
HEADWORD: Spherical harmonics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cosmic microwave background | Electron configuration | Fourier series | Laplace's equation | Partial differential equation | Pierre-Simon Laplace | Sphere
ENTRY:
Special functions defined on the surface of a [[Sphere|sphere]]. They are often used in solving [[Partial differential equation|partial differential equations]] in many scientific fields. Because they form a complete orthogonal set, a function on a sphere can be written as a sum of them, much as a periodic function can be expanded in [[Fourier series]]. === KEY: Spice_69ad80
HEADWORD: Spice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black pepper | Cinnamon | Clove | Herb | Humorism | Maluku Islands | Monsoon | Vasco da Gama
ENTRY:
Seed, fruit, root, bark, or other plant substance used primarily for flavoring or coloring food. Spices are distinguished from [[Herb|herbs]], the leaves, flowers, or stems of plants used for flavoring or garnish; both are seasonings. Spices are sometimes used in medicine, religious rituals, cosmetics, and perfume, and plant sweeteners such as sugar are not counted among them. A whole dried spice keeps longest, while a fresh spice such as ginger is usually more flavorful but costlier and shorter-lived. A 2019 review found that most claimed health benefits were not supported by clinical evidence, though it noted that polyphenols in spices may be beneficial. India contributes 75% of global spice production.

Defining spice is difficult. The *Oxford English Dictionary* places spices in the tropics, but caraway shows that they can come from temperate climates, and *The Oxford Companion to Food* notes that ambergris, of animal origin, is often called a spice.

Spices were used in small quantities, leaving few archaeological remains. A spice trade across the Indian subcontinent and the Middle East existed by 2000 BC, in [[Black pepper|black pepper]] and [[Cinnamon|cinnamon]]; [[Clove|cloves]] were used in Mesopotamia by 1700 BC. Discovery of the [[Monsoon|monsoon]] winds enabled sea trade to replace overland caravan routes. Spices appear in the Old Testament, and Pliny the Elder wrote about cloves. In medieval Europe they were among the most demanded and expensive goods, valued for balancing the "humors" under [[Humorism|humorism]] and for their association with "paradise." European demand helped drive exploration in the early modern period. [[Vasco da Gama]] reached India by sea in 1498, and European powers established outposts in spice-producing regions such as the [[Maluku Islands]].

=== KEY: Spin_physics_530c3c
HEADWORD: Spin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Angular momentum | Pauli exclusion principle | Photon | Planck constant | Quantum field theory | Quantum mechanics
ENTRY:
Intrinsic form of [[Angular momentum|angular momentum]] carried by elementary particles, atomic nuclei and atoms. Spin is quantized, so it takes only discrete values, and accurate models of its interaction require relativistic [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] or [[Quantum field theory|quantum field theory]]. The existence of electron spin was inferred from the Stern–Gerlach experiment, in which silver atoms showed two possible discrete angular momenta despite having no orbital angular momentum. The relativistic spin–statistics theorem connects spin to the [[Pauli exclusion principle|Pauli exclusion principle]]: particles that obey exclusion have half-integer spin, and half-integer spin implies exclusion. Spin is described mathematically as a vector for particles such as [[Photon|photons]] and as a spinor for particles such as electrons. In practice, spin is given as a dimensionless spin quantum number, found by dividing the spin angular momentum by the reduced [[Planck constant]] ħ.

=== KEY: Spore_42e3ce

ENTRY:
Unit of sexual (in fungi) or asexual reproduction, adapted for dispersal and survival. Spores form part of the life cycles of many [[Plant|plants]], [[Algae|algae]], fungi and protozoa, and were thought to have appeared as early as the mid-late [[Ordovician]] period as an adaptation of early land plants. === KEY: Sporophyte_2fa946

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Diploid multicellular phase of the life cycle of a plant or alga. The sporophyte develops from a zygote formed when a haploid egg is fertilized by a haploid sperm, so each of its cells has two sets of chromosomes, one from each parent. It produces spores by meiosis, and these develop into the haploid [[Gametophyte|gametophyte]] phase, which alternates with the sporophyte in all land plants and most multicellular algae. In [[Bryophyte|bryophytes]] such as mosses, liverworts and hornworts, the gametophyte is dominant and the small sporophyte depends on it for nutrition. In seed plants, including [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperms]] and angiosperms, the sporophyte is the more prominent phase and is the familiar plant with roots, stems, leaves and cones or flowers. During the [[Devonian]] period, several plant groups independently evolved heterospory, an early step in the evolution of seeds.

They are usually triggered by positive [[Lightning|lightning]] discharges between an underlying thundercloud and the ground. Although sometimes called upper-atmospheric lightning, they are cold plasma phenomena, more akin to fluorescent tube discharges. The earliest known report dates from 1886, and they were first photographed in 1989 by scientists from the University of Minnesota. Their root cause remains unknown beyond a general association with positive cloud-to-ground lightning.

First artificial Earth satellite, launched by the [[Soviet Union]] on October 4, 1957. Regular quadrilateral with four equal sides and four right angles. American comic book writer, editor, publisher and producer, who was the primary creative leader of [[Marvel Comics]] for two decades. He rose through the ranks of Timely Comics, the family-run business that later became Marvel. With [[Jack Kirby]] and Steve Ditko, he co-created characters including [[Spider-Man]], the [[X-Men]], [[Iron Man]], Thor, the [[Hulk]], Ant-Man, the Fantastic Four, Black Panther and Doctor Strange. Introduced in the 1960s, these characters pioneered a more naturalistic approach in superhero comics. In the 1970s he challenged the restrictions of the Comics Code Authority. After retiring from Marvel in the 1990s, he remained its public figurehead, making cameo appearances in films based on Marvel properties. He received an executive producer credit on them, which made him the actor with the highest-grossing film total ever.

Art form in which a comic delivers humorous and satirical monologues on stage to a live audience, often interacting with the crowd. Routines consist of one-liners, stories, observations and shticks, and may employ props, music, impressions or [[Magic (illusion)|magic tricks]]. Stand-up draws on popular entertainments including [[Vaudeville]], minstrel shows, dime museums and humorist monologues such as [[Mark Twain]]'s 1866 touring show *Our Fellow Savages of the Sandwich Islands*. Charles Farrar Browne, known as Artemus Ward, is considered America's first stand-up comedian. Common genres include observational, anecdotal, deadpan, topical and [[Satire|satire]]. Performances take place in comedy clubs, festivals, bars, colleges and theaters, and during the [[COVID-19 pandemic]] comics performed by video conferencing and on social media.

=== KEY: Standard_Model_18bbbd
HEADWORD: Standard Model
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: CERN | Dark matter | Elementary particle | Fermion | Gravity | Higgs boson | Lepton | Quark
ENTRY:
Theory of particle physics describing the electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions and classifying all known [[Elementary particle|elementary particles]]. It excludes [[Gravity|gravity]], the fourth known fundamental force. The model was developed in stages during the latter half of the 20th century, and its current formulation was finalized in the mid-1970s when the existence of [[Quark|quarks]] was confirmed experimentally. The top quark (1995), the tau neutrino (2000), and the [[Higgs boson]] (2012) were confirmed later.

Key steps included Paul Dirac's 1928 equation, which implied antimatter; the 1954 extension of gauge theory to nonabelian groups by Yang Chen-Ning and Robert Mills; Chien-Shiung Wu's 1957 demonstration that parity is not conserved in the weak interaction; the introduction of quarks by Murray Gell-Mann and George Zweig in 1964; and the incorporation of the Higgs mechanism into Sheldon Glashow's electroweak theory by Steven Weinberg and Abdus Salam in 1967. Neutral weak currents were discovered at [[CERN]] in 1973, and the W and Z bosons in 1983. Glashow, Salam, and Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The model includes 12 matter particles of spin 1/2, the [[Fermion|fermions]], divided into six quarks and six [[Lepton|leptons]] in three generations. Forces are carried by bosons: the photon, eight gluons, and the W and Z bosons. The Higgs boson was observed on July 4, 2012, by the ATLAS and CMS experiments at the Large Hadron Collider.

The model remains incomplete. It does not incorporate gravitation as described by general relativity, explain the excess of matter over antimatter, or contain a viable [[Dark matter|dark matter]] particle. Without modification it also lacks neutrino masses and oscillation.

=== KEY: Standard_Oil_ca8096
HEADWORD: Standard Oil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: John D. Rockefeller
ENTRY:
Petroleum trust (1882–1911) that held a near monopoly on the U.S. oil industry from 1899. Standard Oil Company (Ohio) was founded in 1870 by [[John D. Rockefeller]] and associates, and grew through acquisitions and discounted railroad freight rates. In 1882 its holdings were pooled into a trust of 40 companies valued at $70 million and managed by nine trustees. In 1899 Standard Oil Company of New Jersey became the holding company for the trust and the largest corporation in the United States. In *Standard Oil Co. of New Jersey v. United States* (1911), the Supreme Court found it guilty of anticompetitive practices and ordered it broken up. The severed companies had a net value of $375 million, 57 percent of Jersey Standard's value. Jersey Standard, renamed Exxon in 1973 and ExxonMobil in 1999, remained one of the largest public oil companies. Reporting by Ida Tarbell contributed to the charges.

=== KEY: Standard_atmosphere_unit_1bd240
HEADWORD: Standard atmosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemistry | Sea level
ENTRY:
Unit of pressure (symbol atm) defined as 101325 Pa, approximately equal to Earth's average atmospheric pressure at [[Sea level|sea level]]. Originally defined as the pressure exerted by a 760 mm column of mercury at 0 °C under standard gravity, it was adopted for general use by the 10th General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1954, defined independently of any particular substance. In [[Chemistry|chemistry]], the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry recommended in 1982 a standard pressure of exactly 100 kPa (1 bar) for specifying the physical properties of substances. One standard atmosphere equals 760 mmHg, 29.92 inHg and 2116.22 pounds-force per square foot.
