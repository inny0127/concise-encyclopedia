=== KEY: Air_conditioning_67cfd8
HEADWORD: Air conditioning
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Brooklyn | Heat pump
ENTRY:
Removal of heat from an enclosed space to achieve a more comfortable interior temperature, and in some cases control of humidity. Mechanical units typically use vapor-compression refrigeration, while passive and ventilative cooling are alternative methods. [[Heat pump|Heat pumps]], which use a reversing valve, can both heat and cool a space. In hot weather, air conditioning can prevent heat stroke and other hyperthermia-related harm, and an estimated 190,000 heat-related deaths are averted annually. The International Energy Agency counted 1.6 billion air conditioning units worldwide in 2016. As of 2022, air conditioning used about 7% of global electricity and emitted 3% of greenhouse gas. [[Ancient Egypt|Ancient Egyptian]] buildings used passive techniques, which fell out of fashion in the 20th century. Willis H. Carrier designed the first modern electrical unit in 1902, to stabilize humidity in a [[Brooklyn]] printing plant.

=== KEY: Air_mass_9f1579
HEADWORD: Air mass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Humidity
ENTRY:
Large body of air defined by its temperature and [[Humidity|humidity]]. Air masses take on the characteristics of the surface beneath them and are classified by latitude and by continental or maritime source region. Continental air is dry, while maritime and monsoon air is moist. Weather fronts separate air masses of different density. The Bergeron classification, the most widely accepted scheme, uses three letters. The first gives moisture (c for continental, m for maritime). The second gives the source region (T tropical, P polar, A arctic or Antarctic, M monsoon, E equatorial, S for air dried and warmed by downward motion). The third gives stability (k if the air is colder than the surface, w if warmer). Thus cP denotes a cold, dry continental polar air mass.

=== KEY: Air_pollution_dae775
HEADWORD: Air pollution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asthma | Fossil fuel | Lung cancer | Stroke | Wildfire
ENTRY:
Presence in the air of substances harmful to humans, other living things or the environment. Pollutants include gases such as ozone and nitrogen oxides, and particles such as soot and dust. Outdoor pollution comes mainly from burning [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] for electricity and transport, [[Wildfire|wildfires]], industry, waste management and agriculture. The Montreal Protocol almost eliminated harmful ozone-depleting chemicals.

Ground-based service that directs aircraft to prevent collisions and expedite air traffic. === KEY: Aircraft_b95086
HEADWORD: Aircraft
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: French Revolution | Helicopter | Leonardo da Vinci | Montgolfier brothers | Unmanned aerial vehicle | World War II | Wright brothers
ENTRY:
Vehicle or machine that flies by gaining support from the air. An aircraft counters gravity with static lift or the dynamic lift of an airfoil, or, in a few cases, with direct downward thrust from its engines. Examples include airplanes, drones, rotorcraft such as [[Helicopter|helicopters]], airships, gliders, and hot-air balloons. Aircraft are classified by lift type, propulsion, and use; a crewed craft has an onboard pilot, while an [[Unmanned aerial vehicle|unmanned aerial vehicle]] is remotely controlled or autonomous.

Lighter-than-air craft, or aerostats, float by buoyancy in cells filled with helium, hydrogen, or hot air. Heavier-than-air craft, or aerodynes, are denser than air and must generate lift, either aerodynamically, with air flowing past a wing, or by powered lift from downward engine thrust.

Kites flown in China several hundred years BC are considered the earliest man-made flight. In the 15th century [[Leonardo da Vinci]] designed flying machines that proved unworkable. The [[Montgolfier brothers]] invented the hot-air balloon in the late 18th century, and the discovery of hydrogen led to the hydrogen balloon; France established military balloon companies during the [[French Revolution]]. Advances in fluid dynamics and Newton's laws of motion produced modern aerodynamics, notably through George Cayley.

The [[Wright brothers]] built and flew the first airplane in 1903, after research on wing design and aircraft control. Their flight of December 17 at Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, covered 120 feet in 12 seconds. Rigid airships pioneered by Ferdinand von Zeppelin dominated long-distance flight until the 1930s, when large flying boats took over trans-oceanic routes. After [[World War II]], land-based airplanes replaced flying boats, made more capable first by improved propeller engines and then by jet engines, which transformed civilian air travel and military aviation. Digital electronics later brought fly-by-wire systems, and pilotless drones now serve military, commercial, and recreational purposes.

ENTRY:
Warship designed to carry, launch, recover and support aircraft at sea. Its full-length flight deck and hangars make it a mobile airbase, and it is usually the capital ship of a carrier battle group. === KEY: Airship_de7505
HEADWORD: Airship
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Helium | Hydrogen | Zeppelin
ENTRY:
Powered lighter-than-air aircraft, also called a dirigible. Airships rise on a lifting gas less dense than air. Early airships used [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]], whose flammability caused fatal accidents, and most built since the 1960s use non-flammable [[Helium|helium]]. === KEY: Akbar_e7c419
HEADWORD: Akbar
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Bengal | Gujarat | Humayun | Indian subcontinent | Islam | Mughal Empire
ENTRY:
Third Mughal emperor of India (r. 1556–1605), known as Akbar the Great. He succeeded his father, [[Humayun]], at age 13 under the regency of Bairam Khan.

Born in exile at Amarkot in Sindh, Akbar was raised in Kabul and never learned to read or write. He took the throne in 1556 while the Mughals were fighting the Sur dynasty for Delhi and Agra. Over his reign he enlarged the [[Mughal Empire]] to cover much of the [[Indian subcontinent]] by military campaigns, including conquests of [[Gujarat]] in 1572 and [[Bengal]] in 1576, supported by artillery, matchlocks, and war elephants. Some conquests brought harsh reprisals; after the 1568 siege of Chittor he reportedly ordered the killing of about 30,000 mostly noncombatant Rajputs.

To unify the state, Akbar built a centralized administration and conciliated defeated rulers through marriage and diplomacy. He introduced the mansabdari system, a hierarchy of military and civil ranks, and in 1579–80 standardized land revenue through detailed surveys, building on the precedents of Sher Shah Suri. To maintain peace in a religiously diverse empire he abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims in 1564 and appointed non-Muslims to high civil and military posts. From 1575 he convened debates among scholars of many faiths at the Ibadatkhana, and he promoted a policy of universal peace (*sulh-i kul*).

Disillusioned with orthodox [[Islam]], he promulgated *Din-i Ilahi* (c. 1582), a syncretic creed drawing mainly on Islam and Hinduism, with elements of Zoroastrianism and Christianity. It attracted few followers beyond the court, and orthodox critics viewed his religious policies as heretical.

His courts at Delhi, Agra, and Fatehpur Sikri drew poets, architects, artisans, and holy men, and a distinctive Mughal style of painting and architecture blended Timurid and Perso-Islamic traditions with Indian elements. His vizier Abul Fazl chronicled the reign in the *Akbarnama*. His son Salim, later Jahangir, succeeded him.

Ancient state centered on the city of Akkad in [[Mesopotamia]], often considered the first empire in world history (c. 2334–2154 BC). [[Sargon of Akkad|Sargon]] founded it by defeating the king Lugal-zage-si and unifying the city-states of southern Mesopotamia under centralized rule. The Semitic [[Akkadian language|Akkadian]] became its lingua franca, although Sumerian remained important in religion and literature. Sargon's grandson Naram-Sin consolidated the empire through conquest and reforms, and declared himself a living god. [[Enheduanna]], Sargon's daughter, served as high priestess and is often recognized as the first known named author. The empire collapsed amid internal revolts, severe drought linked to the 4.2-kiloyear climate event, and attacks from the Gutians, [[Amorites]] and [[Elam|Elamites]].

=== KEY: Akkadian_language_b0b4b6
HEADWORD: Akkadian language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cuneiform | Hellenistic period | Mesopotamia | Semitic languages
ENTRY:
Extinct East [[Semitic languages|Semitic]] language of ancient [[Mesopotamia]], and the earliest documented Semitic language. It takes its name from the city of Akkad and was written in [[Cuneiform]] script, which had originally been developed for Sumerian. Texts fully written in Akkadian appear from about the 24th century BC, and by the 20th century BC two varieties, Assyrian and Babylonian, were in use. Akkadian became the lingua franca of much of the Ancient Near East by the Bronze Age collapse, c. 1150 BC, but from the 8th century BC it was gradually replaced by Old Aramaic. By the [[Hellenistic period]] it was largely confined to scholars and priests, and the last known cuneiform document dates from the 1st century AD. Hundreds of thousands of texts survive, including the *Epic of Gilgamesh* and the Laws of Hammurabi.

=== KEY: Al_Jabr_aebe7a
HEADWORD: Al-Jabr
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Khwarizmi | Algebra | Baghdad
ENTRY:
Arabic treatise on algebra written in [[Baghdad]] around 820 by [[Al-Khwarizmi]], whose title gave the word [[Algebra|algebra]] its name. Its full title is *The Concise Book of Calculation by Restoration and Balancing*. It was the first text to teach elementary algebra, and the first to teach algebra for its own sake. It gave an exhaustive account of solving the positive roots of polynomial equations up to the second degree, classifying quadratic equations into six basic types. It introduced *al-jabr*, the transposition of terms to the other side of an equation, and *muqabalah*, the cancellation of like terms on both sides. Robert of Chester translated it into Latin in 1145, and it was used as the principal mathematical textbook of European universities until the sixteenth century.

=== KEY: Al_Khwarizmi_2adfc0
HEADWORD: Khwarizmi, al-
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Abbasid Caliphate | Algebra | Algorithm | Baghdad | House of Wisdom | Islamic Golden Age
ENTRY:
Persian-descended mathematician, astronomer, and geographer of the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. Full name Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi. He wrote in Arabic and worked from about 820 at the [[House of Wisdom]] in [[Baghdad]], capital of the [[Abbasid Caliphate]], as astronomer and head of its library.

Little is known with certainty of his life. His name means "from Khwarazm," a region now in Turkmenistan and Uzbekistan, though some accounts place his birth near Baghdad. The sources differ on whether he was a Zoroastrian by background; a pious preface to his algebra indicates he was a Muslim. Most of his work dates from 813 to 833.

His treatise *Al-Jabr* (*The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing*) gave the first systematic solution of linear and quadratic equations, solving quadratics by completing the square and supplying geometric justifications. Because he treated [[Algebra|algebra]] as an independent discipline and introduced the methods of "reduction" and "balancing," he has been called the father or founder of algebra; the word derives from *al-jabr* in the title. Translated into Latin by Robert of Chester in 1145, the book served as the principal mathematics textbook of European universities until the 16th century.

His book on Indian arithmetic, written about 825, spread the Hindu–Arabic numeral system through the Middle East and Europe. Its 12th-century Latin translation, *Algoritmi de numero Indorum*, introduced the decimal positional system to the West, and his Latinized name, Algoritmi, is the origin of the word [[Algorithm|algorithm]].

He also revised Ptolemy's *Geography*, listing the longitudes and latitudes of cities and localities, and produced astronomical tables based on Indian and Greek sources, as well as works on the calendar, the astrolabe, and the sundial. His sine and cosine tables made important contributions to trigonometry.

=== KEY: Alan_Turing_c342cd
HEADWORD: Turing, Alan
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Artificial intelligence | Battle of the Atlantic | Enigma machine | Princeton University | Turing machine | Turing test | World War II
ENTRY:
English mathematician, logician, and cryptanalyst, widely regarded as the father of theoretical computer science. In his 1936 paper "On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem," he formalized algorithm and computation through the [[Turing machine]], a model of a general-purpose computer,.

Born in London, Turing graduated from King's College, Cambridge, and earned a doctorate from [[Princeton University]] in 1938. During [[World War II]] he worked at Bletchley Park, Britain's codebreaking center, where he headed Hut 8 and focused on German naval messages. His techniques improved on the prewar Polish *bomba* method and hastened the breaking of the [[Enigma machine|Enigma]] cipher. The resulting intelligence, known as Ultra, aided the Allies in the [[Battle of the Atlantic]]. His wartime work, covered by the Official Secrets Act, went unrecognized in his lifetime.

After the war he joined the National Physical Laboratory, where he designed the Automatic Computing Engine, an early stored-program computer. From 1948 he worked at the University of Manchester, contributing to early Manchester computers. His 1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" proposed an imitation game, later called the [[Turing test]], as a criterion for machine intelligence and helped found the philosophy of [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]]. He also turned to mathematical biology, studying the chemical basis of morphogenesis through reaction–diffusion models, a field that gave rise to the Turing pattern concept.

In 1952, after acknowledging a sexual relationship with another man, Turing was prosecuted for gross indecency. He received probation conditional on chemical castration and lost his security clearance. He died of cyanide poisoning in 1954, and an inquest ruled the death a suicide; some biographers have questioned that verdict. Following a 2009 campaign, Prime Minister Gordon Brown apologized for "the appalling way [Turing] was treated," and in 2013 Queen Elizabeth II granted him a pardon.

=== KEY: Alans_50a8cc

Iranic nomadic pastoral people who migrated to the [[Caucasus|North Caucasus]]. Generally regarded as part of the [[Sarmatians]], they were mentioned by Roman sources in the 1st century CE. After the Hunnic defeat of the [[Goths]] around 375 CE, many Alans migrated westward with the [[Vandals]] and Suebi, crossing the [[Rhine]] in 406 CE and entering the Iberian Peninsula around 409 CE. The Iberian Alans were defeated by the [[Visigoths]] in 418 CE. In 428 CE the Vandals and Alans crossed the Strait of Gibraltar into [[North Africa]], where they founded a kingdom that lasted until its conquest by Byzantine emperor [[Justinian I]] in 534 CE. === KEY: Albedo_f879d0
HEADWORD: Albedo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Climate change | NASA | Snow | Sunlight
ENTRY:
Fraction of [[Sunlight|sunlight]] diffusely reflected by a body, on a scale from 0 to 1. Albedo is 0 for a black body that absorbs all incident radiation and 1 for a body that reflects all of it. Ocean surfaces and most forests have low albedos, while fresh [[Snow|snow]] has a visible-light albedo of about 0.9 to 0.95. Most land areas have albedos between 0.1 and 0.4. Earth's average albedo of about 0.3 is far higher than the ocean's, largely because of clouds. Ice–albedo feedback, in which changes in ice cover alter surface albedo and temperature, plays an important role in [[Climate change|global climate change]]. Earth's surface albedo is estimated from satellite sensors such as the MODIS instruments of [[NASA]].

=== KEY: Albert_Einstein_7560af
HEADWORD: Einstein, Albert
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Adolf Hitler | Cosmological constant | Manhattan Project | Nobel Prize | Photoelectric effect | Quantum mechanics | World War II
ENTRY:
German-born theoretical physicist, best known for developing the theory of relativity. He also made major contributions to [[Quantum mechanics|quantum theory]], and his mass–energy equivalence formula, E = mc², arising from special relativity, has been called the world's most famous equation. He received the 1921 [[Nobel Prize]] in Physics for his discovery of the law of the [[Photoelectric effect|photoelectric effect]].

Einstein was born in Ulm in the Kingdom of Württemberg and grew up in Munich. He studied at the Swiss federal polytechnic school in Zurich, became a Swiss citizen in 1901, worked at the Swiss Patent Office in Bern, and received a doctorate in 1905. In 1914 he moved to Berlin to join the Prussian Academy of Sciences. When [[Adolf Hitler]] came to power in 1933, Einstein remained in the U.S., and he became a citizen in 1940. Shortly before [[World War II]] he endorsed a letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt urging American nuclear research, later carried out as the [[Manhattan Project]]. He advocated pacifism and civil rights and supported Zionism.

In 1905, his *annus mirabilis*, he published four papers that outlined a theory of the photoelectric effect, explained Brownian motion, introduced special relativity, and demonstrated the equivalence of mass and energy. In 1915 he proposed general relativity, extending mechanics to include gravitation. A 1917 paper applying it to the universe as a whole introduced the [[Cosmological constant|cosmological constant]] and is regarded as the first step in modern theoretical cosmology. A 1917 paper introduced stimulated emission, the basis of the laser. His work on radiation, in which light consists of particles later called photons, and his collaboration with Satyendra Nath Bose laid the groundwork for Bose–Einstein statistics.

In later decades he objected to the fundamental randomness of quantum theory ("God does not play dice") and sought a unified field theory joining gravitation and electromagnetism. Neither effort succeeded, and he became increasingly isolated from mainstream physics. The element einsteinium is named for him.

=== KEY: Alcohol_intoxication_433241
HEADWORD: Alcohol intoxication
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acetaldehyde
ENTRY:
Behavioral and physical changes caused by recent consumption of alcohol. Intoxication at lower doses causes mild sedation and poor coordination. Higher doses produce slurred speech, impaired vision, mood swings and vomiting, and extreme doses can cause respiratory depression, coma or death. Intoxication is often defined legally as a blood alcohol concentration above 25–80 mg/dL (0.025–0.080%). [[Acetaldehyde|Acetaldehyde]], a metabolite of alcohol, contributes to the hangover that may follow hours after drinking. Management is supportive, including keeping the person in the recovery position, keeping them warm and ensuring that breathing is sufficient. Gastric lavage and activated charcoal have not been found useful.
