=== KEY: Kilogram_9d71f8
HEADWORD: Kilogram
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: French Revolution | International System of Units | Litre | Platinum | Speed of light | Water
ENTRY:
Base unit of mass in the [[International System of Units]] (SI), equal to 1,000 grams. It was defined in 1795, during the [[French Revolution]], as the mass of one [[Litre|liter]] of [[Water|water]] at 0 °C, later adjusted to the temperature of water's maximum density, about 4 °C. From 1889 until 2019 the standard was the International Prototype of the Kilogram, a cylinder of [[Platinum|platinum]]–iridium alloy. The current definition fixes the numerical value of the Planck constant at 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J·s, which, with the [[Speed of light|speed of light]] and the cesium-133 transition frequency, defines the mass. The current definition agrees with the original within 30 parts per million. The kilogram is the only SI base unit with an SI prefix in its name.

Unit of length equal to 1,000 meters, from the [[International System of Units|SI]] prefix *kilo-*, meaning one thousand. It is the preferred unit for distances between places on land in most of the world. === KEY: Kinematics_8d5c85
HEADWORD: Kinematics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astrophysics | Force | Human skeleton | Lorentz transformation | Mechanical engineering | Robotics | Time dilation | Velocity
ENTRY:
Branch of physics and geometry describing motion without regard to the [[Force|forces]] that cause it. It specifies the positions and [[Velocity|velocities]] of points and bodies, along with the mathematical transformations between coordinate systems. A problem begins with the geometry of a system and known initial conditions, from which unknown positions, velocities and accelerations are determined. The scholar Ibn al-Haytham is credited with first treating geometry and kinematics as a unified concept. The field is distinguished from dynamics, which studies the effect of forces on bodies. It describes celestial motion in [[Astrophysics|astrophysics]] and multi-link systems in [[Mechanical engineering|mechanical engineering]], [[Robotics|robotics]] and biomechanics, such as engines, robotic arms and the [[Human skeleton|human skeleton]]. Relativistic kinematics, applying special relativity, encompasses [[Time dilation|time dilation]] and the [[Lorentz transformation|Lorentz transformation]].

=== KEY: Kinetic_energy_9c1631
HEADWORD: Kinetic energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Joule | Lord Kelvin | Mass | Speed of light | Work (physics)
ENTRY:
Energy of a moving physical body, equal to one-half its [[Mass|mass]] times the square of its speed. In classical mechanics, a non-rotating object of mass *m* moving at speed *v* has kinetic energy ½*mv*². This equals the [[Work (physics)|work]] needed to accelerate the object from rest to that speed, and the object does the same amount of work when it decelerates to rest. The SI unit is the [[Joule|joule]]. The formula is a good approximation only when speed is much less than the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. The term was coined in the mid-19th century by [[Lord Kelvin|William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin]], and the word derives from the Greek *kinesis*, meaning motion.

ENTRY:
Tragedy by [[William Shakespeare|William Shakespeare]], written in late 1605 or early 1606. Set in pre-Roman Britain, the play depicts an aging king who divides his kingdom according to his daughters' professions of love. When his youngest daughter, Cordelia, refuses to flatter him, he disinherits her, and his descent into madness unleashes betrayal and civil strife. === KEY: Kinship_bc16dc

ENTRY:
Web of social relationships based on descent and marriage, central to human societies. In anthropology, kinship refers both to these relationships and to the study of them. Ties through descent are distinguished from [[Affinity (law)|affinity]], the ties created by marriage. Kinship organizes people into social groups, roles and [[Genealogy|genealogies]]. === KEY: Kirchhoff_s_circuit_laws_eb6d03
HEADWORD: Kirchhoff's circuit laws
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Georg Ohm | Gustav Kirchhoff | James Clerk Maxwell | Maxwell's equations | Voltage
ENTRY:
Two equalities describing current and potential difference (commonly known as [[Voltage|voltage]]) in electrical circuits. First described in 1845 by German physicist [[Gustav Kirchhoff]], the laws generalized the work of [[Georg Ohm]] and preceded that of [[James Clerk Maxwell]]. The current law states that the algebraic sum of currents at any node (junction) is zero. The voltage law states that the directed sum of potential differences around any closed loop is zero. Both can be understood as corollaries of [[Maxwell's equations]] in the low-frequency limit. They are accurate for direct-current (DC) circuits and for alternating-current circuits at frequencies whose wavelengths are very large compared with the circuit. A matrix version of the current law is the basis of most circuit simulation software, such as SPICE. The laws do not apply where the lumped-element model fails, as in high-frequency AC circuits.

=== KEY: Knife_d39d7a
HEADWORD: Knife
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze | Ceramic | Dagger | Forging | Obsidian | Stainless steel | Steel | Titanium
ENTRY:
Tool or weapon with a cutting edge or blade, usually attached to a handle or hilt. Knives are among the earliest tools used by humanity, appearing at least 2.5 million years ago, as evidenced by Oldowan tools. They were originally made of wood, bone, and stone such as flint and [[Obsidian|obsidian]]; with advances in metallurgy and manufacturing, blades came to be made of copper, [[Bronze|bronze]], iron, [[Steel|steel]], ceramic, and titanium.

A modern knife consists of a blade and a handle. The blade has a point, an edge (plain, serrated, or both), a grind (its cross-section shape), and a spine. The handle may include a tang extending into it, partial or full, as well as a bolster and a guard that protects the hand.

Blade materials have differing advantages. Carbon steel, an alloy of iron and carbon, takes a very sharp edge and is easy to sharpen but is vulnerable to rust and stains. [[Stainless steel]] contains chromium, possibly nickel and molybdenum, and little carbon; it takes a slightly less sharp edge but resists corrosion. [[Titanium]] has a higher strength-to-weight ratio and is more flexible than steel but is less hard. [[Ceramic|Ceramic]] blades are hard, brittle, lightweight, and noncorroding; they hold an edge for years but break if dropped or twisted. Steel blades are shaped by [[Forging|forging]], in which a heated piece is hammered or pressed, or by stock removal, in which metal is ground away; both methods are followed by heat treatment.

Knives serve many purposes, from hunting, combat, and camping to kitchen work (the chef's knife, paring knife, bread knife, and cleaver), table use, throwing, and religious ceremony or display, such as the *kirpan*. Weapons include the [[Dagger|dagger]] and the switchblade.

Practical knots are classified by function: hitches fasten a rope to another object, bends fasten two ends of a rope to each other, loop knots form a loop, and splices are multi-strand knots. === KEY: Knot_theory_c15a11
HEADWORD: Knot theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euclidean space | Lord Kelvin | Topology
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Topology|topology]] concerned with mathematical knots. A mathematical knot is a loop whose ends are joined so that it cannot be undone, formally an embedding of a circle in three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]]. Two knots are equivalent if one can be deformed into the other without cutting the string or passing it through itself. A fundamental problem is determining when two descriptions, such as knot diagrams, represent the same knot. Knots are distinguished by invariants, quantities that remain the same for all descriptions of a knot, including knot polynomials, knot groups and hyperbolic invariants. In the 1860s, [[Lord Kelvin]]'s theory that atoms were knots in the aether led Peter Guthrie Tait to create the first knot tables. More than six billion knots and links have been tabulated since the 19th century.

=== KEY: Knowledge_1e984d
HEADWORD: Knowledge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epistemology | Inference | Introspection | Memory | Perception | Plato | Scientific method | Sociology
ENTRY:
Awareness of facts (descriptive knowledge), familiarity with individuals and situations (knowledge by acquaintance), or practical skill (procedural knowledge). Descriptive knowledge, also called propositional knowledge, is often characterized as true belief distinguished from opinion or guesswork by justification. Philosophers widely agree that propositional knowledge is a form of true belief, but controversies surround justification: how to understand it, whether it is needed at all, and whether something besides it is needed. It is closely related to intelligence, which concerns the ability to acquire, process, and apply information, whereas knowledge concerns information and skills a person already possesses.

The English word comes from the 12th-century Old English *cnawan*. Ancient Greek used four important terms: *epistēmē* (unchanging theoretical knowledge), *technē* (expert technical knowledge), *mētis* (strategic knowledge), and *gnōsis* (personal intellectual knowledge).

The main discipline investigating knowledge is [[Epistemology|epistemology]], which studies what people know, how they come to know it, and what it means to know something. It also considers the value of knowledge and philosophical skepticism, which questions whether knowledge is possible.

A long-discussed definition, which has roots in [[Plato]]'s exploration of the question, treats knowledge as justified true belief. Truth is a widely accepted feature, since one cannot know something false. Justification is included because some true beliefs, such as lucky guesses or beliefs based on superstition or erroneous reasoning, do not seem to be knowledge. Some philosophers hold that a belief is justified if it rests on evidence; others hold that it is justified if produced by a reliable process, such as sensory perception or logical reasoning.

In 1963, Edmund Gettier presented counterexamples of justified true beliefs that fail to constitute knowledge because of epistemic luck. In a well-known example, a driver in a country with many barn facades and one real barn stops by chance in front of the real barn and believes it is a barn; the belief is justified and true, yet the driver could not have told the difference. Such cases prompted alternative definitions, including reliabilism.

Knowledge can be produced in many ways. The main source of empirical knowledge is [[Perception|perception]], the use of the senses to learn about the external world. [[Introspection]] reveals one's own mental states and processes. Other sources include [[Memory|memory]], rational intuition, [[Inference|inference]], and testimony. According to foundationalism, some sources are basic and can justify beliefs without depending on other mental states. Coherentists hold that a sufficient degree of coherence among all of a believer's mental states is necessary, and infinitism holds that an infinite chain of beliefs is needed.

Knowledge is relevant to many fields. The sciences aim to acquire it through the [[Scientific method|scientific method]], based on repeatable experimentation, observation, and measurement. Various religions hold that humans should seek knowledge and that God or the divine is its source. The anthropology of knowledge studies how it is acquired, stored, retrieved, and communicated across cultures, and the [[Sociology|sociology]] of knowledge examines the circumstances in which it arises.

=== KEY: Korean_mythology_cc51e6

Body of [[Myth|myths]] told by historical and modern Koreans, in two main forms. Literary mythology, recorded in traditional Hanja histories such as *Samguk sagi* and *Samguk yusa*, consists mostly of state-foundation myths about the founders of ancient kingdoms. The best known is that of Gojoseon and its legendary king Dangun, which became the founding myth of the whole Korean nation. Oral mythology is much larger and more varied.
