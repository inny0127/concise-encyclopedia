=== KEY: Disease_e7067a
HEADWORD: Disease
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Immune system | Pathogen | Stroke
ENTRY:
Particular abnormal condition that adversely affects the structure or function of all or part of an organism and is not immediately due to external injury. Diseases are usually associated with specific signs and symptoms. They may be caused by external factors such as [[Pathogen|pathogens]] or by internal dysfunctions; for example, internal dysfunctions of the [[Immune system|immune system]] can produce immunodeficiency, hypersensitivity, allergies, and autoimmune disorders.

In humans, the term is often used more broadly for any condition that causes or can cause pain, dysfunction, distress, social problems, or death to the person affected or to those in contact with them. In this sense it sometimes includes injuries, disabilities, disorders, syndromes, infections, and isolated symptoms, though in other contexts these are treated as separate categories.

Four main types are recognized: infectious, deficiency, hereditary, and physiological diseases. Diseases are also classified as communicable or non-communicable. Infectious diseases result from pathogenic agents including viruses, bacteria, fungi, protozoa, multicellular organisms, and prions. A colonization that produces no clinically evident impairment, such as the normal bacteria and yeasts of the gut, is not considered a disease. Non-infectious diseases include most forms of [[Cancer|cancer]], heart disease, and genetic disease.

Several terms describe disease by course or origin. An acute disease is short-term; a chronic disease persists, often for at least six months. A congenital disorder is present at birth, while an acquired disease begins later in life. A genetic disease is caused by one or more mutations, and a hereditary disease is a genetic disease that runs in families. Iatrogenic disease is caused by medical intervention, and idiopathic disease has an unknown cause. A primary disease arises from a root cause, whereas a secondary disease is a complication of a prior one. A terminal disease is expected to result in death.

The term *illness* is sometimes used for the patient's subjective experience, so a person can have a disease without being ill, or be ill without being diseased. The deadliest diseases in humans are ischemic heart disease, followed by [[Stroke|stroke]] and COPD, a lung disease. In developed countries, neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety cause the most sickness overall. Pathology is the study of disease, including its causes.

[[Emotion|Emotional]] response of rejection or revulsion to something potentially contagious or offensive. Numerical or occasionally qualitative measure of how far apart objects, points, people, or ideas are. === KEY: Division_mathematics_145d63

ENTRY:
One of the four basic operations of [[Arithmetic|arithmetic]], alongside [[Addition|addition]], [[Subtraction|subtraction]], and [[Multiplication|multiplication]]. The number being divided is the dividend, the number dividing it is the divisor, and the result is the quotient. Division of natural numbers may leave a remainder: if 21 apples are divided between four people, everyone receives five apples and one apple remains. Extending the numbers to [[Rational number|rational]] or [[Real number|real numbers]] makes division the inverse of multiplication, so a = c / b means a × b = c, but division by zero is not defined. Division is not commutative or associative: (24 / 6) / 2 = 2, while 24 / (6 / 2) = 8. Division also appears in algebraic structures such as [[Field (mathematics)|fields]], where division by every nonzero element is defined, and in Euclidean domains, where a Euclidean division with remainder is defined.

=== KEY: Dmitri_Mendeleev_91a0ed
HEADWORD: Mendeleev, Dmitri
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Gallium | Germanium | Metric system | Oil refinery | Periodic table | Scandium | Siberia | Uranium
ENTRY:
Russian chemist and inventor, best known for formulating the periodic law and creating a version of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]] of elements.

Mendeleev was born in a village near Tobolsk in [[Siberia]], the youngest of many siblings. His father lost his sight and his teaching position, and his mother restarted a glass factory. He entered the Main Pedagogical Institute in Saint Petersburg in 1850. After contracting tuberculosis, he moved to the Crimean Peninsula in 1855, returning to Saint Petersburg in 1857 with his health restored. He worked in Heidelberg from 1859 to 1861, and his textbook *Organic Chemistry* (1861) won the Demidov Prize. He became a professor at Saint Petersburg State University in 1865, the year he earned his doctorate.

Other scientists, including John Newlands and Lothar Meyer, had earlier identified periodicity among the elements. While writing his textbook *Principles of Chemistry* (1868–70), which became the definitive textbook of its time, Mendeleev made his most important discovery. In 1869 he published a first draft of his table, arranging 63 known elements by atomic weight and similar properties and leaving gaps for undiscovered elements. He used the periodic law to correct the accepted properties of some known elements, such as the valence and atomic weight of [[Uranium|uranium]], and to predict the properties of three elements: [[Gallium|gallium]], discovered in 1875; [[Scandium|scandium]], in 1879; and [[Germanium|germanium]], in 1886.

He was a founder of the Russian Chemical Society in 1868. He established Russia's first [[Oil refinery|oil refinery]], promoted the [[Metric system|metric system]] in Russia, and directed the Bureau of Weights and Measures from 1893. He received the Copley Medal in 1905. The synthetic element mendelevium, element 101, was named in his honor in 1955.

=== KEY: Dog_c935d1

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Domesticated descendant of [[Wolf|wolves]], *Canis familiaris* or *Canis lupus familiaris*, selectively bred during the Late Pleistocene by hunter-gatherers. Dogs and the modern gray wolf share a common ancestor. Dogs were the first species to be domesticated, over 14,000 years ago and before the development of agriculture, though genetic studies suggest the process may have begun over 25,000 years ago. The earliest remains conclusively identified as a domesticated dog, found in Bonn–Oberkassel, Germany, date to 14,000–15,000 years ago. Dogs have been bred for specific behaviors, sensory abilities, and physical traits, and breeds vary widely in shape, size, and color. They have powerful jaws containing about 42 teeth. Compared with humans, they have a superior sense of smell and hearing but less acute vision.

Dogs serve humans in hunting, herding, pulling loads, protection, companionship, therapeutic support, assisting people with disabilities, and police and military operations. The global dog population is estimated at 700 million to 1 billion; about 75% are in developing countries, mainly as feral and street dogs. The dog is the most popular [[Pet|pet]] in the United States, present in 34–40% of households.

=== KEY: Domestication_cd648d
HEADWORD: Domestication
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Barley | Dog | Maize | Rice | Western honey bee | Wheat
ENTRY:
Multigenerational mutualistic relationship in which one species takes over the control and care of another to obtain a steady supply of resources such as meat, milk, or labor. The process is gradual and based on trial and error. In animals it affects genes for behavior, making them less aggressive; in plants it affects genes for morphology, such as increasing seed size and preventing the shattering of cereal seedheads. These changes make domesticated organisms easier to handle and reduce their ability to survive in the wild.

The term derives from the Latin *domesticus*, "belonging to the house." The archaeologist Melinda A. Zeder defined it as a long-term relationship in which humans take over control and care of another organism to gain a predictable supply of a resource, with mutual benefits. She noted that it is not synonymous with [[Agriculture|agriculture]], which depends on domesticated organisms but does not automatically result from domestication. Domestication syndrome is the set of traits distinguishing domesticated species from wild ancestors; in mammals these include increased docility, floppy ears, and reduced brain size.

The [[Dog|dog]] was the earliest vertebrate domesticated, diverging from wolves at least 15,000 years ago. Goats, sheep, and cows followed around 11,000 years ago, and the horse around 5,500 years ago in central Asia. The silkworm and the [[Western honey bee]] were domesticated over 5,000 years ago for silk and honey. Plant domestication began around 13,000–11,000 years ago with cereals such as [[Wheat|wheat]] and [[Barley|barley]] in the Middle East. Beginning around 10,000 years ago, Indigenous peoples in the Americas cultivated peanuts, squash, [[Maize|maize]], potatoes, cotton, and cassava, and [[Rice|rice]] was first domesticated in China some 9,000 years ago.

Ambrosia beetles, leafcutter ants, and fungus-growing termites have independently domesticated fungi on which they feed.

=== KEY: Dopamine_d7e7bc

Organic catecholamine, both a [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitter]] and a hormone. In the brain it is synthesized from L-DOPA and released by neurons along several pathways, one of which plays a major role in reward-motivated behavior. Popular culture often portrays dopamine as the chemical of pleasure, but current pharmacological opinion holds that it confers motivational salience, signaling the desirability or aversiveness of an outcome. Injected dopamine treats severe [[Heart failure|heart failure]] or cardiogenic shock.

=== KEY: Doppler_effect_44a49c
HEADWORD: Doppler effect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Sound | Ultrasound
ENTRY:
Change in the frequency, or period, of a wave for an observer moving relative to the wave's source. Named after Christian Doppler, who proposed it in 1842, it explains the change of pitch heard as a vehicle approaches and recedes. A source moving toward the observer compresses successive wave cycles and raises the frequency; a receding source lowers it. For waves in a vacuum, such as light, only relative velocity matters, but for [[Sound|sound]] the speed of the medium also counts. In 1845 Buys Ballot confirmed the effect for sound, and Hippolyte Fizeau found it for light in 1848. In astronomy it measures the radial velocities of stars, and in medicine [[Ultrasound|Doppler ultrasound]] assesses blood flow.

Small, mostly nocturnal [[Rodent|rodent]] of the family Gliridae.
