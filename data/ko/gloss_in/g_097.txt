=== KEY: Norman_Conquest_ebf387
HEADWORD: Norman Conquest
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Battle of Hastings | Battle of Stamford Bridge | Domesday Book | Edward the Confessor | William the Conqueror
ENTRY:
11th-century invasion of England by William, Duke of Normandy, later [[William the Conqueror|William the Conqueror]]. The army included thousands of Norman, French, Flemish and Breton troops. William's claim derived from his relationship with the childless Anglo-Saxon king [[Edward the Confessor|Edward the Confessor]]. After Edward's death in January 1066, Harold Godwinson became king, and Harald Hardrada was defeated at the [[Battle of Stamford Bridge|Battle of Stamford Bridge]] on 25 September. William landed at Pevensey on 28 September. Harold was killed at the [[Battle of Hastings|Battle of Hastings]] on 14 October, and William became king. William was not secure until after 1072. He confiscated the lands of the resisting English elite, granted lands to his followers and built castles. The [[Domesday Book|Domesday Book]] was completed by 1086. The conquest introduced a dialect of French as the language of the elites.

=== KEY: Norovirus_9e0829
HEADWORD: Norovirus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diarrhea | Gastroenteritis | Vaccine
ENTRY:
Virus that is the most common cause of [[Gastroenteritis|gastroenteritis]], also known as Norwalk virus. Infection, sometimes called the winter vomiting disease or stomach flu, causes non-bloody [[Diarrhea|diarrhea]], vomiting and stomach pain, usually within 24 hours of exposure. Recovery typically takes one to three days, though dehydration can be a complication. Norovirus causes about 685 million cases and 200,000 deaths globally each year, including about 50,000 deaths among children under five in the developing world. In the United States it causes about half of all foodborne disease outbreaks. There is no [[Vaccine|vaccine]] or specific treatment.

=== KEY: Norse_mythology_073891

ENTRY:
Body of myths of the North Germanic peoples of Scandinavia, preserved mainly in medieval Icelandic manuscripts. Its principal sources are the anonymous *Poetic Edda* and the *Prose Edda* of [[Snorri Sturluson]], both recorded in the 13th century, along with sagas and archaeological evidence. The cosmos comprises Nine Worlds linked by the world tree [[Yggdrasil]]. Scholars have studied the myths since the 17th century, and adherents of Germanic Neopaganism revive them in a religious context.

Northern portion of the African continent. === KEY: North_America_5ffec2
HEADWORD: North America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Arctic Ocean | Asia | Atlantic Ocean | Caribbean Sea | Greenland | Pacific Ocean | South America
ENTRY:
Continent in the Northern and Western hemispheres, bordered by the [[Arctic Ocean]] to the north, the [[Atlantic Ocean]] to the east, [[South America]] and the [[Caribbean Sea]] to the southeast, and the [[Pacific Ocean]] to the south and west. It covers about 24,709,000 sq km, roughly 16.5% of Earth's land area and 4.8% of its total surface area. It is the third-largest continent by size after [[Asia]] and [[Africa]], and the fourth largest by population, estimated as of 2021 at over 592 million people in 23 independent states and territories, about 7.5% of the world's population.

Definitions vary. The continent includes the subregions of Northern America (Canada, [[Greenland]], the continental United States, Bermuda, and Saint Pierre and Miquelon) and Middle America (Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean). The UN statistics division recognizes three subregions: Northern America, Central America (which includes Mexico), and the Caribbean. In human geography the term often refers only to Northern America, and in regional trade agreements such as NAFTA it denotes Canada, Mexico, and the United States. The Americas were named after the Italian explorer Amerigo Vespucci by the cartographers Martin Waldseemüller and Matthias Ringmann; Waldseemüller's 1507 world map placed "America" on present-day South America, and mapmakers later extended the name northward.

It is unknown exactly how and when the first human populations arrived. People are known to have lived in the Americas at least 20,000 years ago, and various evidence points to earlier dates. The traditional theory holds that hunters crossed the Bering Land Bridge between eastern Siberia and present-day Alaska from 27,000 to 14,000 years ago; a growing viewpoint is that the first inhabitants sailed from Beringia some 13,000 years ago. The Paleo-Indian period followed the Last Glacial Period and lasted until about 10,000 years ago, when the Archaic period began. Norse exploration and colonization began in the late 10th century, with several settlements in Greenland and at least one in Newfoundland. From 1492 the voyages of Christopher Columbus brought a transatlantic exchange and migration of European settlers.

Colonization led to most North Americans speaking European languages such as English, Spanish, and French. The continent is divided linguistically and culturally into Anglo-America and Latin America, with large Francophone populations in Louisiana and Quebec. Significant indigenous populations retain pre-colonial traditions, and Latin America lies to the south. Canada and the United States are the largest countries by land area.

=== KEY: North_China_Plain_5d42ae
HEADWORD: North China Plain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beijing | Great Wall of China | Han Chinese | Neogene | Paleogene | Yellow River
ENTRY:
Largest alluvial plain of China, in the north of the country. Formed in a downfaulted rift basin during the late [[Paleogene]] and [[Neogene]] and later built up by Yellow River deposits, it covers about 409,500 sq km (158,100 sq mi), mostly under 50 meters (160 ft) above sea level. It is bordered by the Yanshan, Taihang and Dabie mountains. Its fertile soils produce wheat, maize, sorghum, millet, peanuts, cotton and vegetables, and it is one of the most densely populated regions in the world. The Central Plain along the middle and lower [[Yellow River]] was the cradle of Chinese civilization and the region from which the [[Han Chinese]] emerged. [[Beijing]], the national capital, lies on its northeast edge. Floods and droughts are both common, and many historians argue that the plain's flat geography encouraged a centralized state to manage granaries, waterworks and fortifications against steppe peoples, prompting construction of the [[Great Wall of China]].

=== KEY: North_Pole_0d3bc0
HEADWORD: North Pole
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arctic Ocean | Greenland | Roald Amundsen | South Pole
ENTRY:
Northernmost point on Earth, at 90° North latitude, where the planet's axis of rotation meets the surface. It is also called the True North Pole to distinguish it from the magnetic north pole. The Pole lies in the middle of the [[Arctic Ocean]], on constantly shifting sea ice rather than on land as the [[South Pole]] does. The nearest land is Kaffeklubben Island, about 700 km (430 mi) away off northern [[Greenland]]. Sea depths of more than 4,000 meters (13,100 feet) make a permanent station impractical, although Soviet and later Russian drifting stations have passed close to the Pole. Earlier claims by Frederick Cook, Robert Peary and Richard Byrd were later doubted. The first undisputed expedition to reach it was the airship *Norge*, which overflew the area in 1926 under [[Roald Amundsen]]. The first complete land expedition arrived in 1968, using snowmobiles and air support.

=== KEY: North_Sea_d22ce7

Shallow sea of the [[Atlantic Ocean]] on the European continental shelf, lying between [[Great Britain]] and northern continental Europe. It is more than 970 km (600 mi) long and 580 km (360 mi) wide, with a mean depth of 90 meters (300 ft). It connects to the Atlantic through the [[English Channel]] in the south and to the Norwegian Sea in the north. Period of political division in Chinese history, from 420 to 589 AD, following the [[Sixteen Kingdoms]] and the Eastern Jin dynasty. It is sometimes considered the latter part of the Six Dynasties (220–589 AD). Civil war and political chaos were common, but the era also saw advances in technology, arts and culture. Historians note advances in medicine, astronomy, mathematics and cartography. The period ended when Emperor Wen of the [[Sui dynasty]] unified China proper in 589 AD.

=== KEY: Novel_075084
HEADWORD: Novel
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Herman Melville | Historiography | J. R. R. Tolkien | Mary Shelley | Prose | The Golden Ass | To Kill a Mockingbird | Walter Scott
ENTRY:
Extended work of narrative fiction, usually written in [[Prose|prose]] and published as a book. The word derives from the Italian *novella*, meaning "new," "news," or "short story (of something new)," from the Latin *novella*, a diminutive of *novus*. According to Margaret Doody, the novel has "a continuous and comprehensive history of about two thousand years," with origins in the ancient Greek and Roman novel, medieval chivalric romance, and the Italian Renaissance novella.

Writers have long distinguished the novel from the romance. M. H. Abrams and [[Walter Scott]] argued that a novel depicts society realistically, as in [[To Kill a Mockingbird]], while a romance emphasizes marvelous or uncommon incidents. Works of the latter kind, such as [[Mary Shelley|Mary Shelley's]] *Frankenstein* and [[J. R. R. Tolkien|Tolkien's]] *The Lord of the Rings*, are nevertheless commonly called novels. [[Herman Melville]] preferred the term romance, which should not be confused with the genre of romance novel.

Characteristics often cited include fictional narrative, literary prose, intimacy of experience, and length. Fictionality is commonly used to distinguish novels from [[Historiography|historiography]], though the criterion is problematic, since early modern historians also embellished. E. M. Forster suggested a novel should have at least fifty thousand words.

The earliest novels include Greek and Latin prose narratives from the first century BC to the second century AD such as Petronius's *Satyricon* and Apuleius's *[[The Golden Ass]]*. Harold Bloom credits Lady Murasaki's *The Tale of Genji* with anticipating Cervantes. Printed books in China led to vernacular classic novels during the Ming dynasty (1368–1644) and Qing dynasty (1616–1911). Miguel de Cervantes, author of *Don Quixote* (first part 1605), is frequently cited as the first significant European novelist of the modern era, while Ian Watt argued in 1957 that the modern novel was born in the early 18th century with Robinson Crusoe.

Splitting of an atomic nucleus into two or more smaller nuclei, releasing large amounts of energy. The process often produces [[Neutron|neutrons]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]. The fission of one [[Uranium-235]] atom releases about 100 million times the energy of burning one [[Carbon|carbon]] atom in air to produce CO₂. [[Otto Hahn]] and Fritz Strassmann proved in December 1938 that fission had occurred, and [[Lise Meitner]] and Otto Frisch explained it theoretically in January 1939. Hahn and Strassmann predicted additional neutrons, opening the possibility of a chain reaction. Three isotopes, U-233, U-235 and Pu-239, can sustain such a chain reaction, which allows reactors to release energy in a controlled way and weapons to release it uncontrollably within about a microsecond. Most fissions are binary, producing two charged fragments, but three fragments form occasionally (2 to 4 times per 1000 events).

=== KEY: Nuclear_fusion_52eaab
HEADWORD: Nuclear fusion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Deuterium | Fusion power | Hans Bethe | Helium-3 | Nucleosynthesis | Thermonuclear weapon
ENTRY:
Combination of atomic nuclei into a larger nucleus, with release or absorption of energy. The energy comes from the mass difference between reactants and products, which arises from differences in nuclear binding energy. Fusion powers active stellar cores, and [[Nucleosynthesis|nucleosynthesis]] in the [[Big Bang]] and in stars creates all elements lighter than nickel. On Earth, fusion occurs only in [[Thermonuclear weapon|thermonuclear weapons]], boosted fission weapons and experiments, because it requires temperatures in the hundred million kelvin range, sufficient density and a long enough confinement time. The most fusible nuclei are the lightest, especially [[Deuterium]], tritium and [[Helium-3]]. [[Fusion power]] research seeks to generate energy, with tokamaks and stellarators as the dominant magnetic-confinement designs. In 1920 Arthur Eddington predicted that fusion of hydrogen into helium could be the primary source of stellar energy, and [[Hans Bethe]] described the proton–proton chain in 1938.

=== KEY: Nuclear_physics_7ef0f0
HEADWORD: Nuclear physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic physics | Ernest Rutherford | Henri Becquerel | James Chadwick | Nuclear power | Particle physics | Radiocarbon dating
ENTRY:
Field of physics that studies atomic nuclei, their constituents and their interactions. It is distinct from [[Atomic physics|atomic physics]], which studies the whole atom including its electrons, and from nucleonics, the engineering application of the field. Discoveries have led to [[Nuclear power|nuclear power]], nuclear weapons, nuclear medicine, magnetic resonance imaging, industrial and agricultural isotopes, and [[Radiocarbon dating|radiocarbon dating]] in geology and archaeology. [[Particle physics]] evolved out of nuclear physics, and nuclear astrophysics explains the workings of stars and the origin of the chemical elements. [[Henri Becquerel]] discovered radioactivity in 1896, and [[Ernest Rutherford]]'s 1911 gold foil experiment showed the nucleus to be a dense, positively charged core holding most of an atom's mass. [[James Chadwick]] identified the neutron in 1932.

=== KEY: Nuclear_power_f14cc7
HEADWORD: Nuclear power
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chernobyl disaster | Manhattan Project | Nuclear fission | Nuclear fusion | Plutonium | University of Chicago | Uranium | Voyager 2
ENTRY:
Use of nuclear reactions to produce electricity. It can be obtained from [[Nuclear fission|nuclear fission]], nuclear decay, and [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]], but the vast majority comes from fission of [[Uranium|uranium]] and [[Plutonium|plutonium]] in nuclear power plants. Decay powers niche applications such as space probes like [[Voyager 2]]. Reactors producing controlled fusion have operated since 1958 but have yet to generate net power.

Fission was discovered in 1938, and a self-sustaining chain reaction was confirmed experimentally in 1939. The first human-made reactor, Chicago Pile-1, achieved criticality on December 2, 1942, at the [[University of Chicago]] as part of the [[Manhattan Project]]. A reactor first generated electricity on December 20, 1951, at EBR-I near Arco, Idaho.  The pressurized water reactor, developed by the U.S. Navy for submarines, became the standard for power generation. The Obninsk plant in the USSR became the first to supply a power grid on June 27, 1954, and Calder Hall in England became the first commercial station in 1956.

Global installed capacity reached 100 GW in the late 1970s and 300 GW by 1990. The 1979 Three Mile Island accident and the 1986 [[Chernobyl disaster]] brought increased regulation and public opposition. Nuclear plants supplied 2,602 terawatt hours of electricity in 2023, about 9% of global generation and the second largest low-carbon source after hydroelectricity. As of November 2025, 416 civilian fission reactors operated worldwide with a capacity of 376 GW. The United States has the largest fleet.

Supporters point to one of the lowest fatality rates per unit of energy and to the absence of direct greenhouse gas emissions. The anti-nuclear movement cites radiological hazards and accidents such as Fukushima in 2011, and contends that nuclear power is too expensive compared with alternatives.

=== KEY: Nuclear_reactor_3e67cf
HEADWORD: Nuclear reactor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Manhattan Project | Nuclear fission | Turbine
ENTRY:
Device for sustaining a controlled [[Nuclear fission|fission]] chain reaction, used for commercial electricity, marine propulsion, research and weapons production. Heat from fission is passed to a coolant, usually water, which drives [[Turbine|turbines]]. The first artificial critical reactor, Chicago Pile-1, was built in 1942 as part of the [[Manhattan Project]]. The pressurized water design, used in about 70% of commercial reactors, originated in US Navy submarine propulsion. Accidents at Three Mile Island in 1979, Chernobyl in 1986 and Fukushima in 2011 had major effects on the nuclear industry.

=== KEY: Nuclear_technology_e0161c
HEADWORD: Nuclear technology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Henri Becquerel | Marie Curie | Nuclear fission | Nuclear reactor | Nuclear weapon | Pierre Curie | Radium | Sun
ENTRY:
Technology involving the nuclear reactions of atomic nuclei, used in [[Nuclear reactor|reactors]], [[Nuclear weapon|weapons]] and nuclear medicine. It is also used in smoke detectors and gun sights. [[Henri Becquerel]] discovered radioactivity in 1896 while investigating uranium salts, and [[Marie Curie]] and [[Pierre Curie]] isolated [[Radium|radium]]. Early researchers suffered radiation burns, and many later died of cancer. Radium salts were used to make glowing dials, and radiation was recognized as ionizing, able to pose a severe long-term hazard even in small quantities. [[Nuclear fission]] splits a nucleus into roughly equal parts, releasing neutrons that can cause further fissions. A mass of fissile material large enough to sustain such a chain reaction is called a critical mass. Most terrestrial energy ultimately derives from nuclear processes, including fusion in the [[Sun]] and radioactive decay of uranium within Earth.

=== KEY: Nuclear_weapon_6da6dc
HEADWORD: Nuclear weapon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hiroshima | Manhattan Project | Nagasaki | Nuclear fission | Nuclear fusion | Tsar Bomba
ENTRY:
Explosive device powered by [[Nuclear fission|nuclear fission]], or by fission combined with [[Nuclear fusion|fusion]]. Most have yields of 100 to 1,000 kilotons of TNT, and yields have ranged from 50 megatons for the [[Tsar Bomba]] down to 10 tons. Effects include blast, heat, ionizing radiation, firestorms and radioactive fallout. The first weapons were developed by the United States with the United Kingdom and Canada in the [[Manhattan Project]] during World War II. Nuclear weapons have been used twice in war, against [[Hiroshima]] and [[Nagasaki]] in 1945, killing between 150,000 and 246,000 people. Nine states are known or believed to possess them. The global stockpile peaked at more than 64,000 weapons in 1986 and was around 9,600 as of 2025.

=== KEY: Nucleic_acid_4ea1f5
HEADWORD: Nucleic acid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Biomolecule | DNA | Francis Crick | Gene | James Watson | RNA
ENTRY:
Large [[Biomolecule|biomolecule]] made of nucleotides, essential in all cells and viruses. Each nucleotide consists of a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. The two main classes are [[DNA]], which contains deoxyribose, and [[RNA]], which contains ribose. The bases are adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine and uracil; thymine occurs only in DNA and uracil only in RNA. In DNA, the bases pair in a double helix, with adenine paired to thymine and guanine to cytosine. Sequences of DNA store coded instructions as [[Gene|genes]], and RNA sequences help build the proteins that determine most chemical processes of life. Nucleic acids occur in all life forms, including bacteria, archaea, mitochondria, chloroplasts and viruses. Friedrich Miescher discovered the substance in 1869, and [[Francis Crick]] and [[James Watson]] proposed the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953.

=== KEY: Nucleosynthesis_794bc8
HEADWORD: Nucleosynthesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Deuterium | Hydrogen | Iron | Lithium | Nickel | Supernova
ENTRY:
Process of creating new atomic nuclei from existing nucleons and nuclei. A few minutes after the [[Big Bang]], nuclei formed in Big Bang nucleosynthesis, leaving [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] and helium with traces of [[Lithium|lithium]] and [[Deuterium|deuterium]]. In stars, fusion of light elements in the core produces elements up to [[Iron|iron]] and [[Nickel|nickel]] in the most massive stars, while neutron capture by the r-process and s-process creates heavier elements. [[Supernova|Supernovae]] are largely responsible for elements from oxygen to rubidium, and neutron star mergers are a major source of r-process elements. Cosmic ray spallation fragments nuclei to produce lighter isotopes such as helium-3, beryllium-9 and boron isotopes. On Earth, radiogenesis from long-lived radionuclides such as uranium, thorium and potassium-40 also creates new nuclei.

=== KEY: Nuclide_ccb591
HEADWORD: Nuclide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical element | Half-life | Isotope | Neutron | Proton
ENTRY:
Class of atoms defined by the number of [[Proton|protons]] and [[Neutron|neutrons]] in the nucleus and by its energy state. The term was coined by Truman P. Kohman in 1947, in distinction from [[Isotope|isotope]]. Nuclides with equal proton numbers are isotopes of the same [[Chemical element|element]], while those with equal mass numbers are isobars, and those with equal neutron numbers are isotones. Nuclear isomers share proton and mass numbers but differ in excitation state, as with technetium-99m and technetium-99. The longest-lived non-ground-state isomer, tantalum-180m, has a [[Half-life|half-life]] in excess of 10¹⁷ years. Of the nuclides found in nature, 251 have never been observed to decay. Unstable nuclides are called radionuclides, and their decay products are radiogenic nuclides.

=== KEY: Nudity_8ba5e4
HEADWORD: Nudity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clothing | Roman Empire | Sauna
ENTRY:
State of a human being without [[Clothing|clothing]]. Estimates vary, but for the first 90,000 years of prehistory anatomically modern humans were naked. Indigenous peoples in warm climates used clothing for decoration or ceremony but were often nude. In the ancient Mediterranean, from Mesopotamia to the [[Roman Empire]], proper attire signaled social standing, while slaves might be naked. Through much of Western history, people of any status were unclothed for labor, athletics and bathing, usually segregated by sex. The association of nudity with sin spread through Europe from Abrahamic beliefs in the post-classical period. Japan and Finland maintain communal nudity in baths and [[Sauna|saunas]]. Most contemporary societies require clothing in public, though some accept non-sexual nudity for recreation, and norms differ for men and women. Nudity is also used as protest, and imposed as punishment.

=== KEY: Number_b2ee91
HEADWORD: Number
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hindu–Arabic numeral system | ISBN | Natural number | Negative number | Numeral system | Rational number | Real number | Square root
ENTRY:
Mathematical object used to count, measure, order, and label. The most basic examples are the [[Natural number|natural numbers]]: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and so forth. Numbers are represented in language by number words and by symbols called numerals; "eleven" is a number word and "11" the corresponding numeral. Because only a limited list of symbols can be memorized, a [[Numeral system|numeral system]] represents any number in an organized way. The most common is the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], a decimal system that displays any non-negative integer using ten digits. Numerals serve for counting, labeling (telephone numbers), ordering (serial numbers), and codes (as with [[ISBN|ISBNs]]).

Over the centuries the notion of number was extended to include zero, [[Negative number|negative numbers]], [[Rational number|rational numbers]] such as one half, [[Real number|real numbers]] such as the [[Square root|square root]] of 2 and pi (π), and complex numbers, which extend the reals with a square root of −1 (i). Calculations use the operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation; their study is called arithmetic, a term that may also refer to number theory, the study of the properties of numbers.

Bones with cut marks, possibly tallies, include the Lebombo bone (about 43,000 years old) and the Ishango bone (about 22,000 to 30,000 years old), though their interpretation as arithmetic artifacts is disputed. The earliest unambiguous numbers in the archaeological record are the Mesopotamian base 60 system (c. 3400 BC), with place value emerging in the 3rd millennium BC. The earliest known base 10 system dates to 3100 BC in Egypt. A Babylonian tablet of 1900–1600 BC gives 3.125 as an estimate of the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, possibly the oldest approximation of π. The Egyptians invented the first ciphered numeral system and the Greeks followed. Roman numerals dominated Europe until the Hindu–Arabic system spread around the late 14th century. Its key was the symbol for zero, developed by ancient Indian mathematicians around 500 AD.

The first known recorded use of zero as an integer dates to AD 628, in the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* of Brahmagupta, who treated 0 as a number and gave rules for its use with positive and negative numbers. By 130 AD Ptolemy used a symbol for zero within a sexagesimal system. The Maya developed zero as a cardinal number, and the concept reached Europe through Islamic sources around the year 1000. The abstract concept of negative numbers was recognized as early as 100–50 BC in China, in *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.

=== KEY: Number_theory_18c5c2
HEADWORD: Number theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Diophantus | Euclidean algorithm | Fermat's Last Theorem | Integer | Prime number | RSA cryptosystem | Riemann zeta function
ENTRY:
Branch of pure mathematics devoted primarily to the study of the [[Integer|integers]] and arithmetic functions. Number theorists study [[Prime number|prime numbers]], together with objects constructed from integers, such as rational numbers, or defined as generalizations of them, such as algebraic integers. Integers can be considered in themselves or as solutions to equations (Diophantine geometry). Analytic number theory studies objects such as the [[Riemann zeta function]] that encode properties of integers and primes, while Diophantine approximation studies how irrational numbers can be approximated by fractions. Elementary number theory uses elementary methods, and algebraic number theory employs structures such as fields and rings.

The subject is one of the oldest branches of mathematics, alongside geometry. Plimpton 322, a broken clay tablet dated c. 1800 BC, lists Pythagorean triples. Euclid devoted part of his *Elements* to primes and divisibility, giving the [[Euclidean algorithm]] and a proof implying the infinitude of primes. [[Diophantus]] of Alexandria, who probably lived in the 3rd century AD, wrote the *Arithmetica*, a collection of problems seeking rational solutions to polynomial equations.

Number theory deals with statements that are simple to understand but very difficult to solve. [[Fermat's Last Theorem]] was proved 358 years after its original formulation, and Goldbach's conjecture has remained unsolved since the 18th century. [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] remarked, "Mathematics is the queen of the sciences—and number theory is the queen of mathematics." The field was regarded as pure mathematics with no outside applications until the 1970s, when prime numbers became the basis of public-key cryptography algorithms such as the [[RSA cryptosystem]].

=== KEY: Numeral_system_085f8c
HEADWORD: Numeral system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brahmagupta | Decimal | Fibonacci | Hindu–Arabic numeral system | Roman numerals
ENTRY:
Notation for expressing numbers with digits or other symbols in a consistent manner. The same symbols can denote different numbers in different systems: "11" is eleven in [[Decimal|decimal]], three in binary and two in unary. Positional systems, such as the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], give a symbol a value that depends on its place, while sign-value systems such as [[Roman numerals]] combine fixed symbols. Roman, Greek and Egyptian numerals lack an official zero. The Babylonians used a base-60 system, and the Maya developed a base-20 system with a symbol for zero. The first widely acknowledged use of zero dates to 876, and [[Brahmagupta]] formalized its arithmetic in the 7th century. By the 13th century Western Arabic numerals were accepted in European mathematics, as [[Fibonacci]] used them in *Liber Abaci*, and the printing press in the 15th century helped standardize them.

=== KEY: Numerical_analysis_a41761
HEADWORD: Numerical analysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algorithm | Computer | Discrete mathematics | Gaussian elimination | Newton's method | Ordinary differential equation
ENTRY:
Study of [[Algorithm|algorithms]] for problems of continuous mathematics, using numerical approximation alongside symbolic manipulation. Its methods involve real or complex variables, in contrast to [[Discrete mathematics]]. The field applies to engineering and the physical sciences, and increasingly to economics, medicine and business. Examples include [[Ordinary differential equation|ordinary differential equations]] in celestial mechanics, numerical linear algebra in data analysis, and Markov chains for simulating living cells. Before modern computers, practitioners relied on hand interpolation formulas and printed tables; since the mid-20th century, [[Computer|computers]] calculate the required functions instead. Named methods include [[Newton's method]] and [[Gaussian elimination]]. A Babylonian tablet, YBC 7289, gives a sexagesimal approximation of the square root of 2.

=== KEY: Numerical_digit_bec0f7
HEADWORD: Numerical digit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Decimal | Hexadecimal | Hindu–Arabic numeral system | Positional notation
ENTRY:
Single symbol used to represent numbers in [[Positional notation|positional notation]], such as 0 to 9 in base 10. The name comes from the Latin *digiti*, meaning fingers. A system needs as many digits as its base: [[Decimal|decimal]] uses ten, binary two, and [[Hexadecimal|hexadecimal]] sixteen, usually 0 to 9 and A to F. Each digit represents an integer from zero up to one less than the base. A numeral's value is the sum of each digit multiplied by its place value, which is the base raised to a power that depends on the digit's position. In 10.34, for example, the 1 is worth 10 and the 4 is worth 4 × 10⁻², or 4/100. The first true written positional system is the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system|Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], established in India by the 7th century, before zero was widely accepted.

=== KEY: Numerical_integration_0ec0e1
HEADWORD: Numerical integration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Area | Error function | Integral
ENTRY:
Family of algorithms for calculating the numerical value of a definite [[Integral|integral]]. Also called numerical quadrature, the term is used mainly for one-dimensional integrals, while higher-dimensional integration is called cubature. Numerical integration is needed when the integrand is known only at sampled points, when no elementary antiderivative exists, as for the [[Error function|error function]], or when a numerical approximation is easier to compute than an antiderivative. The term first appears in 1915 in a book by David Gibb. Its roots lie in the ancient problem of quadrature, finding a square with the same [[Area|area]] as a given figure; [[Archimedes]] squared the surface of a sphere and a parabola segment. Common methods include the trapezoidal rule, Simpson's rule and Gaussian quadrature, which approximate the integral by summing weighted evaluations of the function.

=== KEY: Nutrition_cf6586

ENTRY:
Biochemical and physiological process by which an organism uses food and water to support its life. The intake of these substances provides nutrients, divided into macro- and micronutrients, which are metabolized to create energy and chemical structures; too much or too little of an essential nutrient can cause [[Malnutrition|malnutrition]]. Nutritional science typically emphasizes human nutrition.

The type of organism determines which nutrients it needs and how it obtains them: by consuming organic matter, consuming inorganic matter, absorbing light, or some combination. All forms of life require carbon, energy, and water. Animals require complex nutrients such as [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]], [[Lipid|lipids]], and [[Protein|proteins]], obtained by consuming other organisms. Humans developed [[Agriculture|agriculture]] and cooking to replace foraging. Plants acquire nutrients through the soil and atmosphere, and fungi absorb them through the mycelium.

Approximately 30 elements are found in organic matter, with nitrogen, carbon, and phosphorus the most important. Micronutrients, needed in trace amounts, are classified as vitamins (organic) and minerals (inorganic). Essential nutrients are those the body cannot synthesize. Over-nutrition of macronutrients is a major cause of [[Obesity|obesity]] and raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, stroke, hypertension, coronary heart disease, osteoporosis, and some cancers.

Scientific analysis of food began during the chemical revolution of the late 18th century, and modern nutrition science began in the 1910s as micronutrients were identified. Thiamine was the first vitamin chemically identified, in 1926, and in 1932 [[Vitamin C]] was identified as a protection against [[Scurvy|scurvy]]. The first recommended dietary allowances were developed to address fears of deficiency diseases during the Great Depression and the Second World War.

Capital and most populous city of [[Greenland]], an autonomous territory of Denmark (pop., 2025: 20,113). Founded in 1728 as the colony of Godthaab, it lies at the end of the Nuup Kangerlua [[Fjord|fjord]] on the [[Labrador Sea]]. Its latitude makes it the world's northernmost capital, a few kilometers north of [[Reykjavík]]. Nuuk is the Greenlandic word for "cape." Since home rule in 1979, Greenlandic names have been preferred, and Nuuk replaced Godthaab. A [[Smallpox|smallpox]] epidemic in 1733 and 1734 killed most of the native population.

ENTRY:
Family of synthetic [[Polymer|polymers]] characterized by [[Amide|amide]] linkages. Nylons are thermoplastics that can be melt-processed into fibers, films and molded shapes. [[DuPont]] began its nylon research in 1927, building on its experience with [[Rayon|rayon]]. Chemist Wallace Hume Carothers synthesized the first nylon, nylon 66, on February 28, 1935. DuPont announced nylon in 1938 and obtained a patent for the polymer that year. Some varieties have a [[Silk|silk]]-like appearance. During [[World War II]], nylon was used in [[Parachute|parachutes]] and tires. Today nylons are used for apparel, carpeting, rubber reinforcement, automotive and electrical parts, and food packaging films.

=== KEY: Nymph_biology_d5888d
HEADWORD: Nymph
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Insect | Invertebrate | Larva | Pupa | Spider | William Harvey
ENTRY:
Juvenile form of some [[Invertebrate|invertebrates]], especially [[Insect|insects]], resembling the adult without wings. Unlike a [[Larva|larva]], a nymph already has the adult's form, apart from lacking wings and not yet having developed genitalia. It undergoes gradual metamorphosis without a [[Pupa|pupal]] stage, and its final molt produces the adult. Many insect orders have nymphs, including Orthoptera, Hemiptera, mayflies, termites, cockroaches, mantises and dragonflies, as do arachnids such as [[Spider|spiders]], mites and ticks. Aquatic nymphs are called naiads. In 1628 English physician [[William Harvey]] hypothesized that pupal forms came from imperfect eggs, an outdated idea that still contributed to understanding nymphs. Nymph imitations account for over half of the fly-fishing patterns regularly used in the United States.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Minor female nature deity in ancient [[Greek mythology|Greek]] folklore. Nymphs were typically tied to a specific place, landform or tree and depicted as maidens. Because of their association with springs, they were often seen as having healing powers. Their other divine powers included [[Divination|divination]] and shapeshifting, and though divine, they were not immortal. Subgroups included the Dryads of [[Oak|oak]] trees, the Naiads of springs and the Nereids of the sea. Often attendants of goddesses such as [[Artemis]], they appear in myths of love with heroes and deities. Since the [[Middle Ages]], they have sometimes been confused with fairies.

=== KEY: Nzinga_of_Ndongo_and_Matamba_c3c401
HEADWORD: Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Angola | Luanda | Military tactics | Portuguese Empire
ENTRY:
Ambundu queen of Ndongo and Matamba in present-day northern [[Angola]]. Born into Ndongo's ruling family, she was trained in military and political affairs and served as an ambassador to the [[Portuguese Empire]]. She took power in 1624, during rapid growth of the slave trade and Portuguese encroachment. After Portugal declared war in 1626 she went into exile, conquered Matamba from 1631 to 1635, and in 1641 allied with the Dutch West India Company, reclaiming much of Ndongo. Portugal retook [[Luanda]] in 1648, and a peace treaty was signed in 1656. Late in life she promoted Catholicism and aligned closely with Portugal, which granted her a monopoly of slave exports in exchange for trade and a military alliance. She is remembered for her diplomacy and [[Military tactics|military tactics]].

=== KEY: OECD_bdbed4
HEADWORD: OECD
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Paris | World War II
ENTRY:
Intergovernmental economic organization of 38 member countries, founded in 1961. It was created to stimulate economic progress and world trade, and its members describe themselves as committed to democracy and the market economy. The OECD is headquartered at the Château de la Muette in [[Paris]] and funded by member contributions. It succeeded the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation, which had distributed American aid for reconstruction after [[World War II]]. Japan joined in 1964. As of 2026 its members had a combined population of 1.38 billion and produced 58.4% of global nominal GDP. The OECD publishes annual economic reports, evaluations and rankings. Critics argue that it has shifted from growth-oriented policy toward regulatory harmonization that burdens smaller economies.
