Capital and special municipality of [[Taiwan]] (pop., March 2023: 2,494,813). Taipei lies in northern Taiwan on the Taipei Basin, an ancient lakebed. === KEY: Taiping_Rebellion_f8bb68

ENTRY:
Civil war in late imperial China (1850–1864) between the [[Qing dynasty]] and the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. Led by [[Hong Xiuquan]], an ethnic Hakka who proclaimed himself the younger brother of [[Jesus|Jesus Christ]], the movement sought to convert the Han people to a syncretic Christianity and to overthrow the Qing. === KEY: Talcott_Parsons_233db3

ENTRY:
American sociologist of social action theory and structural functionalism. He taught at [[Harvard University]] from 1927 to 1973 and was one of the first professors in its new sociology department in 1930. Parsons translated [[Max Weber]]'s work and analyzed the writings of Weber, [[Émile Durkheim]] and [[Vilfredo Pareto]]. His social action theory, based on empirical data, is described as the first broad, systematic and generalizable theory of social systems developed in the United States and Europe. Although generally considered a structural functionalist, in 1975 he argued that those terms did not describe his theory. From the 1970s, a new generation of sociologists criticized his theories as socially conservative and his writing as overly complex. He was elected president of the American Sociological Association in 1949 and promoted the professionalization of sociology.

=== KEY: Tang_dynasty_f72ec3
HEADWORD: Tang dynasty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: An Lushan rebellion | Du Fu | Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period | Li Bai | Silk Road | Sui dynasty | Wu Zetian | Xi'an
ENTRY:
Imperial dynasty of China that ruled from 618 to 907, with an interregnum between 690 and 705.

The Tang followed the [[Sui dynasty]] and preceded the [[Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period]]. Historians generally regard it as a high point of Chinese civilization and a golden age of cosmopolitan culture. Li Yuan, who became Emperor Gaozu, founded it after rebelling against the declining Sui; he declared himself emperor on June 18, 618, on news of the murder of Emperor Yang of Sui. Under early rulers such as Emperor Taizong (r. 626–649), military campaigns extended Tang territory beyond that of the Han dynasty, and the dynasty contested nomadic powers for control of Inner Asia and the trade routes of the [[Silk Road]]. The Tang exerted strong cultural influence on Japan and Korea.

The interregnum began in 690, when [[Wu Zetian]] seized the throne and proclaimed the Wu Zhou dynasty, becoming the only legitimate Chinese female emperor. The [[An Lushan rebellion]] (755–763) devastated the empire and weakened central authority. In the 9th century the rise of regional military governors, the *jiedushi*, undermined civil order, and agrarian rebellions caused mass population loss, displacement, and poverty. Zhu Wen overthrew the dynasty in 907.

The capital Chang'an (present-day [[Xi'an]]) was the world's most populous city for much of the dynasty. Two censuses of the 7th and 8th centuries estimated the empire's population at about 50 million, growing to an estimated 80 million by the end.

The era is traditionally considered the greatest age of Chinese poetry; [[Li Bai]] and [[Du Fu]] lived then, with Wang Wei, contributed to the *Three Hundred Tang Poems*. Woodblock printing developed. Buddhism became a major influence, but in the 840s Emperor Wuzong enacted policies to suppress it.

=== KEY: Taoism_31ae79
HEADWORD: Taoism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buddhism | Confucianism | Han dynasty | Laozi | Tao Te Ching | Warring States period
ENTRY:
Philosophical and religious tradition indigenous to China, emphasizing harmony with the Tao (道); also spelled Daoism.

Translations of *Tao* include "way," "road," "path," and "technique"; in the Taoist sense it is generally understood as an enigmatic process of transformation underlying reality. A common goal of practice is self-cultivation, a more harmonious existence in accord with nature. Taoist ethics emphasize the three treasures of compassion, humility, and frugality, along with effortless action (*wu wei*), naturalness, and simplicity.

The core of Taoist thought crystallized during the early [[Warring States period]] (c. 450–c. 300 BC), when the [[Tao Te Ching]], traditionally credited to [[Laozi]], and the *Zhuangzi* were largely compiled. These are widely regarded as the fundamental texts of Taoist philosophy. Monks assembled the growing body of writings into the *Daozang* canon starting in the 5th century. Early Taoism drew on diverse influences, including [[Confucianism]] and Legalist theories. Chinese usage distinguishes *Daojia*, the philosophical tradition of introspective self-cultivation, from *Daojiao*, religious Taoism with its own scriptures, priestly lineages, and rituals, long intertwined with Chinese folk religion so that the boundary is often blurred. Taoist secret societies precipitated the Yellow Turban Rebellion in the late [[Han dynasty]].

Taoism is often compared with Confucianism, and both combined with [[Buddhism]] after its introduction to China, giving rise by the 6th century to the discourse of the Three Teachings.

Today Taoism is one of five religious doctrines officially recognized by the Chinese government, has official status in Hong Kong and Macau, and is a major religion in Taiwan. It also has adherents throughout Southeast Asia, and in the West has taken forms ranging from historical practice to synthesized new religious movements.

It is the only lepton with enough mass to decay into [[Hadron|hadrons]]. Its partner is the [[Neutrino|tau neutrino]]. Tau particles were detected between 1974 and 1977 at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center through anomalous collision events, and Martin Lewis Perl, whose group made the detection, shared the 1995 Nobel Prize in Physics. The symbol τ comes from the Greek word for "third", as it was the third charged lepton discovered.

=== KEY: Tax_4b78ac
HEADWORD: Tax
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Augustus | Roman Republic
ENTRY:
Mandatory financial charge imposed on an individual or legal entity by a governmental organization to support public expenditure or to regulate economic activity.

Taxation transfers wealth from households and businesses to the government. Its effect on economic growth and welfare can be increased (the fiscal multiplier) or decreased (the excess burden of taxation). Critics of high tax burdens argue that they reduce incentives for work, investment, and innovation; supporters of progressive structures argue that they mitigate inequality, while opponents say such structures undermine merit-based incentives. Direct taxes fall on income and wealth, and indirect taxes include value-added and sales taxes. Global tax revenues average around 17% of GDP, while those of OECD members reach 33.9% as of 2023.

The first known system of taxation was in [[Ancient Egypt]] around 3000–2800 BC. The earliest and most widespread forms were the corvée, forced labor provided by peasants too poor to pay otherwise, and the tithe. The Rosetta Stone is itself a decree of Ptolemy V containing tax concessions. In the [[Roman Republic]], taxes of between 1% and 3% of assessed property value were auctioned annually to tax farmers called *publicani*, who paid the revenue in advance and kept what they collected. [[Augustus]] replaced this with a direct system under which each province paid 1% tax on wealth and a flat rate on each adult.

In the 17th century taxation may have reached 15–20% of production in places such as France, the Netherlands, and Scandinavia. Rates rose dramatically in Europe in the 18th and early 19th centuries as war became more expensive and governments more centralized, most sharply in England.

=== KEY: Taxonomy_30d108

ENTRY:

=== KEY: Taxonomy_biology_4e1478
HEADWORD: Taxonomy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Binomial nomenclature | Biodiversity | Carl Linnaeus | Taxonomic rank
ENTRY:
Science of naming, defining, and classifying groups of biological organisms on the basis of shared characteristics. The term, from Ancient Greek *taxis* ('arrangement') and *-nomia* ('method'), was introduced in 1813 by de Candolle in his *Théorie élémentaire de la botanique*. Modern approaches prioritize common ancestry and evolutionary relationships.

Organisms are grouped into taxa (singular: taxon), and each group is given a [[Taxonomic rank|taxonomic rank]]; groups of a given rank can be aggregated into a more inclusive group of higher rank, creating a taxonomic hierarchy. The principal ranks in modern use are domain, kingdom, phylum (division is sometimes used in botany instead), class, order, family, genus, and species. The Swedish botanist [[Carl Linnaeus]] is regarded as the founder of the current system. His *Systema Naturae* (1735) introduced [[Binomial nomenclature|binomial nomenclature]], naming organisms by genus and species.

The Linnaean system has since been transformed into a classification intended to reflect the evolutionary relationships among living and extinct organisms. Contemporary taxonomy emphasizes cladistics, which groups organisms into monophyletic clades based on shared derived characters and common ancestry; debate continues over nomenclatural stability versus strict monophyly.

Definitions of the field vary: some place taxonomy within systematics, others reverse the relationship, and sources disagree on whether nomenclature belongs to it. Europeans tend to use "systematics" for the study of [[Biodiversity|biodiversity]], and North Americans "taxonomy". Taxonomy underpins conservation, medicine, and ecology.

=== KEY: Taylor_series_3bbd6c
HEADWORD: Taylor series
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Derivative | Polynomial
ENTRY:
Infinite sum of terms expressed through a function's [[Derivative|derivatives]] at a single point. For most common functions, the function and its Taylor series are equal near that point. Brook Taylor introduced the series in 1715; when the point is 0, it is called a Maclaurin series, after Colin Maclaurin, who made extensive use of this case in the 18th century. The first n + 1 terms form the nth Taylor polynomial, a [[Polynomial|polynomial]] of degree n that generally becomes more accurate as n increases. Taylor's theorem estimates the error of such approximations. A function is analytic at a point if it equals the sum of its Taylor series on an open interval containing that point. Taylor series can be differentiated and integrated term by term, and they combine algebraically through sums, products and compositions. Standard Maclaurin series serve as starting points for computing others.

=== KEY: Technology_e7e767
HEADWORD: Technology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Automation | Internet | Neolithic Revolution | Printing press
ENTRY:
Application of conceptual knowledge to achieve practical goals, especially in a reproducible way. The word also denotes the products of such efforts, both tangible, such as tools and machines, and intangible, such as software. Technology plays a critical role in science, engineering, and everyday life.

The term dates to the early 17th century, when it meant "systematic treatment"; it derives from Greek *tekhnē* ('craft, art') and *-logia* ('study, knowledge'). From the 19th century, continental Europeans used *Technik* (German) or *technique* (French) for a "way of doing" and *Technologie* for the academic study of the "methods of arts and crafts"; English translated both as technology. In the 20th century, with scientific progress and the Second Industrial Revolution, it came to mean the systematic use of knowledge to practical ends.

The earliest known technology is the stone tool. Hominids learned to make the first stone tools about 2 mya (million years ago) by hammering flakes off a pebble, and the practice was refined 75 kya (thousand years ago) into pressure flaking. Evidence points to continuous human use of fire at least 1.5 mya; Charles Darwin described its discovery as "possibly the greatest ever made by man". Fire allowed cooking, which improved nutrient value. The cooking hypothesis holds that cooking promoted growth in hominid brain size, though some researchers find the evidence inconclusive. Hearths dated to 790 kya may have intensified socialization and contributed to the emergence of language. Evidence of clothing dates to 90–120 kya and of shelter to 450 kya.

The [[Neolithic Revolution]] accelerated innovation and social complexity. Polished stone axes allowed large-scale forest clearance and farming, and agriculture fed larger populations. Increased labor specialization, trade, war, and the need for collective action such as irrigation are thought to have contributed to the first cities, such as Uruk, and the first civilizations, such as Sumer. Writing became the basis for history, libraries, schools, and scientific research. Furnaces and bellows enabled smelting of metals; native copper was probably used from about 10 kya, alloys such as bronze and brass appeared about 4,000 BC, and iron alloys such as steel date to around 1,800 BC.

The earliest record of a ship under sail is a Nile boat dating to around 7,000 BC. The Sumerians in Mesopotamia diverted the Tigris and Euphrates by canals and levees for irrigation. The wheel was invented independently in Mesopotamia, the Northern Caucasus, and Central Europe; estimates range from 5,500 to 3,000 BC, with most experts favoring closer to 4,000 BC. Drawings of wheeled carts date from about 3,500 BC.

Later inventions, including the [[Printing press|printing press]], telephone, and the [[Internet]], lowered barriers to communication and ushered in the knowledge economy. Technology contributes to economic development and prosperity but can also cause pollution, resource depletion, and technological unemployment resulting from [[Automation|automation]]. Philosophical and political debates continue over its role, its ethics, and ways to mitigate its downsides.

Traditionally worn by adult [[Jews|Jewish]] men on weekday mornings during prayer, the arm and head tefillin are bound to the arm and placed on the forehead. In [[Orthodox Judaism|Orthodox]] communities only men wear them, and [[Halakha|Jewish law]] generally exempts women. Tefillin fulfill Torah instructions to keep a sign of the Exodus from [[Egypt]]. Archaeological finds at Qumran show that Jews wore some form of tefillin by the 1st century CE, and the [[New Testament]] also refers to them.

Capital and largest city of [[Honduras]], with its sister city Comayagüela. Spanish settlers claimed the site in 1578. It became the national capital in 1880, when President Marco Aurelio Soto moved the seat of government from Comayagua. The two sister cities form a Central District that serves as the permanent national capital, and the city hosts 25 foreign embassies and the country's most important public university. Its infrastructure has not kept pace with population growth, and roads struggle to handle more than 400,000 vehicles. The economy relies on government services, [[Commerce|commerce]], light manufacturing such as [[Textile|textiles]] and food processing, and remittances from abroad. Gang activity and [[Organized crime|organized crime]] have driven high homicide rates.

Capital and largest city of [[Iran]], at the southern foothills of the Alborz Mountains. Tehran has about 9 million residents in the city and 16.8 million in its metropolitan area, making it the most populous city in Iran and [[West Asia]]. Agha Mohammad Khan of the Qajar dynasty made it the capital in 1786, partly for its proximity to the [[Caucasus]]. Under Reza Shah it gained Iran's first institute of higher learning, bank and railway line. The city was central to the [[Iranian Revolution]] and suffered bombing during the [[Iran–Iraq War]]. Its Golestan Palace is a [[World Heritage Site]], and the Milad Tower is the world's sixth-tallest self-supporting tower. Air pollution, earthquakes and water scarcity have prompted plans to relocate the capital, though none has been approved.

Mediterranean coastal city in central [[Israel]], the economic and technological center of the country. Officially Tel Aviv-Yafo, it is the most populous city in the Gush Dan metropolitan area and home to most of Israel's foreign embassies. The city was founded in 1909 as *Ahuzat Bayit*, a Jewish housing estate on the outskirts of the ancient port of Jaffa, then part of the [[Ottoman Empire]]. Its name was taken from [[Theodor Herzl]]'s 1902 novel *Altneuland*. In 1948 Israel's Declaration of Independence was proclaimed in the city. Its White City, with the world's largest concentration of International Style buildings, including [[Bauhaus]] architecture, has been a [[World Heritage Site|UNESCO World Heritage Site]] since 2003. Tel Aviv University, the country's largest, has more than 30,000 students.

State in southern [[India]] on the Deccan Plateau. Formed in 2014 after decades of protest, it corresponds to the Telugu-speaking regions of the former princely state of [[Hyderabad]], which is its capital and largest city and a major center for information technology. Telugu is the main official language, and Urdu has second official status. Earlier rulers included the [[Satavahana dynasty]], which traded as far as the [[Roman Empire]], and the Kakatiyas, whose Ramappa Temple is a [[World Heritage Site|UNESCO World Heritage Site]]. The state is an important base for Indian defense and research laboratories.
