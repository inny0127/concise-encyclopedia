=== KEY: Electrical_resistance_and_conductance_3293c9
HEADWORD: Electrical resistance and conductance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Copper | Diode | Incandescent light bulb | Ohm | Ohm's law | Siemens (unit) | Transformer
ENTRY:
Measure of an object's opposition to electric current, and its reciprocal, conductance. Resistance R is the ratio of the voltage across an object to the current through it, and conductance G is the reciprocal, G = 1/R. The SI unit of resistance is the [[Ohm|ohm]] (Ω), and conductance is measured in [[Siemens (unit)|siemens]] (S). Resistance depends largely on material: insulators such as rubber have very high resistance, while metals such as [[Copper|copper]] have very low resistance. It also depends on shape, since a long, thin wire has higher resistance than a short, thick one. Superconductors have zero resistance. For ohmic materials, voltage and current are directly proportional, a relation known as [[Ohm's law]]. In devices such as a [[Diode|diode]], [[Transformer|transformer]] or [[Incandescent light bulb|incandescent light bulb]], they are not proportional, so the ratio V/I (chordal or static resistance) or the derivative dV/dI (differential resistance) may be more useful. In metals, resistance typically rises linearly with temperature; in semiconductors it often falls.

=== KEY: Electrical_resistivity_and_conductivity_667d51
HEADWORD: Electrical resistivity and conductivity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Copper | Electrical resistance and conductance
ENTRY:
Intrinsic properties of a material that measure its ability to resist or conduct electric current; each is the reciprocal of the other. Resistivity, commonly denoted ρ (rho), is measured in ohm-meters (Ω⋅m). Conductivity, commonly denoted σ (sigma), is measured in siemens per meter (S/m). Unlike [[Electrical resistance and conductance|resistance]], resistivity does not depend on an object's size or shape. [[Copper|Copper]] has a resistivity of about 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω⋅m at 20 °C, while glass has a resistivity on the order of 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁴ Ω⋅m.

=== KEY: Electrical_wiring_4f4da8
HEADWORD: Electrical wiring
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Switch
ENTRY:
Installation of cabling and associated devices, such as [[Switch|switches]], distribution boards, outlets and light fittings, in a building or other structure. Wire and cable types and sizes are specified by circuit voltage and current capacity, and by environmental conditions such as temperature, moisture, sunlight and chemicals. The International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) sets international wire sizes in IEC 60228, while North America uses the American Wire Gauge. Common conductors are copper or aluminum. In the United States, installation is governed by the National Electrical Code (NFPA 70). Wiring codes, including wire colors, vary by country, and the IEC is working to harmonize them. Insulation prevents short circuits.

=== KEY: Electricity_656e43
HEADWORD: Electricity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Benjamin Franklin | Electric charge | Electric current | Electromagnetism | Maxwell's equations | Michael Faraday | Nikola Tesla
ENTRY:
Set of physical phenomena associated with the presence and motion of matter possessing an electric charge. Electricity is related to magnetism, both being parts of [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]], as described by [[Maxwell's equations]]. Common phenomena include lightning, static electricity, electric heating, and electric discharges.

A positive or negative [[Electric charge|electric charge]] produces an electric field. The motion of charge carriers is an [[Electric current|electric current]] and produces a magnetic field. In most applications, Coulomb's law determines the force on a charged particle. Electric potential is the work done to move a charge within an electric field, typically measured in volts. Electricity serves in electric power and in electronics, which deals with circuits involving active components such as vacuum tubes, transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits. It is integral to transport, heating and cooling, lighting, communications, and computation.

People were aware of shocks from electric fish long before any knowledge of electricity. Ancient Egyptian texts dating from 2750 BC described them as the "protectors" of all other fish. Thales of Miletus made observations on static electricity around 600 BC, noting that rubbed amber attracts light objects. In 1600 the English scientist William Gilbert wrote *De Magnete*, distinguishing the lodestone effect from static electricity produced by rubbing amber, and coined the Neo-Latin word *electricus*, from the Greek *elektron*, "amber." The words "electric" and "electricity" first appeared in print in Thomas Browne's *Pseudodoxia Epidemica* of 1646.

[[Benjamin Franklin]] conducted extensive research in the 18th century; in June 1752 he is reputed to have flown a kite in a storm-threatened sky, and sparks jumping from a metal key showed that lightning was electrical. In 1791 Luigi Galvani published his discovery that electricity was the medium by which neurons passed signals to muscles. [[Alessandro Volta]]'s voltaic pile of 1800 gave scientists a more reliable source of electrical energy than electrostatic machines. Hans Christian Ørsted and André-Marie Ampère recognized the unity of electric and magnetic phenomena in 1819–1820. [[Michael Faraday]] invented the electric motor in 1821, and Georg Ohm mathematically analyzed the electrical circuit in 1827. James Clerk Maxwell definitively linked electricity, magnetism, and light in 1861 and 1862.

The late 19th century brought the greatest progress in electrical engineering, through figures including Alexander Graham Bell, Thomas Edison, [[Nikola Tesla]], and George Westinghouse. This expansion drove the Second Industrial Revolution. In 1905 Albert Einstein explained the photoelectric effect as light energy carried in discrete quantized packets. John Bardeen and Walter Houser Brattain invented the first working transistor, a germanium-based point-contact device, at Bell Labs in 1947.

=== KEY: Electricity_generation_510c26
HEADWORD: Electricity generation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alternating current | Coal | Michael Faraday | Steam engine
ENTRY:
Process of generating electric power from primary energy sources, usually at a power station. Most electricity is produced by electromechanical generators driven by heat engines fueled by combustion or nuclear fission, or by the kinetic energy of flowing water and wind. The basic principles were discovered in the 1820s and early 1830s by [[Michael Faraday]], who showed that a loop of wire moving between the poles of a magnet generates electricity. Commercial production began when dynamos were coupled to hydraulic turbines, and central stations became practical with [[Alternating current|alternating current]] transmission. In 1882 a [[Steam engine|steam engine]] driving a dynamo at Pearl Street Station in New York began supplying direct current for public lighting. Global output exceeded 30,000 terawatt-hours in 2024, with fossil fuels such as [[Coal|coal]] and natural gas still comprising around 60% of generation. Low-carbon sources accounted for 80% of the 1,200 terawatt-hour growth in 2024.

=== KEY: Electrocardiography_e46796
HEADWORD: Electrocardiography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arrhythmia | Atrial fibrillation | Heart | Myocardial infarction
ENTRY:
Recording of the [[Heart|heart]]'s electrical activity using electrodes placed on the skin, producing a graph of voltage over time called an electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG). Each heartbeat produces a P wave, representing atrial depolarization; a QRS complex, representing ventricular depolarization; and a T wave, representing ventricular repolarization. A conventional 12-lead ECG uses ten electrodes and records the heart's electrical potential from twelve angles, usually over ten seconds, with the patient lying down. Augustus Waller recorded the first human ECG in 1887, and Willem Einthoven introduced the modern ECG in 1902. The standardized 12-lead system was in use by 1954. An ECG can reveal [[Arrhythmia|arrhythmias]] such as [[Atrial fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]], [[Myocardial infarction|myocardial infarction]] and electrolyte disturbances, and it is used to monitor pacemaker function and assess risk before surgery.

=== KEY: Electrochemistry_cf93d1
HEADWORD: Electrochemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Electric battery | Electrolysis | Luigi Galvani | Michael Faraday | Redox
ENTRY:
Branch of physical chemistry concerned with the relationship between electrical potential difference and chemical change. Electrochemical reactions involve electrons moving through an external circuit between electrodes separated by an ionically conducting electrolyte, rather than transferring directly between atoms or molecules. Electrolytic cells use external electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous reactions, as in [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]], while galvanic cells, such as [[Electric battery|batteries]], harness spontaneous [[Redox|redox]] reactions to generate electricity. The field began in the late 18th century with the work of [[Luigi Galvani]] on muscle contraction. [[Alessandro Volta]] developed the voltaic pile in 1800, the first device to produce a steady current from chemical reactions. [[Michael Faraday]] formulated the laws of electrolysis in the 1830s. Applications include rechargeable lithium-ion batteries, corrosion prevention, electroplating and fuel cells.

=== KEY: Electrolysis_c26a21
HEADWORD: Electrolysis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alessandro Volta | Chlorine | Copper | Humphry Davy | Hydrogen | Michael Faraday | Oxygen | Sodium hydroxide
ENTRY:
Technique that uses direct electric current to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction, typically decomposing an electrolyte into simpler substances. The reaction takes place in an electrolytic cell, with oxidation at the anode and reduction at the cathode. Its discovery followed the invention of the voltaic pile by [[Alessandro Volta]] in 1800, when William Nicholson and Anthony Carlisle decomposed water into [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] and [[Oxygen|oxygen]] gases. [[Michael Faraday]] introduced the term in 1834 and formulated the quantitative laws of electrolysis in 1833. [[Humphry Davy]] isolated several alkali and alkaline earth metals by electrolyzing molten salts. Industrial uses include the chlor-alkali process, which produces [[Chlorine|chlorine]] and [[Sodium hydroxide|sodium hydroxide]]; the Hall-Héroult process for aluminum extraction from bauxite; and electrorefining of [[Copper|copper]]. Water electrolysis is increasingly used to produce hydrogen for steelmaking and transportation, though cost-effective efficiency at scale remains a challenge.

=== KEY: Electromagnet_0c1692
HEADWORD: Electromagnet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Copper | Electric motor | Hans Christian Ørsted | Iron | Loudspeaker | Magnet | Relay
ENTRY:
Magnet in which the magnetic field is produced by an electric current, usually flowing through a coil of [[Copper|copper]] wire. The coil is often wound around a ferromagnetic core such as [[Iron|iron]], which concentrates the magnetic flux and strengthens the field by a factor equal to the core's relative permeability, often 200–5,000 for soft iron. Unlike a [[Magnet|permanent magnet]], an electromagnet's field can be changed quickly by adjusting the current and switched off entirely, but it requires a continuous current to maintain it. [[Hans Christian Ørsted]] showed in 1820 that electric currents produce magnetic fields. In the early 1830s Joseph Henry built practical electromagnets with insulated wire that allowed multiple coil layers around an iron core; one design lifted over 2,000 pounds. Electromagnets are used in [[Electric motor|motors]], [[Relay|relays]], [[Loudspeaker|loudspeakers]] and MRI machines, and in industry to lift scrap iron.

=== KEY: Electromagnetic_field_958ded
HEADWORD: Electromagnetic field
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetic radiation | James Clerk Maxwell | Lorentz force | Maxwell's equations | Photoelectric effect | Quantum electrodynamics
ENTRY:
Physical field, varying in space and time, made up of electric and magnetic fields produced by, and acting on, electric charges. A disturbance in one field can create a disturbance in the other, producing an oscillation that propagates through space as an [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic wave]]. [[Maxwell's equations]] describe how charges and currents produce the fields, and the [[Lorentz force]] gives the force on a moving charge. Classical electrodynamics describes the field and many macroscopic phenomena, but it cannot explain the [[Photoelectric effect|photoelectric effect]]. Explaining such atomic-scale effects required quantization of the field and [[Quantum electrodynamics|quantum electrodynamics]]. [[James Clerk Maxwell]] unified the theory in 1865, showing that electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light.

=== KEY: Electromagnetic_induction_6966c8
HEADWORD: Electromagnetic induction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric generator | Electric motor | Faraday's law of induction | Inductor | James Clerk Maxwell | Michael Faraday | Transformer
ENTRY:
Production of an electromotive force (emf) across an electrical conductor in a changing magnetic field, which can drive an electric current in a closed circuit. [[Michael Faraday]] demonstrated it in 1831 at the Royal Institution in London, first by winding a copper coil on an iron ring, and then by rotating a copper disc between the poles of a horseshoe magnet, the precursor of the [[Electric generator|electric generator]]. Joseph Henry discovered similar effects around 1832. [[James Clerk Maxwell]] described the process mathematically as [[Faraday's law of induction|Faraday's law]], and Lenz's law gives the direction of the induced field. The effect underlies [[Inductor|inductors]], [[Transformer|transformers]], [[Electric motor|motors]] and generators.

=== KEY: Electromagnetic_radiation_76dfda
HEADWORD: Electromagnetic radiation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Maxwell's equations | Wave–particle duality
ENTRY:
Self-propagating wave of the electromagnetic field that carries momentum and radiant energy through space. It is classified by frequency, which is inversely proportional to wavelength, in a spectrum running from radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, to gamma rays. All forms travel at the speed of light in a vacuum and exhibit [[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]], behaving both as waves and as discrete particles called photons.

The radiation is produced by accelerating charged particles and may be emitted naturally, as by the Sun, or generated artificially. Its energy needs no propagating medium. Its interaction with matter depends on wavelength, which shapes its uses. Radio waves enable broadcasting and wireless communication, infrared is used in thermal imaging, and visible light is essential for vision. X-rays and gamma rays are applied in medical imaging, cancer treatment, and industrial inspection, and radiation in the ultraviolet and above has been associated with negative health effects.

Electric and magnetic fields obey superposition, so light waves can interfere constructively or destructively. In homogeneous, isotropic media the radiation is a transverse wave: the electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, as [[Maxwell's equations]] require. The waves can be polarized, reflected, refracted, or diffracted. Composite light disperses into a visible spectrum when passing through a prism, because the refractive index depends on wavelength.

In quantum mechanics, the radiation consists of photons, uncharged elementary particles with zero rest mass that are the quanta of the electromagnetic field. Wave characteristics are more apparent over large timescales and distances, particle characteristics over small ones; the self-interference of a single photon displays both. Quantum electrodynamics is the theory of how the radiation interacts with matter on an atomic level; further sources include electron transitions to lower energy levels and black-body radiation.

=== KEY: Electromagnetic_spectrum_062afd
HEADWORD: Electromagnetic spectrum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gamma ray | Infrared | Light | Microwave | Radio wave | Ultraviolet | X-ray
ENTRY:
Full range of electromagnetic radiation, organized by frequency or wavelength. From low to high frequency, the bands are [[Radio wave|radio waves]], [[Microwave|microwaves]], [[Infrared|infrared]], [[Light|visible light]], [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]], [[X-ray|X-rays]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]. Radio waves have the longest wavelengths, thousands of kilometers or more, and are used for communication and broadcasting. Microwaves penetrate clouds and are used in radar and satellite transmissions. Infrared radiation is associated with thermal emission and is detected as heat. Gamma rays, X-rays and extreme ultraviolet are ionizing, because their photons can ionize atoms, whereas visible light is non-ionizing. All electromagnetic waves travel at the speed of light in a vacuum. Spectroscopy, which measures intensity as a function of frequency, is used to study the interaction of electromagnetic waves with matter.

=== KEY: Electromagnetism_5290ab
HEADWORD: Electromagnetism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electric charge | Electricity | Electromagnetic radiation | Maxwell's equations
ENTRY:
Interaction between particles with [[Electric charge|electric charge]], mediated by electromagnetic fields. The electromagnetic force is one of the four fundamental forces of nature and the dominant force in the interactions of atoms and molecules. It relates [[Electricity|electricity]], magnetism, and optics, studied in the subdisciplines of electrostatics, magnetostatics, and electrodynamics. It is the second strongest of the four known fundamental forces and has unlimited range; at high energy it unifies with the weak force as the electroweak interaction.

The electrostatic attraction between atomic nuclei and their electrons holds atoms together, and electric forces allow atoms to combine into molecules. The force underlies electrical energy production and distribution, fiber optic and wireless communication, sensors, computation, electrolysis, electroplating, and mechanical motors and actuators.

It has been studied since ancient times to explain lightning, static electricity, and the attraction between magnetized pieces of iron ore and compasses. In the 18th and 19th centuries scientists developed mathematical relationships between these phenomena and electric charges and currents, and showed that they could create self-sustaining electromagnetic waves, which form visible light and other [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]].

Mathematically, the phenomenon is described by electromagnetic fields. A disturbance in the electric field can create one in the magnetic field, which in turn affects the electric field, producing an oscillation that propagates through space as an electromagnetic wave. [[Maxwell's equations]] encapsulate these relations.

The requirement that observations stay consistent across moving frames of reference, and the fixing of the speed of light by the permeability and permittivity of the medium, helped inspire Einstein's theory of special relativity in 1905. Quantum electrodynamics modifies Maxwell's equations to be consistent with the quantized nature of matter, expressing changes in the field as discrete excitations called photons.

=== KEY: Electromotive_force_473be5
HEADWORD: Electromotive force
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetic induction | Solar cell | Volt
ENTRY:
Energy transferred to an electric circuit per unit of electric charge, measured in [[Volt|volts]] and denoted ℰ. Devices that convert other forms of energy into electrical energy produce an emf, including batteries, which convert chemical energy, generators, which convert mechanical energy, and [[Solar cell|solar cells]]. Despite the name, emf is not a physical force, and ISO/IEC standards have deprecated the term in favor of source voltage. The emf of a two-terminal device can be measured as its open-circuit voltage. Real sources have internal resistance, so their terminal voltage falls below the ideal emf as current flows. In [[Electromagnetic induction|electromagnetic induction]], the emf around a closed loop is the work done on a unit charge traveling once around the loop.

=== KEY: Electron_0df2da
HEADWORD: Electron
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Pauli exclusion principle | Positron | Robert Millikan | Subatomic particle
ENTRY:
[[Subatomic particle]] whose electric charge is negative one elementary charge. It is an elementary particle contained in the matter that makes up the universe. Its charge is −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs and its rest mass approximately 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg, about 1/1836 that of a proton. In atoms, an electron's matter wave occupies orbitals around the positively charged nucleus, and the configuration of an atom's electrons determines its chemical properties. The outermost, or valence, electrons are the least tightly bound and form chemical bonds between atoms.

Electrons belong to the first generation of the lepton family, elementary particles that do not interact with the strong nuclear force, and have no known substructure. Their intrinsic angular momentum (spin) is ħ/2. As fermions, no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state, according to the [[Pauli exclusion principle]]. They show properties of both particles and waves, and can be diffracted like light. Their antiparticle, the [[Positron|positron]], is identical except for opposite charge; when the two collide they can annihilate, producing gamma ray photons.

In metals the outermost electrons are delocalized and move freely, accounting for high electrical and thermal conductivity. In semiconductors the number of mobile charge carriers, electrons and holes, can be tuned by doping, temperature, voltage, and radiation, the basis of modern electronics. Free electron beams in a vacuum are used in cathode ray tubes, electron microscopes, electron beam welding, lithography, and particle accelerators.

The ancient Greeks noticed that amber attracted small objects when rubbed with fur. In 1600 William Gilbert coined the Neo-Latin term *electrica* for such substances, from the Greek *ēlektron*, "amber." The British physicist J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 through experiments with cathode rays, showing that they consist of negatively charged particles much smaller than atoms. [[Robert Millikan]] measured its charge in 1909.

=== KEY: Electron_configuration_d9e95a
HEADWORD: Electron configuration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical bond | Pauli exclusion principle | Periodic table
ENTRY:
Distribution of electrons among the atomic or molecular orbitals of an atom or molecule. For example, the configuration of neon is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, meaning that the 1s, 2s and 2p subshells hold two, two and six electrons. Each electron is described by four quantum numbers, and the [[Pauli exclusion principle]] states that no two electrons can share the same set of four. The Aufbau principle dictates that electrons fill orbitals from the lowest energy level upward, producing the ground state, the lowest-energy configuration. An electron can move to a different configuration by emitting or absorbing a photon. Electron configuration explains the structure of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]], the nature of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]] and molecular geometry, and the behavior of lasers and semiconductors.

=== KEY: Electron_microscope_803de8
HEADWORD: Electron microscope
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | Vacuum
ENTRY:
Microscope that uses a beam of accelerated [[Electron|electrons]], rather than light, to produce magnified images of specimens. Electron optics, using electromagnetic lenses, focus the beam. Because the wavelength of an electron can be more than 100,000 times smaller than that of visible light, electron microscopes resolve detail to about 0.1 nm, compared with about 200 nm for light microscopes. In a transmission electron microscope (TEM), electrons pass through a specimen less than 0.5 μm thick, revealing internal atomic structure at magnifications exceeding 1 million times. A scanning electron microscope (SEM) scans a focused beam over a surface and detects secondary or backscattered electrons, producing three-dimensional images at resolutions around 1 nm. The instrument operates in a [[Vacuum|vacuum]], with electrons generated by a tungsten filament and accelerated at 20 to 120 kV.

=== KEY: Electron_shell_bdd743
HEADWORD: Electron shell
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bohr model | Periodic table
ENTRY:
Group of atomic orbitals that share the same principal quantum number, n, and therefore the same energy level and average distance from the nucleus. Shells are labeled 1, 2, 3 and so on, or K, L, M in X-ray notation, starting from the innermost. A shell can hold up to 2n² electrons: two in the first shell, eight in the second and 18 in the third. Each shell consists of one or more subshells, and each subshell consists of one or more atomic orbitals. The concept originated in the [[Bohr model]] of the atom, which is now understood through quantum mechanics as discrete energy states rather than fixed circular orbits. Each period of the conventional [[Periodic table|periodic table]] represents an electron shell.

=== KEY: Electronic_musical_instrument_ff4b4f

=== KEY: Electronics_ee3c6e
HEADWORD: Electronics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electrical engineering | Integrated circuit | Jack Kilby | John Bardeen | Lee de Forest | Transistor
ENTRY:
Scientific and engineering discipline that applies the principles of physics to design, create, and operate devices that manipulate electrons and other electrically charged particles. A subfield of physics and [[Electrical engineering|electrical engineering]], it uses active devices such as [[Transistor|transistors]], diodes, and [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]] to control and amplify electric current and to convert it from one form to another, such as alternating to direct current or analog to digital signals. The semiconductor industry is the main driving force behind its advance and had annual revenues exceeding $481 billion in 2018.

Karl Ferdinand Braun's crystal detector in 1874, the first semiconductor device, and the identification of the electron in 1897 by Joseph John Thomson, followed by the vacuum tube, inaugurated the field. Ambrose Fleming's diode and [[Lee de Forest]]'s triode of the early 1900s made small voltages, such as radio signals, detectable. Vacuum tubes, the first active components, enabled radio, television, radar, and long-distance telephony. [[John Bardeen]] and Walter Houser Brattain invented the first working point-contact transistor at Bell Labs in 1947. Vacuum tubes remained important in microwave and high-power transmission and television receivers until the middle of the 1980s.

In April 1955 the IBM 608, with more than 3,000 germanium transistors, became the first all-transistorized calculator made for the commercial market. The MOSFET, invented at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960, was the first truly compact transistor that could be miniaturized and mass-produced. The integrated circuit of [[Jack Kilby]] and Robert Noyce placed all components on one block of semiconductor material, leading to small-scale integration in the early 1960s. In 2008 billion-transistor processors became commercially available.

Components are active, such as transistors and thyristors, or passive: capacitors, inductors, and resistors. Circuits are analog, using a continuous range of voltage or current, or digital, using discrete levels.

=== KEY: Electronvolt_f152f2
HEADWORD: Electronvolt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | Joule | Particle accelerator | Volt
ENTRY:
Unit of energy equal to the kinetic energy gained by a single [[Electron|electron]] accelerating through a potential difference of one [[Volt|volt]] in a vacuum, symbol eV. It equals exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ [[Joule|joules]]. The unit suits energies in particle physics, quantum mechanics and spectroscopy, where joules would be impractically small. Multiples include keV (10³ eV), MeV (10⁶ eV), GeV (10⁹ eV) and TeV (10¹² eV), the last relevant to high-energy [[Particle accelerator|particle accelerators]]. The unit also describes photon energies and binding energies in atoms and molecules. It was devised for electrostatic accelerator science, because a particle with charge q gains energy E = qV after passing through a voltage V.

=== KEY: Electrostatics_380ded
HEADWORD: Electrostatics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amber | Classical antiquity | Coulomb's law | Electric charge | Photocopier | Static electricity
ENTRY:
Branch of physics that studies stationary [[Electric charge|electric charges]] and the electric fields they produce, in cases where quantum and magnetic effects can be neglected. Since [[Classical antiquity]] it has been known that rubbed [[Amber|amber]] attracts light particles, the observation behind the word electricity, from the Greek for amber. [[Coulomb's law]] gives the force between two point charges as proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Electric charge is quantized in units of the elementary charge, about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, and the total charge of an isolated system is conserved. Gauss's law relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge, which simplifies calculations for symmetric charge distributions. Familiar effects include [[Static electricity|static electricity]] and the operation of [[Photocopier|photocopiers]].

=== KEY: Elizabeth_I_39f350
HEADWORD: Elizabeth I
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Queen of England and Ireland (r. 1558–1603), the last and longest-reigning monarch of the [[House of Tudor]]. Her reign gave its name to the Elizabethan era.

Elizabeth was the only surviving child of [[Henry VIII]] and his second wife, Anne Boleyn. When she was two years old her parents' marriage was annulled, her mother was executed, and she was declared illegitimate; Henry restored her to the line of succession when she was 10. Her half-brother [[Edward VI]] ruled from 1547 until 1553, and the Catholic [[Mary I|Mary]] then became queen. During Mary's reign Elizabeth was imprisoned for nearly a year on suspicion of supporting Protestant rebels.

On Mary's death in 1558 Elizabeth set out to rule by good counsel, relying on advisers led by William Cecil, whom she created Baron Burghley. She established an English church, of which she became supreme governor; this Elizabethan Religious Settlement would evolve into the [[Church of England]]. She never married despite numerous courtships and was sometimes called the "Virgin Queen." She was succeeded by James VI of Scotland.

Elizabeth was more moderate in government than her father and siblings, and relatively tolerant in religion. After the pope declared her illegitimate in 1570, several conspiracies threatened her life; all were defeated with the help of the secret service run by Francis Walsingham. She was cautious in foreign affairs, maneuvering between France and Spain, but by the mid-1580s England could no longer avoid war with Spain.

A cult of personality grew around her. The Elizabethan era is known for English drama, led by [[William Shakespeare]] and [[Christopher Marlowe]], for maritime adventurers such as [[Francis Drake]], and for the defeat of the Spanish Armada. Some historians depict her as short-tempered and sometimes indecisive, and economic and military problems weakened her popularity late in her reign. Her 44 years on the throne provided stability after the short reigns of her half-siblings and helped forge a sense of national identity.

=== KEY: Ellipse_119518

Closed plane curve around two focal points, with a constant sum of distances to them. It generalizes a [[Circle|circle]], which is the case in which the two foci coincide. An ellipse's elongation is measured by its eccentricity, which ranges from 0 for a circle to 1 for the limiting case of a [[Parabola|parabola]]. === KEY: Emotion_a3eb8a
HEADWORD: Emotion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Aristotle | Charles Darwin | David Hume | William James
ENTRY:
Complex physical and mental state arising from neurophysiological and neuropsychological changes and variously associated with subjective experience, behavioral response, and a degree of pleasure or displeasure. There is no scientific consensus on a definition. Emotions are often intertwined with mood, temperament, personality, disposition, and creativity.

Emotions are generally treated as multicomponential, involving subjective experience, cognitive processes, expressive behavior, psychophysiological changes, and instrumental behavior. At one time academics tried to identify emotion with a single component: [[William James]] with subjective experience, behaviorists with instrumental behavior, psychophysiologists with physiological changes. Later work has described emotion as consisting of all the components. The sociologist Peggy Thoits described emotions as involving physiological components, cultural or emotional labels (anger, surprise), expressive body actions, and the appraisal of situations. In Scherer's Component Process Model, five crucial elements must become coordinated and synchronized for a short period, driven by appraisal. Cognitive processes such as reasoning are often regarded as separate from emotional ones, a division between "thinking" and "feeling", though not all theories accept it. Emotions may be occurrences (panic) or dispositions (hostility), and short-lived (anger) or long-lived (grief).

Major theories differ on how the components relate. The James-Lange theory holds that physiological arousal precedes and causes the emotional experience; the Cannon-Bard theory holds that experience and physiological response occur simultaneously; the Schachter-Singer two-factor theory adds cognitive labeling of arousal. Appraisal theories, such as those of Richard Lazarus, stress ongoing evaluation of an event's relevance, while constructionist theories treat emotions as predictions assembled from core affect and conceptual knowledge. Paul Ekman identified six basic emotions (anger, disgust, fear, happiness, sadness, surprise) as universal on the basis of consistent facial expressions, a view contested by constructionists who emphasize cultural variability. Some cross-cultural studies indicate that categories such as "anger" and "sadness" are not universal; others argue for universal bases.

The word was coined in 1543 by the French humanist Étienne Dolet, in his translation of Cicero's *Tusculanae Disputationes*, and entered English in 1579. It became a theoretical term in the early 1800s, and the modern concept emerged around the 1830s. Earlier thinkers including [[Aristotle]], Plato, Descartes, Aquinas, and Hobbes proposed competing theories of passions and motivation. In the [[Age of Enlightenment]], [[David Hume]] argued that actions are motivated by "fears, desires, and passions". Theorizing about the evolutionary origin of emotion dates to [[Charles Darwin]]. Current research uses positron emission tomography and functional magnetic resonance imaging to study emotion in the brain, and clinical research examines the dynamics of emotions in daily life. An inability to express or perceive emotion is sometimes called alexithymia.

He is traditionally regarded as a co-founder of the dynasty for encouraging his father to rebel against the [[Sui dynasty]] in 617. His Reign of Zhenguan is considered a golden age, and his reign became the model by which later emperors were measured. He asked officials to be loyal to policies rather than people, to eliminate [[Corruption|corruption]]. In 630 his general Li Jing defeated the Eastern Turks, and Taizong took the title Khan of Heaven. Tang armies annexed Karakhoja in 640, Karasahr in 644 and Kucha in 648. The empire covered most territory once held by the [[Han dynasty]], as well as parts of modern-day Korea, Vietnam, Russia, Mongolia, Xinjiang and Central Asia.

Founding emperor of the [[Song dynasty]] of China (r. 960–976). Personal name Zhao Kuangyin. A distinguished general of the Later Zhou dynasty, he came to power after staging a [[Coup d'état|coup]] and forcing the last Later Zhou ruler to abdicate. He conquered Southern Tang, Later Shu, Southern Han and Jingnan, reunifying most of China proper. To strengthen his control he reduced the power of military generals and relied on civilian officials. He was succeeded by his younger brother, Zhao Kuangyi (Emperor Taizong).
