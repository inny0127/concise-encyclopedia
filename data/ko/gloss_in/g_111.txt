=== KEY: Quantum_entanglement_9a4caa
HEADWORD: Quantum entanglement
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Quantum computing
ENTRY:
Quantum-mechanical phenomenon in which the states of particles in a group cannot be described independently, even when they are far apart. Measurements of properties such as spin or polarization on entangled particles can be found to be perfectly correlated. A 1935 paper by [[Albert Einstein|Einstein]], Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen described the apparent paradox, and Einstein called the effect "spooky action at a distance." In 1964 John Bell derived inequalities that local hidden-variable theories must satisfy but quantum mechanics violates; experiments beginning in the 1970s confirmed these violations. Entanglement cannot transmit information faster than light, because individual outcomes cannot be controlled. It has been demonstrated with photons, electrons, molecules and even small diamonds, and is studied for [[Quantum computing|quantum computing]] and communication.

=== KEY: Quantum_field_theory_d9c47e
HEADWORD: Quantum field theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Maxwell's equations | Michael Faraday | Quantum electrodynamics | Quantum mechanics | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Theoretical framework combining field theory, special relativity and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]]. It models subatomic particles in particle physics and quasiparticles in condensed matter physics, and the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics is based on it. Development began in the 1920s with the interaction of light and electrons, culminating in [[Quantum electrodynamics|quantum electrodynamics]]. Infinities in perturbative calculations were resolved by renormalization in the 1950s. A second barrier, the apparent inability to describe the weak and strong interactions, was overcome by gauge theory and the completion of the Standard Model in the 1970s. [[Michael Faraday]] coined the English term "field" in 1845, and [[Maxwell's equations]], completed in 1864, implied electromagnetic waves traveling at the speed of light.

=== KEY: Quantum_gravity_ab43cb
HEADWORD: Quantum gravity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Black hole | General relativity | M-theory | String theory | Theory of everything
ENTRY:
Field of theoretical physics that seeks to unify gravity with the principles of quantum mechanics. Gravity is the only one of the four fundamental interactions not fully accommodated by quantum mechanics and quantum field theory. [[General relativity]] describes gravity successfully, but its predictions break down at extremely small distances, such as inside [[Black hole|black holes]] and in the early universe near the [[Big Bang]]. Quantum effects are expected to matter near the Planck scale, around 10⁻³⁵ meters, far smaller than particle accelerators can probe, so experimental data are lacking. The most popular approaches are [[M-theory]] and loop quantum gravity, while [[String theory]] and others seek a [[Theory of everything|theory of everything]] describing all fundamental forces. Phenomenological quantum gravity studies indirect tests that may become feasible in coming decades.

=== KEY: Quantum_harmonic_oscillator_48de0e
HEADWORD: Quantum harmonic oscillator
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bohr model | Harmonic oscillator | Zero-point energy
ENTRY:
Quantum-mechanical analog of the classical [[Harmonic oscillator|harmonic oscillator]]. Because an arbitrary smooth potential can usually be approximated as harmonic near a stable equilibrium point, it is one of the most important model systems in quantum mechanics. It is also one of the few such systems with an exact analytical solution. Its energy levels are quantized and equally spaced, Eₙ = ħω(n + ½), unlike those of the [[Bohr model]] of the atom. The lowest level lies ħω/2 above the bottom of the potential well, a feature called [[Zero-point energy|zero-point energy]].

=== KEY: Quantum_mechanics_e185f8
HEADWORD: Quantum mechanics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albert Einstein | Classical mechanics | Double-slit experiment | Max Planck | Quantum tunnelling | Uncertainty principle | Wave function | Wave–particle duality
ENTRY:
Fundamental physical theory describing the behavior of matter and light at and below the scale of atoms. It describes systems that [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] cannot, and classical mechanics can be derived from it as an approximation valid at ordinary scales. Bound quantum systems have energy, momentum, angular momentum, and other quantities quantized to discrete values, and measurements show characteristics of both particles and waves ([[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]]). There are limits to how accurately a physical quantity can be predicted before measurement, expressed in the [[Uncertainty principle|uncertainty principle]]: no preparation allows precise prediction of both position and momentum at once.

The theory arose from observations classical physics could not explain, such as [[Max Planck]]'s 1900 solution to the black-body radiation problem and the correspondence between energy and frequency in [[Albert Einstein]]'s 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect. The full theory developed in the mid-1920s through these men, Max Born, Paul Dirac, and others.

In one formalism, the [[Wave function|wave function]] gives probability amplitudes for the results of measurements. Under the Born rule, a probability is the square of the absolute value of a complex number, so the theory usually predicts only probabilities, not certain outcomes. Interference is illustrated by the [[Double-slit experiment|double-slit experiment]], in which light or electrons form bright and dark bands yet are absorbed at discrete points. Another non-classical effect is [[Quantum tunnelling|quantum tunnelling]], in which a particle crosses a potential barrier even if its kinetic energy is smaller than the barrier. Applications include semiconductors, lasers, and medical imaging. Interpretation, including the measurement problem, remains debated, and reconciling the theory with general relativity is an open challenge.

=== KEY: Quantum_superposition_bc4bff
HEADWORD: Quantum superposition
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Double-slit experiment | Fourier transform | Quantum mechanics | Qubit | Schrödinger equation
ENTRY:
Principle of [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] stating that linear combinations of solutions to the [[Schrödinger equation]] are also solutions. Because the equation is linear and homogeneous, any combination of its solutions is valid, and a system's state is a superposition of eigenstates weighted by complex coefficients. A [[Qubit|qubit]] is generally a superposition of the basis states |0⟩ and |1⟩, and measurement probabilities follow the Born rule. Interference fringes in the [[Double-slit experiment|double-slit experiment]] provide another example. Position and momentum descriptions are related by a [[Fourier transform]], which is itself a superposition.

=== KEY: Quantum_tunnelling_7cd8b7
HEADWORD: Quantum tunneling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diode | Nuclear fusion
ENTRY:
Quantum-mechanical phenomenon in which a particle, such as an electron or atom, passes through a potential energy barrier that classical mechanics says should be impassable. Tunneling follows from the wave nature of matter: part of a particle's wave function can extend beyond a narrow barrier, giving a probability of transmission. That probability falls exponentially with barrier height, barrier width and particle mass, so tunneling is most prominent for light particles such as electrons. It plays an essential role in [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]] and in alpha decay of atomic nuclei. Applications include the [[Diode|tunnel diode]], flash memory and the scanning tunneling microscope. Tunneling also limits how small microelectronic devices can be, because electrons tunnel through insulating layers thinner than about 1 nm. The effect was predicted in the early 20th century and gained general acceptance mid-century.

=== KEY: Quark_fdc0f4
HEADWORD: Quark
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Cosmic ray | Hadron | James Joyce | Murray Gell-Mann | Particle accelerator | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Elementary particle and fundamental constituent of matter. Quarks combine to form [[Hadron|hadrons]], the most stable of which are the proton and neutron, the components of [[Atomic nucleus|atomic nuclei]]. Owing to color confinement, quarks are found only within hadrons or in quark–gluon plasmas. There are six flavors: up, down, charm, strange, top and bottom. Up and down quarks are the lightest and most common, while the others arise only in high-energy collisions, such as those involving [[Cosmic ray|cosmic rays]] and in [[Particle accelerator|particle accelerators]]. Quarks are the only particles in the [[Standard Model]] to experience all four fundamental interactions. [[Murray Gell-Mann|Gell-Mann]] and George Zweig proposed the quark model in 1964. Gell-Mann took the name from the phrase "Three quarks for Muster Mark" in [[James Joyce]]'s *Finnegans Wake*.

=== KEY: Quarry_241945
HEADWORD: Quarry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Ancient Greece | Ancient Rome | Drainage
ENTRY:
Place where rock and earth materials, such as limestone, marble, granite, gravel and sand, are excavated. Quarry operations are regulated in some jurisdictions to manage safety risks and environmental impact. Granite and marble were quarried in [[Ancient Egypt]], and crushed stone was used extensively by [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]] and [[Ancient Rome|Rome]]. Stone is removed by digging, heating, wedging or blasting, the last using controlled explosives placed in drilled holes. Many stones are cut into slabs, polished and made into tiles or countertops. Quarries in flat terrain near groundwater often face [[Drainage|drainage]] problems; the Coquina quarry is excavated to more than 60 feet (18 m) below sea level. Many people consider quarries eyesores, and Butchart Gardens in Victoria, British Columbia, Canada, is a well-known example of successful restoration.

=== KEY: Quartz_8f116d
HEADWORD: Quartz
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agate | Amethyst | Crystallography | Lithosphere | Mohs scale | Silicon dioxide
ENTRY:
Hard [[Silicon dioxide|silica]] mineral, the second most common mineral in Earth's [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]], making up about 12% by mass. Its atoms form a continuous framework of SiO₄ tetrahedra, giving the formula SiO₂, and it defines 7 on the [[Mohs scale]] of hardness. Quartz exists as normal α-quartz and high-temperature β-quartz. The transition between them occurs abruptly at 573 °C (846 K; 1,063 °F) and can cause microfracturing in ceramics and rocks. Many varieties are gemstones, such as [[Amethyst]] and [[Agate]], and quartz has been used in jewelry and hardstone carvings since antiquity. The name derives from German *Quarz*, from the Polish dialect term *kwardy*, meaning "hard." In the 17th century Nicolas Steno found that its long prism faces always meet at 60°, which paved the way for modern [[Crystallography|crystallography]].

=== KEY: Quasar_d19ab7
HEADWORD: Quasar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Active galactic nucleus | Hubble Space Telescope | Light-year | Milky Way | Solar mass | Supermassive black hole
ENTRY:
Extremely luminous [[Active galactic nucleus|active galactic nucleus]] (AGN), powered by a [[Supermassive black hole|supermassive black hole]]. Gas falling onto a black hole of millions to tens of billions of [[Solar mass|solar masses]] releases energy, and the most powerful quasars are thousands of times more luminous than the [[Milky Way]]. The name is a contraction of "quasi-stellar radio source," because quasars were first identified in the 1950s as radio sources that resembled faint, star-like points of light. [[Hubble Space Telescope|Hubble]] images show that quasars lie at galaxy centers, some in merging galaxies. About a million quasars have reliable spectroscopic redshifts, and the nearest known is about 600 million [[Light-year|light-years]] from Earth. Quasar activity was more common about 10 billion years ago.

=== KEY: Quaternary_c21798
HEADWORD: Quaternary
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropocene | Bering Strait | Holocene | Ice sheet | Milankovitch cycles | Neogene | Pleistocene
ENTRY:
Current and most recent period of the Cenozoic Era, spanning from 2.58 million years ago to the present. It follows the [[Neogene]] period and comprises two epochs: the [[Pleistocene]], to about 12,000 years ago, and the [[Holocene]]. A proposed third epoch, the [[Anthropocene]], was rejected in 2024 by the International Union of Geological Sciences. The period is defined by Quaternary glaciation, the cyclic growth and decay of continental [[Ice sheet|ice sheets]] related to [[Milankovitch cycles]]. It spans the time during which recognizable humans have existed. The Last Glacial Maximum, about 26,500–19,000 years ago, saw ice sheets cover much of North America and Eurasia. Periodic closings of the [[Bering Strait]] formed a land bridge between Asia and North America.

=== KEY: Quaternion_3743af
HEADWORD: Quaternion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Complex number | Computer graphics | Robotics | Three-dimensional space | William Rowan Hamilton
ENTRY:
Four-dimensional number system that extends the [[Complex number|complex numbers]]. Quaternions have four real-number components, written a + bi + cj + dk, and their multiplication is not commutative. Irish mathematician and physicist [[William Rowan Hamilton]] first described them in 1843. They form a four-dimensional associative normed division algebra over the real numbers, and they represent rotations in [[Three-dimensional space|three-dimensional space]]. Applications include [[Computer graphics|computer graphics]], [[Robotics]] and magnetic resonance imaging. Unit quaternions form a group isomorphic to SU(2), the universal cover of the rotation group SO(3).

=== KEY: Qubit_5f83fc
HEADWORD: Qubit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bit | Electron | Photon
ENTRY:
Basic unit of quantum information, the quantum counterpart of the classical [[Bit|bit]]. A qubit can be physically realized with any two-level quantum system, such as the spin of an [[Electron|electron]] or the polarization of a [[Photon|photon]]. Unlike a bit, which is either 0 or 1, a qubit can be in a coherent superposition of both states. Measuring it yields one of two outcomes and disturbs the superposition. The term was coined by Benjamin Schumacher, who wrote in a 1995 paper that it was created in jest during a conversation with William Wootters.

=== KEY: Quechua_people_c76aeb
HEADWORD: Quechua people
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bolivia | Ecuador | Inca Empire | Peru | Quechuan languages
ENTRY:
Indigenous people of South America who speak the [[Quechuan languages]]. Their languages originated among the Indigenous people of [[Peru]], where most Quechua speakers live, with significant populations in [[Ecuador]], [[Bolivia]], Chile, Colombia and Argentina. The [[Inca Empire]], the largest empire of the pre-Columbian era, was one of many peoples who spoke Quechua, and it promoted the language as its official tongue. Other historical groups include the Chanka and Huanca of Peru and the Cañari of Ecuador, who adopted Quechua from the Incas. Traditional life is tied to local economies, with agriculture at lower altitudes and pastoral farming in the higher Puna. Land is usually owned by the local community, or ayllu. Speakers number about 5.1 million in Peru, 1.8 million in Bolivia and 2.5 million in Ecuador, though some dialects are not mutually intelligible.

=== KEY: Quechuan_languages_d5e34f
HEADWORD: Quechuan languages
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catholic Church | Inca Empire | Túpac Amaru II
ENTRY:
Indigenous language family of the Andes that originated in central Peru. Derived from a common ancestral Proto-Quechua language, it is the most widely spoken pre-Columbian language family of the Americas, with an estimated 8–10 million speakers in 2004 and just under 7 million in the most recent census data available up to 2011. Quechua was the primary language of the [[Inca Empire]], though it had spread across the central Andes for centuries before the Incas. Spanish authorities officially recognized it, and the [[Catholic Church]] used it for evangelization; Domingo de Santo Tomás published a grammar in 1560. After the [[Túpac Amaru II]] rebellion, colonial officials banned it from public use in Peru in the late 18th century. Today it is the majority language in several regions of Peru and, as Kichwa, the second most-spoken language of Ecuador after Spanish.

=== KEY: Question_a97ea5
HEADWORD: Question
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Information | Linguistics | Philosophy of language | Pragmatics | Semantics
ENTRY:
Utterance that serves as a request for [[Information|information]]. Questions are distinct from interrogatives, the grammatical forms typically used to express them. Polar questions can be answered with "yes" or "no", alternative questions present a list of possibilities, and open questions allow many possible answers. Rhetorical questions are interrogative in form but are not expected to be answered. Questions are widely studied in [[Linguistics]] and [[Philosophy of language|philosophy of language]]. In [[Pragmatics|pragmatics]], questions are regarded as illocutionary acts that raise an issue to be resolved in discourse. In approaches to [[Semantics|formal semantics]], questions are identified with sets of the propositions that answer them. Questions can also serve as indirect speech acts, such as requests for action phrased as interrogatives.

=== KEY: Quicksort_19474a
HEADWORD: Quicksort
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Sorting algorithm | Unix
ENTRY:
Divide-and-conquer [[Sorting algorithm|sorting algorithm]] developed by British computer scientist Tony Hoare in 1959 and published in 1961. It selects a pivot element, partitions the other elements into sub-arrays of lesser and greater values, and sorts those recursively. On average, it takes O(n log n) comparisons to sort n items, but O(n²) in the worst case. Most implementations are not stable, and on randomized data it is slightly faster than merge sort and heapsort. It was widely adopted, appearing as the default library sort in [[Unix]].

Central religious text of [[Islam]], believed by Muslims to be a revelation directly from God (Allāh). Its name, also romanized Qur'an or Koran, is Arabic for "the recitation." === KEY: RNA_78c445
HEADWORD: RNA
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Gene expression | Nucleic acid | Ribosome | Virus
ENTRY:
Polymeric molecule, ribonucleic acid, essential for most biological functions, either by acting itself (non-coding RNA) or by forming a template for protein production (messenger RNA). RNA and [[DNA]] are [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]], one of the four major macromolecule classes essential to all known life. RNA is a chain of nucleotides, each with a ribose sugar, a phosphate group, and a base: adenine, cytosine, guanine, or uracil. Adenine pairs with uracil and cytosine with guanine through hydrogen bonds. Unlike DNA, it is typically single-stranded and forms short helices packed into structures akin to those of proteins. It differs chemically from DNA by a hydroxyl group at the 2' position of the ribose, which makes the backbone prone to cleavage. Bases and sugars can be modified in numerous ways as RNAs mature.

Cellular organisms use messenger RNA (mRNA) to convey genetic information that directs synthesis of specific proteins, and many [[Virus|viruses]] have RNA genomes. In protein synthesis on [[Ribosome|ribosomes]], transfer RNA (tRNA) delivers amino acids and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) links them into coded proteins. Other RNAs catalyze reactions, control [[Gene expression|gene expression]], or sense and respond to cellular signals; the ribosome's active site is composed entirely of RNA, making it a ribozyme. Small nuclear RNAs form spliceosomes that remove introns from pre-mRNA, and microRNAs and small interfering RNAs mediate gene silencing. Dysfunctions in RNA processing are implicated in diseases such as myotonic dystrophy and certain cancers, and mRNA vaccines use RNA to encode antigens.

It is widely accepted that early in the history of life, before the evolution of DNA and possibly of protein enzymes, an "RNA world" existed in which RNA both stored genetic information and catalyzed reactions.

In its core sense it is the belief that groups of humans possess different behavioral traits corresponding to inherited attributes and can be ranked by the superiority of one race or ethnicity over another. Modern variants are often based in social perceptions of biological differences between peoples. Racism can take the form of social actions, practices, or beliefs, or of political systems such as [[Apartheid|apartheid]], and may involve nativism, xenophobia, segregation, and supremacism.

Attempts to legitimize racist beliefs through "scientific racism" have been overwhelmingly shown to be unfounded. Most biologists, anthropologists, and sociologists reject a taxonomy of races, and [[Human genome]] research indicates that race is not a meaningful genetic classification. The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination declares superiority based on racial differentiation scientifically false, morally condemnable, socially unjust, and dangerous. Contemporary social science treats race and [[Ethnicity|ethnicity]] as separate concepts, though popular usage has long equated them.

The word came into widespread use in the Western world in the 1930s. Racism is frequently described as a modern phenomenon that evolved during the age of European [[Imperialism|imperialism]] and was shaped by the [[Atlantic slave trade]]. It underlay racial segregation in the United States in the 19th and early 20th centuries and played a role in genocides including [[The Holocaust|the Holocaust]] and the Rwandan genocide. [[Indigenous peoples]] have often been subject to racist attitudes.

Interpretation of present-day disparities is contested. Some scholars attribute them to "systemic racism," while critics argue that this overemphasizes fixed structures over factors such as family, education, and culture.

=== KEY: Radar_dc1f6c
HEADWORD: Radar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air traffic control | Baltic Sea | Cavity magnetron | Cologne | Doppler effect | Heinrich Hertz | Lidar | World War II
ENTRY:
System that uses radio waves to determine the distance, direction, and radial velocity of objects relative to the site. It is used to detect and track aircraft, ships, spacecraft, guided missiles, motor vehicles, weather formations, and terrain. The term RADAR was coined in 1940 by the U.S. Navy as an acronym for "radio detection and ranging" and has since become a common noun.

A radar system consists of a transmitter producing electromagnetic waves in the radio or microwave domain, a transmitting antenna, a receiving antenna (often the same one), and a receiver and processor. Waves reflect off objects and return to the receiver, giving their locations and speeds; range comes from time of flight and velocity from the [[Doppler effect]]. The device was developed secretly for military use by several countries before and during [[World War II]].

In 1886 [[Heinrich Hertz]] showed that radio waves could be reflected from solid objects. In 1897, while testing ship-to-ship communication in the [[Baltic Sea]], Alexander Popov noted interference caused by a third vessel but did not pursue it. In 1904 the German inventor Christian Hülsmeyer demonstrated detecting a ship in dense fog and obtained a British patent for a full system, the *telemobiloscope*; it was tested in [[Cologne]] and Rotterdam harbor but rejected. U.S. Navy researchers observed ships and then aircraft disturbing radio signals in 1922 and 1930, and British work led by Robert Watson-Watt produced a national network by 1937. The [[Cavity magnetron]], developed in the United Kingdom, allowed small systems with sub-meter resolution.

Modern uses include [[Air traffic control]], radar astronomy, air defense, marine navigation, precipitation monitoring, and self-driving cars. The related [[Lidar]] uses predominantly infrared light from lasers instead of radio waves.

=== KEY: Radian_50c62e
HEADWORD: Radian
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculus | Circle | Degree (angle) | International System of Units | Mathematics | Sine and cosine
ENTRY:
Dimensionless [[International System of Units|SI]] unit of angle, the standard unit of angular measure in many areas of [[Mathematics|mathematics]]. One radian is the angle subtended at the center of a [[Circle|circle]] by an arc equal in length to the radius, so an angle in radians equals the arc length divided by the radius. A complete revolution is 2π radians, equal to 360 [[Degree (angle)|degrees]], and one radian is approximately 57.2958 degrees. The radian was formally recognized as an SI coherent derived unit in 1995. It is preferred in [[Calculus]] because the derivatives of [[Sine and cosine|sine and cosine]] hold without additional conversion factors only when angles are in radians.

=== KEY: Radiation_bff9b6
HEADWORD: Radiation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cancer | Electromagnetic radiation | Gamma ray | Heat | Inverse-square law | Ionizing radiation | X-ray
ENTRY:
Emission or transmission of energy in the form of waves or particles through space or a material medium. Radiation includes [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]], such as radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, [[X-ray|X-rays]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]; particle radiation, such as alpha, beta, proton and neutron radiation; acoustic radiation, such as sound and ultrasound; and gravitational waves. It is often categorized as [[Ionizing radiation|ionizing]] or non-ionizing according to the energy of the radiated particles. Ionizing radiation can knock electrons from atoms and break chemical bonds, damaging living cells and DNA, so exposure increases the risk of [[Cancer|cancer]]. Lower-energy radiation can cause vibrations in molecular bonds that are sensed as [[Heat|heat]]. Because radiation expands as it passes through space and its energy is conserved in vacuum, the intensity of radiation from a point source follows an [[Inverse-square law|inverse-square law]] with distance.

=== KEY: Radical_chemistry_c2d4ac
HEADWORD: Radical
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Biochemistry | Combustion | Ether | Ion | Molecule | Redox
ENTRY:
[[Atom|Atom]], [[Molecule|molecule]] or [[Ion|ion]] that has at least one unpaired valence electron. With some exceptions, these unpaired electrons make radicals highly reactive. === KEY: Radio_1f32d2
HEADWORD: Radio
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander Graham Bell | Antenna (radio) | Guglielmo Marconi | Heinrich Hertz | James Clerk Maxwell | Lee de Forest | Radar | Wi-Fi
ENTRY:
Technology of communicating by radio waves, electromagnetic waves with frequencies up to 300 gigahertz (GHz). Waves are generated by a transmitter connected to an [[Antenna (radio)|antenna]], which radiates them; other antennas connected to a receiver pick them up. Information is carried by modulating the signal. Radio is used in broadcasting, cell phones, two-way radios, [[Wi-Fi|wireless networking]], and satellite communication, and also in [[Radar]], navigation systems such as GPS, and remote control.

[[James Clerk Maxwell]] predicted electromagnetic waves in his 1873 theory of electromagnetism. On November 11, 1886, [[Heinrich Hertz]] first observed radio waves that he had generated with a spark-gap transmitter. In 1895 [[Guglielmo Marconi]] sent a wireless Morse code message to a recipient over a kilometer away, and on December 12, 1901, the first transatlantic signal. Reginald Fessenden made an amplitude-modulation voice broadcast in 1906, the year of [[Lee de Forest]]'s Audion vacuum tube. The first commercial broadcast was transmitted on November 2, 1920, when Westinghouse in Pittsburgh, under the call sign KDKA, broadcast the live returns of the U.S. presidential election.

The word derives from the Latin *radius*, meaning "spoke of a wheel, beam of light, ray." It was first applied to communications in 1881, when [[Alexander Graham Bell]], at the suggestion of Ernest Mercadier, adopted *radiophone* as an alternate name for his photophone. Early radio was called wireless telegraphy. The 1906 Berlin Radiotelegraphic Convention adopted "radio" internationally, and the public came to prefer it in the 1920s with the introduction of broadcasting. The emission of radio waves is regulated by law, coordinated by the International Telecommunication Union, which allocates frequency bands.

=== KEY: Radio_telescope_952e2f
HEADWORD: Radio telescope
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antenna (radio) | Optical telescope
ENTRY:
Specialized [[Antenna (radio)|antenna]] and receiver used to detect radio waves from astronomical radio sources. === KEY: Radio_wave_c1bcaf
HEADWORD: Radio wave
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antenna (radio) | Electromagnetic radiation | Electromagnetic spectrum | Heinrich Hertz | James Clerk Maxwell | Lightning | Speed of light
ENTRY:
Type of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] with the lowest frequencies and longest wavelengths in the [[Electromagnetic spectrum|electromagnetic spectrum]], typically below 300 gigahertz (GHz). Radio waves travel at the [[Speed of light|speed of light]] in a vacuum and are generated by charged particles undergoing acceleration, such as time-varying electric currents. Natural sources include [[Lightning|lightning]] and astronomical objects, while artificial radio waves are produced by a transmitter connected to an [[Antenna (radio)|antenna]]. Their behavior depends on frequency: long waves diffract around mountains and follow the Earth's contour, shorter waves reflect off the ionosphere to return beyond the horizon, and much shorter waves travel along a line of sight. [[James Clerk Maxwell|James Clerk Maxwell]]'s theory of electromagnetism predicted them in 1867, and [[Heinrich Hertz|Heinrich Hertz]] demonstrated them in 1887. Their use is strictly regulated by the International Telecommunication Union to prevent interference.

=== KEY: Radioactive_decay_61b9cb
HEADWORD: Radioactive decay
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atomic nucleus | Half-life | Henri Becquerel | Marie Curie | Polonium | Radium | Uranium | Wilhelm Röntgen
ENTRY:
Process by which an unstable [[Atomic nucleus|atomic nucleus]] loses energy by radiation. The three most common types are alpha, beta, and gamma decay. In alpha decay the nucleus ejects a helium-4 nucleus (two protons and two neutrons), lowering the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. In beta decay a neutron converts to a proton or vice versa, emitting an electron or positron and changing the atomic number by 1.

Decay is a random process at the level of single atoms: according to quantum theory, it is impossible to predict when a particular atom will decay. For many identical atoms, however, the overall rate can be expressed as a decay constant or a [[Half-life|half-life]]. Half-lives range from nearly instantaneous to far longer than the age of the universe, from 164 microseconds for polonium-214 to 5,730 years for carbon-14. Except in gamma decay, the parent nuclide is transmuted into a daughter with a different number of protons or neutrons; when the number of protons changes, a different chemical element results.

Twenty-eight naturally occurring elements on Earth are radioactive, consisting of 35 primordial radionuclides that predate the formation of the Solar System. [[Uranium]] and thorium are examples.

Radioactivity was discovered in 1896 by [[Henri Becquerel]], who found that uranium salts blackened a photographic plate wrapped in black paper. In 1897 [[Marie Curie]] began studying the radiation and showed it to be a property of atoms; she and Pierre Curie isolated two new elements, [[Polonium|polonium]] and [[Radium|radium]], and coined the term "radioactivity." With Frederick Soddy, Ernest Rutherford realized that decay can transmute one element into another. The dangers of ionizing radiation, including that of X-rays discovered by [[Wilhelm Röntgen]] in 1895, were not immediately recognized.

=== KEY: Radiocarbon_dating_35aa09
HEADWORD: Radiocarbon dating
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cosmic ray | Half-life | Mass spectrometry | Nitrogen | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Method for determining the age of organic material by measuring its radioactive carbon-14 (¹⁴C) content. Carbon-14 is constantly created in the atmosphere when [[Cosmic ray|cosmic rays]] interact with [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]], and it enters living organisms through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] and food. After an organism dies, its carbon-14 decays with a [[Half-life|half-life]] of about 5,730 years, so the oldest dates that can be reliably measured reach approximately 50,000 years ago. The method was developed in the late 1940s at the University of Chicago by Willard Libby, who received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1960. Calibration curves convert measurements into calendar ages. Burning fossil fuels and nuclear testing in the 1950s and 1960s altered atmospheric levels, requiring further corrections. [[Mass spectrometry|Accelerator mass spectrometry]], which needs very small samples, is now the method of choice.

=== KEY: Radiography_e8db8f

Modern rail transport began with the [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]] in the United Kingdom at the beginning of the 19th century, and railways were a key component of the [[Industrial Revolution|Industrial Revolution]]. The first passenger railway, the Stockton and Darlington Railway, opened in 1825. The first high-speed railway opened in Japan in 1964, and rail has revived in recent decades as governments invest in it to reduce CO₂ emissions.

=== KEY: Railway_track_df8b60
HEADWORD: Railway track
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Creosote | Steam locomotive
ENTRY:
Structure of a railway consisting of rails, fasteners, sleepers and ballast on a subgrade. Also called the permanent way, the track gives trains a dependable, low-friction surface on which steel wheels can roll. Early tracks used wooden or cast-iron rails and wooden or stone sleepers, and since the 1870s rails have almost universally been made of steel. The first railway in Britain, the Wollaton Wagonway, was built in 1603 with wooden rails. When [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotives]] appeared in 1804, the existing track proved too weak, and engineers later adopted flexible structures that allowed elastic movement. Most heavy-traffic lines now use continuously welded rails attached to sleepers with base plates, and timber sleepers are often treated with [[Creosote|creosote]].

=== KEY: Rain_de4eda

ENTRY:
Form of atmospheric [[Precipitation|precipitation]] composed of water droplets that have condensed from atmospheric water vapor and fall by gravity. By intensity it is described as drizzle (very light, sporadic), a shower (brief, moderate), a cloudburst (brief, heavy), or a downpour (prolonged, heavy). Rain is a major component of the [[Water cycle]] and deposits most of the fresh water on Earth, supplying hydroelectric plants, crop irrigation, and ecosystems.

Rain forms when air is cooled to its [[Dew point|dew point]], the temperature at which it becomes saturated. Four mechanisms cool air to that point: adiabatic cooling as air rises and expands, conductive cooling on contact with a colder surface, radiational cooling by infrared emission, and evaporative cooling. Water vapor then [[Condensation|condenses]] on nuclei such as dust, ice, and salt to form a [[Cloud|cloud]]. Droplets grow by coalescence, fusing when turbulence makes them collide, until they are heavy enough to fall.

The major cause of rain is moisture moving along [[Weather front|weather fronts]]. Mountains force moist air upward so that rain falls on windward slopes, while desert climates can occur on the leeward side. The movement of the monsoon trough, or [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]], brings rainy seasons to savanna climates. The urban heat island effect increases rainfall downwind of cities.

Globally averaged annual precipitation is 715 mm over land and 990 mm over the whole Earth. Rainfall is measured with rain gauges and estimated by weather radar. Intense rain can cause flooding and erosion, while prolonged deficits contribute to [[Drought|droughts]].

=== KEY: Rainbow_4aca5e
HEADWORD: Rainbow
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dew | Guinness World Records | Red | Refraction | Taipei | Taiwan | Violet (color)
ENTRY:
Optical phenomenon in which [[Refraction|refraction]], internal reflection and dispersion of light in airborne water droplets form a multicolored arc in the sky. Rainbows can be caused by rain, mist, spray and airborne [[Dew|dew]], and those caused by sunlight always appear in the part of the sky directly opposite the sun. In a primary rainbow, [[Red|red]] is on the outer edge and [[Violet (color)|violet]] on the inner side. A double rainbow has a second arc about 10° outside the primary, with its colors in reverse order. Rainbows are usually seen in the western sky in the morning and the eastern sky in the early evening. From an aircraft, a rainbow can appear as a full circle. The longest-lasting rainbow recorded by [[Guinness World Records|Guinness World Records]] lasted 8 hours 58 minutes, in the Yangmingshan range of [[Taipei|Taipei]], [[Taiwan|Taiwan]], in November 2017.
