=== KEY: Guillotine_0e4150
HEADWORD: Guillotine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capital punishment | French Revolution | Reign of Terror
ENTRY:
Apparatus designed for carrying out executions by beheading. A weighted, angled blade is released from the top of a tall frame and falls onto the neck of the condemned person. The guillotine is best known for its use in France during the [[French Revolution]], where supporters celebrated it as the people's avenger and opponents vilified it as the symbol of the [[Reign of Terror]]. It remained France's standard method of judicial execution until [[Capital punishment|capital punishment]] was abolished in 1981. The last person executed by a government via guillotine was Hamida Djandoubi, a Tunisian murderer, on September 10, 1977, in France.

=== KEY: Guilt_emotion_d6903f
HEADWORD: Guilt
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anxiety | Conscience | Obsessive–compulsive disorder | Shame | Sigmund Freud
ENTRY:
Moral emotion that arises when a person believes, accurately or not, that they have compromised their own standards of conduct or violated universal moral standards. Guilt is closely related to remorse, regret and [[Shame|shame]]. Understood as a collection of basic emotions, it is composed of self-directed anger, sadness for the victim, [[Anxiety|anxiety]] and fear of damaging relationships. Although unpleasant to feel, guilt can prompt efforts to restore relationships and make amends. [[Sigmund Freud]] attributed guilt to the [[Conscience|conscience]], or superego, and to a fear of loss of love that begins in childhood, and he rejected the role of God. Martin Buber distinguished Freudian guilt, based on internal conflicts, from existential guilt, based on actual harm done to others. Guilt is an important factor in perpetuating [[Obsessive–compulsive disorder]] symptoms.

Domesticated [[Rodent|rodent]], *Cavia porcellus*, from South America. Domestic guinea pigs typically weigh 700 to 1,200 g (1.5 to 2.6 lb) and measure 20 to 25 cm (8 to 10 in) in length. They are social animals that are skittish in response to noise or danger. They have since been largely replaced in [[Laboratory|laboratory]] testing by mice and rats, though they are still used in research.

=== KEY: Gulag_9291a1
HEADWORD: Gulag
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn | Joseph Stalin | Nikita Khrushchev | Norilsk | Soviet Union
ENTRY:
Soviet penal labor camp system, operating from the 1930s to the early 1950s. The Gulag was a major instrument of political repression in the [[Soviet Union]], and its camps held both ordinary criminals and political prisoners, many convicted under simplified procedures. The agency that ran the system was established in 1930. After the death of [[Joseph Stalin]], a mass amnesty was granted, and under [[Nikita Khrushchev]] the Gulag was officially abolished on January 25, 1960. Of the 14 million prisoners who passed through the camps and 4 million through the colonies from 1930 to 1953, roughly 1.5 to 1.7 million perished or died soon after release, according to the emerging scholarly consensus. [[Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn]] gave the term international recognition with *The Gulag Archipelago* (1973). Many mining and industrial towns in northern Russia, eastern Russia and Kazakhstan, such as [[Norilsk]], began as blocks of camps built by prisoners.

=== KEY: Gulf_Stream_d70dc5
HEADWORD: Gulf Stream
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atlantic Ocean | Benjamin Franklin | Florida | Gulf of Mexico | Juan Ponce de León | Virginia
ENTRY:
Warm and swift [[Atlantic Ocean|Atlantic]] current, part of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation. It originates in the [[Gulf of Mexico]], flows through the Straits of Florida and up the eastern coast of the United States, then veers east near 36°N latitude and moves toward Northwest Europe as the North Atlantic Current. Around 40°N, 30°W it splits in two. The Gulf Stream influences the climate of the coast from [[Florida]] to southeast [[Virginia]] and, to a greater degree, the climate of Northwest Europe. European discovery of the current dates to the 1512 expedition of [[Juan Ponce de León]]. A chart of its path by [[Benjamin Franklin]], based on the observations of a Nantucket whaling captain, was printed in 1769, and satellites from the late 1970s onward have mapped its position in near-real time.

Arm of the [[Pacific Ocean]] defined by the curve of the southern coast of [[Alaska]], between Kabuch Point on the Alaska Peninsula and Cape Spencer. === KEY: Gull_1ea1c8

Seabird of the subfamily Larinae, with approximately 50 species worldwide. Gulls are most closely related to [[Tern|terns]] and skimmers, and more distantly to [[Auk|auks]] and skuas. Most species are gray and white, often with black markings, and have strong bills and webbed feet. Most are ground-nesting piscivores or carnivores that take live food or scavenge; live prey includes crustaceans, mollusks, fish, and, for larger species, birds and mammals. === KEY: Gun_25510a

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Device that propels a projectile using pressure or explosive force. Projectiles are typically solid but can be pressurized liquid or gas. Solid projectiles may be free-flying, as with bullets and artillery shells, or tethered, as with Tasers and harpoon guns. A large-caliber gun is called a [[Cannon|cannon]], and a hand-held gun may be called a [[Firearm|firearm]]. Guns were designed as [[Military|military]] weapons before they were adopted for [[Hunting|hunting]]. By the end of the 13th century they had become "true guns," metal-barreled firearms. [[Gunpowder]] and gun technology spread throughout Eurasia during the 14th century. === KEY: Gunpowder_9f0493
HEADWORD: Gunpowder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artillery | Charcoal | Dynamite | Firearm | Potassium nitrate | Song dynasty | Sulfur | Tang dynasty
ENTRY:
Mixture of [[Sulfur|sulfur]], [[Charcoal|charcoal]], and [[Potassium nitrate|potassium nitrate]] (saltpeter), and the earliest known chemical explosive. It is also called black powder. The sulfur and charcoal act as fuels, while the saltpeter is an oxidizer.

Gunpowder is classified as a low explosive: it deflagrates, burning at subsonic speeds, whereas high explosives detonate and produce a supersonic shockwave. When ignited behind a projectile it generates enough pressure to force the shot from the muzzle at high speed, but usually not enough to rupture the barrel. It has therefore served widely as a propellant in [[Firearm|firearms]], [[Artillery|artillery]], rocketry, and pyrotechnics, and as a blasting agent in quarrying, mining, and the building of pipelines, tunnels, and roads. Its low brisance suits it to blasting slate and monumental stone. Combustion converts less than half of its mass to gas; the residue fouls barrels and corrodes wrought iron and steel, so gunpowder arms need regular cleaning.

The first confirmed reference to what can be considered gunpowder in China dates to the 9th century, during the [[Tang dynasty]], in a formula of six parts sulfur, six parts saltpeter, and one part birthwort herb in the *Taishang Shengzu Jindan Mijue* (808). Daoist alchemists seeking an elixir of life probably discovered it by accident; its Chinese name, *huoyao*, means "fire medicine." It was first used for warfare around AD 904. The earliest chemical formula appears in the *Wujing Zongyao*, a [[Song dynasty]] military text written by Zeng Gongliang between 1040 and 1044. High explosives came into use in the second half of the 19th century, and smokeless powder replaced gunpowder in weapons, while [[Dynamite|dynamite]] and ammonium nitrate/fuel oil replaced it in industry.

=== KEY: Gupta_Empire_9b3bd1
HEADWORD: Gupta Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aryabhata | Central Asia | Chandragupta II | Indian subcontinent | Kalidasa | Mahabharata | Samudragupta | Uttar Pradesh
ENTRY:
Indian empire of the classical period, existing from the mid-3rd to the mid-6th century AD. At its peak the dynasty, founded by a king named Gupta, ruled much of the northern [[Indian subcontinent]].

The dynasty's homeland is uncertain. Eastern [[Uttar Pradesh]], Magadha (Bihar), and Bengal have each been proposed. Early rulers were Gupta, his son Ghatotkacha, and Chandragupta I; the Allahabad Pillar inscription calls the first two Maharaja ("Great King") and Chandragupta I Maharajadhiraja ("King of Great Kings"). The high points of the period came under [[Samudragupta]], [[Chandragupta II]], Kumaragupta I, and Skandagupta.

The era produced scholars including [[Kalidasa]], [[Aryabhata]], Varahamihira, and Vatsyayana, who made significant advances in many fields. The [[Mahabharata]] and *Ramayana* were canonized, and the Puranas are thought to have been committed to writing around this time. Architecture, sculpture, and painting of the period "set standards of form and taste [that] determined the whole subsequent course of art, not only in India but far beyond her borders." The rulers followed Hinduism, and Brahmins flourished, but the Guptas were tolerant of other faiths. The period is sometimes described as Pax Gupta and has been considered a Golden Age of India by some historians, a characterization others dispute.

The empire declined after its own former feudatories took much of its territory and authority, and after invasions by the Huna peoples (Kidarites and Alchon Huns) from [[Central Asia]]. After its collapse in the 6th century, India was again ruled by numerous regional kingdoms.

=== KEY: HIV_AIDS_c30cc9

LINK TARGETS: Blood transfusion | Immune system | Life expectancy | Pandemic | Primate | Tuberculosis | Vaccine
ENTRY:
Spectrum of conditions caused by infection with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV), a retrovirus that attacks the [[Immune system|immune system]]. Without treatment, HIV infection can progress to acquired immunodeficiency syndrome (AIDS).

HIV spreads primarily through unprotected sex, contaminated needles, [[Blood transfusion|blood transfusions]], and from mother to child during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding. Saliva, sweat, and tears do not transmit it. Initial infection may bring no symptoms or an influenza-like illness 2–4 weeks after exposure. A prolonged stage of clinical latency follows, lasting from about three years to over 20 years without treatment (on average, about eight years). Eventually the infection raises the risk of opportunistic infections such as [[Tuberculosis|tuberculosis]] and of tumors rare in people with normal immune function. Without treatment, life expectancy is around 11 years.

There is no cure or [[Vaccine|vaccine]], but antiretroviral treatment, taken for life, suppresses the virus to undetectable levels and can extend [[Life expectancy|life expectancy]] to a nearly standard level. A person with an undetectable viral load has effectively no risk of transmitting HIV sexually, a message campaigns by UNAIDS have communicated as "Undetectable = Untransmittable." Prevention includes safe sex, pre-exposure and post-exposure prophylaxis, and needle exchange programs.

HIV passed from other [[Primate|primates]] to humans in west-central Africa in the early-to-mid-20th century. AIDS was first recognized by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in 1981, and its cause was identified in the early part of that decade. The disease is estimated to have caused at least 42.3 million deaths through 2024. In 2025, 1.2 million people acquired HIV and an estimated 40.9 million were living with it. It is considered a [[Pandemic|pandemic]] and has been a source of discrimination and large economic costs.

ENTRY:
Short form of Japanese [[Poetry|poetry]], traditionally of 17 on in a 5, 7, 5 pattern across three phrases. Traditional haiku include a kireji, or cutting word, and a kigo, a seasonal reference. It originated as the opening stanza, or hokku, of linked renga verse and became a standalone poem; the name was given at the end of the 19th century by Masaoka Shiki. [[Matsuo Bashō]] raised the form to literary prominence in the late 17th century, and Zen principles of simplicity influenced it. Haiku in English often appear in three lines and vary widely in following traditional rules. Poems that do not follow the rules are generally classed as senryū.

=== KEY: Hail_96924c
HEADWORD: Hail
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crop | Cumulonimbus cloud
ENTRY:
Form of solid atmospheric precipitation, made of balls or irregular lumps of ice called hailstones. Hail is distinct from ice pellets, though the two are often confused. Hailstones usually measure between 5 mm (0.2 in) and 15 cm (6 in) in diameter. They form in the strong upward air currents of [[Cumulonimbus cloud|cumulonimbus clouds]], growing layer by layer as they are carried up and down, and they fall faster as they grow. Hail is most frequent in mid-latitude continental regions such as the central United States, while in the tropics it tends to be confined to high elevations. Hail causes serious damage to property and, most commonly, to [[Crop|crops]]. Severe weather warnings are issued when stones reach a damaging size, which the U.S. National Weather Service sets at 1 in (2.5 cm) in diameter.
