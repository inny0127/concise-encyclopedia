=== KEY: Cheese_a67778

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dairy | Inanna | Lactic acid | Latin | Milk | Pasteurization | Water buffalo | Xinjiang
ENTRY:
[[Dairy]] food made by curdling [[Milk|milk]], usually from cows, goats, or sheep and sometimes [[Water buffalo|water buffalo]]. Milk is usually acidified, and either the enzymes of rennet or bacterial enzymes with similar activity cause the protein casein to coagulate. The solid curds, consisting of proteins and milkfat, are separated from the liquid whey and pressed into finished cheese. Most cheeses are acidified by bacteria that turn milk sugars into [[Lactic acid|lactic acid]]; a few are curdled with acids such as vinegar or lemon juice. Some have aromatic molds on the rind or throughout.

Over a thousand types exist. Style, texture, and flavor depend on the origin of the milk (including the animal's diet), [[Pasteurization|pasteurization]], butterfat content, bacteria and mold, processing, and aging time. Cheese is valued for its portability, long shelf life, and high content of fat, protein, calcium, and phosphorus; hard cheeses such as Cheddar and Parmesan keep longer than soft ones such as Brie. High saturated fat and sodium content are associated with cardiovascular disease risk if intake is excessive, although some studies link moderate intake to lower stroke risk.

The word derives from [[Latin]] *caseus*, which also gave casein. Cheesemaking predates recorded history, and no conclusive evidence shows whether it began in Europe, Central Asia, or the Middle East. The earliest archaeological evidence, from 5500 BC, is strainers coated with milk-fat molecules found in Kuyavia, Poland. In Sumer, cheese figured in the sacred marriage rite of Dumuzi and [[Inanna]], in which the king of Uruk supplied the goddess with cheese and cream. The earliest preserved cheese, found on mummies at Xiaohe Cemetery in [[Xinjiang]], China, dates to as early as 1615 BC.

=== KEY: Chemical_biology_dac0fe

ENTRY:
Scientific discipline between [[Chemistry|chemistry]] and [[Biology|biology]]. It applies chemical techniques, and often small molecules made by synthetic chemistry, to study and manipulate biological systems. Although often confused with [[Biochemistry]], which studies the chemistry of biomolecules, chemical biology focuses on using chemical tools to address biological questions. In 1828 Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea from inorganic starting materials, an early example of synthetic chemistry advancing biology that weakened the notion of vitalism. === KEY: Chemical_bond_0c65bd
HEADWORD: Chemical bond
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Covalent bond | Electron | Hydrogen bond | Ion | Metallic bonding | Molecule | Valence bond theory
ENTRY:
Association of [[Atom|atoms]] or [[Ion|ions]] to form [[Molecule|molecules]], crystals, and other structures. A bond may result from the electrostatic force between oppositely charged ions, as in ionic bonds; from the sharing of [[Electron|electrons]], as in [[Covalent bond|covalent bonds]]; or from some combination of these effects. Strong, or primary, bonds include covalent, ionic, and [[Metallic bonding|metallic bonds]]; weak, or secondary, bonds include dipole–dipole interactions, the London dispersion force, and [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonds]].

The attraction can be seen as the result of the behavior of the outermost, or valence, electrons of atoms. These behaviors merge into each other, so no clear line separates the types. In a covalent bond, one or more electrons, often a pair, are drawn into the space between two nuclei. Such bonds have a direction in space and often form small molecules, long chains as in polymers such as nylon, or networks as in diamond and quartz. In an ionic bond, an electron is transferred from one atom to another, leaving one positively and the other negatively charged; the bond results from electrostatic attraction between the ions. Ionic bonds are strong but brittle, generally have no particular orientation in space, and give rise to crystals such as table salt. In metallic bonding, each metal atom donates one or more electrons to a "sea" of electrons shared among many atoms.

All bonds can be described by quantum theory, but simplified rules such as the octet rule and VSEPR theory allow chemists to predict the strength, directionality, and polarity of bonds. More sophisticated treatments include [[Valence bond theory|valence bond theory]] and molecular orbital theory. Bonds determine the structure and properties of matter.

=== KEY: Chemical_compound_13fc34
HEADWORD: Chemical compound
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical bond | Chemical element | Chemical formula | Chemical reaction | Robert Boyle
ENTRY:
Substance composed of many identical molecules containing atoms from more than one [[Chemical element|chemical element]] held together by [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]]. A molecule consisting of atoms of only one element is therefore not a compound. A compound can be transformed into a different substance by a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]], in which bonds between atoms may be broken, formed, or both.

Four major types are distinguished by how the atoms are bonded: molecular compounds, held together by covalent bonds; ionic compounds, held together by ionic bonds; intermetallic compounds, held together by metallic bonds; and coordination complexes, held together by coordinate covalent bonds. Non-stoichiometric compounds, whose proportions may be reproducible but are not integral, as in palladium hydride, PdHₓ (0.02 < x < 0.58), form a disputed marginal case.

A [[Chemical formula|chemical formula]] specifies the number of atoms of each element in a molecule of a compound, using standard chemical symbols with numerical subscripts. Water, for example, consists of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom, with the formula H₂O. Many compounds have a unique CAS number assigned by the Chemical Abstracts Service. Globally, more than 350,000 chemical compounds (including mixtures of chemicals) have been registered for production and use.

The term "compound", with a meaning similar to the modern one, has been used at least since 1661, when [[Robert Boyle|Robert Boyle]]'s *The Sceptical Chymist* was published. In his *Logick* (1724), the English minister and logician Isaac Watts contrasted element with compound in clear, modern terms.

=== KEY: Chemical_element_5dec41
HEADWORD: Chemical element
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Atomic number | Dmitri Mendeleev | Isotope | Noble gas | Periodic table | Synthetic element
ENTRY:
Species of [[Atom|atom]] defined by its number of protons, called its [[Atomic number|atomic number]]. For example, oxygen has an atomic number of 8: each oxygen atom has 8 protons in its nucleus. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons, and are known as [[Isotope|isotopes]] of the element. Atoms of one element can be transformed into atoms of another in nuclear reactions, which change the atomic number. Almost all baryonic matter in the universe is composed of elements; neutron stars are among the rare exceptions.

The term has two closely related meanings: a kind of atom, and a pure chemical substance consisting of a single kind of atom. Water (H₂O), for instance, consists of the elements hydrogen and oxygen even though it contains neither H₂ nor O₂ molecules. Historically, an element meant a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions, a definition that remains valid for most practical purposes. The properties of elements as atoms include atomic number, atomic weight, isotopes, ionization energy, electron affinity, oxidation states, and electronegativity; as substances, they include density, melting point, boiling point, and electrical and thermal conductivity.

Early human societies discovered native minerals such as carbon, sulfur, copper, and gold, though the modern concept of an element was not yet understood. Much of the modern understanding developed from the work of [[Dmitri Mendeleev|Dmitri Mendeleev]], a Russian chemist who published the first recognizable [[Periodic table|periodic table]] in 1869. The table orders elements by increasing atomic number into rows (periods) and columns (groups) that share recurring properties. Elements are commonly sorted into metals, nonmetals, and metalloids, the last forming a diagonal boundary with intermediate properties.

By November 2016 the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) recognized 118 elements. The first 94 occur naturally on Earth; the remaining 24 are [[Synthetic element|synthetic]], produced in nuclear reactions. Six of the natural ones, technetium, promethium, astatine, francium, neptunium, and plutonium, occur only in extreme trace amounts. Element 118 (oganesson) was reported in October 2006 and element 117 (tennessine) in April 2010.

Hydrogen and helium were created by Big Bang nucleosynthesis in the first 20 minutes of the universe, in a ratio of around 3:1 by mass, along with traces of lithium and beryllium. Almost all other natural elements were made by other forms of nucleosynthesis. Fewer than twenty elements, including gold, platinum, and the iron group metals, are sometimes found uncombined as native minerals; the rest occur as compounds or mixtures. Argon, a [[Noble gas|noble gas]], is chemically inert.

=== KEY: Chemical_equation_695c8d
HEADWORD: Chemical equation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catalysis | Chemical formula | Chemical reaction | Hydrochloric acid | Sodium
ENTRY:
Symbolic representation of a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] using [[Chemical formula|formulas]] and symbols. Reactants are listed on the left and products on the right, separated by an arrow that shows the direction of the reaction. Coefficients give the number of entities of each substance, and a coefficient of 1 is left unwritten. For example, [[Hydrochloric acid|hydrochloric acid]] reacting with [[Sodium|sodium]] is written 2HCl + 2Na → 2NaCl + H₂. Physical states may be added in parentheses: (s) for solid, (l) for liquid, (g) for gas and (aq) for aqueous solution. Symbols above the arrow can denote heat (Δ), light (hν) or a [[Catalysis|catalyst]]. The first chemical equation was diagrammed by Jean Beguin in 1615.

=== KEY: Chemical_equilibrium_a3142e
HEADWORD: Chemical equilibrium
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical reaction | Temperature
ENTRY:
State of a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] in which reactants and products have concentrations that no longer change with time. It results when the forward reaction proceeds at the same rate as the reverse reaction, a condition known as dynamic equilibrium. Molecules keep reacting even though net concentrations stay constant. The concept was developed in 1803, after Berthollet found that some reactions are reversible. In 1865 Guldberg and Waage proposed the law of mass action, which defines an equilibrium constant that depends on [[Temperature|temperature]], though the law applies only to single-step reactions. Le Châtelier's principle (1884) predicts that a disturbed equilibrium shifts to partly reverse the change. A catalyst speeds both reactions equally, so it does not change the equilibrium constant.

=== KEY: Chemical_formula_142264
HEADWORD: Chemical formula
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ammonium sulfate | Butane | Polyethylene
ENTRY:
Notation for the composition and structure of chemical compounds. Formulas use element symbols with subscripts for atom counts. An empirical formula gives the simplest ratio of elements, such as C₂H₅ for [[Butane]], while a molecular formula gives the exact count, C₄H₁₀. === KEY: Chemical_kinetics_cddfc2
HEADWORD: Chemical kinetics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arrhenius equation | Chemical reaction | Physical chemistry | Reaction mechanism | Temperature | Thermodynamics
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Physical chemistry]] concerned with the rates of [[Chemical reaction|chemical reactions]]. Unlike [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]], which indicates the direction a reaction takes but says nothing about its rate, kinetics examines how the physical state of reactants, their concentrations, [[Temperature]] and catalysts influence reaction speed, and it reveals the [[Reaction mechanism|reaction mechanism]]. Ludwig Wilhelmy studied the rate of sucrose inversion in 1850. In 1864 Peter Waage and Cato Guldberg published the law of mass action, which states that reaction speed is proportional to the quantity of reacting substances. === KEY: Chemical_plant_16d51a
HEADWORD: Chemical plant
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Oil refinery | Petrochemical
ENTRY:
Industrial process plant that manufactures or otherwise processes chemicals, usually on a large scale. Its general objective is to create new material wealth through the chemical or biological transformation or separation of materials, using specialized equipment, units and technology. Feedstock, the raw material fed into a plant, is converted into products through steps called unit operations carried out in interconnected units, in either continuous or batch operation. === KEY: Chemical_reaction_18c5ad
HEADWORD: Chemical reaction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antoine Lavoisier | Chemical bond | Chemical equation | Enzyme | Friedrich Wöhler | Nuclear chemistry | Reaction mechanism | Redox
ENTRY:
Process of chemical transformation of one set of substances into another, with a rearrangement of atoms and an accompanying energy change. Classically, reactions involve only the positions of electrons in the forming and breaking of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]], with no change to the nuclei, and can often be described by a [[Chemical equation|chemical equation]]. [[Nuclear chemistry|Nuclear chemistry]] covers reactions of unstable and radioactive elements, in which both electronic and nuclear changes occur.

The substances initially involved are called reactants or reagents, and the products usually have different properties from them. Reactions often consist of a sequence of elementary reactions, and the precise course of action is the [[Reaction mechanism|reaction mechanism]]. Each reaction proceeds at a characteristic rate at a given temperature and concentration; rates typically increase with temperature because more thermal energy is available to reach the activation energy needed to break bonds. Reactions that produce heat are exothermic, and those that require heat are endothermic. A reaction may be classified as [[Redox|redox]], involving oxidation and reduction, or as non-redox. In biochemistry, consecutive reactions form metabolic pathways, often catalyzed by protein [[Enzyme|enzymes]].

Combustion, fermentation, and the reduction of ores to metals were known since antiquity. Medieval alchemists tried to convert lead into gold. The Haber process (1909–1910) synthesized ammonia. The phlogiston theory, proposed in 1667 by Johann Joachim Becher, was shown false in 1785 by [[Antoine Lavoisier|Antoine Lavoisier]], who explained combustion as a reaction with oxygen from the air. Vitalism, the belief that organic matter was endowed with a "vital force", ended with the synthesis of urea from inorganic precursors by [[Friedrich Wöhler|Friedrich Wöhler]] in 1828.

=== KEY: Chemical_synthesis_9bdc1c
HEADWORD: Chemical synthesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chemical reaction | Laboratory | Organic compound | Paracetamol
ENTRY:
Artificial execution of [[Chemical reaction|chemical reactions]] to obtain one or more products. Reagents, or reactants, undergo transformation under set conditions, and many reactions require a work-up or purification step to isolate the final product. Yield is expressed as a mass in grams in a [[Laboratory|laboratory]] setting, or as a percentage of the theoretical quantity based on the limiting reagent, and a side reaction can reduce it. Multistep synthesis requires a separate work-up for each stage; the synthesis of [[Paracetamol]] typically needs three reactions. One-pot synthesis carries out several reactions in one vessel without isolating intermediates. Organic synthesis, a major branch of the field, builds [[Organic compound|organic compounds]] from basic lab compounds or modifies natural products.

=== KEY: Chemistry_84cf60
HEADWORD: Chemistry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alchemy | Antoine Lavoisier | Atom | Biochemistry | Chemical bond | Chemical element | Chemical reaction | Periodic table
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the properties and behavior of matter. A physical science within the natural sciences, it studies the composition, structure, properties, and behavior of matter and the changes it undergoes during reactions with other substances, including the nature of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]] in compounds. Chemistry occupies an intermediate position between physics and biology and is sometimes called the central science because it provides a foundation for understanding both basic and applied scientific disciplines. It explains aspects of plant growth (botany), the formation of igneous rocks (geology), how atmospheric ozone forms and pollutants degrade (ecology), how medications work (pharmacology), and how DNA evidence is collected at a crime scene (forensics). Its applications are used widely in the chemical industry.

The word comes from a modification, during the Renaissance, of *alchemy*, which referred to earlier practices encompassing chemistry, metallurgy, philosophy, astrology, astronomy, mysticism, and medicine. [[Alchemy]] is often associated with the quest to turn lead or other base metals into gold. The word derives from the Arabic *al-kīmīā*, which may have Egyptian origins through Ancient Greek *χημία* and *Kemet*, the ancient name of Egypt; alternatively it may derive from *χημεία*, "cast together." Chemistry became a rigorous science in the 18th century, when [[Antoine Lavoisier|Antoine Lavoisier]] established principles such as the conservation of mass through precise experimentation.

The current model of atomic structure is the quantum mechanical model. The [[Atom|atom]] is the basic unit of chemistry: a dense nucleus of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. A nucleon is approximately 1,836 times the mass of an electron, yet an atom's radius is about 10,000 times that of its nucleus. A pure substance composed of a single type of atom is a [[Chemical element|chemical element]], characterized by its atomic number; atoms of one element with different mass numbers are isotopes, so that carbon atoms may have mass numbers of 12 or 13. Elements are organized in the [[Periodic table|periodic table]] by atomic number into groups (columns) and periods (rows). A compound is a pure substance composed of more than one element, named under rules set by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) and indexed by CAS registry number. Molecules are typically sets of atoms bound by covalent bonds into electrically neutral units.

A [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] is a transformation of substances into others through the rearrangement of electrons in bonds, and it can be depicted by a chemical equation with equal numbers of atoms on each side. Energy and entropy considerations matter in almost all studies. Substances are analyzed with tools such as spectroscopy and chromatography. Major branches include analytical, organic, inorganic, physical chemistry, and [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], which explores chemical processes within living organisms.

=== KEY: Chi_squared_test_0c99bf
HEADWORD: Chi-squared test
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chi-squared distribution | Karl Pearson | P-value
ENTRY:
Statistical hypothesis test for comparing observed and expected frequencies in categorical data. Published by [[Karl Pearson|Karl Pearson]] in 1900, Pearson's chi-squared test is considered one of the foundations of modern statistics. It determines whether there is a statistically significant difference between expected and observed frequencies in one or more categories of a contingency table, and it can test whether two categorical variables are independent. The test statistic, the sum of (observed − expected)² / expected across categories, approximately follows a [[Chi-squared distribution|chi-squared distribution]] when samples are large. Fisher's exact test is used instead for contingency tables with small samples. Its [[P-value|p-value]] is used to reject or retain the null hypothesis of no association.

=== KEY: Chicken_a9514f

Domesticated subspecies (*Gallus gallus domesticus*) of the [[Red junglefowl|red junglefowl]], originally native to [[Southeast Asia]]. One of the most common and widespread domesticated animals in the world, it is cultivated primarily as [[Poultry|poultry]] for meat and [[Egg|eggs]] and occasionally kept as a pet. As of 2023, the global live population exceeded 26.5 billion, with more than 50 billion birds slaughtered annually for consumption. Specialized [[Breed|breeds]] have been developed for meat and egg yield, and a hen bred for egg production can lay over 300 eggs per year. Critics of [[Intensive farming|industrial-scale rearing]] raise welfare concerns over confined conditions, though assessments vary with management practices.

Chickens are relatively large, day-active birds with round bodies, short wings, and, in most breeds, unfeathered legs. Domestic chickens are too heavy to fly more than a short distance. Chicks weigh about 37 g at hatching; by day 35 a Ross 708 broiler may weigh 1.8 kg, against 1.05 kg for a heritage chicken. Omnivores, they may live 5–10 years; the oldest known lived 16 years.

Chickens are gregarious and live in flocks with a dominance hierarchy, called the "pecking order" after its description in female chickens by Thorleif Schjelderup-Ebbe in 1921. They use varied vocalizations, including different warning calls for aerial and ground predators. Their cognition, emotions, and sociality are comparable with those of other birds and mammals.

Domestic chimneys first appeared in large dwellings in northern Europe in the 12th century, and the earliest surviving English example, at the keep of [[Castle|Conisbrough Castle]] in [[Yorkshire]], dates from 1185. Chimneys became common in ordinary houses only in the 16th and 17th centuries. In the 18th and 19th centuries, the extraction of [[Lead|lead]] from ore produced toxic fumes, and long near-horizontal chimneys in northern England, often more than 3 km (2 mi) long, carried them to a remote vertical stack.

Political grouping of the Chimú culture on the northern coast of Peru. The Chimú culture arose about 900 CE, succeeding the [[Moche culture]], and developed on a desert strip where rivers carved fertile valleys suited to irrigation. Chimor was the largest kingdom of the Late Intermediate Period, encompassing 1,000 km (620 mi) of coastline. Its capital, Chan Chan, was founded, according to legend, by Taycanamo. Irrigation [[Canal|canals]] extended up to 80 km, and the Chimú are known for distinctive monochromatic [[Pottery|pottery]], usually with a shiny black finish, and for metalwork in [[Gold|gold]] and [[Silver|silver]]. The Chimú worshipped the [[Moon]] and traded Spondylus shells from the coast of present-day Ecuador. The [[Inca Empire]] conquered Chimor around 1470, 50 years before the Spanish arrived in the region.

Medium-sized great ape species of equatorial Africa. Chimpanzees live in forests and savannas, and four subspecies are confirmed. They share the genus *Pan* with the [[Bonobo]], and fossil and DNA evidence show that the two species are the closest living relatives of [[Human|humans]]. Chimpanzees eat mainly fruit, but also insects, and will hunt smaller monkeys. Nearly all populations use tools, such as modified sticks, for hunting and for obtaining [[Honey]], [[Termite|termites]], and [[Ant|ants]]. Groups can number more than 200 members, with a male-dominated hierarchy in which disputes are generally settled without violence. The species is listed as [[Endangered species|endangered]] on the IUCN Red List, with habitat loss, poaching, and disease as the main threats. Some hundreds have been kept in laboratories, mainly in the United States. Attempts to teach them sign language have been made, but academics such as [[Noam Chomsky]] have questioned whether they can acquire language.

National space agency of [[China|the People's Republic of China]]. Founded in 1993 and headquartered in Haidian, Beijing, the agency manages China's civil space programs and international space cooperation. It is a national bureau under the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology. Its Chang'e program made China the first space agency to land on the [[Far side of the Moon|far side of the Moon]], with Chang'e 4, and returned lunar material with Chang'e 5 and 6. Tianwen-1 made China the second agency to land a rover on Mars. Construction of the [[Tiangong space station]] began in 2021 and was completed in late 2022. Since the Wolf Amendment of 2011, Congress has required [[NASA]] to exclude cooperation with the agency, although this has been periodically overcome.
