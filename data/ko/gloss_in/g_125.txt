=== KEY: Snow_b46d3c
HEADWORD: Snow
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albedo | Glacier | Great Lakes | Low-pressure area | Northern Hemisphere | Precipitation | Skiing
ENTRY:
Solid atmospheric [[Precipitation|precipitation]] consisting of individual ice crystals that grow while suspended in the air, usually within clouds, then fall and accumulate on the ground. Snow is frozen crystalline water throughout its life cycle: crystals form in the atmosphere, grow to millimeter size, precipitate, accumulate, then metamorphose in place and finally melt, slide, or sublimate away.

Snowflakes nucleate around particles in the atmosphere by attracting supercooled water droplets, which freeze into hexagonal crystals. Basic shapes include platelets, needles, columns, and rime. Snow clouds usually occur within larger weather systems, chiefly the [[Low-pressure area|low-pressure area]], with its warm and cold fronts. Other productive sources are lake-effect snow, produced when cold air crosses warmer water and deposits snow on downwind shores, and orographic snowfall, created when moist air is forced up mountains. Snowbelts include areas east of the [[Great Lakes]], the west coast of northern Japan, and the Kamchatka Peninsula.

Accumulated snow, or snowpack, may blow into drifts and changes over time by sintering, sublimation, and freeze-thaw. Where the climate is cold enough for year-to-year accumulation, a [[Glacier|glacier]] may form; otherwise snow melts seasonally, causing runoff into streams and rivers and recharging groundwater. Snow has high [[Albedo|albedo]], reflecting up to 90% of sunlight, and low thermal conductivity. It covers an average of 44 million sq km of the [[Northern Hemisphere]] each winter.

Major snow-prone areas are the polar regions, the northern half of the Northern Hemisphere, and mountains worldwide; in the Southern Hemisphere snow is largely confined to mountains and Antarctica. Snow affects transportation, agriculture, sports such as [[Skiing|skiing]], and warfare, and it insulates plants and animals through winter.

=== KEY: Snowball_Earth_651684
HEADWORD: Snowball Earth
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian explosion
ENTRY:
Hypothesis that Earth's surface was nearly entirely frozen during one or more icehouse climates. It is most commonly associated with the Cryogenian Period, which had two major glacial episodes: the Sturtian, c. 717–660 million years ago, and the Marinoan, c. 650–635 million years ago. === KEY: Social_class_933f67
HEADWORD: Social class
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Bourgeoisie | Jean-Jacques Rousseau | Karl Marx | Max Weber | Middle class | Upper class | Working class
ENTRY:
Grouping of people into hierarchical social categories, the most common being the [[Working class|working class]], the [[Middle class|middle class]], and the [[Upper class|upper class]].

Class is analyzed by sociologists, political scientists, anthropologists, and social historians. The term has sometimes conflicting meanings, and there is no broad consensus on a definition. Some argue that, because of social mobility, class boundaries do not exist. Academics distinguish social class, a relatively stable cultural background, from socioeconomic status, a person's current and more changeable social and economic situation.

[[Karl Marx]] defined class by one's relationship to the means of production: the proletariat work but do not own them, while the [[Bourgeoisie|bourgeoisie]] live off the surplus they generate. [[Max Weber]] contrasted class, determined by economic position, with social status (*Stand*), determined by prestige. The word derives from Latin *classis*, which census takers used to categorize citizens by wealth for military service. In the late 18th century, class began to replace estates, rank, and orders as the main hierarchical division of society, as hereditary characteristics counted for less and wealth and income for more.

Class systems are old. In [[Ancient Egypt]], scribes and officials formed the upper class, the "white kilt class," yet a man of humble origins could rise to a high post. In medieval Europe, sumptuary laws regulated dress by rank. [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]], who influenced the French Revolution, held that private property is the main source of inequality. Studies report that class origins predict health outcomes and cognitive styles, and that intergenerational mobility has stagnated or declined in advanced economies, particularly the United States.

=== KEY: Social_control_9df4ea
HEADWORD: Social control
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Political science | Socialization | Sociology | Émile Durkheim
ENTRY:
Regulations, sanctions, mechanisms, and systems that restrict the behavior of individuals in accordance with social norms and orders. Social control is considered one of the foundations of social order. Sociologists identify two basic forms. Informal control operates through the internalization of norms and values in [[Socialization]], while formal control comprises external sanctions enforced by government to prevent chaos or anomie in society. Some theorists, such as [[Émile Durkheim]], call formal control regulation. Criminal persecutions emerged around the sixth century BC in Athens, Greece, as an early form of formal control. The term was introduced to [[Sociology|sociology]] by Albion Woodbury Small and George Edgar Vincent in 1894. In the 1930s its meaning shifted to an individual's conversion to conformity. It is studied in anthropology, criminology, law, [[Political science|political science]], and sociology.

=== KEY: Social_democracy_824b9a
HEADWORD: Social democracy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capitalism | Democratic socialism | Liberalism | Poverty | Russian Revolution | Trade union | Welfare state
ENTRY:
Center-left political ideology favoring gradual, reformist, and democratic pursuit of social equality. It supports political and economic democracy and aims to curb inequality, eradicate [[Poverty|poverty]], and uphold universally accessible public services such as child care, education, elderly care, health care, and workers' compensation. In modern practice it has taken the form of a predominantly [[Capitalism|capitalist]] economy with a robust [[Welfare state|welfare state]], social justice policies, market regulation, and income redistribution. It has a long-standing connection with [[Trade union|trade unions]] and the labor movement. Originally a catch-all term for socialists, after the [[Russian Revolution]] it came to mean reformist socialists opposed to revolution and to the Soviet model, though abolishing capitalism remained an end goal. By the 1990s social democrats had accepted mixed economies dominated by private property. The Third Way of the 1990s sought to fuse [[Liberalism|economic liberalism]] with social democratic policies, a movement some analysts characterize as effectively neoliberal. Modern social democracies retain a capitalist system, whereas [[Democratic socialism|democratic socialists]] advocate its abolition.

=== KEY: Social_equality_8e9b81
HEADWORD: Social equality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek philosophy | Aristotle | Caste | Discrimination | French Revolution | Plato | Reformation | Social class
ENTRY:
State of affairs in which all individuals within a society have equal rights, liberties, and status, possibly including civil rights, freedom of expression, and equal access to public goods and social services.

Social equality requires the absence of legally enforced [[Social class|social class]] or [[Caste|caste]] boundaries and of [[Discrimination|discrimination]] based on an inalienable part of identity. Advocates support equality before the law regardless of sex, gender, ethnicity, age, religion, disability, and other grounds. Formal equality means equal opportunity based on merit; substantive equality, also called social equity, means equality of outcomes for groups.

The concept is defined variously, as equality of power, rights, goods, opportunities, capabilities, or some combination. Societies promoting it generally make no distinctions of rank or class, and relationships within them rest on mutual respect rather than hierarchy or honor. Its advocacy is egalitarianism. Communism, anarchism, multiculturalism, democracy, socialism, and social democracy all draw on it. It can also apply between states, where power disparities create hierarchies, particularly under globalization.

Early conceptions appear in [[Ancient Greek philosophy]]. The Stoics held that human reason is universal; [[Plato]] considered equality in the *Republic*; [[Aristotle]] developed a conception regarding citizenship but rejected total equality in favor of hierarchy. Equality became practicable in Europe during the [[Reformation]], when religious hierarchies were challenged. Milestones cited include abolitionist campaigns, the 1776 Declaration of Independence ("all men are created equal"), the [[French Revolution]] (1789), the Seneca Falls Convention (1848), and the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990). Critics of remedial policies such as preferential hiring argue that they may erode merit-based hierarchies.

=== KEY: Social_group_a9838e
HEADWORD: Social group
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Kinship
ENTRY:
Two or more people who interact with one another, share similar characteristics, and collectively have a sense of unity. Social groups come in many sizes and varieties; a society can be viewed as a large social group. Members may share interests, values, ethnic or social background, and [[Kinship|kinship ties]], which are social bonds based on common ancestry, marriage, or adoption. Some researchers consider social interaction the defining characteristic. According to Dunbar's number, on average people cannot maintain stable social relationships with more than 150 individuals. Social psychologists Muzafer Sherif and Carolyn Sherif defined a social unit as individuals interacting with respect to common motives and goals, an accepted division of labor, established status relationships, accepted norms and values, and accepted sanctions for norms that are respected or violated. The study of behaviors and psychological processes within or between groups is known as group dynamics.

Problem that affects many people within a society. Social issues are often the consequence of factors beyond an individual's control and are a source of conflicting opinions about what is morally correct or incorrect in personal and social life. === KEY: Social_movement_f6ae4b

=== KEY: Social_network_381609

=== KEY: Social_norm_ef6d61
HEADWORD: Social norm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Institution | Social control
ENTRY:
Shared standard of acceptable behavior within a group, either informal or codified into rules and laws. Norms are shared social beliefs about behavior, distinct from privately held ideas, attitudes, and values that need not concern behavior. They are contingent on context, social group, and historical circumstances, and [[Institution|institutions]] are composed of multiple norms. Scholars distinguish regulative norms, which constrain behavior; constitutive norms, which shape interests; and prescriptive norms, which prescribe what actors ought to do. Actors follow norms either because they are socially appropriate or because of cost-benefit calculations. Norms can promote [[Social control|social control]] across many cultures, and those who do not conform are considered "deviants." A norm typically emerges as norm entrepreneurs persuade others of its desirability, cascades when it gains broad acceptance, and is internalized when it becomes taken for granted. Sociologists, psychologists, political scientists, and economists study norms, including political norms and gender norms.

=== KEY: Social_order_3d7893
HEADWORD: Social order
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amish | Jürgen Habermas | Karl Marx
ENTRY:
Term denoting either a particular system of social structures and institutions or a stable state of society. In the first sense, it refers to a particular system, such as the ancient, feudal, or capitalist social order. In the second, it is contrasted with social chaos or disorder and describes a stable state in which the existing social structure is accepted and maintained by its members. Thomas Hobbes is recognized as the first to formulate the problem of order clearly, asking how and why social orders exist at all, and conceived the social contract to answer it. Social theorists including [[Karl Marx]], Émile Durkheim, Talcott Parsons, and [[Jürgen Habermas]] have proposed different bases: economic structure for Marx, shared social norms for Durkheim, and social institutions and cultural values for Parsons. The principle of extensiveness holds that the more numerous and important a society's norms, the better they hold the group together, as with the [[Amish]], whose shared religion and values uphold their way of life.

=== KEY: Social_psychology_8f6e94
HEADWORD: Social psychology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Culture
ENTRY:
Methodical study of how thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are influenced by the actual, imagined, or implied presence of others. Psychological social psychology studies many of the same topics as sociology but places more emphasis on the individual than on society, examining how social structure and [[Culture|culture]] influence personality, behavior, and position in social hierarchies. It depends more heavily on experimental and laboratory research. Social psychologists typically explain human behavior as the result of the relationship between mental states and social situations. They study the social conditions under which thoughts, feelings, and behaviors occur and how these variables influence social interactions.

=== KEY: Social_reality_2ffcb3
HEADWORD: Social reality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Herbert Spencer | Reality | Speech act | Émile Durkheim
ENTRY:
Socially constructed perspective of the world, made up of a community's accepted social tenets, including laws and social representations. It is distinct from material, biological, or individual cognitive [[Reality|reality]], existing at a subjective level created through social interaction and transcending individual motives and actions. [[Émile Durkheim]] stressed its distinct nature, and [[Herbert Spencer]] coined the term super-organic for the social level above the biological and psychological. Alfred Schütz called it the "social world." John Searle used the theory of [[Speech act|speech acts]] to explore institutional realities such as marriage, property, and governments, and argued that language is the key to forming social reality. Peter L. Berger described the social construction of reality as a process of externalization, objectivation, and internalization. Debate continues over whether social reality exists independently of people's involvement with it, or is created only through ongoing interaction, as social constructionism holds.

=== KEY: Social_research_a02835
HEADWORD: Social research
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Census | Data analysis
ENTRY:
Research conducted by social scientists following a systematic plan. Social research methodologies are classified as quantitative or qualitative. Quantitative designs approach social phenomena through quantifiable evidence, often relying on statistical analysis of many cases or experimental treatments to make valid and reliable general claims. Qualitative designs emphasize understanding through direct observation, communication with participants, or analysis of texts, and may stress contextual subjective accuracy over generality. Most methods combine elements of both; [[Data analysis|qualitative data analysis]] often codes raw data into systematic information and quantifies intercoder reliability. Social scientists analyze everything from [[Census|census]] survey data covering millions of people to historical documents. Methods rooted in classical sociology and statistics underpin research in political science and media studies, and are also used in program evaluation and market research.

=== KEY: Social_science_01f921
HEADWORD: Social science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Age of Enlightenment | Anthropology | Auguste Comte | Economics | Max Weber | Psychology | Sociology
ENTRY:
Branch of science devoted to the study of societies and the relationships among their members.

The term was formerly used for sociology, the original "science of society," established in the 18th century. It now encompasses [[Anthropology|anthropology]], archaeology, [[Economics|economics]], geography, history, linguistics, management, communication studies, [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Sociology|sociology]], and political science. Most positivist social scientists use methods resembling those of the natural sciences. Interpretivist scientists may instead use social critique or symbolic interpretation rather than empirically falsifiable theories. Researchers are often eclectic, combining quantitative and qualitative methods, and increasingly use big data and computational tools.

The social sciences began in the [[Age of Enlightenment]] after 1651, emerging from moral philosophy and influenced by the Industrial Revolution and the French Revolution. The term "social science" was coined in French by Mirabeau in 1767. [[Auguste Comte]] used *science sociale*, taken from Charles Fourier, and also called the field social physics, holding that it resembled the natural sciences; his positivism favored knowledge based on sense experience over metaphysical speculation. Five paths followed: large statistical surveys; Émile Durkheim's study of "social facts" and Vilfredo Pareto's theories; the interpretive approach of [[Max Weber]]; an economics-based path treating the subject as a hard science; and a path correlating knowledge with social values.

Its subfields became very quantitative, and statistics became a free-standing discipline in the first half of the 20th century. Social phenomena often resist precise prediction because of human agency, prompting debates over causal inference. Critics cite a replication crisis and ideological imbalance among academics; reforms such as preregistration and open data aim to strengthen rigor.

=== KEY: Social_stratification_3ebb79
HEADWORD: Social stratification
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Caste | Hunter-gatherer | Kinship
ENTRY:
Hierarchical categorization of a society's people into groups based on socioeconomic factors such as wealth, income, education, and ethnicity. Stratification gives groups different levels of privilege and describes the relative social position of persons within a group, category, region, or social unit. The Russian-American sociologist Pitirim Sorokin introduced the concept, along with social mobility, in his 1927 book *Social Mobility*. In modern Western societies, stratification is defined in three social classes (upper, middle, and working), each of which may be subdivided into upper, middle, and lower strata. A stratum may be formed on the basis of [[Kinship|kinship]], clan, tribe, or [[Caste|caste]], or all four. Stratification is most clear in complex state-based, polycentric, or feudal societies. Whether it first appeared among [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherers]] or began with [[Agriculture|agriculture]] remains debated. Generally, the greater a society's social complexity, the more stratification exists.

=== KEY: Social_theory_04fbaf
HEADWORD: Social theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Confucius | Ibn Khaldun
ENTRY:
Analytical framework, or paradigm, used to study and interpret social phenomena. Social theories relate to historical debates over the validity and reliability of methodologies, such as positivism and antipositivism, and over the primacy of structure or agency. As a distinct discipline, social theory emerged in the 20th century and was largely equated with critical thinking. Dynamic social theory holds that institutions and patterns of behavior are the social science equivalent of theories in the natural sciences, serving as models for achieving similar outcomes. Among early thinkers, [[Confucius]] envisaged a just society, and [[Aristotle]] did not distinguish politics from society. Early Muslim sociology appeared in the 14th century, when [[Ibn Khaldun]]'s *Muqaddimah*, the introduction to a seven-volume analysis of universal history, advanced theories of social cohesion and social conflict. Informal social theory outside academic social and political science may be called social criticism or social commentary.

=== KEY: Social_work_156afc
HEADWORD: Social work
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economics | Great Depression | Industrial Revolution | Management
ENTRY:
Academic discipline and practice-based profession concerned with meeting the basic needs of individuals, families, groups, communities, and society. It draws on liberal arts, social science, and fields such as psychology, sociology, health, political science, community development, law, and [[Economics|economics]] to conduct assessments and develop interventions. Its goals include improving people's lives, empowering individuals and communities, and achieving social reform. === KEY: Socialism_4194cd
HEADWORD: Socialism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Communism | Democratic socialism | Eastern Bloc | Friedrich Engels | Karl Marx | Revolutions of 1989 | Social democracy | Soviet Union
ENTRY:
Economic ideology and political philosophy encompassing diverse systems characterized by social ownership of the means of production, as opposed to private ownership.

Social ownership may be public, community, collective, cooperative, or employee. Non-market forms seek to eliminate the inefficiencies and crises socialists associate with capital accumulation and the profit system; market socialism retains monetary prices, factor markets, and sometimes the profit motive. [[Social democracy]] originated within the movement and, after World War II, embraced a mixed economy based on Keynesianism, with redistribution, regulation, and a welfare state.

The movement grew from revolutionary movements of the mid-to-late 18th century and from concern over the social problems socialists associated with capitalism. The word comes from Latin *sociare*, "to combine or to share." After [[Karl Marx]] and [[Friedrich Engels]], socialism by the late 19th century signified anti-capitalism and advocacy of a post-capitalist system. By the early 1920s [[Communism|communism]] and social democracy were its two dominant tendencies, and it had become the most influential secular movement of the 20th century.

The [[Soviet Union]], the first nominally socialist state, led to a widespread association of socialism with the Soviet economic model; the term has since shifted toward [[Democratic socialism|democratic socialism]]. After the [[Revolutions of 1989]], many socialist parties adopted "Third Way" politics, abandoning public ownership. Socialist politics revived in the West in the 2010s amid economic inequality and dissatisfaction with the 2008 financial crisis.

Assessments of 20th-century socialist states, including the [[Eastern Bloc]], are contested. Critics cite slower growth, shortages, famines, and suppression of dissent, and argue that central planners cannot allocate resources efficiently without market prices. Supporters point to gains in literacy, healthcare, and gender equality in some socialist states.

=== KEY: Sociality_cc7fea
HEADWORD: Sociality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Larva | Leopard | Lion | Predation
ENTRY:
Degree to which individuals in an animal population tend to associate in social groups and form cooperative societies. The inclination to associate is known as gregariousness. Sociality is a survival response to evolutionary pressures: when a mother wasp stays near her [[Larva|larvae]] in the nest, parasites are less likely to eat them, and biologists suspect that pressure from parasites and other [[Predation|predators]] selected this behavior in wasps of the family Vespidae. This illustrates parental investment, any expenditure of time, energy, or social capital to benefit offspring. An animal that cares for its young but shows no other sociality traits is said to be subsocial. The highest degree of sociality is eusociality, which involves overlapping adult generations, reproductive division of labor, cooperative care of young, and, in the most refined cases, a biological caste system. Social mammals such as spotted hyenas and [[Lion|lions]] have been found to solve innovation problems better than nonsocial predators such as [[Leopard|leopards]].

=== KEY: Socialization_0f2d25
HEADWORD: Socialization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Behaviorism | Developmental psychology | Sociology
ENTRY:
Lifelong process by which individuals internalize the norms, customs, values, and ideologies of their society. In [[Sociology|sociology]], it involves both learning and teaching and is the primary means of maintaining social and cultural continuity over time. It shapes the behavior, beliefs, and actions of adults as well as children. Socialization is closely linked to [[Developmental psychology|developmental psychology]] and [[Behaviorism|behaviorism]]. Humans need social experiences to learn their culture and to survive. Socialization may produce outcomes a society considers moral, and individual views tend toward what that society finds acceptable or normal. It provides only a partial explanation of human beliefs and behaviors, since people are shaped by both social influences and genes. Genetic studies show that a person's environment interacts with their genotype to influence behavioral outcomes.

=== KEY: Society_7ea002
HEADWORD: Society
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ant | Ibn Khaldun | José Rizal | Karl Marx | Max Weber | Roman Republic | Sociology | Émile Durkheim
ENTRY:
Large social group sharing the same spatial or social territory, typically subject to the same political authority and dominant cultural expectations.

Societies are characterized by patterns of relationships between individuals who share a distinctive culture and institutions; a society may be described as the sum total of such relationships. Human social structures are complex and highly cooperative, featuring specialization of labor through social roles. Societies construct roles and other patterns of behavior by deeming certain actions acceptable or unacceptable; these expectations are known as societal norms. Societies vary by level of technology and type of economic activity, and larger societies with larger food surpluses often show stratification or dominance patterns. Humans shape society, but society in turn shapes human beings.

The word dates to at least 1513 and comes from the 12th-century French *societe*, meaning "company," from Latin *societas* ("fellowship," "alliance," or "association") and *socius* ("comrade," friend, or ally). "Social" derives from Latin *socii*, "allies," particularly the Italian allies of the [[Roman Republic]], who rebelled against Rome in the Social War of 91–87 BC.

Humans, bonobos, and chimpanzees are highly social animals, but human society differs from chimpanzee and bonobo groups in the parental role of males, the use of language, the specialization of labor, and the tendency to build multigenerational camps, towns, or cities. Some biologists, including E. O. Wilson, classify humans with the [[Ant|ants]] as eusocial, though others disagree.

In Western [[Sociology|sociology]], three paradigms dominate. Functionalism, associated with Auguste Comte and [[Émile Durkheim]], likens individuals to organs of a body and treats society as a distinct level of reality. Conflict theory, represented by [[Karl Marx]], sees society as an economic base with a superstructure of government, family, religion, and culture, and change as driven by conflict between laborers and owners of the means of production. Symbolic interactionism is a microsociological theory of how shared language creates symbols and meanings that shape behavior. [[Max Weber]] defined action as "social" if it takes account of the behavior of others. Peter L. Berger described society as "dialectic": created by humans, it in turn molds them.

These paradigms have been criticized as Eurocentric. The Malaysian sociologist Syed Farid al-Attas points to [[Ibn Khaldun]], who distinguished nomadic life, with high social cohesion (*asabiyya*), from sedentary life, marked by secularization and a taste for luxury, and to [[José Rizal]], who argued that colonial occupation caused the "indolence" the Spanish cited to justify it.

Sociologists usually classify societies as pre-industrial, industrial, or post-industrial. Gerhard Lenski lists hunting and gathering, horticultural, agricultural, and industrial societies, along with specialized ones such as fishing or herding.

=== KEY: Sociolinguistics_a0abff
HEADWORD: Sociolinguistics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dialect | Ethnography | Linguistic anthropology | Pragmatics
ENTRY:
Descriptive and scientific study of how language is shaped by and used differently within a society. The field examines how a language varies between distinct social groups and under the influence of cultural norms, expectations, and contexts, including how that variation drives language change. Combining the older field of dialectology with the social sciences, it identifies regional [[Dialect|dialects]], sociolects, ethnolects, and other varieties. A major branch of linguistics since the second half of the 20th century, it is closely related to [[Pragmatics|pragmatics]], [[Linguistic anthropology|linguistic anthropology]], and the sociology of language. Researchers study how varieties differ across groups separated by ethnicity, religion, status, gender, education, age, or geographic barriers. Methods include [[Ethnography|ethnography]], participant observation, analysis of recordings, matched-guise tests, dialect surveys, and analysis of existing corpora.

=== KEY: Sociological_theory_d48b66
HEADWORD: Sociological theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Institution
ENTRY:
Supposition advanced by social scientists to explain facts about the social world. Sociological theories draw connections between concepts to organize and substantiate sociological knowledge, and range in scope from concise descriptions of a single social process to broad paradigms for analysis and interpretation. Some explain specific aspects of the social world and allow predictions about future events, while others serve as broad frameworks that guide further analysis. Dynamic social theory holds that [[Institution|institutions]] and patterns of behavior are the social science equivalent of theories in the natural sciences, because they embody knowledge of how society works and act as models for predictable outcomes. Prominent theorists include Talcott Parsons, Robert K. Merton, Randall Collins, James Samuel Coleman, Peter Blau, Niklas Luhmann, Immanuel Wallerstein, George Homans, Theda Skocpol, Gerhard Lenski, Pierre van den Berghe, and Jonathan H. Turner.

=== KEY: Sociology_2363eb
HEADWORD: Sociology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Auguste Comte | Ibn Khaldun | Karl Marx | Max Weber | Muqaddimah | Positivism | Social science
ENTRY:
Scientific study of human society, focusing on social behavior, patterns of social relationships, social interaction, and aspects of culture associated with everyday life.

As a [[Social science|social science]], sociology uses empirical investigation and critical analysis to build knowledge about social order and social change. Its subject matter ranges from micro-level analyses of individual interaction to macro-level analyses of social systems. Traditional focuses include social stratification, social class, social mobility, religion, law, gender, race, social norms, and deviance; it has expanded to health, the economy, education, social capital, and the Internet, where digital sociology examines the effect of digital technologies on behavior and institutions. Researchers draw on qualitative and quantitative techniques, including agent-based modeling and social network analysis.

Sociological reasoning predates the discipline. The survey can be traced to the Domesday Book of 1086, and Confucius wrote about social roles. Some sources consider [[Ibn Khaldun]], a 14th-century Muslim scholar from Tunisia, the father of sociology; his [[Muqaddimah]] was among the first works to advance social-scientific reasoning on social cohesion and conflict, though European contributors to modern sociology do not cite him.

The word combines Latin *socius* ("companion") with the Greek-derived suffix *-logy*. It was first coined in 1780 by Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès in an unpublished manuscript and was defined independently by [[Auguste Comte]] in 1838. Writing after the French Revolution, Comte proposed that social ills could be remedied through sociological [[Positivism|positivism]], outlined in the *Course in Positive Philosophy* (1830–1842). Émile Durkheim stressed empirical analysis of social facts, such as the effect of social integration on suicide rates; [[Max Weber]] introduced interpretive understanding of social action; and [[Karl Marx]] analyzed class conflict. Critics argue that left-leaning ideological bias among academics constrains the field's research.

=== KEY: Socrates_ceab0d
HEADWORD: Socrates
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Aristophanes | Classical Athens | Friedrich Nietzsche | Peloponnesian War | Socratic method | Søren Kierkegaard | Xenophon
ENTRY:
Ancient Greek philosopher from [[Classical Athens]], perhaps the first Western moral philosopher.

A major inspiration on his student Plato, Socrates wrote no texts and is known mainly through the posthumous accounts of Plato and [[Xenophon]], both his pupils, and of the comic dramatist [[Aristophanes]], his contemporary. These accounts, largely dialogues, are often contradictory and make reconstruction of his philosophy nearly impossible, a difficulty called the Socratic problem. Plato's Socrates is more ironic and humorous and practices elenchus, questioning by short questions and answers; Xenophon's stresses self-control. Scholars debate how faithfully Plato represents him.

In Plato's dialogues, Socrates and his interlocutors examine an issue, usually one of the virtues, and end at an impasse, unable to define what they thought they understood. He often proclaims his ignorance, saying he is sure only that he does not know. The [[Socratic method]] and Socratic irony take his name. In his defense he described himself as a gadfly stinging the complacent Athenian society.

He served as a hoplite in the [[Peloponnesian War]], at Potidaea, Delium, and Amphipolis. A polarizing figure, he was accused of impiety and corrupting the youth in 399 BC. After a one-day trial he was sentenced to death and, as Plato relates, refused offers of escape and drank hemlock.

Socrates influenced philosophers in later antiquity, was studied by medieval and Islamic scholars, and played an important role in Italian Renaissance humanism. Interest continued in the works of [[Søren Kierkegaard]] and [[Friedrich Nietzsche]].

=== KEY: Socratic_method_5b7898
HEADWORD: Socratic method
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dialectic | Plato | Socrates
ENTRY:
Form of argumentative dialogue in which questions and clarifications lead a conversation partner to a conclusion on their own, or else expose the breakdown of their reasoning and force them to admit ignorance. The method is also called Socratic debate, the maieutic method, or the Socratic [[Dialectic|dialectic]], and is sometimes equated with the Greek term *elenchus*. Socratic dialogues feature in many works of the ancient Greek philosopher [[Plato]], in which a fictionalized version of his teacher [[Socrates]] debates philosophical issues with a partner. In Plato's dialogue *Theaetetus*, Socrates describes his method as "midwifery" (*maieutikós*), because it helps interlocutors bring out their understanding as a child develops in the womb until ready for birth. It begins with commonly held beliefs and scrutinizes them to determine their internal consistency and coherence with other beliefs, bringing everyone closer to the truth. Modified forms are used today in a variety of pedagogical contexts.

=== KEY: Soil_e4b9dc
HEADWORD: Soil
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archean | Carbon | Cenozoic | Pedosphere | Pleistocene | Soil science | Weathering
ENTRY:
Mixture of organic matter, minerals, gases, water, and organisms that together support the life of plants and soil organisms.

Also called earth, soil is a three-state system of solids, liquids, and gases. A typical soil is about 50% solids (45% mineral, 5% organic matter) and 50% pores, half filled with water and half with gas. Most soils have a dry bulk density between 1.1 and 1.6 g/cm³, though particle density is 2.6 to 2.7 g/cm³. Compaction reduces pore space and keeps air and water from reaching roots and organisms.

Soil is a product of climate, relief, organisms, and parent materials interacting over time, through processes that include [[Weathering|weathering]] and erosion. Given time, it develops layers called horizons. Little soil is older than the [[Pleistocene]] and none is older than the [[Cenozoic]], though fossilized soils survive from the [[Archean]]. The Earth's body of soil is the [[Pedosphere|pedosphere]], which interfaces with the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and biosphere.

Soil is a medium for plant growth, a means of water storage and purification, a modifier of the atmosphere, and a habitat; a gram can contain billions of organisms belonging to thousands of species, mostly microbial and largely unexplored. It can degrade contaminants, a property called natural attenuation. As a reservoir of [[Carbon|carbon]], it is potentially one of the most reactive to human disturbance and climate change; the prediction that warming will make soils release more carbon dioxide has been questioned.

[[Soil science]] has two branches: edaphology, which studies the influence of soils on living things, and pedology, which studies their formation, morphology, and classification. In engineering, soil belongs to the broader concept of regolith, which includes loose material above bedrock, as on the Moon.

=== KEY: Soil_fertility_3c7a53

=== KEY: Solar_System_5c65f7
HEADWORD: Solar System
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Asteroid belt | Dwarf planet | Gas giant | Kuiper belt | Oort cloud | Sun | Terrestrial planet
ENTRY:
Gravitationally bound system of the Sun and the objects that orbit it, most prominently its eight planets, of which Earth is one.

The Solar System is an isolated single-star planetary system within the Milky Way. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago (4.568 billion years ago, by one measure) when a dense region of a molecular cloud, likely several light-years across, collapsed, creating the Sun and a protoplanetary disc of roughly 200 AU in diameter from which the orbiting bodies assembled by accretion. Within 50 million years the pressure and density of hydrogen at the protostar's center sufficed for thermonuclear fusion. The [[Sun]] holds 99.86% of the system's total mass, and solar wind from it created the heliosphere and swept the remaining gas and dust into interstellar space.

The four [[Terrestrial planet|terrestrial planets]], Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, lie closest to the Sun; only Earth and Mars orbit within its habitable zone. Beyond the frost line, at about five astronomical units (AU), are two [[Gas giant|gas giants]], Jupiter and Saturn, and two ice giants, Uranus and Neptune. Jupiter and Saturn hold nearly 90% of the non-stellar mass. Objects of planetary mass that do not dominate their orbits are [[Dwarf planet|dwarf planets]]: the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center lists Ceres, Pluto, Eris, Makemake, and Haumea, and four others, Orcus, Quaoar, Gonggong, and Sedna, are generally identified as such. Ceres lies in the [[Asteroid belt]] between Mars and Jupiter, which holds most asteroids; the other dwarf planets are trans-Neptunian objects, found in the [[Kuiper belt]] just outside Neptune or in the farther scattered disc.

Many bodies are natural satellites, or moons. None orbit Mercury or Venus, while more than 400 orbit Jupiter and Saturn combined. Ganymede and Titan are larger than Mercury, though less massive, and the seven most massive moons, including Earth's Moon, are larger and more massive than any dwarf planet. Potential habitability has been identified on moons such as Europa and Enceladus.

The solar wind fills the system with charged plasma particles, which together with interplanetary dust, gas, and cosmic rays form the interplanetary medium. At around 70–90 AU the interstellar medium halts the solar wind, forming the heliopause; Voyager 1 crossed it at about 122 AU and Voyager 2 at about 119 AU. The theorized [[Oort cloud]], the source of long-period comets, extends from somewhere beyond 2,000 AU to the edge of the Sun's Hill sphere, at 178,000–227,000 AU (2.81–3.59 ly), where the Sun's gravitational potential equals the galactic potential. The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 269,000 AU (4.25 ly) away. Both lie within the Local Bubble, a region of the Milky Way about 1,000 light-years wide.

=== KEY: Solar_energy_103bad
HEADWORD: Solar energy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Convection | Fossil fuel | Photosynthesis | Renewable energy | Solar power | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Radiant energy from the Sun's light and heat, harnessed by technologies such as solar electricity, solar thermal energy, and solar architecture.

It is an essential source of [[Renewable energy|renewable energy]], and its technologies are classed as passive or active. Active techniques use photovoltaic systems, concentrated solar power, solar thermal collectors, pumps, and fans to produce [[Solar power|solar power]] and heat. Passive techniques include designing buildings for daylighting, choosing materials with favorable thermal mass, and organizing spaces to circulate air. All renewable energy except geothermal and tidal power derives from the Sun.

Earth receives 174 petawatts (PW) of incoming solar radiation at the upper atmosphere. About 30% is reflected and the remaining 122 PW is absorbed, about 3,850,000 exajoules (EJ) per year. Absorbed sunlight drives [[Convection|convection]] and the [[Water cycle|water cycle]] and keeps the surface at an average of 14 °C. Through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], plants capture approximately 3,000 EJ per year in biomass, the source of food, wood, and [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]]. Geography, cloud cover, and available land limit what humans can use; a 2000 estimate put the global potential at 1,600 to 49,800 EJ per year. In 2021 the Carbon Tracker Initiative estimated that all energy from solar alone would need 450,000 km², about the area of Sweden.

At the 1878 Universal Exposition in Paris, Augustin Mouchot demonstrated a solar steam engine but could not continue because of cheap coal. In 1908 Frank Shuman formed the Sun Power Company to build larger plants.

Photovoltaic additions reached a record 452 gigawatts (GW) in 2024, about 77% of new renewable capacity that year. Commercial modules typically convert 15–22% of sunlight. Critics cite intermittency, which requires storage or backup, and manufacturing emissions.

=== KEY: Solid_e41480
HEADWORD: Solid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amorphous solid | Diamond | Elastic modulus | Gallium | Kinetic energy | Materials science | Solid-state physics | Zero-point energy
ENTRY:
State of matter in which atoms are closely packed and difficult to move past one another. Solids resist compression, expansion, and external forces that would alter their shape, to a degree that depends on the material. A solid has the least [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] per atom or molecule of any phase and forms when a fluid, liquid or gas, is cooled below the substance's melting point, an intrinsic property independent of quantity. Unlike liquids, solids do not flow to take the shape of a container. They expand when heated, though far less than other phases, and on further heating melt into a liquid or sublimate directly into a gas. Melting points range from 0.10 K for helium-3 under 30 bars (3 MPa) to about 4,100 K at 1 atm for hafnium carbonitride.

Atoms in a solid are bound either in regular geometric lattices, forming crystalline solids such as metals and water ice, or in irregular arrangements, forming [[Amorphous solid|amorphous solids]] such as glass and plastic. Single-element phases are called allotropes, as with [[Diamond|diamond]] and graphite for carbon. Non-porous solids strongly resist compression because of repulsion between neighboring electron clouds, and resistance to deformation is quantified by the [[Elastic modulus|elastic modulus]] and tensile strength. For most substances the solid is denser than the liquid; water, [[Gallium|gallium]], and plutonium are exceptions. Among naturally occurring elements, helium stays liquid even at absolute zero because of [[Zero-point energy|zero-point energy]], while arsenic and carbon sublimate at around 900 K and 3,950 K respectively. The branch of physics concerned with solids is [[Solid-state physics|solid-state physics]], a major part of condensed matter physics; [[Materials science|materials science]] studies how a solid's composition and properties are linked.

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aerospace engineering | Composite material | Continuum mechanics | Force | Materials science | Tensor
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Continuum mechanics|continuum mechanics]] that studies the behavior of solid materials, especially their motion and deformation under the action of [[Force|forces]], temperature changes, phase changes and other agents. It is fundamental to civil, [[Aerospace engineering|aerospace]], nuclear, biomedical and mechanical engineering, to geology, and to branches of physics and chemistry such as [[Materials science|materials science]]. A solid can support a substantial shearing force over a given time scale, which distinguishes it from a fluid. One of the most common practical applications is the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation. Solid mechanics uses [[Tensor|tensors]] to describe stresses, strains and the relationship between them. Its subfields include stability of structures, vibrations, fracture and damage mechanics, and the study of [[Composite material|composite materials]]. Its computational methods include the finite element method.

=== KEY: Solid_state_drive_ca2038
HEADWORD: Solid-state drive
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bit | Hard disk drive | Integrated circuit | Mobile device | Personal computer
ENTRY:
Computer storage device that uses [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]], typically NAND flash memory, to store data persistently. Unlike traditional [[Hard disk drive|hard disk drives]] (HDDs), SSDs have no moving parts, which gives faster data access, lower latency, greater resistance to physical shock, lower power consumption and silent operation. SSDs are used in [[Personal computer|personal computers]], enterprise servers and [[Mobile device|mobile devices]], often interfacing with a system in the same way as HDDs. They are generally more expensive per gigabyte and have a finite number of write cycles. Each memory cell stores one [[Bit|bit]] in single-level cells (SLC), while multi-level, triple-level and quad-level cells (QLC) store more data but offer lower performance and endurance. Solid-state hybrid drives (SSHDs) combine SSD and HDD technologies to improve performance at a lower cost than pure SSDs.

=== KEY: Solid_state_physics_5584ea
HEADWORD: Solid-state physics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Condensed matter physics | Covalent bond | Crystal | Crystallography | Materials science | Quantum mechanics | Semiconductor | Transistor
ENTRY:
Branch of physics that studies rigid matter, or solids, through methods such as [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], [[Crystallography|crystallography]], electromagnetism and metallurgy. It is the largest branch of [[Condensed matter physics|condensed matter physics]] and forms the theoretical basis of [[Materials science|materials science]]. Along with solid-state chemistry, it has direct applications in [[Transistor|transistors]] and [[Semiconductor|semiconductors]]. Solids may be held together by ionic bonds, as in sodium chloride, by [[Covalent bond|covalent bonds]], by metallic bonding, or by van der Waals forces, as in the noble gases. Most work focuses on [[Crystal|crystals]], whose periodic atomic arrangement facilitates mathematical modeling. Although solids had been studied for centuries, the field emerged as a separate discipline in the 1940s, when the Division of Solid State Physics was established within the American Physical Society. By the early 1960s it was the society's largest division.

Genus of about 100 to 120 species of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] in the family [[Asteraceae|Asteraceae]], commonly called goldenrod. Most are herbaceous perennials found in open areas such as meadows, prairies and savannas. Most species are native to [[North America|North America]], including Mexico, with a few native to South America and Eurasia. Some species produce abundant nectar when moisture is plentiful or the weather is warm and sunny.

=== KEY: Solidarity_2022ac
HEADWORD: Solidarity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Altruism | Auguste Comte | Conservatism | French Revolution | Napoleonic Code | Émile Durkheim
ENTRY:
Mutual moral and material support among the members of a group in times of need. In the [[Napoleonic Code|Napoleonic Code]], solidarity meant joint liability of debtors toward a common creditor. After the [[French Revolution|French Revolution]], [[Conservatism|conservatives]] detached the concept from the law as a reaction against rapid social change. [[Auguste Comte|Auguste Comte]], the so-called founder of sociology, adopted it to describe social interdependence and linked it to [[Altruism|altruism]]. By the turn of the 20th century the term had become a generic word for progressive values, and the Paris Communards replaced the revolutionary slogan of fraternity with solidarity. [[Émile Durkheim|Émile Durkheim]] distinguished mechanical solidarity, based on the homogeneity of traditional small-scale societies, from organic solidarity, based on the interdependence of specialized work in modern industrial societies, in *The Division of Labour in Society* (1893).

=== KEY: Solifugae_e070a8
HEADWORD: Solifugae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arachnid | Arthropod | Scorpion | Spider
ENTRY:
Order of [[Arachnid|arachnids]] known as solifuges, sun spiders, camel spiders or wind scorpions, with more than 1,000 described species in about 147 genera. Despite their common names, they differ from both [[Spider|spiders]] and [[Scorpion|scorpions]]. Most species live in dry climates and feed opportunistically on ground-dwelling [[Arthropod|arthropods]] and other small animals. The largest reach 12–15 cm (5–6 in) including legs, though most are closer to 5 cm (2 in) in body length. Unlike spiders, they lack spinnerets and silk and cannot spin webs. Urban legends exaggerate their size, speed and danger to humans, which is negligible.

=== KEY: Solipsism_64722d
HEADWORD: Solipsism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mind | René Descartes | Skepticism
ENTRY:
Philosophical position that only one's own [[Mind|mind]] is certain to exist. Knowledge of anything outside one's own mind, including the external world and other minds, is uncertain and cannot be conclusively known. Metaphysical solipsism holds that the self is the only existing reality and that other realities are its representations. Epistemological solipsism regards the existence of an external world as an unresolvable question rather than a falsehood. Methodological solipsism is an agnostic variant, often used as a thought experiment to assist [[Skepticism|skepticism]], as in the Cartesian skepticism of [[René Descartes|René Descartes]].

=== KEY: Solomon_161a5f
HEADWORD: Solomon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David | Hebrew Bible | Idolatry | New Testament | Quran | Yahweh
ENTRY:
Biblical king of ancient Israel (r. c. 970–931 BC), the son and successor of [[David|David]]. According to the [[Hebrew Bible|Hebrew Bible]], his reign brought commercial prosperity through alliances and trade, but his accumulation of wealth, horses and foreign wives, who introduced [[Idolatry|idolatry]], led to divine punishment. After his death his son Rehoboam's harsh policies led the northern Israelites to reject the Davidic line and split the kingdom into Israel and Judah. Solomon is portrayed as wealthy, wise and a dedicated follower of [[Yahweh|Yahweh]], and is traditionally regarded as the author of Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and Song of Songs. In the [[New Testament|New Testament]] he is a teacher of wisdom, and the [[Quran|Quran]] regards him as a major prophet. Current scholarship generally allows for a historical Solomon, but the opulence of his biblical empire is considered highly likely to be an anachronistic exaggeration.

=== KEY: Solomon_Islands_a573f4

ENTRY:
Archipelagic country of six major islands and more than 1,000 smaller ones in [[Melanesia|Melanesia]] and [[Oceania|Oceania]], northeast of [[Australia|Australia]]. It has a total area of 28,896 sq km and a population of 828,857 (pop., 2025). Its capital and largest city, Honiara, is on Guadalcanal. === KEY: Solon_4244e7
HEADWORD: Solon
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Athens | Demosthenes | Draco (legislator) | Herodotus | Plutarch | Sparta
ENTRY:
Archaic Athenian statesman, lawmaker, political philosopher and poet, one of the Seven Sages of Greece. He is credited with laying the foundations of democracy in [[Athens|Athens]]. His reforms included debt relief known as *seisachtheia* ("shaking off of burdens"), and he repealed all of [[Draco (legislator)|Draco's laws]] except those on homicide. According to Diogenes Laërtius he was chosen archon, or chief magistrate, in 594 BC. Around 595 BC, Athenian forces under Solon defeated the Megarians in the dispute over Salamis, and [[Sparta|Spartan]] arbitrators later awarded the island to Athens. [[Aristotle|Aristotle]] called him "the first people's champion", and [[Demosthenes|Demosthenes]] credited his reforms with starting a golden age. Solon's works survive only in fragments, and the main sources, including [[Herodotus|Herodotus]] and [[Plutarch|Plutarch]], wrote long after his death.

=== KEY: Solstice_490e0f
HEADWORD: Solstice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equator | Equinox | Summer solstice | Sun | Winter solstice
ENTRY:
Point in the year when the [[Sun|Sun]]'s path reaches its most northerly or southerly extent relative to the celestial equator. Two solstices occur each year, around 20–22 June and 20–22 December, and the term also refers to the day on which this occurs. In many countries the seasons are defined by reference to the solstices and [[Equinox|equinoxes]]. Away from the [[Equator|equator]] and the poles, the summer and winter solstices are the days with the longest and shortest periods of daylight. The word derives from Latin *sol* (sun) and *sistere* (to stand still), because the Sun's motion in declination appears to stop before reversing. When it is the [[Summer solstice|summer solstice]] at one pole, it is the [[Winter solstice|winter solstice]] at the other.

=== KEY: Solution_chemistry_35eecc
HEADWORD: Solution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alloy | Bronze | Ethanol | Mixture | Steel | Water
ENTRY:
Homogeneous [[Mixture|mixture]] of a solute and a solvent, in which the solvent is the substance present in the greatest amount. The IUPAC defines a solution as a liquid or solid phase containing more than one substance, and calls it dilute when the solutes make up a small share of the mixture. Solvents can be gases, liquids or solids. Air is a gaseous solution, and oxygen dissolved in [[Water|water]] allows fish to breathe underwater. [[Ethanol|Ethanol]] dissolved in water makes alcoholic beverages, and sugar water contains dissolved sucrose. Solid solutions include [[Steel|steel]], essentially carbon atoms in a crystalline matrix of iron atoms, and [[Alloy|alloys]] such as [[Bronze|bronze]]. Hydrogen dissolves well in palladium, a property studied for hydrogen storage. Liquids that can completely dissolve in each other are miscible, and once a solution is saturated, no more solute can dissolve.

=== KEY: Somali_Civil_War_b8636e
HEADWORD: Somali Civil War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: African Union | Insurgency | Somalia
ENTRY:
Ongoing civil war in [[Somalia|Somalia]] that grew out of resistance to the military junta of Siad Barre in the 1980s. Clan-based armed groups overthrew Barre in 1991, and the country became a failed state. UN missions began in 1992; the UN withdrew in 1995 after a 1993 armed conflict between [[Insurgency|insurgents]] and UN troops. In 2006 Ethiopian troops invaded to depose the Islamic Courts Union and install the Transitional Federal Government. Al-Shabaab then fought the Somali government and [[African Union|African Union]] peacekeepers. The Federal Government of Somalia, the first permanent central government since the war began, was established in August 2012.

=== KEY: Somali_language_f167d3
HEADWORD: Somali language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arabic | Djibouti | Ethiopia | Kenya | Latin script | Somalia | Somaliland
ENTRY:
Cushitic language of the Afroasiatic family, spoken primarily in Greater Somalia and by the Somali diaspora. It is an official language in [[Somalia|Somalia]], [[Somaliland|Somaliland]] and [[Ethiopia|Ethiopia]], a national language in [[Djibouti|Djibouti]], and a recognized minority language in [[Kenya|Kenya]]. As of 2021 there were approximately 24 million speakers, about 17 million of them in Somalia. Somali is the best-documented Cushitic language, with academic studies dating back to the late 19th century. Its official script is the [[Latin script|Latin alphabet]], adopted in 1972, although an [[Arabic|Arabic]] alphabet and indigenous scripts such as Osmanya are used informally. Since January 1973 it has been the primary language of administration and education in Somalia. The Regional Somali Language Academy, established in June 2013 in Djibouti City, is mandated to preserve the language.
