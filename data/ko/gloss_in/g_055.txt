=== KEY: Great_Plains_9e0872
HEADWORD: Great Plains
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alberta | American bison | Manitoba | Prairie | Rocky Mountains | Saskatchewan | Steppe
ENTRY:
Broad expanse of flatland in North America, east of the [[Rocky Mountains]]. It is the western part of the Interior Plains and is largely covered by [[Prairie|prairie]], [[Steppe|steppe]] and grassland. It covers most or all of Kansas, Nebraska, and North and South Dakota, eastern parts of Colorado, Montana and Wyoming, and the southern parts of [[Alberta]], [[Saskatchewan]] and [[Manitoba]]. Much of the region was once home to [[American bison]] herds, which were hunted to near extinction in the mid- to late 19th century. Nevin Fenneman's 1916 study *Physiographic Subdivision of the United States* brought the term "Great Plains" into wider use; before then the area was usually called the High Plains. Since European settlement, much of the native grassland has been converted to cropland and rangeland, making the area central to North American agriculture. In Canada the term is rarely used, and the region is known as the Canadian Prairies.

=== KEY: Great_Purge_6166b4
HEADWORD: Great Purge
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bolsheviks | Communist Party of the Soviet Union | Gulag | Joseph Stalin | Leon Trotsky | Nikolai Bukharin
ENTRY:
Political purge in the Soviet Union, 1936–1939. Launched by [[Joseph Stalin]] after the 1934 assassination of Sergei Kirov, it used show trials and mass operations to remove suspected dissenters from the [[Communist Party of the Soviet Union]], particularly [[Bolsheviks|Old Bolsheviks]]. The secret police, the NKVD, carried out most of the purge. Figures such as [[Nikolai Bukharin]] were removed, imprisoned or executed, and the purge reached the Red Army high command. Hundreds of thousands were accused of political crimes and were executed or sent to the [[Gulag]] labor camps. The purge peaked from September 1936 to August 1938 under NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov, hence the name *Yezhovshchina*. Scholars estimate the death toll at 700,000 to 1.2 million. In 1938 Stalin criticized the NKVD for mass executions and oversaw the executions of its chiefs, Genrikh Yagoda and Yezhov. [[Leon Trotsky]], who had been exiled, survived the purge but was later assassinated in Mexico on Stalin's orders.

=== KEY: Great_Pyramid_of_Giza_e275ae
HEADWORD: Great Pyramid of Giza
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aswan | Cairo | Nile | Pharaoh | Seven Wonders of the Ancient World | World Heritage Site
ENTRY:
Largest of the Egyptian pyramids, on the Giza Plateau near [[Cairo]], Egypt. It was built as the tomb of [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]] Khufu of the Fourth Dynasty, c. 2600 BC, over about 26 years. Hemiunu, Khufu's vizier, is believed by some to have been its architect. Initially 146.6 m (481 ft) tall, it was the world's tallest human-made structure for more than 3,700 years. Most of its smooth white limestone casing has since been removed, lowering it to 138.5 m (454.4 ft). The pyramid used an estimated 2.3 million large blocks weighing 6 million tonnes in total. Granite blocks weighing up to 80 tonnes were shipped by boat on the [[Nile]] from [[Aswan]] for the King's Chamber. It is the oldest of the [[Seven Wonders of the Ancient World]] and the only one that remains largely intact. It forms part of the UNESCO [[World Heritage Site]] Memphis and its Necropolis.

=== KEY: Great_Rift_Valley_eb7836
HEADWORD: Great Rift Valley
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dead Sea | Lake Tanganyika | Mount Kilimanjaro | Mozambique | Red Sea | Syria
ENTRY:
Series of contiguous depressions, about 6,500 km long, running from northern [[Syria]] to [[Mozambique]]. In its original definition the valley runs through the [[Red Sea]]. British geologist John Walter Gregory introduced the term in 1896, following work by Austrian geologist Eduard Suess. Geologists now prefer the Afro-Arabian Rift System, but the term remains in use in geography, zoology, ecology and paleontology. The northern section includes the [[Dead Sea]] transform boundary. The East African Rift splits south of the Afar triangle into the Albertine and Gregory rifts. Rift lakes include [[Lake Tanganyika]], which at 673 km is the longest freshwater lake in the world. Fossils and early stone tools from Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania date back 2.6 million years. Volcanism has built peaks such as [[Mount Kilimanjaro]], an inactive stratovolcano standing 5,895 m high.

Limestone statue of a resting sphinx on the Giza Plateau, Egypt. The mythical creature has the head of a human and the body of a [[Lion|lion]]. Supreme life force in the traditional beliefs of many Indigenous cultures in [[Canada]] and the [[United States]]. Series of conflicts between the [[Ottoman Empire]] and the Holy League, 1683–1699. === KEY: Great_Victoria_Desert_61193c
HEADWORD: Great Victoria Desert
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Sparsely populated desert ecoregion in [[Western Australia]] and [[South Australia]]. It is [[Australia|Australia's]] largest desert, covering 348,750 sq km (134,650 sq mi) from the Eastern Goldfields of Western Australia to the Gawler Ranges. Explorer Ernest Giles was the first European to cross it, in 1875, and named it after [[Queen Victoria]]. The desert has sandhills, grassland plains, gibber plains and salt lakes. === KEY: Great_Wall_of_China_a40279

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: China | Eurasian Steppe | Ming dynasty | Qin dynasty | Silk Road
ENTRY:
Series of fortifications along the historical northern borders of [[China]]. Built to protect against nomadic groups from the [[Eurasian Steppe]], the first walls date to the 7th century BC and were joined together in the [[Qin dynasty]]. Successive dynasties expanded the system, and the best-known sections were built by the [[Ming dynasty]] (1368–1644). By the 19th century, "the Great Wall of China" had become the standard English name.

=== KEY: Great_Zimbabwe_87747e

ENTRY:
Ruined medieval city in southeastern [[Zimbabwe]]. The city stands near Masvingo and served as the capital of the Kingdom of Great Zimbabwe from the 13th century. === KEY: Great_white_shark_c66950
HEADWORD: Great white shark
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apex predator | Orca | Shark
ENTRY:
Large [[Shark|shark]] species, *Carcharodon carcharias*, of the mackerel shark family Lamnidae. It has a grayish upperside and a white underside. Adults commonly measure 4 to 5 m, and the largest reliably measured specimen was 6 m long. The species is an [[Apex predator|apex predator]] and is partially warm-blooded, an adaptation that allows it to remain active in colder waters. It has about 300 triangular, serrated teeth that are continuously replaced, and its fatty liver can exceed a quarter of its body weight, providing buoyancy. It preys on seals, dolphins, squid, fish and other sharks, and it is occasionally preyed on by [[Orca|orcas]]. The 1974 novel *Jaws* and its 1975 film adaptation portray it as a ferocious man-eater, but white sharks normally do not prey on humans, and most bites are due to curiosity or possibly mistaken identity. The IUCN lists the species as vulnerable worldwide and critically endangered in European and Mediterranean waters, and several governments have banned catching and killing it.

=== KEY: Greed_7d7a11

ENTRY:
Insatiable desire for material or social gain, often at the expense of others. Also called avarice, greed may target food, money, land, possessions, status, or power. Modern economic thought frequently distinguishes greed from self-interest. In Classical Greek thought, [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] discussed an unjust desire to gain at others' expense. === KEY: Greek_War_of_Independence_ebf10e
HEADWORD: Greek War of Independence
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Successful revolt by Greek revolutionaries against the [[Ottoman Empire]], fought from 1821 to 1829. The secret Filiki Eteria had planned an uprising for March 25, 1821, but its discovery forced an earlier start. Greece was recognized as independent under the London Protocol of February 1830, and Prince Otto of Bavaria became its first king. The revolution's slogan, "Freedom or death", became the national motto.

=== KEY: Greek_alphabet_ce3f12
HEADWORD: Greek alphabet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consonant | Cyrillic script | Greek language | Latin script | Phoenician alphabet | Vowel
ENTRY:
Script used to write the [[Greek language]] since the late 9th or early 8th century BC. It was derived from the earlier [[Phoenician alphabet]] and is the earliest known alphabetic script to write [[Vowel|vowels]] systematically as well as [[Consonant|consonants]]. The Ionic-based Euclidean alphabet of 24 letters, ordered from alpha to omega, became standard by the end of the 4th century BC and is still used for Greek writing. Throughout antiquity Greek had only one uppercase form of each letter, and Byzantine scribes had begun using lowercase forms by the 9th century. The alphabet is the ancestor of the [[Latin script]], the [[Cyrillic script]], and other scripts, and its letters serve as international symbols in mathematics, science, and other fields. Ancient and Modern Greek use different sound values and diacritics.

=== KEY: Greek_language_2f0690
HEADWORD: Greek language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Byzantine Empire | English language | European Union | Greek alphabet | Homer | Indo-European languages | Septuagint
ENTRY:
[[Indo-European languages|Indo-European language]] forming an independent Hellenic branch of that family. It is native to Greece, Cyprus, Egypt, Turkey, Italy (in Calabria and Salento), southern Albania, and other regions of the Balkans, Caucasus, Black Sea coast, and Eastern Mediterranean. It has the longest documented history of any Indo-European language, with at least 3,400 years of written records. Its writing system, the [[Greek alphabet]], has been used for approximately 2,800 years; earlier, Greek was written in Linear B and the Cypriot syllabary. The earliest written evidence is a Linear B tablet found in Messenia that dates to between 1450 and 1350 BC.

The language is conventionally divided into Proto-Greek, Mycenaean Greek, Ancient Greek, Koine Greek, Medieval or Byzantine Greek, and Modern Greek. Koine, the first common Greek dialect, arose from the fusion of Ionian with Attic and can be traced to the armies and conquered territories of [[Alexander the Great]]. It was spoken from Egypt to the fringes of India and became the first or second language of the Roman Empire. Koine was the original language of the New Testament, and the Hebrew Bible was translated into it as the [[Septuagint]]. Medieval Greek continued Koine until the end of the [[Byzantine Empire]] in the 15th century.

The epic poetry of [[Homer]] begins an ancient Greek literature of lasting importance, and many foundational texts of science and philosophy were composed in Greek. In its modern form Greek is the official language of Greece and Cyprus and one of the 24 official languages of the [[European Union]]. It is spoken by at least 13.5 million people. Greek roots are widely used to coin new words, and Greek and Latin remain the predominant sources of international scientific vocabulary in [[English language|English]] and other languages.

=== KEY: Greek_mythology_f28837
HEADWORD: Greek mythology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Heracles | Hesiod | Homer | Minotaur | Mount Olympus | Roman mythology | Zeus
ENTRY:
Body of myths and genre of folklore originating among the ancient Greeks, concerning the origin and nature of the world, the lives of deities and heroes, and the significance of cult and ritual practices. Together with [[Roman mythology]] it forms classical mythology. Modern scholars study the myths for what they reveal about the religious and political institutions of [[Ancient Greece]] and about mythmaking itself.

The myths were first transmitted in an oral-poetic tradition, most likely by Minoan and Mycenaean singers beginning in the 18th century BC. The myths of the heroes of the Trojan War and its aftermath became part of the oral tradition behind [[Homer]]'s epic poems, the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*. [[Hesiod]]'s *Theogony* and *Works and Days* contain accounts of the genesis of the world, the succession of divine rulers, the human ages, and the origin of sacrificial practices. Myths also survive in the Homeric Hymns, the Epic Cycle, lyric poetry, fifth-century BC drama, and Hellenistic and Roman-era writers such as Plutarch and Pausanias.

Pictorial evidence is equally important. Vase paintings, votive gifts, and other artifacts depict gods, heroes, and mythic episodes; geometric pottery of the eighth century BC shows scenes from the Epic Cycle and the adventures of [[Heracles]].

The Olympian gods, who reside on [[Mount Olympus]] under [[Zeus]], include Poseidon, Hades, Hera, Athena, and Apollo. Heroes such as Heracles and Theseus, slayer of the [[Minotaur]], serve as intermediaries between gods and humans. The myths have had an extensive influence on the culture, arts, and literature of Western civilization.

=== KEY: Green_Revolution_3f2089
HEADWORD: Green Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fertilizer | Irrigation | Norman Borlaug | Pesticide | Rice | Wheat
ENTRY:
Agricultural movement in which technology transfer significantly increased crop yields. It emerged in developed countries in the early 20th century and spread globally until the late 1980s. Its high-yielding varieties of [[Wheat|wheat]] and [[Rice|rice]] required chemical [[Fertilizer|fertilizers]], [[Pesticide|pesticides]], and controlled [[Irrigation|irrigation]]. === KEY: Green_algae_8f136c
HEADWORD: Green algae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cellulose | Chloroplast | Flagellate | Lichen | Mitosis | Paramecium | Starch
ENTRY:
Paraphyletic group of chlorophyll-containing autotrophic algae. Green algae range from unicellular and colonial [[Flagellate|flagellates]] to coccoid and filamentous forms, and to macroscopic seaweeds. There are about 22,000 species. Their [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] contain chlorophyll a and b, and their cell walls usually contain [[Cellulose|cellulose]]. They store carbohydrate as [[Starch|starch]]. Together with land plants, they form the clade Viridiplantae. Some green algae live symbiotically in the ciliate [[Paramecium]], and others form [[Lichen|lichens]] with fungi. Some live on humid soil, rocks, or tree bark, and in Antarctica *Prasiola crispa* forms large carpets near bird colonies. The chloroplasts of some dinoflagellates, euglenids, and chlorarachniophytes were acquired from ingested green algae. Members of the class Chlorophyceae undergo [[Mitosis|closed mitosis]], while charophytes and land plants undergo open mitosis.

=== KEY: Greenhouse_effect_9d8ff5
HEADWORD: Greenhouse effect
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon dioxide | Earth | Greenhouse gas | Industrial Revolution | Joseph Fourier | Jupiter | Methane | Sun
ENTRY:
Atmospheric heat retention by heat-trapping [[Greenhouse gas|gases]]. Greenhouse gases absorb the longwave radiation emitted by a planet's surface, so less heat escapes to space and the surface warms. Surface heating can come from an internal source, as on [[Jupiter]], or from a host star, as with the [[Sun]] for [[Earth]]. Without the effect, Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C, compared with a 20th-century average of about 14 °C. Burning fossil fuels has increased [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and [[Methane|methane]], and global warming of about 1.2 °C had occurred by 2023 since the [[Industrial Revolution]]. [[Joseph Fourier]] proposed the effect in 1824, and Nils Gustaf Ekholm first applied the term greenhouse to it in 1901.

Austrian biologist and Augustinian friar. Born into a German-speaking family in the Silesian part of the [[Austrian Empire]], he later became abbot of St. Thomas' Abbey in Brno. === KEY: Group_mathematics_00f732

ENTRY:
Set with an associative operation, an identity element and an inverse for every element. For example, the [[Integer|integers]] form a group under addition. The concept was developed to treat numbers, geometric shapes and polynomial roots in a unified way, and some authors regard it as a central organizing principle of contemporary mathematics. In geometry, the symmetries of an object form a group, and Lie groups appear in the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics. Association of artisans or merchants overseeing the practice of a craft or trade in a particular territory. Guilds were most extensive in medieval Europe, though similar groups existed before and after that period and in other parts of the world. Typically, the key privilege of guild members was control of trade in a given city, and guilds might set minimum or maximum prices, hours of trading and numbers of apprentices. Critics argued that these rules reduced free competition, but defenders maintained that guilds protected professional standards. Craft guilds transmitted skills through apprenticeship, journeymanship and mastery, and in major cities such as Florence, [[Paris]] and [[Barcelona]] they became central to economic and civic life. Guilds also maintained welfare funds for sick or elderly members and supported widows and orphans.

=== KEY: Guillaume_Du_Fay_3e6940

ENTRY:
Early Renaissance composer and music theorist, variously described as French or Franco-Flemish. Considered the leading European composer of his time, his music was widely performed and reproduced. He was associated with the Burgundian School, particularly his colleague Gilles Binchois, but was never a regular member of the Burgundian chapel. Having joined the papal choir in [[Rome]] in 1428, he wrote his most admired work, the motet *Nuper Rosarum Flores*, which celebrated the consecration of [[Filippo Brunelleschi]]'s dome for the [[Florence Cathedral]]. His later cyclic masses, based on cantus firmus, include *Missa Se la face ay pale*. He has been described as leading the first generation of European musicians who were primarily considered composers by occupation.

ENTRY:
French composer and poet, central figure of the *ars nova* style in late medieval music. Regarded as the most significant French composer and poet of the 14th century, he is often seen as the century's leading European composer. Modern musicologists use his death to separate the *ars nova* from the subsequent *ars subtilior* movement. He was crucial in developing the motet and secular song forms, including the lai and the formes fixes: rondeau, virelai and ballade. His *Messe de Nostre Dame* is the earliest known complete setting of the Ordinary of the Mass attributable to a single composer. He was secretary to John I, Count of [[Luxembourg]] and King of [[Bohemia]], from 1323 to 1346, and survived the [[Black Death]]. His poetry was admired and imitated by [[Geoffrey Chaucer]] and Eustache Deschamps.

Mexican filmmaker and actor. His work, strongly connected to [[Fairy tale|fairy tales]], gothicism and horror, often blends the genres. He is known for pioneering dark fantasy in the film industry and has a lifelong fascination with [[Monster|monsters]], which he considers symbols of great power. His films include *Cronos* (1993), *Pan's Labyrinth* (2006) and *The Shape of Water* (2017). His accolades include three [[Academy Awards]], three BAFTA Awards and two Golden Globe Awards. In 2022 he created the [[Netflix]] anthology horror series *Guillermo del Toro's Cabinet of Curiosities*.
