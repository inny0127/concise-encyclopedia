=== KEY: Twilight_01d072
HEADWORD: Twilight
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Horizon | Sun
ENTRY:
Daylight illumination from diffuse sky radiation while the [[Sun]] is below the [[Horizon|horizon]], as sunlight from the upper atmosphere is scattered. The sky dims as the Sun sinks, and illumination is nearly zero when it reaches 18° below the horizon. Civil twilight, when the Sun's center is between the horizon and 6° below it, gives enough light that artificial light is unnecessary for most outdoor activity. Photographers and painters call it the blue hour, from the French *l'heure bleue*. Metaphorically, twilight implies something losing strength and nearing its end.

=== KEY: Twin_f207e9
HEADWORD: Twin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Doppelgänger | Embryo | Pregnancy | Sibling | Yoruba people
ENTRY:
One of two offspring produced by the same [[Pregnancy|pregnancy]]. Identical, or monozygotic, twins develop from one zygote that splits into two [[Embryo|embryos]], and always share the same chromosomal sex. Fraternal, or dizygotic, twins develop from two separate eggs, each fertilized by its own sperm, and share on average 50 percent of their genes, like ordinary [[Sibling|siblings]]. Twin birth rates vary widely: the [[Yoruba people|Yoruba]] have the highest, at 45–50 twin sets per 1,000 live births, while South America, South Asia and Southeast Asia have the lowest, at six to nine twin sets. Twin pregnancies last on average 37 weeks, three weeks less than full term. Fraternal twinning runs in families through the mother. Unrelated look-alikes are called [[Doppelgänger|doppelgängers]].

=== KEY: Tycho_Brahe_1cde29
HEADWORD: Brahe, Tycho
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Johannes Kepler | Observatory | Prague | Scientific Revolution | Supernova
ENTRY:
Danish astronomer, whose unprecedentedly accurate observations helped launch the [[Scientific Revolution]]. In 1572 he noticed a new star, now designated SN 1572, brighter than any star or planet, and he devoted the following years to more accurate instruments. His measurements showed that such [[Supernova|supernovae]] lay beyond the Moon and that comets were not atmospheric phenomena. King Frederick II gave him the island of Hven, where he built Uraniborg, the first large [[Observatory|observatory]] in Christian Europe. He devised the Tychonic system, in which the planets orbit the Sun while the Sun orbits the Earth. After King Christian IV forced him to leave Denmark in 1597, he became imperial astronomer in [[Prague]]. [[Johannes Kepler]], who later used Brahe's data to formulate his laws of planetary motion, assisted him for a year before his death.

=== KEY: Typewriter_6647e8
HEADWORD: Typewriter
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer keyboard | India | Ink | QWERTY | Word processor
ENTRY:
Mechanical or electromechanical machine for typing characters. Each key causes a type element to strike an [[Ink|inked]] ribbon against paper, producing a legible document. The first commercial typewriters appeared in 1874, but became common in U.S. offices only after the mid-1880s. By the end of the 19th century, a person who used one was also called a typist. Typewriters were standard office equipment until the 1980s, when personal computers running [[Word processor|word processing]] software largely replaced them. The typewriter was an early accessibility tool, and its use for the blind accelerated its development. The [[QWERTY]] layout, developed for typewriters in the 1870s, remains the de facto standard for English-language [[Computer keyboard|computer keyboards]]. Typewriters are still used in many [[India|Indian]] cities and towns, especially in roadside and legal offices, where electricity is unreliable.

=== KEY: Typha_6e5af1

ENTRY:
[[Genus]] of about 30 species of [[Monocotyledon|monocotyledonous]] flowering plants in the family Typhaceae. Known as bulrushes, reedmaces or cattails depending on region, these aquatic or semiaquatic perennials grow to 0.8–2.4 m tall, with long, narrow leaves. They are largely distributed across the Northern Hemisphere, in [[Wetland|wetland]] habitats. === KEY: Typhoid_fever_a41831
HEADWORD: Typhoid fever
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Salmonella | Typhus | Vaccination
ENTRY:
Enteric disease caused by the bacterium [[Salmonella]] Typhi, spread through food or water contaminated with feces. Symptoms usually begin six to 30 days after exposure, with a gradual onset of high fever, weakness, abdominal pain, constipation and headache. Some people develop a rash with rose-colored spots, and others carry the bacterium without symptoms while remaining contagious. Diagnosis is by culturing *S. Typhi* from patient samples or detecting an immune response in blood. A [[Vaccination|vaccine]] can prevent about 40–90% of cases during the first two years, and antibiotics such as azithromycin are used, though resistance has developed. Untreated, the risk of death may be as high as 20%, and with treatment 1% to 4%. In 2015 about 149,000 people died of typhoid worldwide, down from 181,000 in 1990. Its name means *resembling [[Typhus|typhus]]*, a different disease.

=== KEY: Typhus_8259dc

Group of bacterial infectious diseases, including epidemic, scrub and murine typhus. Epidemic typhus, caused by *Rickettsia prowazekii* and spread by body lice, occurs in outbreaks where sanitation is poor and people are crowded. === KEY: Typography_603850

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible and readable when displayed. Typography involves selecting typefaces, point sizes, line lengths, line spacing and letter spacing. === KEY: Tyrannosaurus_9f9776
HEADWORD: Tyrannosaurus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cretaceous | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event
ENTRY:
Genus of large theropod dinosaurs of the Late [[Cretaceous]] period, best known from *Tyrannosaurus rex*. The bipedal carnivore had a massive skull balanced by a long, heavy tail, and its two-clawed forelimbs were short but unusually powerful for their size. The most complete specimen measures 12.3–12.4 m (40–41 ft) long, although most modern estimates suggest it could have exceeded 13 m (43 ft). Its estimated bite force is the largest of any terrestrial animal. Fossils from the late Maastrichtian age, 69.4 to 66 million years ago, have been found across western North America, and it was among the last non-avian dinosaurs before the [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]]. Whether it was a predator or a scavenger was long debated, but most paleontologists now accept that it was a predator. Soft tissue and proteins have been reported in at least one specimen.

=== KEY: Tyrant_flycatcher_e7b687
HEADWORD: Tyrant flycatcher
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Americas | Passerine | Songbird
ENTRY:
Family of [[Passerine|passerine]] birds native to the [[Americas]]. With more than 400 species, it is the world's largest bird family and the most diverse bird family in every country of the Americas except the United States and Canada. Members vary greatly in shape, size and color, though most species are plain brown, gray or white. The smallest, the short-tailed pygmy tyrant and black-capped pygmy tyrant of the genus *Myiornis*, measure 6.5–7 cm (2.6–2.8 in) and weigh 4 to 5 g. The largest is the great shrike-tyrant, at 29 cm (11 in). Most species are almost entirely insectivorous, and many North American species catch insects in mid-air by flying from a perch and returning to it. The name echoes that of the Old World flycatchers, to which the family is not closely related. It belongs to the suboscines, which lack the vocal capabilities of [[Songbird|songbirds]].

=== KEY: Tyrian_purple_6cd9c3

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Reddish-purple [[Dye|dye]] secreted by predatory sea snails of the family Muricidae. The name refers to the ancient [[Phoenicia|Phoenician]] city of Tyre. Extracting it required tens of thousands of snails, making the dye costly and a marker of status. Production began by the Phoenicians as early as 1200 BC and continued under the Greeks and Romans until the [[Fall of Constantinople|fall of Constantinople]] in 1453. The color did not easily fade and grew brighter with weathering and sunlight. Sumptuary laws restricted purple garments, and senior Roman magistrates wore togas edged in it.

ENTRY:
Genus of [[Owl|owls]], including the barn owls, in the family Tytonidae. It is the most widely distributed owl genus, found almost everywhere except polar and desert regions and Asia north of the [[Himalayas]]. Most species measure 33–39 cm (13–15 in) in length, with large, heart-shaped facial discs and mottled gray or brown upperparts. Barn owls hunt mainly small mammals by sound, mostly at night. Pairs often mate for life, and the female incubates an average clutch of four eggs laid in a hollow tree, an old building or a cliff. Populations can expand rapidly when prey is plentiful. The barn owl is listed as Least Concern globally, though some subspecies with restricted ranges are more threatened.

Industry standard for digital data transmission and power delivery between computers, peripherals and hubs. Variable-width character encoding for [[Unicode]] that is backward compatible with [[ASCII]]. The name is short for Unicode Transformation Format – 8-bit. === KEY: Uncertainty_principle_9ac143
HEADWORD: Uncertainty principle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fourier transform | Hermann Weyl | Momentum | Quantum mechanics | Werner Heisenberg
ENTRY:
Fundamental limit in [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] on how precisely certain paired properties can be known together. Also called Heisenberg's indeterminacy principle, it states that the more accurately one property, such as position, is measured, the less accurately its complementary property, such as [[Momentum|momentum]], can be known. German physicist [[Werner Heisenberg]] introduced it in 1927. Earle Hesse Kennard derived the formal inequality, relating the standard deviations of position and momentum, later that year, and [[Hermann Weyl]] did so in 1928. In wave mechanics, the relation arises because the position and momentum wavefunctions are [[Fourier transform|Fourier transforms]] of each other, and a nonzero function and its Fourier transform cannot both be sharply localized. The principle extends to other pairs, such as energy and time. Its effects are significant only for microscopic objects and negligible for macroscopic ones.

LINK TARGETS: Abraham Lincoln | American Civil War | Slavery | Stereotype
ENTRY:
Anti-slavery novel by American author Harriet Beecher Stowe. Stowe wrote it in response to the second Fugitive Slave Act, passed in 1850. It first appeared as a serial in the abolitionist newspaper *The National Era* from June 5, 1851, and was published in two volumes in 1852. The novel centers on Uncle Tom, a long-suffering enslaved man, and asserts that Christian love could overcome [[Slavery|slavery]]. It was the best-selling novel in the United States in the 19th century and the second best-selling book after the Bible, and it is said to have helped lay the groundwork for the [[American Civil War|American Civil War]]. An apocryphal story has [[Abraham Lincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] greeting Stowe as "the little lady who started this great war." The book and the plays it inspired popularized negative [[Stereotype|stereotypes]] of Black people, and "Uncle Tom" came to mean an excessively subservient person.

=== KEY: Underclass_f9a343
HEADWORD: Underclass
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Poverty | Working class
ENTRY:
Segment of the population occupying the lowest possible position in a class hierarchy, below the core of the [[Working class|working class]]. The idea of a population beneath the working class has a long tradition in the social sciences, as in the *lumpenproletariat*, but the specific term was popularized in the last half of the 20th century, first by social scientists of American [[Poverty|poverty]] and then by American journalists. Writing in the early 1960s, Gunnar Myrdal is generally credited as the first proponent of the term. He described an underclass as a "class of unemployed, unemployables, and underemployed" set apart from the nation at large. According to sociologist Herbert Gans, many journalists and academics later replaced this structural conception with a behavioral one that fused Myrdal's term with Oscar Lewis's "culture of poverty." The concept remains controversial, and its definitions and remedies are highly debated.

=== KEY: Underwater_diving_86650b
HEADWORD: Underwater diving
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Pressure
ENTRY:
Practice of descending below the water's surface to interact with the environment. Immersion and high [[Pressure|ambient pressure]] limit the depths and durations humans can reach, so equipment has been developed to extend them. Breath-hold diving, or freediving, goes back at least to classical times. Self-contained breathing systems developed at an accelerated rate after the Second World War, and scuba and surface-supplied diving use breathing apparatus. Saturation diving reduces the risk of decompression sickness (DCS) after long, deep dives. Recreational scuba diving is restricted to maximum depths of about 40 meters (130 ft), commercial saturation diving to 530 meters (1,740 ft), and diving in atmospheric suits to 610 meters (2,000 ft). Diving remains a relatively dangerous activity. Professional diving is usually regulated by occupational health and safety law, while recreational diving may be entirely unregulated.

=== KEY: Unemployment_dbd2e4
HEADWORD: Unemployment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Globalization | International trade | Natural disaster | War
ENTRY:
State of being without paid employment or self-employment while available for work. Unemployment is measured by the unemployment rate, the number of unemployed people as a percentage of the labor force. Causes include the state of the economy, such as a recession; [[Globalization|globalization]] and [[International trade|international trade]] competition; new technologies; government policies; market regulation; and [[War|war]], civil disorder, and [[Natural disaster|natural disasters]]. Economists distinguish several types, including cyclical, frictional, structural, and classical unemployment. Voluntary unemployment reflects individuals' decisions, while involuntary unemployment results from the economic environment, such as workers fired after a company bankruptcy or industrial decline. The International Labour Organization counted 172 million people without work worldwide in 2018, or 5% of the reported global workforce. Because measuring unemployment through surveys or registers is difficult, the employment-to-population ratio is sometimes considered more suitable.

=== KEY: Ungulate_8d5530

ENTRY:
Member of the clade Euungulata, a group of mostly large hoofed mammals. Living ungulates fall into two orders: odd-toed Perissodactyla, including [[Horse|horses]], [[Rhinoceros|rhinoceroses]], and tapirs, and even-toed Artiodactyla, including [[Cattle|cattle]], [[Antelope|antelopes]], [[Pig|pigs]], giraffes, and camels. Character encoding standard maintained by the Unicode Consortium, designed to support text in all of the world's digitizable writing systems. Version 18.0 defines 172,808 characters and 175 scripts. === KEY: Unification_of_Germany_b4d14a
HEADWORD: Unification of Germany
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: German Empire | Kingdom of Prussia | Otto von Bismarck
ENTRY:
Process of 1866–71 that built the first German nation-state, excluding Austria. It began on August 18, 1866, with the North German Confederation Treaty, a military alliance dominated by the [[Kingdom of Prussia|Kingdom of Prussia]]. It symbolically concluded on January 18, 1871, when the southern German states joined and the [[German Empire|German Empire]], with 25 member states, was proclaimed under Prussian leadership. Legally, the decisive steps were January 1, 1871, when the southern states acceded and the name German Empire was adopted; May 4, 1871, when the permanent constitution took effect; and May 10, 1871, with the Treaty of Frankfurt. Historians debate whether [[Otto von Bismarck|Otto von Bismarck]] had a master plan or simply sought to expand Prussian power. Prussian victories in three regional wars generated national pride that politicians could harness. The Peace of Prague settled the question of a "small Germany" without Austria against a "greater Germany" with it in favor of the former.

Nineteenth-century political and social movement, also called the Risorgimento, that unified most of the Italian Peninsula. Trento and [[Trieste|Trieste]], annexed in 1918 after [[World War I|World War I]], are often considered the culmination of the movement. Italy celebrates the anniversary on March 17, the date the kingdom was proclaimed.

=== KEY: Unitarianism_985877

ENTRY:
Nontrinitarian movement of [[Christianity]] affirming the unitary nature of God as a single, unique creator. Unitarians reject the [[Trinity]] as a later theological corruption, and most regard [[Jesus]] as a moral and spiritual teacher subordinate to God. Accordingly, they reject the ecumenical councils and creeds. The movement emerged during the Radical Reformation in the mid-16th century, in the [[Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth]] and in [[Transylvania]], where Ferenc Dávid founded the Unitarian Church of Transylvania. Unitarianism is not a single denomination but a collection of existing and extinct groups that share belief in God's unity.

=== KEY: Unitary_state_f76c79
HEADWORD: Unitary state
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Federation | France | French colonial empire | Hundred Years' War | Napoleonic Wars | Sovereign state | United Kingdom
ENTRY:
[[Sovereign state]] governed as a single entity, in which the central government holds supreme authority. The central government may create or abolish sub-national divisions and decides which powers to delegate to them. It may alter the statute that devolves power, overriding or expanding the authority of devolved governments. The concept originated in [[France]], where national feeling after the [[Hundred Years' War]] unified the country and transformed it from a feudal monarchy. Conquests during and after the [[Napoleonic Wars]] and the vast [[French colonial empire]] spread the model. Unitary states contrast with [[Federation|federations]], whose constituent units hold powers that the central government cannot unilaterally change. About 166 of the 193 UN member countries have unitary systems, though federations cover a significant share of the world's population and land. The [[United Kingdom]], Spain and China are examples.

=== KEY: United_Kingdom_89f9c9

ENTRY:
Country in Northwestern Europe comprising England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland (pop., 2025: over 69 million). Officially the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland, it is also called the UK or Britain. It covers 244,376 sq km, including the island of Great Britain, the northeastern part of Ireland, and most smaller islands of the [[British Isles]], and shares a land border with the [[Republic of Ireland]]. The capital is [[London]]; Edinburgh, Cardiff, and Belfast are the national capitals of Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland.

The Roman conquest began in AD 43, Anglo-Saxon settlement around 450, and the [[Normans|Norman]] conquest of England in 1066. In 1707 England and Scotland united to form the [[Kingdom of Great Britain]]. The Acts of Union 1800 incorporated the Kingdom of Ireland, creating the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland in 1801. Most of Ireland seceded in 1922, and the Royal and Parliamentary Titles Act 1927 created the present state.

Britain was the first industrialized country, in the 18th century, and the foremost world power for most of the 19th and early 20th centuries, notably during the Pax Britannica of 1815–1914. At its height in the 1920s the [[British Empire]] encompassed around a quarter of the world's landmass and population. Decolonization led to the independence of most colonies, many of which joined the [[Commonwealth of Nations]].

The UK is a [[Constitutional monarchy|constitutional monarchy]] and parliamentary democracy with three jurisdictions: England and Wales, Scotland, and Northern Ireland. Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland have their own governments and parliaments with devolved powers. It is a developed country with an advanced economy and nuclear weapons. "Great Britain" conventionally means the island, or England, Scotland, and Wales together.

Principal organ of the [[United Nations]] responsible for international peace and security.
