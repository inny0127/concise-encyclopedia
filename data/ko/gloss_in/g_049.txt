=== KEY: Freedom_of_speech_1cdf23
HEADWORD: Freedom of speech
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Erasmus | Internet | John Stuart Mill | Universal Declaration of Human Rights | Voltaire
ENTRY:
Right to communicate one's opinions and ideas without fear of retaliation, censorship or legal sanction. It is recognized as a human right in the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] (UDHR) and protected by many national constitutions. Article 19 of the UDHR includes the freedom to seek, receive and impart information, but the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights allows restrictions for the rights or reputation of others and for national security, public order, public health or morals. Common limits include libel, incitement, hate speech, classified information and copyright violation. In *On Liberty*, [[John Stuart Mill]] proposed the harm principle, that power may rightly be exercised over members of a civilized community only to prevent harm to others. [[Erasmus]] and [[Voltaire]] championed free expression, and England's Bill of Rights of 1689 established parliamentary privilege. In the digital age, countries may use [[Internet|internet]] censorship, and social media platforms moderate content that breaches their terms of service, even when it is legal.

=== KEY: Freemasonry_4c1b22
HEADWORD: Freemasonry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antisemitism | Benjamin Franklin | Bible | Catholic Church | George Washington | Quran
ENTRY:
Group of fraternal organizations that trace their origins to medieval guilds of stonemasons. Freemasonry is considered the oldest existing secular fraternal organization, with documents dating to the 14th century. Its core tenets are brotherly love, relief and truth. The basic unit is the lodge, usually supervised by a Grand Lodge, and there is no worldwide Grand Lodge. The United Grand Lodge of England, dating to June 1717, is regarded as the mother Grand Lodge. Regular Freemasonry, the largest branch, requires that a volume of sacred law such as the [[Bible]] or the [[Quran]] be open in lodge, that members profess belief in a supreme being, and that women not be admitted. Liberal or Continental Freemasonry has removed some of these requirements. The [[Catholic Church]] and some Protestant denominations have opposed membership, and some Islamic countries have banned it. Opposition is sometimes rooted in [[Antisemitism|antisemitism]] or conspiracy theories. Enlightenment-era figures such as [[Benjamin Franklin]] and [[George Washington]] are associated with the movement.

=== KEY: Freethought_fac46c
HEADWORD: Freethought
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agnosticism | Bertrand Russell | Deism | Humanism | Secularism | Thomas Paine | Voltaire
ENTRY:
Social movement espousing unorthodox beliefs formed independently of authority, tradition, revelation or dogma. Freethinkers hold that knowledge should be grounded in facts, scientific inquiry and logic, free from confirmation bias, prejudice and conventional wisdom. The term came into use in the 17th century for people who examined traditional beliefs. Today freethought is most closely linked with [[Agnosticism|agnosticism]], [[Deism|deism]], [[Secularism|secularism]], [[Humanism|humanism]] and religious critique. Freethinkers typically hold that there is insufficient evidence for supernatural phenomena. The British mathematician and philosopher William Kingdon Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief," published in the 1870s, argued that believing anything on insufficient evidence is always wrong, and it became a rallying cry for freethinkers. [[Bertrand Russell]] wrote that a freethinker is one whose beliefs rest on evidence rather than bias, and that atheists and agnostics are not necessarily freethinkers. Key historical figures include [[Voltaire]] and [[Thomas Paine]].

=== KEY: Freezing_64a54c
HEADWORD: Freezing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Food preservation | Glass | Glycerol | Helium-3 | Water
ENTRY:
Phase transition in which a liquid turns into a solid when cooled below its freezing point. For most substances the melting and freezing points are the same, though some, such as agar, show hysteresis. Most liquids freeze by crystallization, in which molecules gather into nanoscale clusters that arrange in a periodic crystal structure and then grow. Pure liquids can supercool below their freezing point until nucleation begins; [[Water|water]] can supercool to −40 °C in the absence of nucleators. Freezing is almost always exothermic, releasing latent heat. Low-temperature [[Helium-3|helium-3]] is the known exception, with a negative enthalpy of fusion below 0.3 K. Materials such as [[Glass|glass]] and [[Glycerol|glycerol]] harden into amorphous solids without a sharp freezing point. Water is unusual in expanding by about 9% when it freezes, and freezing is essential to [[Food preservation|food preservation]].

=== KEY: Freight_transport_b533da
HEADWORD: Freight transport
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camel | Industrial Revolution | Mesopotamia | Wheel | Wright brothers
ENTRY:
Physical process of transporting commercial goods and cargo, also called freight forwarding. Modes include air, water, rail, pipelines and road, with pipelines carrying liquids and gases. Early goods were carried by animals such as horses, [[Camel|camels]] and donkeys. The invention of the [[Wheel|wheel]] in [[Mesopotamia]] in 5000 BC allowed carts, and Silk Road trade moved goods by camel caravan. During the [[Industrial Revolution]], England's canal system and railways carried freight over long distances. After the [[Wright brothers]] invented the airplane, the first air freight shipment was delivered in 1910, and FedEx's founding in 1973 revolutionized the industry with specialized cargo flights. In 2015, 108 trillion tonne-kilometers were transported worldwide: 70% by sea, 18% by road, 9% by rail, 2% by inland waterways and under 0.25% by air. Merchant shipping carries 80–90% of international trade and 60–70% by value.

Overseas department and region of France on the northern coast of South America. It is bordered by [[Suriname]] to the west and Brazil to the east and south, and covers 84,000 sq km (32,000 sq mi). === KEY: French_Indochina_6b8035
HEADWORD: French Indochina
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambodia | First Indochina War | Hanoi | Ho Chi Minh | Laos
ENTRY:
Group of French-dependent territories in Southeast Asia, from 1887 to 1954. It was first a federation of French colonies and from 1949 a confederation of French associated states. It comprised [[Cambodia]], [[Laos]], the Chinese territory of Guangzhouwan (1898–1945), and the Vietnamese regions of Tonkin, Annam and Cochinchina, with [[Hanoi]] and Saigon as capitals. === KEY: French_Polynesia_8e49db
HEADWORD: French Polynesia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ferdinand Magellan | Tahiti | United Nations
ENTRY:
Overseas collectivity of France in the South Pacific Ocean. It comprises 121 islands and atolls spread over more than 2,000 km, with a total land area of 3,521 sq km and a population of 282,596 as of September 2025. The islands form five groups, and [[Tahiti]], the most populous, was home to nearly 69% of residents in 2017. Papeete, on Tahiti, is the capital. === KEY: French_Revolution_27ab5e
HEADWORD: French Revolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Revolution | Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen | French Revolutionary Wars | Louis XVI | Maximilien Robespierre | Napoleon | Reign of Terror | Storming of the Bastille
ENTRY:
Period of political and social upheaval in France (1789–99) that overthrew the monarchy and ended with Napoleon Bonaparte as First Consul. Many of its ideas are considered fundamental principles of liberal democracy, and remain central to French political discourse.

The revolution resulted from social, economic, financial, and political crises that the regime could not manage. Between 1715 and 1789 the population grew from 21 to 28 million. Recession from 1785 and bad harvests in 1787 and 1788 brought high unemployment and food prices. By 1788 half of state revenue went on servicing debt. Reform was blocked by the regional parlements, and [[Louis XVI]] often backed down before conservative opposition in the nobility. The [[American Revolution]] and Enlightenment critiques shaped public debate.

The crisis led to the convocation of the Estates General in May 1789, its first meeting since 1614. The Third Estate became a National Assembly in June. The [[Storming of the Bastille]] on July 14 was followed by radical measures, including the abolition of feudalism, state control over the Catholic Church, and the [[Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen]].

The king's attempted flight to Varennes in June 1791 discredited the monarchy further. Military defeats after the outbreak of the [[French Revolutionary Wars]] in April 1792 led to the insurrection of August 10, 1792. The French First Republic replaced the monarchy in September, and Louis XVI was executed in January 1793. Power then passed to the Committee of Public Safety, dominated by Jacobins led by [[Maximilien Robespierre]]. About 16,000 people were sentenced by the Revolutionary Tribunal and executed in the [[Reign of Terror]], which ended in July 1794. The Directory took over in November 1795, and the coup of 18 Brumaire on November 9, 1799, established the Consulate under [[Napoleon]].

=== KEY: French_Revolutionary_Wars_0c5720
HEADWORD: French Revolutionary Wars
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conscription | France | French Revolution | Napoleon
ENTRY:
Series of military conflicts from 1792 to 1802 arising from the [[French Revolution]], in which [[France]] fought Great Britain, Austria, Prussia, Russia and other states. They comprised the War of the First Coalition (1792–1797) and the War of the Second Coalition (1798–1802). After France declared war on Austria and Prussia in spring 1792, a coordinated invasion was turned back at Valmy in September. Mass [[Conscription|conscription]] sustained the army, which conquered the Low Countries and the Rhineland. In 1796 [[Napoleon|Napoleon Bonaparte]] began a campaign in Italy that led to the Treaty of Campo Formio on October 17, 1797. The Second Coalition began in 1798 with Napoleon's invasion of Egypt. After victories at Marengo and Hohenlinden, Austria sought peace, and Britain signed the Treaty of Amiens in 1802.

=== KEY: French_Wars_of_Religion_313670
HEADWORD: French Wars of Religion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Catherine de' Medici | Henry IV of France | Louis XIV
ENTRY:
Series of civil wars between French Catholics and Protestants, called Huguenots, from 1562 to 1598. Between two and four million people died from violence, famine or disease caused by the conflict, which severely damaged the power of the French monarchy. Both sides received foreign support, with Spain and Savoy backing the Catholics and England and the Dutch Republic the Protestants. [[Catherine de' Medici]] initially backed moderates but later supported the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of 1572, in which Catholic mobs killed between 5,000 and 30,000 Protestants throughout France. The wars ended with the Edict of Nantes, issued in 1598 by [[Henry IV of France|Henry IV]], who had converted to Catholicism in 1593. Catholic opposition persisted, and [[Louis XIV]] revoked the edict in 1685.

=== KEY: French_and_Indian_War_d8c27e
HEADWORD: French and Indian War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: George Washington | Mississippi River | Quebec | Saint Pierre and Miquelon | Seven Years' War
ENTRY:
North American theater of the [[Seven Years' War]], fought from 1754 to 1763 between Great Britain and France with their Indigenous allies. Historians generally treat it as part of the global Seven Years' War, but in the United States it is often viewed as a distinct conflict. It began in May 1754 at the Battle of Jumonville Glen, when Virginia militia led by [[George Washington]] ambushed a French patrol. Edward Braddock's expedition was defeated at the Battle of the Monongahela in 1755, and Britain captured [[Quebec]] in 1759 and Montreal the following year. The Treaty of Paris in 1763 transferred France's Canadian possessions and its claims east of the [[Mississippi River]] to Britain. Spain received French Louisiana west of the river, and France retained only [[Saint Pierre and Miquelon]].

=== KEY: French_colonial_empire_c83562
HEADWORD: French colonial empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algiers | Charles de Gaulle | Franco-Prussian War | Seven Years' War | World War I | World War II
ENTRY:
Overseas colonies, protectorates and mandate territories ruled by France. A first empire, which existed until 1814 and lost most of its holdings, was followed by a second that began with the conquest of [[Algiers]] in 1830. On the eve of [[World War I]], France's empire was the second-largest after the British Empire. Losses in the [[Seven Years' War]] removed most of its North American possessions, and Louisiana, returned to France in 1800, was sold to the United States in 1803. After the [[Franco-Prussian War]], colonies seemed a way to restore French prestige, and the empire was rebuilt mostly after 1850 in Africa, Indochina and the South Pacific. Its ideology, the *mission civilisatrice*, promised French language and institutions, but colonial subjects were rarely granted full citizenship. [[Charles de Gaulle]] and the Free French used the colonies as a base in [[World War II]]. Revolts in Indochina and Algeria cost France both colonies after 1945.

=== KEY: French_cuisine_de8519
HEADWORD: French cuisine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Italian cuisine | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Cooking traditions and practices of France. In the 14th century the court chef Guillaume Tirel, known as "Taillevent," wrote *Le Viandier*, one of the earliest recipe collections of medieval France. In the 17th and 18th centuries, chefs François Pierre La Varenne and Marie-Antoine Carême led a shift away from foreign influences, such as [[Italian cuisine|Italian]] cooking, toward a distinctly French style. Cheese and wine are central, with regional variations governed by *appellation d'origine contrôlée* (AOC) laws. From the 20th century, culinary tourism and the Guide Michelin helped commoners discover urban *cuisine bourgeoise* and peasant cooking. In November 2010 [[UNESCO]] added French gastronomy to its lists of intangible cultural heritage.

=== KEY: French_invasion_of_Russia_99cecb
HEADWORD: French invasion of Russia
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cossacks | Moscow | Russian Empire | Typhus
ENTRY:
Napoleon's 1812 offensive against the [[Russian Empire]], launched to force Russia to comply with the continental blockade of the United Kingdom. The campaign began on June 24, 1812, when the multinational Grande Armée crossed the Neman River with nearly half a million men. Napoleon captured Smolensk in August after a two-day battle, but the Russian army avoided encirclement and kept retreating. At Borodino he failed to destroy it, and on September 14 he entered [[Moscow]], which had been abandoned and set ablaze. Waiting there for a peace that never came, he began a retreat in October. Hunger, cold, [[Typhus]] and attacks by [[Cossacks|Cossacks]] devastated the army. Around a million soldiers and civilians died in less than six months, and the campaign shattered Napoleon's reputation for invincibility.

=== KEY: French_language_99dcda
HEADWORD: French language
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: European Union | Roman Empire | Romance languages | United Nations
ENTRY:
Romance language of the Indo-European family, spoken natively in France and in parts of Canada, Belgium, Switzerland, Luxembourg, and Monaco. French and its closest relatives, the langues d'oïl, historically spoken in northern France and southern Belgium, descended from the Vulgar Latin of the [[Roman Empire]]. Celtic languages of northern Roman Gaul and the Germanic Frankish language of the post-Roman invaders influenced it, and it largely supplanted regional [[Romance languages|Romance]] varieties across France, Belgium, and Switzerland.

Latin was gradually adopted in Gaul under Roman rule, and the local variety evolved into the Gallo-Romance tongues. Gaulish likely survived into the 6th century and left loanwords and calques, including *oui*, "yes"; the *Petit Robert* attributes 154 French words to it. The Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts of 1539 mandated French in legal documents, and the Académie Française was established in 1635.

French is estimated to have about 310 million speakers, about 74 million of them native. It is the 22nd most natively spoken language, the sixth most spoken overall, and among the five most studied. It is an official language in 26 countries, and 54 states belong to the Organisation internationale de la Francophonie. In 2025 approximately 50% of the Francophone population lived in sub-Saharan Africa and the Indian Ocean, 30% in Europe, 15% in North Africa, and 7% in the Americas. Colonialism carried it to the Americas, Africa, and Asia, giving rise to creoles such as Haitian Creole.

French has a long history as an international language of literature, diplomacy, and science. It is a primary or second language of many organizations, including the [[United Nations]], the [[European Union]], NATO, and the International Olympic Committee, and it is the second most widely spoken mother tongue in the European Union.

=== KEY: French_literature_357b9c
HEADWORD: French literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jean-Paul Sartre | Lingua franca | Nobel Prize
ENTRY:
Literature written in the French language, particularly by French citizens. Works by writers from Belgium, Switzerland, Canada and African countries are termed Francophone literature. One of the first known examples is the *Song of Roland*, a *chanson de geste*. From the 11th century, medieval French literature was one of the oldest vernacular literatures in western Europe, and in the 17th century French letters dominated Europe. In the 18th century French became the literary [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]] and diplomatic language of western Europe. France ranks first among countries for [[Nobel Prize|Nobel Prizes]] in literature, and as of 2022 fifteen French authors had won the prize. [[Jean-Paul Sartre]] declined the 1964 award, arguing that a writer must not be turned into an institution.

=== KEY: Frequency_7cf395
HEADWORD: Frequency
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Heinrich Hertz | Hertz
ENTRY:
Number of occurrences of a repeating event per unit of time. Frequency specifies the rate of oscillations and vibrations in science and engineering, including sound, radio waves and light. The period, the time between events, is its reciprocal: a heart beating 120 times per minute (2 [[Hertz|hertz]]) has a period of half a second. The SI unit is the hertz (Hz), named after the German physicist [[Heinrich Hertz]] by the International Electrotechnical Commission in 1930 and adopted by the General Conference on Weights and Measures in 1960. Sixty revolutions per minute equals one hertz. Angular frequency, measured in radians per second, is frequency multiplied by 2π.

=== KEY: Friction_651389
HEADWORD: Friction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Leonardo da Vinci
ENTRY:
Force resisting the relative motion of solid surfaces, fluid layers and other materials sliding or grinding against each other. Types include dry, fluid, lubricated, skin and internal friction. Dry friction arises mainly from surface irregularities called asperities and is divided into static friction between non-moving surfaces and kinetic friction between moving ones. The study of friction, called tribology, has a history of more than 2,000 years. Ancient writers including [[Aristotle]] were interested in its causes, and [[Leonardo da Vinci]] discovered the classic laws of sliding friction in 1493. His notebooks were not published, and Guillaume Amontons rediscovered the laws in 1699. Friction can start fires, as rubbing wood together shows, and its wear degrades performance and damages components.

=== KEY: Fridtjof_Nansen_1d1ce9
HEADWORD: Nansen, Fridtjof
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Arctic | Greenland | League of Nations | Zoology
ENTRY:
Norwegian polymath, explorer and diplomat. In 1888 Nansen led the first crossing of [[Greenland]] interior on cross-country skis. His 1893–1896 *Fram* expedition reached a record northern latitude of 86°14′, and his polar travel techniques influenced later [[Arctic]] and Antarctic expeditions. He studied [[Zoology|zoology]], and his doctoral research on the central nervous system of marine creatures helped establish the neuron doctrine. He then turned to oceanography, making many cruises mainly in the North Atlantic. In 1905 he spoke for ending Norway's union with Sweden and persuaded Prince Carl of Denmark to accept the Norwegian throne. Appointed in 1921 as the [[League of Nations]]' High Commissioner for Refugees, he introduced the "Nansen passport" for stateless people, which more than 50 countries recognized. He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1922.

=== KEY: Friedrich_Engels_74ad77

PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
German philosopher, social theorist and revolutionary socialist. Engels collaborated with [[Karl Marx]] for life, co-authoring *The Communist Manifesto* in 1848 and helping develop what became [[Marxism]]. Born into a prosperous mercantile family in Barmen, Prussia, he was sent to Manchester, England, in 1842 to work in a family cotton mill. For two decades he worked for the family firm of Ermen & Engels while supporting the impoverished Marx family in London. After Marx's death in 1883 he edited the second and third volumes of *Das Kapital*. He died in London in 1895.

=== KEY: Friedrich_Hayek_a5f27d

ENTRY:
Austrian economist and political philosopher. He studied at the [[University of Vienna]], earning doctorates in law in 1921 and political studies in 1923, and as a teenager he fought in [[World War I]]. Hayek became a British national in 1938 and taught at the [[London School of Economics]] and later the [[University of Chicago]]. He was a notable member of the Austrian school of economics. His most popular work, *The Road to Serfdom* (1944), has been republished many times. Although sometimes called a conservative, he preferred the labels classical liberal or libertarian. === KEY: Friedrich_List_f06356

ENTRY:
German economist and political theorist. Born in Reutlingen, he was appointed professor of public administration at Tübingen in 1817 despite lacking a university degree. He developed a nationalist theory of political economy in Europe and the [[United States]], and he argued for the Zollverein, a pan-German customs union. List advocated tariffs on imported goods while supporting free trade within the nation, and he saw the cost of a tariff as an investment in future productivity. He is regarded as a forerunner of the German historical school of economics, and Emmanuel Todd considers him a precursor to [[John Maynard Keynes]] as a theorist of regulated capitalism. His later years were marked by financial failures, and he died by suicide in 1846.

=== KEY: Friendship_a31816
HEADWORD: Friendship
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolescence | Sociology
ENTRY:
Relationship of mutual affection between people, stronger than an acquaintance or association such as that with a classmate, neighbor, or coworker. Common features include choosing to be with one another, enjoying time together, and offering positive support. Friends are sometimes distinguished from family and from lovers, though the line can blur.

Friendship is studied in communication, [[Sociology|sociology]], social psychology, anthropology, and philosophy. Proposed theories include social exchange theory, equity theory, relational dialectics, and attachment styles. A classification originating in classical philosophy distinguishes friendships based on utility, pleasure, and virtue. Research links high-quality friendships to reduced risk of depression, and evolutionary accounts describe friendship as an adaptation that secured non-kin alliances for cooperation and protection.

Children's understanding of friendship focuses on common activities, physical proximity, and shared expectations; friendships give them opportunities for play and practicing self-regulation. Based on reports from teachers and mothers, 75% of preschool children had at least one friend, a figure that rose to 78% through the fifth grade, when 55% had a mutual best friend. About 15% of children were chronically friendless, reporting periods of at least six months without mutual friends.

In [[Adolescence|adolescence]], friendships become more giving, sharing, frank, supportive, and spontaneous, and rest more on similar morals and values, loyalty, and shared interests. A large study of American adolescents found that those who engaged less in problematic behavior had friends who did well in school, took part in school activities, avoided drinking, and had good mental health.

In adulthood, friendship provides companionship, affection, and emotional support and contributes to mental and physical health. Workplace friendships can be hard to maintain. A 2023 Pew Research Center survey reported that 8% of respondents had no close friends and a further 7% had only one.

=== KEY: Fruit_3b2619
HEADWORD: Fruit
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apple | Flowering plant | Pollination | Tomato | Vegetable
ENTRY:
Seed-bearing structure of [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] (angiosperms), formed from the ovary after flowering. Fruits are the means by which angiosperms disperse their seeds. Edible fruits account for a substantial fraction of the world's agricultural output. Some, such as the [[Apple|apple]] and the pomegranate, have acquired extensive cultural and symbolic meanings.

Botanical and culinary usage differ. In botany, a fruit is a ripened ovary or carpel that contains seeds, as in the orange, [[Tomato|tomato]], and pumpkin; a nut is a type of fruit, not a seed, and a seed is a ripened ovule. In culinary language, a fruit is the sweet or sour produce of a specific plant, such as a peach, pear, or lemon, whereas vegetables are typically savory. Cucumbers, squashes, beans, peanuts, peas, corn, eggplants, and bell peppers are botanical fruits usually called [[Vegetable|vegetables]]. Many spices, including black pepper and cumin, are also fruits. Rhubarb, often treated as a fruit in pies, is in fact the leaf stalk of the plant. A cereal grain such as rice or wheat is a fruit termed a caryopsis, though the edible part is almost entirely seed.

The outer layer of most fruits is the pericarp, typically formed from the ovary and divisible into the epicarp, mesocarp, and endocarp. [[Pollination]] moves pollen to the stigma, and a pollen tube grows down the style to the ovule. One sperm unites with the egg to form a zygote, which gives rise to the embryo, and the other enters the central cell and gives rise to endosperm, a nutritive tissue; this is double fertilization. The ovules become seeds, while the pericarp may become fleshy, as in berries, or hard, as in nuts. Fruits to which other floral parts contribute significantly, such as the apple and strawberry, are called accessory fruits.

=== KEY: Function_mathematics_b9554e
HEADWORD: Function (mathematics)
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calculus | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Leonhard Euler | Real number | Set theory
ENTRY:
Rule assigning to each element of one set exactly one element of another set. A function from a set X to a set Y assigns to each element of X exactly one element of Y; X is called the domain and Y the codomain. If the function f assigns y to x, one says that f maps x to y and writes y = f(x). The set of values actually taken is the image, a subset of the codomain. A function is often defined by an expression such as f(x) = x² + 1, for which evaluation gives f(4) = 17.

Functions began as an idealization of how one varying quantity depends on another, as the position of a planet depends on time. The concept was elaborated with the infinitesimal [[Calculus|calculus]] at the end of the 17th century. [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Leibniz]] introduced the word "function" in 1673, and [[Leonhard Euler|Euler]] defined functions as analytic expressions in his *Introductio in analysin infinitorum* in 1748. At the end of the 19th century the concept was formalized in terms of [[Set theory|set theory]], which greatly widened its applications.

In the set-theoretic definition, a function is a binary relation between X and Y in which every element of X is related to exactly one element of Y. The set of all pairs (x, f(x)) is the graph of the function. When both sets are sets of [[Real number|real numbers]], each pair can be read as a point in the plane.

Important properties include injectivity (distinct inputs give distinct outputs), surjectivity (every element of the codomain is an output), and bijectivity (both, which allows an inverse). Functions are widely used in science, engineering, and most fields of mathematics, and have been called "the central objects of investigation" in most of them.

=== KEY: Functional_programming_1fed90

[[Programming paradigm]] in which programs are constructed by applying and composing functions. === KEY: Fundamental_group_ac7917

ENTRY:
Group of homotopy classes of loops in a [[Topological space]], based at a chosen point. It records information about the basic shape, or holes, of a space, and it is the first and simplest [[Homotopy]] group. === KEY: Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra_863494
HEADWORD: Fundamental theorem of algebra
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Complex number | Field (mathematics) | Polynomial
ENTRY:
Theorem stating that every non-constant [[Polynomial]] with complex coefficients has at least one complex root. Equivalently, the [[Complex number|complex numbers]] form an algebraically closed [[Field (mathematics)|field]], and a polynomial of degree n has exactly n complex roots when counted with multiplicity. === KEY: Fundamental_theorem_of_arithmetic_3ce6ff
HEADWORD: Fundamental theorem of arithmetic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carl Friedrich Gauss | Euclid | Fermat's Last Theorem | Prime number
ENTRY:
Theorem stating that every integer greater than 1 is either prime or a unique product of primes, up to the order of the factors. For example, 1200 = 2⁴·3·5² has exactly four 2s, one 3 and two 5s in every factorization. Factors must be [[Prime number|prime]]: 12 = 2·6 = 3·4 shows that factorizations into composite numbers need not be unique. The theorem is also called the unique factorization theorem, and it is one of the main reasons why 1 is not considered a prime number. Its existence part traces back to [[Euclid]]'s *Elements*, and [[Carl Friedrich Gauss|Gauss]] gave the first known proof of uniqueness in Article 16 of his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*. The theorem fails for algebraic integers, one reason the proof of [[Fermat's Last Theorem]] was so difficult.

=== KEY: Fundamental_theorem_of_calculus_8325bc
HEADWORD: Fundamental theorem of calculus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Continuous function | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Integral | Isaac Newton
ENTRY:
Theorem linking differentiation and integration as inverse operations. The first part states that, for a [[Continuous function|continuous]] function f, an antiderivative of f can be obtained as the [[Integral|integral]] of f over an interval with a variable upper bound. The second part states that the integral of f over a fixed interval equals the change in any antiderivative between the ends of the interval, which avoids numerical integration when an antiderivative can be found. Historically, its importance lay in recognizing that calculating areas and calculating gradients are closely related. James Gregory published a rudimentary geometric form, Isaac Barrow proved a more general version, and [[Isaac Newton]] completed the surrounding theory. [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz|Gottfried Leibniz]] systematized the work and introduced the notation used today.

=== KEY: Fungus_7367b2
HEADWORD: Fungus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chitin | Hypha | Mycology | Photosynthesis | Spore
ENTRY:
Member of a group of eukaryotic organisms, including yeasts, molds, and mushrooms, classified in the biological kingdom Fungi. Fungi differ from plants, bacteria, and some protists in having [[Chitin|chitin]] in their cell walls. Like animals, they are heterotrophs, absorbing dissolved organic molecules, typically by secreting digestive enzymes into their environment. They do not perform [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]. The study of fungi is called [[Mycology|mycology]]; once regarded as a branch of botany, it is now known that fungi are genetically more closely related to animals than to plants.

The vegetative body of most fungi is a network of threads called [[Hypha|hyphae]], which together form the mycelium. Fungi reproduce both asexually and sexually, with [[Spore|spores]] as the main dispersal units. Most are inconspicuous, becoming noticeable when they fruit as mushrooms or molds. They are the principal decomposers in ecological systems and have fundamental roles in nutrient cycling. Most plants depend on mycorrhizal symbiosis with fungi.

Fungi have long served as food, as in mushrooms and truffles, as a leavening agent for bread, and in the fermentation of wine, beer, and soy sauce. Since the 1940s they have been used to produce antibiotics, and fungal enzymes are used industrially. Some species produce mycotoxins, and crop losses to fungal disease, such as rice blast, affect food supplies. Over 8,000 species are known to be detrimental to plants and at least 300 can be pathogenic to humans.

The kingdom ranges from unicellular aquatic chytrids to large mushrooms, and its diversity is estimated at 2.2 million to 3.8 million species, of which only a small fraction have been described. Classification based on morphology, begun by Carl Linnaeus, Christiaan Hendrik Persoon, and Elias Magnus Fries, has been reshaped by DNA analysis. The kingdom is divided into one subkingdom, seven phyla, and ten subphyla.

=== KEY: Furniture_bb3a8b
HEADWORD: Furniture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Ancient Greece | Ancient Rome | Baroque | Middle Ages | Modernism | Renaissance | Çatalhöyük
ENTRY:
Objects intended to support human activities such as seating, eating, storing items, working, and sleeping. Stools, chairs, sofas, tables, desks, beds, cupboards, shelves, and drawers are all furniture. It can be made from many materials, including metal, plastic, and wood, using woodworking joints that often reflect local culture. Furniture can be a product of design and a form of decorative art, and it may serve a symbolic or religious purpose. The English word derives from French *fourniture*, from *fournir*, "to supply"; French and other Romance languages and German instead use variants of *meubles*, from Latin *mobilia*, "moveable goods".

People have used tree stumps, rocks, and moss as furniture since the beginning of human civilization. From around 30,000 years ago, people began to construct and carve furniture from wood, stone, and animal bones. A seated figure from [[Çatalhöyük]] in Turkey dates to between 6000 and 5500 BC. The houses of Skara Brae, a Neolithic village in Orkney, Scotland, dated 3100 to 2500 BC, contain the earliest surviving furniture: cupboards, dressers, beds, shelves, and seats built of stone because wood was scarce.

Complex construction techniques such as joinery began in the early dynastic period of [[Ancient Egypt]], which saw wooden stools and tables sometimes decorated with valuable metals or ivory. In [[Ancient Greece]] and [[Ancient Rome]], thrones were commonplace, as were the *klinai*, multipurpose couches for relaxing, eating, and sleeping.

Furniture of the [[Middle Ages]] was usually heavy, oak, and ornamented. Design expanded during the Italian [[Renaissance]] of the 14th and 15th centuries, and the 17th century was marked by opulent, often gilded [[Baroque]] designs. The 19th century is usually defined by revival styles, and the first three quarters of the 20th century are often seen as a march toward [[Modernism]].

=== KEY: Fuse_electrical_519c3b

Electrical safety device that provides overcurrent protection to a circuit. A fuse contains a metal wire or strip that melts when too much current flows through it, interrupting the current. Neolithic archaeological site in modern-day [[Turkey]], in [[Mesopotamia|Upper Mesopotamia]]. Its name means Potbelly Hill in Turkish. The settlement was occupied from around 9500 BC to at least 8000 BC, during the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. Its large circular structures contain stone pillars, among the world's oldest known [[Megalith|megaliths]], many carved with animals and human figures. Their purpose is not definitively known, though the carvings offer insights into [[Prehistoric religion|prehistoric religion]]. Findings published in 2019 suggest a settlement with domestic structures and cereal processing, challenging an earlier view of the site as a sanctuary for nomads. Prehistorians disagree on whether farming led to settlement or the reverse, a debate in which the site plays a prominent role. German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt began excavations in 1995. The site was designated a [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Site in 2018, and around 10% had been excavated as of 2021.

=== KEY: G_del_s_incompleteness_theorems_889ec3
HEADWORD: Gödel's incompleteness theorems
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hilbert | Halting problem | Kurt Gödel | Mathematical logic
ENTRY:
Two theorems of [[Mathematical logic|mathematical logic]] on the limits of provability in formal axiomatic systems, published by [[Kurt Gödel]] in 1931. The first states that no consistent system of axioms whose theorems can be listed by an algorithm can prove all truths about the arithmetic of natural numbers; for any such system, some true statements are unprovable within it. The second shows that no such system can demonstrate its own consistency. The theorems are interpreted as showing that [[David Hilbert|Hilbert]]'s program to find a complete and consistent set of axioms for all of mathematics is impossible. Later results, including Turing's proof that no algorithm solves the [[Halting problem|halting problem]], extended these limitations. The theorems also shaped the philosophy of mathematics and computer science.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Turkic peoples|Turkic]] people of medieval Inner Asia, also known as Türks, Celestial Turks or Blue Turks. Under Bumin Qaghan and his sons, they succeeded the Rouran Khaganate as the main power in the region and established the First Turkic Khaganate. By the late 6th century it stretched from Manchuria to the Black Sea. The Göktürks practiced [[Tengri|Tengriism]] and left the earliest extant Turkic writing, the Orkhon inscriptions, in the Old Turkic script. The khaganate split into eastern and western branches around 581 and collapsed in 745 after a [[Uyghurs|Uyghur]] revolt backed by [[China|Tang China]]. The origin of the name Turk is unknown.

French actor and film producer, considered an icon of French cinema. He has appeared in over 200 films since 1967, mostly in leading roles, and won the César Award for Best Actor for *The Last Metro* (1980). He won the Best Actor award at the [[Cannes Film Festival]] for *Cyrano de Bergerac* (1990). He was granted [[Russia|Russian]] citizenship in 2013, and his support of [[Vladimir Putin]] caused controversy in France. He denied wrongdoing, but was charged with rape in December 2020 and convicted of sexual assault in a separate case in May 2025.

Hypothesized erogenous area of the front vaginal wall, also called the Gräfenberg spot after the German gynecologist Ernst Gräfenberg. It is typically reported 5–8 cm (2–3 in) up the wall, toward the urinary system, and may be part of the female prostate. Its existence as a distinct structure has not been proven, nor has the source of [[Female ejaculation|female ejaculation]]. Stimulation may lead to strong arousal and [[Orgasm|orgasm]]. The G-spot may be an extension of the [[Clitoris|clitoris]], which may be the actual cause of vaginal orgasms. Sexologists emphasize that not experiencing G-spot stimulation is normal.
