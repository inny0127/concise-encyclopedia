=== KEY: Allosaurus_707803
HEADWORD: Allosaurus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jurassic
ENTRY:
Genus of large theropod dinosaur from the late [[Jurassic]] period. It lived about 156 to 143 million years ago. Othniel C. Marsh named it in 1877, meaning 'different lizard', for lightweight vertebrae he believed were unique. The genus includes at least three valid species, the best known being *A. fragilis*. Most remains come from the Morrison Formation of North America, and for over half of the 20th century the genus was known as *Antrodemus*. A large bipedal predator, *A. fragilis* averaged 7.5–8 m (25–26 ft) in length, with the largest estimated at 9 m (30 ft). As the most abundant large predator of the Morrison Formation, it probably preyed on large herbivores such as sauropods. Whether it hunted in packs or alone is debated.

=== KEY: Allotropy_c2bb8f
HEADWORD: Allotropy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon | Chemical element | Diamond | Graphene | Graphite | Iron | Ozone | Phosphorus
ENTRY:
Property of some [[Chemical element|chemical elements]] to exist in two or more forms in the same physical state. Each form, called an allotrope, differs in how its atoms are bonded or arranged. The term, from Greek *allos* ('other') and *tropos* ('manner'), was first proposed in 1840 by Jöns Jakob Berzelius. [[Carbon]] has allotropes including [[Diamond]], with a cubic lattice; [[Graphite]], with hexagonal sheets; [[Graphene]], a single sheet of graphite; and fullerenes. Oxygen has two allotropes, dioxygen and [[Ozone]]; ozone is a much stronger oxidizing agent. [[Iron]] changes from a body-centered cubic to a face-centered cubic structure above 906 °C. [[Phosphorus]] has numerous solid allotropes, all reverting to the same P₄ form when melted. The general term for compounds is polymorphism.

=== KEY: Alloy_6c8d4a
HEADWORD: Alloy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bronze | Electrum | Metallic bonding | Stainless steel | Steel
ENTRY:
Mixture of chemical elements, in most cases at least one of them metallic, that retains the characteristics of a metal. Alloys often have properties that differ from those of their constituents. Most metals used commercially are alloyed to improve strength, hardness, or corrosion resistance, or to lower cost, as in alloys of gold and copper. Atoms in an alloy are joined by [[Metallic bonding|metallic bonding]] rather than the covalent bonds typical of chemical compounds.

Alloys are classified as substitutional or interstitial by atomic arrangement, and as homogeneous (a single phase), heterogeneous (two or more phases), or intermetallic. An alloy may be a solid solution in which all crystals have the same composition, or a mixture of phases forming a microstructure of different crystals. Each constituent has a saturation point: iron can hold at most 6.67% carbon, forming cementite.

Alloying changes mechanical behavior. Soft aluminum becomes much stronger when alloyed with copper. Adding a small amount of carbon to iron trades ductility for strength, producing [[Steel|steel]]; adding chromium raises corrosion resistance, producing [[Stainless steel|stainless steel]]. Other examples include red gold (gold and copper), white gold (gold and silver), sterling silver (silver and copper), solder, brass, and pewter.

Some alloys occur naturally, such as [[Electrum|electrum]], of silver and gold; meteorites are sometimes made of natural iron-nickel alloys. [[Bronze]], of tin and copper, was one of the first alloys made by humans and is much stronger and harder than either component. In ancient times steel could arise only as an accidental byproduct of smelting iron ore. Most alloys, including various aluminum, titanium, nickel, and magnesium alloys, were not created until the 1900s.

=== KEY: Alluvial_fan_607d46
HEADWORD: Alluvial fan
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Desert | Indo-Gangetic Plain | Mars | Titan (moon)
ENTRY:
Fan-shaped accumulation of sediment spreading outward from a concentrated source. Fans form where a flow of water, sediment or debris emerges from a confined channel, such as a narrow canyon, onto a flat plain; the reduced flow drops its load. They are most common at the foot of [[Desert|desert]] mountains and characteristic of arid to semiarid climates, though they also form in humid areas with intense rainfall and in glaciated regions. They range from less than 1 sq km (0.4 sq mi) to almost 20,000 sq km (7,700 sq mi), with slopes of 1.5 to 25 degrees. Some of the largest lie along the Himalaya front on the [[Indo-Gangetic Plain]], where a shift of the feeder channel can cause catastrophic flooding, as on the Kosi River fan in 2008. Fans are also found on [[Mars]] and [[Titan (moon)|Titan]].

=== KEY: Alpha_particle_6b7406
HEADWORD: Alpha particle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ernest Rutherford | Ionization | Radioactive decay
ENTRY:
Ionizing particle of two protons and two neutrons, identical to the nucleus of a helium-4 atom. Alpha particles, also called alpha rays or alpha radiation, are named after the Greek letter α. They are generally produced in [[Radioactive decay|alpha decay]] but may also arise in other ways. The term was coined by [[Ernest Rutherford]] while he studied uranium radiation. Standard alpha decay produces alpha particles with a kinetic energy of about 5 MeV and a velocity of about 4% of the speed of light. They are highly [[Ionization|ionizing]] but penetrate little, stopped by a few centimeters of air or by the skin. Helium nuclei, which form 10–12% of cosmic rays, are usually of much higher energy and can penetrate far more deeply. Once an alpha particle gains electrons, it becomes a neutral helium atom.

=== KEY: Alphabet_49c3fc
HEADWORD: Alphabet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Egyptian hieroglyphs | Hebrew alphabet | Phoenician alphabet | Phoneme | Ugarit | Writing system
ENTRY:
[[Writing system]] that uses a standard set of symbols, called graphemes or letters, to represent more or less the sounds of a spoken language. These graphemes largely correspond to [[Phoneme|phonemes]], the smallest sound segments that distinguish one word from another. Peter T. Daniels distinguishes true alphabets, which use letters for both consonants and vowels, from abjads, which generally lack vowel indicators, and abugidas, which mark vowels with diacritics. In this narrower sense the Greek alphabet was the first true alphabet; it derived from the Phoenician alphabet, an abjad.

The first letters were invented in Ancient Egypt to aid in writing [[Egyptian hieroglyphs]]. About 24 hieroglyphs, the uniliterals, gave single sounds; the system was used until the 5th century AD. A script appeared in Egyptian turquoise mines on the Sinai Peninsula c. 1840 BC, and an earlier version dated to c. 1800 BC was found at Wadi el-Hol in 1999. This Proto-Sinaitic script had no vowel characters. The best-attested Bronze Age alphabet is Ugaritic, an alphabetic cuneiform script of 30 signs invented in [[Ugarit]] before the 15th century BC.

Proto-Sinaitic developed into the [[Phoenician alphabet]] before c. 1050 BC. The oldest Phoenician text is an inscription on the sarcophagus of King Ahiram, c. 1000 BC. With only about two dozen letters, it was simple enough for trade, and it is the ultimate ancestor of many modern scripts, including Arabic, Cyrillic, Greek, Hebrew, and Latin. Aramaic gave rise to the [[Hebrew alphabet]].

The word comes from Late Latin *alphabetum* and Greek *alphabētos*, made from *alpha* and *beta*, which in turn came from Phoenician *aleph*, "ox," and *bet*, "house."

=== KEY: Alternating_current_29e119
HEADWORD: Alternating current
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Direct current | Transformer
ENTRY:
Electric current alternating in direction, unlike [[Direct current|direct current]] (DC). It is the form in which electric power is delivered to homes and businesses, and its usual waveform is a sine wave. Power is transmitted at high voltage because the power lost in a wire equals the square of the current multiplied by the wire's resistance (P = I²R); halving the current by doubling the voltage cuts the loss by a factor of four. [[Transformer|Transformers]] step voltage up for transmission and down for use, typically to 100 V–240 V for domestic supply. Alternating current prevailed over direct current in the late 19th century; Niagara Falls was electrified with AC transmission lines in 1895.

=== KEY: Alternation_of_generations_c2ee3f
HEADWORD: Alternation of generations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Flowering plant | Gametophyte | Meiosis | Mitosis | Sporophyte
ENTRY:
Life cycle of plants and algae in which haploid and diploid multicellular phases alternate. A mature [[Sporophyte|sporophyte]] produces haploid spores by [[Meiosis|meiosis]]; the spores grow into haploid [[Gametophyte|gametophytes]], which produce gametes by [[Mitosis|mitosis]]. Two gametes fuse to form a diploid zygote that develops into a new sporophyte. In liverworts, mosses and hornworts the gametophyte is dominant and the sporophyte depends on it. In ferns the gametophyte is a small prothallus, and in [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] it consists of just a few cells growing inside the sporophyte. Animals produce gametes directly and generally have no multicellular haploid phase.

=== KEY: Alternative_dispute_resolution_99d1c6

ENTRY:
Range of processes in which a third party helps disputing parties settle a dispute short of [[Lawsuit|litigation]]. Also called external dispute resolution, it is increasingly used within court systems as well as outside them. The term arose from Frank Sander’s paper “Varieties of Dispute Processing.” In 2008 some courts required parties to try mediation before trial; the European Mediation Directive contemplates compulsory mediation, meaning attendance is required but settlement is not. === KEY: Alternative_medicine_bfaa76

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hydrogen peroxide | Placebo | Scientific method
ENTRY:
Healing practices outside mainstream evidence-based medicine, which aim to produce the effects of medicine without supporting evidence of effectiveness. Unlike modern medicine, these therapies are generally not tested by the [[Scientific method|scientific method]] of clinical trials, and may rely on testimonials, tradition or belief in supernatural energies. Billions of dollars have been spent studying alternative medicine, with few or no positive results.

=== KEY: Altruism_a74d31
HEADWORD: Altruism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Auguste Comte
ENTRY:
Concern for the well-being and life of others, independent of personal benefit or reciprocity. The word was popularized, and possibly coined, by French philosopher [[Auguste Comte]] around 1830 as an antonym of egoism. Altruism is an important moral value in many cultures and religions, and it can extend to other sentient beings and future generations. In biology, altruism is an action that costs the actor, in time, energy or survival probability, while benefiting another without reciprocity. Psychological egoism holds that no act is truly altruistic, since the actor may gain an intrinsic reward; whether such rewards count as benefits is disputed. Effective altruism uses evidence and reason to determine the most effective ways to benefit others.

=== KEY: Aluminium_9b205f

ENTRY:
Chemical element (symbol Al, atomic number 13), a post-transition metal in the [[Boron|boron]] group. Silvery, soft, ductile and nonmagnetic, aluminum has a density about one-third that of steel. It forms a protective oxide layer when exposed to air. It is the third-most abundant element in Earth’s crust, after [[Oxygen|oxygen]] and [[Silicon|silicon]], occurring mainly in rock rather than as free metal, and is obtained industrially from [[Bauxite|bauxite]]. Its discovery was announced in 1825 by [[Hans Christian Ørsted]]. Mass production became possible after the Hall–Héroult process, developed independently in 1886 by Charles Martin Hall in the United States and Paul Héroult in France. In 1954 aluminum became the most produced non-ferrous metal, surpassing [[Copper|copper]]. Most is used in transportation, engineering, construction and packaging. No living thing is known to metabolize aluminum salts, though plants and animals tolerate them well.

=== KEY: Amazon_River_ab6850
HEADWORD: Amazon River
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amazon rainforest | Andes | Atlantic Ocean | Brazil | Manaus | Marajó | Nile | Rio Negro (Amazon)
ENTRY:
River, northern South America, the largest in the world by discharge volume of water. Its length is recognized as 6,400 km (4,000 mi), though reports range from 6,575 to 7,062 km (4,086 to 4,388 mi), and whether it or the [[Nile]] is the longest river system is disputed. A 2014 study found its farthest source to be the headwaters of the Mantaro River in Peru. The Mantaro and Apurímac join with other tributaries to form the Ucayali, which meets the Marañón upstream of Iquitos; countries other than [[Brazil]] regard this as the main stem. Brazilians call the section above the confluence with the [[Rio Negro (Amazon)|Rio Negro]] the Solimões, and the river below it, from the Meeting of Waters at [[Manaus]], the Amazon.

Average discharge is about 215,000 to 230,000 cubic meters per second, greater than the next seven largest independent rivers combined and 20% of global riverine discharge into oceans. Its basin, the largest in the world, covers approximately 7,000,000 sq km (2,700,000 sq mi) and contains the [[Amazon rainforest]].

Geological studies suggest that the river once flowed east to west, until the rising [[Andes]] blocked its flow to the Pacific and redirected it to the [[Atlantic Ocean]]. It became Rio Amazonas after native warriors, led by women, attacked a 16th-century expedition under Francisco de Orellana, recalling the Amazon warriors of Greek mythology.

Archaeological evidence shows complex, large-scale indigenous societies, mainly chiefdoms with towns and cities. Archaeologists estimate that more than 3 million indigenous people lived around the river when Orellana traveled it in 1541, and the island of [[Marajó]] may have supported 100,000. Repeated burning enriched soils into dark earth known as *terra preta*, which supported large-scale agriculture.

=== KEY: Amazon_rainforest_3150ec
HEADWORD: Amazon rainforest
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amazon River | Brazil | Colombia | Deforestation | Indigenous peoples | Peru | Tropical rainforest
ENTRY:
Moist broadleaf [[Tropical rainforest|tropical rainforest]] that covers most of the Amazon basin of South America. The basin encompasses 7 million sq km (2.7 million sq mi), of which 6 million sq km (2.3 million sq mi) are rainforest, spanning nine nations and 3,344 indigenous territories. Sixty percent of the forest lies in [[Brazil]], 13% in [[Peru]], and 10% in [[Colombia]]. It is more than half of the world's remaining rainforest and the largest and most biodiverse tract of tropical rainforest, with an estimated 390 billion trees in about 16,000 species.

More than 30 million people of 350 ethnic groups live there; [[Indigenous peoples]] make up 9% of the population, and 60 groups remain largely isolated. Excavation at Caverna da Pedra Pintada shows humans settled the region at least 11,200 years ago. Some 5 million people may have lived there in AD 1500; the native population fell to 1 million by 1900 and below 200,000 by the early 1980s, and disease, including smallpox, is believed to have devastated earlier civilization. Francisco de Orellana was the first European to travel the length of the [[Amazon River]], in 1542. Since the 1970s numerous geoglyphs dating from AD 1–1250 have been found on deforested land.

Large-scale [[Deforestation|deforestation]], driven by soy, cattle ranching, mining, and logging, has caused cumulative losses approaching 20% of the original forest cover. Annual rates in Brazil fell by nearly 50% in the first ten months of 2023 compared with 2022.

LINK TARGETS: Battles of Lexington and Concord | Boston Tea Party | Constitution of the United States | French and Indian War | George Washington | Siege of Yorktown | Thirteen Colonies | Treaty of Paris (1783)
ENTRY:
Colonial rebellion and political movement (1765–1789) in the [[Thirteen Colonies]] of Great Britain, ending in the creation of the United States.

Discontent with British rule began shortly after the end of the [[French and Indian War]] in 1763, when Parliament imposed new taxes, ostensibly to compensate for wartime costs. In 1765 the Stamp Act Congress in New York City issued the Declaration of Rights and Grievances on the principle of no taxation without representation. The Stamp Act was repealed in 1767, but the Townshend Acts renewed tensions, and the deployment of British troops to Massachusetts led to the Boston Massacre in 1770. In December 1773 the Sons of Liberty staged the [[Boston Tea Party]]; Parliament's punitive Intolerable Acts increased support for revolution.

Twelve colonies sent delegates to the First Continental Congress in 1774. Fighting began with the [[Battles of Lexington and Concord]]. The Second Continental Congress created the Continental Army and appointed [[George Washington]] commander-in-chief in 1775. On July 4, 1776, Congress unanimously adopted the Declaration of Independence. On September 28, 1781, combined French and Continental forces under Washington captured a British army at the [[Siege of Yorktown]]. On September 3, 1783, the [[Treaty of Paris (1783)|Treaty of Paris]] recognized American independence and ceded nearly all territory east of the Mississippi River and south of the Great Lakes.

The Articles of Confederation preceded the [[Constitution of the United States]] took effect in 1789. The new republic had significant democratic limitations; it preserved slavery and displaced Native American populations through expanded settlement. Roughly one-third of colonists were Loyalists, who faced property confiscation and exile.

ENTRY:
Conflict of 1775–1783 between the [[Thirteen Colonies]] and Great Britain, which ended in American independence. The war began with the [[Battles of Lexington and Concord]] in April 1775. The Continental Army, commanded by [[George Washington]], won the decisive [[Siege of Yorktown]] in 1781, after which King George III's government negotiated an end to the war. A Patriot victory at Saratoga in October 1777 convinced France to sign a commercial agreement and a Treaty of Alliance in February 1778. Spain allied with France in 1779 but did not formally ally with the Americans. The [[Treaty of Paris (1783)|Treaty of Paris]], signed September 3, 1783, recognized the sovereignty and independence of the United States. The Treaties of Versailles resolved the conflicts with France and Spain.

Bison species endemic to [[North America]], also called the American buffalo. The species, *Bison bison*, is one of two living bison species, along with the European bison. === KEY: Amoeba_892857

Unicellular organism or cell type that changes shape, mainly by extending and retracting pseudopods. Major taxonomic group of about 2,400 described species of amoeboid [[Protist|protists]]. Its members often have blunt, fingerlike, lobose pseudopods and tubular mitochondrial cristae. Traditionally ranked as a phylum within the kingdom Protista or Protozoa, it is retained by the International Society of Protistologists as an unranked 'supergroup' within [[Eukaryote|Eukaryota]].
