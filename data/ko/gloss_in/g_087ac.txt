=== KEY: Megalith_f909f1
HEADWORD: Megalith
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brittany | Bronze Age | Göbekli Tepe | Neolithic | Stonehenge | Sweden
ENTRY:
Large stone used, alone or with other stones, to construct a prehistoric structure or monument. The word was first used in 1849 by the British antiquarian Algernon Herbert in reference to [[Stonehenge]]; it derives from the Greek *mégas* ("great") and *líthos* ("stone"). More than 35,000 megalithic structures have been identified across Europe, from [[Sweden]] to the Mediterranean. Most were erected between the [[Neolithic]] and the [[Bronze Age]]. The oldest known ceremonial structures, at [[Göbekli Tepe]] in Turkey, date to c. 9000 BC. Common types include the menhir, a single upright stone; the dolmen, a stone table usually covered with earth; and alignments, such as the Carnac Stones in [[Brittany]], France, which consist of thousands of stones. Stone circles are called cromlechs in most languages.

=== KEY: Megalodon_34bd64
HEADWORD: Megalodon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cetacean | Great white shark | Sea turtle | Whale
ENTRY:
Extinct giant mackerel shark, *Otodus megalodon*, that lived from about 23 to 3.58 million years ago. Once classified with the [[Great white shark]], it is now placed in the family Otodontidae. Estimated maximum lengths range from 14.2 to 24.3 m, and its bite force is thought to have been 108,500 to 182,200 newtons. It probably preyed on large marine animals, including [[Whale|whales]], seals and [[Sea turtle|sea turtles]]. Its decline may have been caused by oceanic cooling, falling sea levels, reduced whale prey and competition with whale-eating [[Cetacean|cetaceans]].

=== KEY: Megalopolis_f68217
HEADWORD: Megalopolis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boston | Massachusetts | Metropolitan area | United States | Washington, D.C.
ENTRY:
Group of neighboring [[Metropolitan area|metropolitan areas]] perceived as one continuous urban region, also called a supercity or megaregion. Linked by common systems of transport, economy and ecology, a megalopolis is integrated enough that coordinating policy across it is valuable, although its constituent metropolises keep their own identities. The term comes from the Greek for "big city" and was first applied to the Arcadian city of Megalopolis, founded in the 4th century BC. In the late 1950s and early 1960s the political scientist Jean Gottmann applied it to the urbanized northeastern seaboard of the [[United States]], from [[Boston]], [[Massachusetts]], to [[Washington, D.C.]] Gottmann defined a megalopolis as having a population of about 25 million. In China the official term is "city cluster."

=== KEY: Megathrust_earthquake_cdf9e3
HEADWORD: Megathrust earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Oceanic trench | Thrust fault | Tsunami
ENTRY:
Type of very powerful earthquake occurring at convergent plate boundaries, where one tectonic plate is forced beneath another. It results from slip along a [[Thrust fault|thrust fault]] at the contact between the two plates. Megathrust earthquakes are the planet's most powerful, with moment magnitudes that can exceed 9.0; every earthquake of magnitude 9.0 or greater since 1900 has been one. Because the faults often lie beneath [[Oceanic trench|oceanic trenches]], they can abruptly displace the seafloor and generate [[Tsunami|tsunamis]] that are often more destructive than the earthquake itself, including teletsunamis that cross ocean basins.

=== KEY: Megatsunami_7f7600
HEADWORD: Megatsunami
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1883 eruption of Krakatoa | Alaska
ENTRY:
Extremely large wave created by a sudden displacement of material into water, such as a landslide, meteor impact or volcanic eruption. Ordinary tsunamis, caused by seafloor displacement from earthquakes, rarely exceed 30 m in height, whereas megatsunamis can have initial wave heights in the hundreds of meters because the displacement splashes water upward and outward. Examples include the wave from the [[1883 eruption of Krakatoa|1883 eruption of Krakatoa]] and the 1958 Lituya Bay landslide in [[Alaska]], which produced a runup of 524.6 m. The term has no precise definition but is commonly used for waves over 100 m high.
