=== KEY: United_Nations_a71d9d
HEADWORD: United Nations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Decolonization | Franklin D. Roosevelt | International Court of Justice | League of Nations | New York City | Woodrow Wilson | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
International intergovernmental organization for maintaining peace and security. Its Charter was signed on June 26, 1945, and entered into force on October 24, 1945. With 193 member states, it is the largest international organization; the Holy See and the State of Palestine are observer states.

The UN is headquartered in [[New York City]], with other major offices at Geneva, Nairobi, Vienna, and The Hague. It has six principal organs: the General Assembly, Security Council, Economic and Social Council, Trusteeship Council, [[International Court of Justice]], and Secretariat. Together with specialized agencies such as the [[World Health Organization]], they form the UN System. It operates 11 peacekeeping missions and has deployed over 70 since 1948, receiving the Nobel Peace Prize in 1988. It has focused on economic and social development, particularly during the wave of [[Decolonization|decolonization]] in the mid-20th century.

Its predecessor, the [[League of Nations]], came into being on January 10, 1920, after World War I leaders including U.S. President [[Woodrow Wilson]] advocated a world body. The League effectively closed down when World War II began in 1939. [[Franklin D. Roosevelt]] coined the term "United Nations" for the Allied countries, and the Declaration by United Nations was signed on January 1, 1942, by the United States, the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and China, and by 22 other nations the next day.

Critics cite perceived ineffectiveness, bias, and corruption, including the Oil-for-Food scandal. They also note that vetoes by permanent Security Council members (China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States) have blocked resolutions on conflicts such as those in Syria and Ukraine. Supporters credit it with aiding decolonization and coordinating responses to health crises.

=== KEY: United_States_Bill_of_Rights_99fcfb
HEADWORD: United States Bill of Rights
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constitution of the United States | James Madison | Magna Carta
ENTRY:
First ten amendments to the [[Constitution of the United States]]. Written to address objections raised by Anti-Federalists during the 1787–88 ratification debate, they were largely the work of [[James Madison]]. Ten amendments were ratified on December 15, 1791. The amendments guarantee freedoms of speech, religion, press and assembly, the right to keep and bear arms, and protections in criminal procedure. They also reserve powers not granted to the federal government for the states or the people. Many provisions later applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, a process called incorporation. They built on earlier documents such as the [[Magna Carta]].

ENTRY:
Founding document of the [[United States]], adopted unanimously in [[Philadelphia]] on July 4, 1776, by the Second Continental Congress. Drafted largely by [[Thomas Jefferson]], it explains why the [[Thirteen Colonies]] regarded themselves as independent sovereign states no longer subject to British rule. It cites 27 colonial grievances against King [[George III]] and asserts natural rights, including a right of revolution. The delegates knew they were committing high treason against the Crown. Most signatures were added on August 2, 1776. [[Abraham Lincoln]] viewed the document as the moral standard for the nation and made it the centerpiece of his [[Gettysburg Address]] in 1863. Its second sentence, asserting that all men are created equal, is considered one of the most famed lines in world history.

=== KEY: United_States_customary_units_6e5545
HEADWORD: United States customary units
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Imperial units | Metric system | United States
ENTRY:
Units of measurement used in the [[United States]], derived from English units. The customary system was standardized in 1832 on the basis of the system then in use in the United Kingdom before the imperial system took effect on January 1, 1826. Many units are similar to their [[Imperial units|imperial]] counterparts, though definitions differ. The Mendenhall Order of 1893 redefined most units in terms of the meter and kilogram. Customary units are used in commerce and daily life, while science, medicine and much of industry rely on [[Metric system|metric]] units. The Metric Conversion Act of 1975 made the metric system "the preferred system of weights and measures for U.S. trade and commerce," but implementation has been limited.

=== KEY: United_States_dollar_6d0deb
HEADWORD: United States dollar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Federal Reserve | World War I | World War II
ENTRY:
Official currency of the United States and several other countries, subdivided into 100 cents. The Coinage Act of 1792 established the dollar as the base unit. Banknotes are issued as [[Federal Reserve]] Notes, popularly called greenbacks, and the Federal Reserve System conducts monetary policy. The dollar was originally defined in silver and, from 1834, in gold. It was linked solely to gold in 1900 and revalued to $35 per troy ounce in 1934, and all links to gold were repealed in 1971. After [[World War I]] it became an important reserve currency, and under the Bretton Woods Agreement near the end of [[World War II]] it displaced British sterling as the primary reserve currency. It is the most widely used currency in international transactions. As of January 1, 2025, about US$2.37 trillion in currency was in circulation.

=== KEY: United_States_f253ef
HEADWORD: United States
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alaska | American Revolution | Cold War | Confederate States of America | Hawaii | Thirteen Colonies | Washington, D.C.
ENTRY:
Federal republic primarily in North America, consisting of 50 states and a federal capital district, [[Washington, D.C.]] The 48 contiguous states border Canada and Mexico, with [[Alaska]] in the northwest and the archipelago of [[Hawaii]] in the Pacific. It has the world's fourth-largest total area, 9,833,517 sq km, and third-largest population, exceeding 341 million.

Paleo-Indians migrated from North Asia to North America at least 15,000 years ago. British colonization beginning in 1607 established the [[Thirteen Colonies]]. Disputes over taxation without parliamentary representation sparked the [[American Revolution]]; independence was formally declared on July 2, 1776, in the Lee Resolution, and the 1775–1783 war confirmed international recognition. The Constitution dates from 1787. The country expanded westward through purchase, settlement, and conquest. A sectional division over slavery led 11 Southern states to secede and form the [[Confederate States of America]], fighting the Union in the Civil War of 1861–1865; slavery was abolished with Union victory. By 1900 the U.S. had emerged as a great power. It entered World War II after Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, then rivaled the Soviet Union in the [[Cold War]]; the Soviet collapse in 1991 left it the sole superpower.

The Constitution established a separation of powers among legislative, executive, and judicial branches. Executive authority is vested in a president; Congress is bicameral, with a House of Representatives and a Senate. The Supreme Court exercises judicial review, and the 50 states have substantial autonomy.

The economy accounts for over a quarter of nominal global GDP and has been the world's largest since about 1890; wealth inequality is highly pronounced. The U.S. makes up nearly a third of global military spending. "America" derives from the first name of Amerigo Vespucci and was first used as a place name in 1507.

ENTRY:
Military intervention in [[Grenada]] led by the [[United States]] on October 25, 1983. === KEY: Universal_Declaration_of_Human_Rights_5a46b4

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Eleanor Roosevelt | United Nations
ENTRY:
Declaration adopted by the [[United Nations]] General Assembly that codifies the rights and freedoms of all human beings. Drafted under the chairmanship of [[Eleanor Roosevelt]], it was adopted as Resolution 217 on December 10, 1948, in Paris, with 48 votes in favor, none against, eight abstentions and two not voting. Although not legally binding, it was the first step toward the International Bill of Human Rights, completed in 1966. Its principles have been incorporated into treaties and national constitutions, and it has been translated 577 times.

=== KEY: Universe_89af6c
HEADWORD: Universe
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Cosmic inflation | Cosmic microwave background | Cosmology | Dark energy | Dark matter | Milky Way | Observable universe
ENTRY:
Totality of space, time, matter, and energy, with all the structures they form, from subatomic particles to galactic filaments. The word comes from Latin *universus*, "combined into one"; Greek philosophers from Pythagoras onward called it *to pan* ("the all") and *kosmos*.

[[Cosmology]] has established that the universe has been expanding and cooling for 13.8 billion years since the [[Big Bang]], an age inferred by fitting cosmological models to precision data, especially measurements of the [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]]. The [[Observable universe|observable portion]] is approximately 93 billion light-years in diameter; it is bounded by cosmological horizons, and the total size is not known. Early cosmological models were geocentric; astronomical observations during the Scientific Revolution led to a heliocentric model. The Sun is one of a few hundred billion stars in the [[Milky Way]], which is one of a few hundred billion galaxies in the observable universe. At the largest scale, galaxies are distributed uniformly; at smaller scales they form clusters and superclusters, arranged in filaments around voids in a foam-like structure. General relativity and discoveries of the early 20th century led to the modern view of an expanding universe.

In the Big Bang model, the earliest state was extremely hot and dense. An intense period of expansion called [[Cosmic inflation|cosmic inflation]] is postulated to explain many observations, though much of the earliest time is not understood. Big Bang nucleosynthesis lasted about 15 minutes and produced helium, small amounts of deuterium, and traces of lithium. After about 377,000 years, electrons and nuclei formed the first stable atoms, and the universe became transparent;  the photons released still form that background radiation. Matter's energy density overtook that of photons and neutrinos at around 47,000 years. The first stars formed after around 100–300 million years and gradually reionized the universe, seeding it with elements heavier than helium. After about 9.8 billion years the density of matter fell below that of [[Dark energy|dark energy]], beginning the present era, in which expansion accelerates.

In the widely accepted ΛCDM model, [[Dark matter|dark matter]] accounts for about 25.8%±1.1% of the mass and energy in the universe, dark energy about 69.2%±1.2%, and ordinary baryonic matter only 4.84%±0.1%; stars, planets, and visible gas clouds form about 6% of that ordinary matter. The nature of dark energy remains unknown. Competing hypotheses exist about the ultimate fate of the universe and what, if anything, preceded the Big Bang.

=== KEY: University_50c60a
HEADWORD: University
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Academic degree | Gupta Empire | Latin | Tertiary education | University of Bologna | University of Cambridge | University of Oxford | Xuanzang
ENTRY:
Institution of [[Tertiary education|tertiary education]] and research that awards [[Academic degree|academic degrees]] in several disciplines. The word derives from [[Latin]] *universitas magistrorum et scholarium*, roughly "community of teachers and scholars". Universities typically offer undergraduate and postgraduate programs.

The first universities developed in Europe from schools maintained by the Church to educate priests. The oldest shared several features: they used the word *universitas*, drew students from beyond the local region, taught at least one higher faculty (theology, law, or medicine) beyond the seven liberal arts, awarded degrees through a public oral event managed by a guild of masters, had some autonomy from ecclesiastical authorities, enjoyed academic freedom, and issued secular as well as religious degrees. The oldest by these criteria are the [[University of Bologna]] (established around 1088), Paris, [[University of Oxford|Oxford]], [[University of Cambridge|Cambridge]], and Palencia.

Academic freedom is traced to Bologna's charter, the Constitutio Habita, of 1155 or 1158, which guaranteed traveling scholars unhindered passage. On September 18, 1988, 430 rectors signed the Magna Charta Universitatum. Debate continues over whether academic freedom at publicly funded universities should support public goods such as social justice or also include a prerogative to pursue knowledge for its own sake.

Earlier centers of learning existed. Theodosius II founded the Pandidakterion, later the University of Constantinople, in 425. Nalanda was founded around 427 by Kumaragupta I of the [[Gupta Empire]] and attracted scholars such as [[Xuanzang]]; it was destroyed in 1202, and scholars dispute calling it a university in the modern sense. Cathedral schools arose across France and Germany, and monastic schools flourished in the 9th and 10th centuries.

Public research university in [[Bologna]], [[Italy]], and the oldest university in continuous operation in the world. === KEY: Uranus_76b840
HEADWORD: Uranus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Giant planet | Hipparchus | Magnetic field | Methane | Natural satellite | Sun | Voyager 2 | William Herschel
ENTRY:
Seventh planet from the [[Sun]], a cyan-colored [[Giant planet|ice giant]]. Most of it consists of water, ammonia, and [[Methane|methane]] in a supercritical phase that astronomers call "ice". Its diameter, about 51,118 km, is third-largest among the planets, and its mass fourth-largest. A rocky core lies inside a volatile mantle, surrounded by a thick hydrogen and helium atmosphere. The atmosphere has the lowest minimum temperature of any planet, 49 K (−224 °C), and peak winds of 900 km/h. Internal heat is very low compared with other giant planets, for reasons that remain unclear.

Uranus has an axial tilt of 82.23° and a retrograde rotation period of 17 hours and 14 minutes. Over its 84-Earth-year orbit, each pole gets about 42 years of continuous sunlight, then 42 years of darkness. Its [[Magnetic field|magnetic field]] is highly asymmetric. The extremely dark ring system reflects only about 2% of incoming light. The planet has 29 [[Natural satellite|natural satellites]], including five major moons: Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon.

Visible to the naked eye, Uranus was never recognized as a planet by ancient observers because of its dimness and slow motion. It was apparently observed by [[Hipparchus]] in 128 BC, and John Flamsteed cataloged it as 34 Tauri in 1690. [[William Herschel]] observed it on March 13, 1781, from Bath, England, and first reported it as a comet. It was the first planet found with a telescope, and its discovery effectively doubled the known size of the Solar System. About seven decades later, consensus was reached to name it after the Greek god Ouranos.

[[Voyager 2]] flew past in 1986, the only visit so far.

=== KEY: Urbanization_a0903c
HEADWORD: Urbanization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Birmingham | Industrial Revolution | Manchester | Public transport | Rural area | United Nations | Urban planning
ENTRY:
Process of population shift from [[Rural area|rural]] to urban areas, in which human settlements form and grow as more people live and work in cities and towns. The term refers to the proportion of a national population living in urban areas, not the absolute number of urban residents. It is studied in disciplines including [[Urban planning|urban planning]], geography, sociology, and economics.

Until the 18th century, most people practiced subsistence agriculture, and towns were small centers of trade and manufacture, keeping the rural-to-urban ratio in equilibrium. The British Agricultural Revolution and the [[Industrial Revolution]] of the late 18th century broke this balance. In England and Wales, the share of the population living in cities of more than 20,000 people rose from 17% in 1801 to 54% in 1891. Under a broader definition, the urbanized share in 1891 was 72% in England and Wales, 37% in France, and 28% in the United States. Laborers converged on industrial cities such as [[Manchester]] and [[Birmingham]], and [[Public transport|public transport]] let cities expand. Urbanization spread across the Western world and, since the 1950s, the developing world. About 15% of the world's population lived in cities at the turn of the 20th century; according to the [[United Nations|UN]], more than 50% did by 2007.

The global urban share rose from about 3% in 1800 to 58% in 2025 under national definitions (81% under the UN Degree of Urbanization), about 4.8 billion people. Mechanization of agriculture and the pull of higher wages drive the shift, which correlates strongly with economic development. It brings pressures such as air pollution, resource strain, and informal settlements, especially in low-income countries, but also opportunities for more efficient resource use. The UN's 2025 World Urbanization Prospects project 83% by 2050, mostly in Asia and Africa.

=== KEY: Utilitarianism_28d717
HEADWORD: Utilitarianism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Consequentialism | Jeremy Bentham | John Stuart Mill
ENTRY:
Family of theories in normative ethics that prescribe actions maximizing happiness and well-being. It is a version of [[Consequentialism|consequentialism]], which holds that the consequences of an action alone determine whether it is right or wrong. [[Jeremy Bentham]] founded the modern tradition, describing utility as the capacity of actions to produce pleasure and happiness or to prevent pain. [[John Stuart Mill]] later distinguished higher intellectual pleasures from lower sensual ones. Proponents disagree on whether to judge individual acts by their likely results (act utilitarianism) or to follow rules that maximize utility (rule utilitarianism), and on whether total or average utility should be maximized. The theory has influenced cost-benefit analysis in economics and reforms in law and animal welfare. Critics argue that it can justify violating individual rights when the net gain in utility is large, and that utility is difficult to measure and compare across individuals.

=== KEY: Utopia_5261f3

ENTRY:
Imagined community or society with highly desirable or near-perfect qualities for its residents. [[Thomas More]] coined the term in 1516 for his book [[Utopia (book)|Utopia]], which describes a fictional island society. The word comes from Greek for "no place," but More noted its similarity to eutopia, "good place," and the two are pronounced alike, which may have caused the shift in meaning. The opposite of a utopia is a dystopia, which surpassed utopia in popularity in fiction from the 1950s onward, chiefly because of George Orwell's [[Nineteen Eighty-Four]].

Travel for recreation was once a luxury of the wealthy, as on the Grand Tour, and the modern concept developed over the last two centuries. Paid vacations spread in Europe in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as labor laws mandated holiday entitlement for workers. Mass tourism, enabled by railways and later commercial aviation, made travel accessible to more people. Statutory entitlements differ widely: France and Germany mandate around 30 days, while the United States has no federal minimum and workers take an average of 11–14 days a year. Research on the effects of vacations on health and work performance began in the 1990s.

=== KEY: Vaccination_9bbc2e
HEADWORD: Vaccination
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edward Jenner | Louis Pasteur | Polio | Smallpox | Tetanus | Vaccine | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Administration of a [[Vaccine|vaccine]] to help the immune system develop immunity to a disease. Vaccines contain a microorganism or virus in a weakened or killed state, or proteins or toxins from it. When a sufficiently large share of a population is vaccinated, herd immunity results, protecting people who cannot be vaccinated. Vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious disease, and it was largely responsible for the worldwide eradication of [[Smallpox|smallpox]] and the elimination of [[Polio|polio]] and [[Tetanus|tetanus]] from much of the world. The [[World Health Organization]] estimates that vaccination prevents 3.5–5 million deaths per year. The smallpox vaccine was invented in 1796 by [[Edward Jenner]], and [[Louis Pasteur]] furthered the concept through his work in microbiology. Smallpox was eradicated in 1979.

=== KEY: Vaccine_1f901c
HEADWORD: Vaccine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edward Jenner | Immune system | Louis Pasteur | Measles | Smallpox | Vaccination | Vaccine hesitancy | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity to a particular infectious or malignant disease. A vaccine typically contains an agent resembling a disease-causing microorganism, often made from weakened or killed forms of the microbe, its toxins, or one of its surface proteins. The agent stimulates the [[Immune system|immune system]] to destroy it and to recognize and destroy associated microorganisms encountered later. Vaccines can be prophylactic, preventing future infection, or therapeutic, fighting an existing disease such as cancer. Giving a vaccine is called [[Vaccination|vaccination]]; the science of developing and producing them is vaccinology.

Inoculation against [[Smallpox|smallpox]] was first recorded in 16th-century China, with earlier hints in the 10th century, and was brought from Turkey to Britain in 1721. In 1796, [[Edward Jenner]] showed that exposure to cowpox protected against smallpox. The terms "vaccine" and "vaccination" come from *Variolae vaccinae* ("smallpox of the cow"), the phrase Jenner used in 1798. In 1881 [[Louis Pasteur]] proposed extending the terms to the new protective inoculations.

Vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious diseases and is largely responsible for the eradication of smallpox, declared in 1980, and the restriction of polio, [[Measles|measles]], and tetanus. The [[World Health Organization]] reports licensed vaccines for twenty-five preventable infections. Routine childhood immunizations are credited with preventing an estimated 154 million deaths since 1974. U.S. measles cases fell from 763,094 in 1958, with 552 deaths, to fewer than 150 per year after vaccines were introduced.

Host-related lack of response occurs in an estimated 2–10% of individuals, and adaptive immunity can take 1–2 weeks to develop. Common adverse events are mild, such as injection-site pain and fever; rare serious ones include anaphylaxis (about 1.3 per million doses). The scientific consensus is that vaccines are safe and effective, but debates over mandates, adjuvants, and long-term safety contribute to [[Vaccine hesitancy|vaccine hesitancy]].

=== KEY: Vaccine_hesitancy_d86224
HEADWORD: Vaccine hesitancy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Vaccine | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Delay in accepting, or refusal of, vaccines despite their availability and supporting evidence. Causes include a lack of scientific knowledge, fear of needles, distrust of public authorities or of the vaccine itself, complacency and inconvenience. Although adverse effects are occasionally observed, the scientific consensus that vaccines are generally safe and effective is overwhelming. Hesitancy often results in outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases, and the [[World Health Organization]] characterizes it as one of the top ten global health threats. Myths and misinformation spread by anti-vaccination movements fuel it. Opposition to mandatory vaccination may reflect concern about civil liberties, distrust of the pharmaceutical industry or suspicion of profiteering. When diseases become rare, people may underestimate their danger, which reduces the perceived benefit of [[Vaccine|vaccination]].

=== KEY: Vacuole_5583a3
HEADWORD: Vacuole
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antonie van Leeuwenhoek | Enzyme
ENTRY:
Membrane-bound organelle present in plant, fungal and some protist, animal and bacterial cells. Vacuoles are compartments filled with water containing inorganic and organic molecules, including [[Enzyme|enzymes]], and sometimes engulfed solids. They form by the fusion of membrane vesicles and have no fixed shape or size. [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] described the plant vacuole in 1676. In plant cells the vacuole stores water and maintains turgor pressure, which supports leaves and flowers. Vacuoles also contain waste, maintain an acidic internal pH and export unwanted substances. In seeds, protein-storage vacuoles hold proteins needed for germination. In protists they store absorbed food. In animal cells vacuoles are smaller and more numerous, and they mainly assist exocytosis and endocytosis.
