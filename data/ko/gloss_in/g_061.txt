=== KEY: History_of_North_America_34ea0a
HEADWORD: History of North America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: American Revolution | Cahokia | Christopher Columbus | Paleo-Indians
ENTRY:
Past of the peoples and states of the continent, from first settlement to the modern political landscape.

Humans were long thought to have first arrived by crossing the Bering Sea 40,000 to 17,000 years ago, but later finds complicate that view. Fossil footprints in New Mexico indicate a presence between 23,000 and 21,000 years ago, and some discoveries may push estimates back by at least another 90,000 years. Dates and routes remain under research and debate. Early peoples, called [[Paleo-Indians]], later diversified into communities ranging from the Inuit in the far north to the Maya and Aztecs in the south. Before European contact, societies ranged from small bands of a few families to large empires. Mound-building Mississippian peoples built urban centers such as [[Cahokia]] by around 1000 CE.

European records begin with Norse colonization in the 10th century AD. The Norse founded a settlement on Greenland in 985 that persisted until the early 1400s and explored the east coast of Canada. After the voyages of [[Christopher Columbus]] began in 1492, Europeans arrived in large numbers, and the continent was divided among England, France, and Spain. Spain founded St. Augustine in 1565, followed by English Jamestown in 1607 and French Quebec in 1608. Eurasian diseases such as smallpox caused severe population declines among indigenous peoples.

Wars among the colonial powers, and growing desires for independence, led to revolutions, including the [[American Revolution]] (1775–1783) and Mexican independence in 1821. The Canadian Confederation formed in 1867. The U.S. Civil War (1861–1865) was fought over slavery and states' rights. Since the 19th century the continent's states have developed deepening ties and general peace, with free trade agreements opening in 1994.

=== KEY: History_of_Oceania_8f3a6d
HEADWORD: History of Oceania
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australia | Austronesian peoples | James Cook | Melanesia | Polynesia
ENTRY:
Human occupation of Oceania, including [[Australia]], New Zealand and the Pacific island nations. Settlement of the region began about 70,000 years ago in Near Oceania and about 3,000 years ago in Remote Oceania. Indigenous Australians are thought to have arrived about 50,000 years ago, although estimates reach back to 125,000 years ago. The earliest definite human remains, those of Mungo Man, date to about 40,000 years ago. Settlers reached New Guinea and the nearby islands between 50,000 and 30,000 years ago. The [[Austronesian peoples|Austronesian-speaking]] Lapita peoples spread through Remote Oceania from about 3,500 years ago, bringing pottery and crops such as taro. A 2008 genetic study found that [[Polynesia|Polynesians]] and Micronesians have little genetic relation to [[Melanesia|Melanesians]] but are closely related to East Asians. European contact began in the 16th century, and [[James Cook]] charted the Pacific from 1769 to 1779. Colonization followed in the 19th century.

Pre-independence history of the region that is now Pakistan, shared with neighboring Afghanistan, India, Iran and Tibet. Neolithic farming at Mehrgarh in Balochistan dates to 7000–5500 BCE. The [[Indus Valley Civilisation]], one of three early civilizations of the Old World, flourished from about 3300 BCE in the basin of the [[Indus River]], with major sites at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. Successive empires, including the Achaemenid, Maurya and Kushan, left their mark. After the Islamic conquest, the region was ruled by the Umayyad, Ghaznavid and Ghurid dynasties, the [[Delhi Sultanate]] and the [[Mughal Empire]]. The [[East India Company]] gained control in the first half of the 19th century, and the British Raj ruled directly after 1858. The Pakistan Movement, led by [[Muhammad Ali Jinnah]], ended with the [[Partition of India|partition of British India]] and Pakistan's independence on August 14, 1947.

=== KEY: History_of_Peru_5f7405
HEADWORD: History of Peru
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alberto Fujimori | Francisco Pizarro | Inca Empire | Spanish Empire
ENTRY:
Political and cultural development of Peru, from the first civilizations of the coast and Andes to the republic. The Norte Chico civilization, centered on Caral, was the oldest in the Americas, and its city dates to about 2500 BC. When the Spanish arrived in the 16th century, the highlands of southern Peru were the center of the [[Inca Empire]], the largest and most advanced state in pre-Columbian America. After the conquest of the Incas, led by [[Francisco Pizarro]] in 1532, the [[Spanish Empire|Spanish]] established a viceroyalty over most of their South American domains. Peru declared independence in 1821, but the Battle of Ayacucho in 1824 secured it. In the War of the Pacific (1879–1884), Chile seized Peru's nitrate-rich southern provinces. Cycles of military dictatorship and populist government followed, along with the Maoist Shining Path insurgency. Neoliberal reforms under [[Alberto Fujimori]] in the early 1990s stabilized the economy.

Close ties with [[Lithuania]] under the Jagiellonian dynasty led to the [[Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth]] in 1569, one of Europe's great powers. Political development of Portugal, from the Roman conquest to membership in the [[European Union]]. The [[Umayyad Caliphate]] conquered the Visigoth kingdom in 711–716 and founded [[Al-Andalus]]. === KEY: History_of_Scandinavia_b4c2cf

Northern European region of Denmark, Norway and Sweden, sometimes including Finland and Iceland. Ice covered almost all of Scandinavia during the last glaciation, and permanent though nomadic habitation began around 12,000 BCE. Hunter-gatherers followed the herds and salmon runs, and by the 6th millennium BC temperate forests covered southern Scandinavia. Sweden became a Baltic great power during the [[Thirty Years' War]]. Norway gained independence from Denmark in 1814 and from Sweden in 1905. Neutrality policies during the world wars were followed by postwar social democratic welfare states.

Political development of South Africa, from early human habitation to the post-apartheid era. === KEY: History_of_South_America_d70687
HEADWORD: History of South America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Francisco Pizarro | Gondwana | Inca Empire | War of the Pacific
ENTRY:
Past of the continent's geology, indigenous civilizations, colonial societies, and independent nations.

In the Paleozoic and early Mesozoic eras, South America and Africa formed part of [[Gondwana]] within the supercontinent Pangaea. They began to diverge around 110 million years ago, and about 35 million years ago the continent separated from Antarctica, becoming an island-continent whose species evolved in isolation. About 3 million years ago the Panamanian land bridge joined it to North America, triggering the Great American Interchange. About 60% of present-day South American mammals evolved from North American species.

People crossed from Asia, and genomic research indicates at least three major population dispersals. The Monte Verde II site in Chile may date to around 14,500 years ago. The Norte Chico civilization in Peru, dating to about 3500 BC, is described as the oldest civilization in the Americas and one of the first six independent civilizations in the world. Chavín culture influenced the Andes from approximately 900 to 200 BCE, and the [[Inca Empire]] built a vast network of roads and administrative systems.

The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) divided the continent between Spain and Portugal. [[Francisco Pizarro]] defeated the Inca in 1532. Conquest and demographic collapse, together with the trans-Atlantic slave trade, which made Brazil home to millions of people of African descent, produced new mestizo societies and social structures. Wars of independence from 1810 to 1825 freed most Spanish colonies, and Brazil became independent in 1822. The 19th and 20th centuries brought nation building, European immigration, border conflicts such as the [[War of the Pacific]] (1879–1884), and liberal-conservative strife. Military juntas ruled Argentina (1976–1983) and Chile (1973–1990) before democratic transitions in the 1980s.

Mainland and maritime sub-regions of Southeast Asia, from prehistory to the present. Homo sapiens reached mainland Southeast Asia about 70,000 years ago and the islands at least 50,000 years ago. Millet and wet-rice cultivation emerged around 1700 BCE, and by about 500 BCE the Dong Son culture had developed sophisticated bronze production. The first agrarian kingdoms, such as Funan on the lower [[Mekong]], emerged around the same time. In mainland Southeast Asia, [[Buddhism]] began to affect political structures from the 8th to 9th centuries, and [[Islam|Islamic]] ideas arrived in the islands as early as the 8th century. European colonialism and the [[Cold War]] revealed the limited political significance of most Southeast Asian polities. Most modern states enjoy self-determination and cooperate within [[ASEAN]], founded in 1967.

=== KEY: History_of_agriculture_d07257
HEADWORD: History of agriculture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Andalus | Columbian exchange | Fertilizer | Maize | Potato | Rice
ENTRY:
Record of humanity's shift from foraging to cultivating crops and herding domesticated animals.

Agriculture arose independently in at least eleven regions of the Old and New Worlds, and its development about 12,000 years ago replaced nomadic hunter-gatherer life with permanent settlements. By around 9500 BC the eight Neolithic founder crops, including emmer wheat, einkorn wheat, hulled barley, peas, lentils, and flax, were cultivated in the Levant. Pigs were domesticated in Mesopotamia around 11,000 years ago, and cattle from wild aurochs around 8500 BC. [[Rice]] was domesticated in China by 6200 BC and independently in West Africa. Sorghum was domesticated in the Sahel by 3000 BC. In the Americas, the [[Potato]] was domesticated in the Andes between 8000 and 5000 BC, and [[Maize]] from teosinte in Mesoamerica about 7000 BC. Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea around 7000 BC.

The Bronze Age (from c. 3300 BC) intensified farming in Sumer, Egypt, the Indus Valley, China, and Greece. Rome established the manorial system that underpinned medieval agriculture. In the Middle Ages sugar, rice, cotton, and orange trees reached Europe through [[Al-Andalus]]. After 1492 the [[Columbian exchange]] carried maize, potatoes, tomatoes, and manioc to Europe, and wheat, barley, rice, and livestock to the Americas.

Irrigation, crop rotation, and fertilizers followed the Neolithic Revolution and developed greatly in the past 200 years. Since 1900 mechanization, synthetic [[Fertilizer|fertilizers]], pesticides, and selective breeding have raised productivity, and the Haber-Bosch process made ammonium nitrate fertilizer available on an industrial scale. Modern agriculture has raised issues including water pollution, biofuels, genetically modified organisms, tariffs, and farm subsidies. Organic farming developed in the 20th century as an alternative to synthetic pesticides.

=== KEY: History_of_architecture_636495
HEADWORD: History of architecture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Art Nouveau | Bauhaus | Florence Cathedral | Great Pyramid of Giza | Hagia Sophia | Palace of Versailles | Parthenon | Ziggurat of Ur
ENTRY:
Study of how building traditions, regions, and styles have changed over time.

Architecture is thought to begin in the basic human need for shelter and protection. The term generally refers to buildings but also covers urbanism, civil engineering, and naval, military, and landscape architecture. Great apes build sleeping nests, and the earliest hominins may have built nests or temporary enclosures. The earliest confirmed purpose-built structures are at Terra Amata in France, with the earliest evidence of artificial fire, c. 400,000 years ago. Where bone was available, structures survive better, as in the mammoth-bone dwellings at Mezhirich dated to 15,000 years ago. The Neolithic period (10,000–2000 BC) brought larger, permanent communities and new styles of building and settlement.

Mesopotamia produced ziggurats, step-pyramid temples such as the [[Ziggurat of Ur]], and Egypt the [[Great Pyramid of Giza]], a pharaonic tomb. Greek and Roman builders developed the Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian orders, seen in the [[Parthenon]]. The Byzantine [[Hagia Sophia]] combined a vast dome with mosaics, while Islamic architecture developed mosques with minarets and geometric patterns, and Gothic cathedrals used pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses. Indian stupas and Chinese pagodas carried Buddhist symbolism.

The Renaissance revived classical forms. Brunelleschi's dome crowned the [[Florence Cathedral]], and Michelangelo worked on St. Peter's Basilica. The Baroque style used dramatic ornament and curved facades, as at the [[Palace of Versailles]]. Neoclassicism later returned to simplified Greek and Roman motifs. Steel, cast iron, tile, reinforced concrete, and glass helped [[Art Nouveau]] appear and made Beaux Arts more grandiose. Modernists such as Louis Sullivan, who coined "form follows function," and the [[Bauhaus]] movement rejected ornament, as in the Bauhaus building at Dessau (1926).

=== KEY: History_of_art_e3ba79
HEADWORD: History of art
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cave painting | Code of Hammurabi | Cuneiform | Mesopotamia | Persepolis | Renaissance | Sumer | Venus of Willendorf
ENTRY:
Study of objects made by humans, valued chiefly for their aesthetic visual form.

Such objects serve spiritual, narrative, philosophical, symbolic, decorative, and functional purposes. They can be classified as fine or applied arts, or by medium, such as architecture, sculpture, painting, film, photography, and graphic arts. The field is often told as a chronology of masterpieces, but it can also include folk art and craft.

Among the first art objects are decorative artifacts from Middle Stone Age Africa, including containers that may have held paints as far back as 100,000 years ago. Small prehistoric statuettes known as Venus figurines include the Venus of Hohle Fels and the [[Venus of Willendorf]]. Carved figurines and the oldest musical instruments found so far, dated between 43,000 and 35,000 BC, come from caves in the Swabian Jura. The best-known prehistoric works are the Paleolithic [[Cave painting|cave paintings]] of animals at Lascaux in France. Several hundred decorated caves are known, dating c. 38,000–12,000 BC, mostly in France and Spain.

In [[Mesopotamia]], the first cities and the earliest writing appeared in the 4th millennium BC. [[Sumer]] produced ziggurats, cylinder seals, and [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing, and Assyria is best known for detailed stone reliefs of court life, hunting, and battle. In Babylon, the Ishtar Gate was covered with blue glazed bricks and reliefs of dragons, bulls, and lions, and the [[Code of Hammurabi]] was inscribed on a stele. After conquering Babylon in the mid-6th century BC, the Achaemenid Empire made [[Persepolis]] its capital, with art that drew on elements from across the empire.

Later periods include the [[Renaissance]] (c. 1400–1600), which revived classical antiquity through humanism, perspective, and naturalism, followed by the Baroque and Rococo.

=== KEY: History_of_astronomy_9dc4a3
HEADWORD: History of astronomy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Babylonia | Calendar | Cosmic microwave background | Hipparchus | Isaac Newton | Johannes Kepler | Nicolaus Copernicus | Ptolemy
ENTRY:
Development of human efforts to understand the universe beyond Earth's atmosphere, from prehistoric observation to modern cosmology. Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences and grew from calendrical and religious practices. The Warren Field monument in Scotland, a lunar calendar created around 8,000 BC, is considered the oldest known [[Calendar|calendar]]. [[Babylonia|Babylonian]] astronomers recorded planetary positions and eclipses on clay tablets. [[Hipparchus]] compiled a star catalog around 150 BC, and [[Ptolemy]] formalized a geocentric model in the *Almagest* about 140 AD. In 1543, [[Nicolaus Copernicus]] proposed a heliocentric model, starting the Copernican Revolution. [[Johannes Kepler]] described elliptical planetary orbits, and [[Isaac Newton]] unified gravity as the force behind celestial and terrestrial motion. Later advances included the telescope, spectroscopy, and the [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]], which affirms Big Bang cosmology.

=== KEY: History_of_atheism_172f21
HEADWORD: History of atheism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Atom | Buddhism | Democritus | Hinduism | Jainism | Secularism
ENTRY:
Development of disbelief in deities, from ancient Greek and Indian philosophy to modern [[Secularism|secularism]]. Philosophical atheist thought appeared in Europe and Asia in the 6th or 5th century BCE. In ancient [[Ancient Greece|Greece]], playwrights expressed doubt about the gods, and the atomists Leucippus and [[Democritus]] explained the world through moving [[Atom|atoms]] in the 5th century BCE. In India, the materialist Charvaka school rejected Vedic authority, and the Samkhya school, one of the [[Hinduism|Hindu]] schools, did not accept a creator deity. [[Buddhism]] and [[Jainism]] likewise did not center on deities. The term atheist was used in the 16th century, and atheism appeared in English around 1587. The Enlightenment fueled skepticism and secularism against religion in Europe.

=== KEY: History_of_atomic_theory_58901b
HEADWORD: History of atomic theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antoine Lavoisier | Conservation of mass | John Dalton | Matter | Richard Feynman | Robert Boyle
ENTRY:
Development of the idea that [[Matter|matter]] is composed of indivisible particles, from ancient philosophy to modern physics. The word atom comes from the Greek atomos, meaning indivisible, but ancient atomism rested on philosophical reasoning rather than experiment. Working in the late 17th century, [[Robert Boyle]] developed the concept of a chemical element, and [[Antoine Lavoisier]] later showed that compounds consist of elements in constant proportion, also defining the [[Conservation of mass|law of conservation of mass]]. In 1804 [[John Dalton]] noticed that the amounts of one element combining with a fixed amount of another differ by ratios of small whole numbers, which he took as evidence that elements combine by basic units of weight. [[Richard Feynman]] described the atomic hypothesis as the single most prolific scientific concept. Physicists later found that atoms have an internal structure and can be divided.

=== KEY: History_of_chemistry_8be942

PERSON: no

Development of the study and transformation of matter, from prehistoric crafts to modern science. === KEY: History_of_geography_269587
HEADWORD: History of geography

LINK TARGETS: Babylon | Cartography | Eratosthenes | Herodotus | Homer | Thales of Miletus
ENTRY:
Development of the study and mapping of the Earth, from ancient Mesopotamian maps to an academic discipline. The word geography, from the Greek for Earth-writing, was first used by [[Eratosthenes]], but recognizable practices such as [[Cartography|cartography]] existed earlier. The oldest known world maps date to ancient [[Babylon]], and the best-known Babylonian map, the Imago Mundi of 600 BC, shows Babylon on the Euphrates surrounded by a circular landmass. === KEY: History_of_geology_e8c491
HEADWORD: History of geology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Biruni | Alfred Wegener | Aristotle | Charles Lyell | James Hutton | Plate tectonics | Pliny the Elder | Shen Kuo
ENTRY:
Development of the natural science of geology, from ancient observations of fossils and minerals to plate tectonics. In the 4th century BC [[Aristotle]] observed the slow rate of geological change, and his successor Theophrastus described many minerals and ores in *On Stones*. [[Pliny the Elder]] was among the first to identify amber as fossilized tree resin and laid the basis of crystallography by recognizing the octahedral habit of diamond. [[Al-Biruni|Abu al-Rayhan al-Biruni]] wrote the earliest writings on the geology of India, hypothesizing that the Indian subcontinent was once a sea. In the 18th and 19th centuries [[James Hutton]] introduced uniformitarianism, which [[Charles Lyell]] later expanded. In 1912 [[Alfred Wegener]] proposed continental drift, which laid groundwork for [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]].

=== KEY: History_of_life_25b19c
HEADWORD: History of life
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cambrian explosion | Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event | Cyanobacteria | Eukaryote | Great Oxidation Event | Permian–Triassic extinction event
ENTRY:
Account of how living and extinct organisms evolved, from the first life on Earth to the present.

Earth formed about 4.54 ± 0.05 billion years ago, and life emerged before 3.7 billion years ago. The earliest clear evidence of life is biogenic carbon and stromatolite fossils from 3.7 billion-year-old rocks in western Greenland. Possible "remains of biotic life" were reported from 4.1 billion-year-old rocks in Western Australia in 2015, and microfossils from the Nuvvuagittuq Belt may be as old as 4.28 billion years, though such fossils may have non-biological origins. How life began remains hypothetical.

Microbial mats of bacteria and archaea dominated the early Archean eon. Photosynthesis by [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] around 3.5 billion years ago released oxygen that led to the [[Great Oxidation Event]] around 2.4 billion years ago. The earliest evidence of [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] dates from 1.85 billion years ago, likely resulting from symbiogenesis between archaea and proteobacteria, and multicellular organisms appeared around 1.7 billion years ago. Bilaterian animals were present by 555 million years ago, and animal life diversified in the [[Cambrian explosion]], with early vertebrates by about 525 million years ago. Land plants evolved from green algae, with fossils from about 470 million years ago in the Ordovician.

Synapsids, including the ancestors of mammals, dominated the Permian. The [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]] 252 million years ago killed most complex species, and dinosaurs later dominated the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. The [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]] 66 million years ago killed off the non-avian dinosaurs, after which mammals increased rapidly in size and diversity. Only 1.75–1.8 million species have been named, though one estimate suggests Earth may have 1 trillion, and living species are under one percent of all that have existed.

=== KEY: History_of_linguistics_7eb727
HEADWORD: History of linguistics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Ferdinand de Saussure | Mesopotamia | Noam Chomsky | Pāṇini | Sanskrit | Sociolinguistics
ENTRY:
Development of the scientific study of language, from Mesopotamian word lists to modern subfields. Language use was first systematically documented in [[Mesopotamia]], where lexical lists from the 3rd to the 2nd millennia BCE (3000 to 1000 BCE) offered glossaries of Sumerian and phonetic vocabularies of foreign languages. In ancient India, [[Pāṇini]] systematically described [[Sanskrit]], and [[Aristotle]] laid the foundation of Western linguistics as part of the study of rhetoric. Modern approaches developed in the 18th century. In the early 20th century [[Ferdinand de Saussure]] established linguistics as an autonomous discipline within the social sciences, treating language as a semiotic system with subfields of phonology, morphology, syntax and semantics. [[Noam Chomsky]]'s generative grammar, published in 1957, shifted focus to cognitive mechanisms. Today linguistics includes applied, psycholinguistic, neurolinguistic, [[Sociolinguistics|sociolinguistic]] and computational subfields.

=== KEY: History_of_literature_2735f1
HEADWORD: History of literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Confucius | Divine Comedy | Don Quixote | Epic of Gilgamesh | Epic poetry | Homer | Mesopotamia | Oral tradition
ENTRY:
Development of writings in prose or poetry meant to entertain or educate, and of the techniques used in them.

Literature began in stories told by hunter-gatherer bands through [[Oral tradition|oral tradition]], including myth and folklore. [[Epic poetry]], long narrative poems recounting the feats of mythic heroes, is recognized as the pinnacle of ancient literature. Writing arose independently in [[Mesopotamia]] about 3200 BCE, in China about 1250 BCE, and in Mesoamerica about 650 BCE, and was first used for simple purposes such as accounting. Sumerian literature is the oldest known; its works had no titles and were referred to by their first lines. Akkadian literature, much of it commissioned by kings, spread to Egypt, Ugarit, and Hattusa. Egyptian writers developed autobiographies and wisdom literature, the Instructions, and later fiction and entertainment texts. The [[Epic of Gilgamesh]] (c. 2150 BCE) is among the early epics.

In China, the *Tao Te Ching* and the *Zhuangzi* became foundational texts of Taoism. [[Confucius]] collected the Six Classics, and Confucianism dominated literary taste from the Warring States period. His sayings were later compiled by his students as the *Analects*. In Greece, [[Homer]]'s *Iliad* and *Odyssey* (c. 800–700 BCE) set the model that Virgil adapted in the *Aeneid* (30–19 BCE), and India produced the *Ramayana*.

Medieval works include the *One Thousand and One Nights*, the Persian *Shahnameh*, and Dante's [[Divine Comedy|*Divine Comedy*]] (c. 1308–1321). The Renaissance revived classical antiquity through humanism, producing Shakespeare's plays and Cervantes' [[Don Quixote|*Don Quixote*]] (1605–1615). Later movements included Romanticism and Realism, followed by modernism in works such as Joyce's *Ulysses* (1922) and postcolonial writing such as Achebe's *Things Fall Apart* (1958).

=== KEY: History_of_mathematics_c99943
HEADWORD: History of mathematics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Carl Friedrich Gauss | David Hilbert | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Hindu–Arabic numeral system | Isaac Newton | Maya civilization | Pythagorean theorem
ENTRY:
Study of the origins of mathematical discoveries, methods, and notation.

Before the modern age, written examples of new mathematical developments have come to light in only a few locales. From 3000 BC the Mesopotamian states of Sumer, Akkad, and Assyria, followed closely by [[Ancient Egypt]] and the Levantine state of Ebla, used arithmetic, algebra, and geometry for taxation, commerce, and trade, and in astronomy to record time and formulate calendars. The earliest mathematical texts are Plimpton 322 (Babylonian, c. 2000–1900 BC), the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (Egyptian, c. 1800 BC), and the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (Egyptian, c. 1890 BC). All mention Pythagorean triples, so the [[Pythagorean theorem]] appears to be the most ancient and widespread mathematical development after basic arithmetic and geometry.

Mathematics as a "demonstrative discipline" began in the 6th century BC with the Pythagoreans, who coined the term from the Greek *mathema*, "subject of instruction." Greek mathematics introduced deductive reasoning and rigor in proofs. Chinese mathematics made early contributions, including a place value system and the first use of negative numbers. The [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]] evolved over the first millennium AD in India and reached the Western world through Islamic mathematics and the work of Khwārizmī. The [[Maya civilization]] of Mexico and Central America independently gave zero a standard symbol.

Greek and Arabic texts were translated into Latin from the 12th century. From the 15th century, beginning in Renaissance Italy, new developments came at an increasing pace. [[Isaac Newton]] and [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] developed infinitesimal calculus in the 17th century, and German mathematicians such as [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] and [[David Hilbert]] made later discoveries.

=== KEY: History_of_medicine_3a0563
HEADWORD: History of medicine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Antibiotic | Galen | Hippocrates | Humorism | Mesopotamia | Microscope | Neanderthal | Public health
ENTRY:
Study of the development of medical treatments, practices, and knowledge from prehistory to the present.

The field is multidisciplinary, drawing on economics, health sciences, sociology, and politics, and on archaeology for periods without written sources. Evidence of healing practices exists for [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] and other early humans. Finds include psychoactive psilocybin mushrooms in the Sahara c. 6000 BC and primitive dental care at Riparo Fredian, in present-day Italy (c. 11,900 BC), and at Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan (c. 7000 BC). Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3230 BC) carried what archaeologist Patrick Hunt calls a prehistoric medical kit, including birch polypore fungus, poppy seeds, sloe berries, and sphagnum moss.

Early medical traditions arose in Babylon, Egypt, China, and India. Cuneiform tablets from [[Mesopotamia]] dating to the 18th century BC record recipes against poisoning, and Egyptian papyri such as the Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus describe wound management. [[Hippocrates]] is called the father of medicine for establishing clinical observation, prognosis, and natural explanations of disease. [[Galen]]'s animal dissections informed ideas of human physiology for centuries. Before the 19th century, [[Humorism|humorism]] was thought to explain disease; it was gradually replaced by the germ theory, associated with Pasteur and Koch. Vesalius corrected Galen's errors through human dissection, and Harvey demonstrated the circulation of blood in 1628. The invention of the [[Microscope|microscope]] followed improved understanding during the Renaissance.

[[Public health]] measures developed especially in the 19th century as growing cities required sanitation. Research centers, often connected with major hospitals, opened in the early 20th century, and the mid-20th century brought new biological treatments such as [[Antibiotic|antibiotics]]. Medicine was heavily professionalized in the 20th century; nursing opened to women from the 1870s and medicine especially after 1970.

=== KEY: History_of_music_9ca84c
HEADWORD: History of music
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baroque | Charles Darwin | Indian classical music | Johann Sebastian Bach | Ludwig van Beethoven | Medieval music | Richard Wagner | Silk Road
ENTRY:
Development of musical expression and practice from prehistory to the present.

Music is a cultural universal, though definitions of it vary. Its origins remain highly contentious and are often tied to the origin of language. Scholars divide among three views: that music began as a proto-language that led to language, that it is a by-product of language, or that both derive from a common antecedent. [[Charles Darwin]] proposed in *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex* (1871) that music arose through sexual selection, a view criticized for lack of evidence of sexual dimorphism in musicality. Herbert Spencer and the composer [[Richard Wagner]] held instead that music and language share a precursor. No theory has achieved broad approval.

Prehistoric music is first firmly dated to c. 38,000 BC by Upper Paleolithic bone flutes. Ancient music in Chinese, Egyptian, Greek, Indian, Persian, Mesopotamian, and Middle Eastern societies was often characterized by monophony and improvisation, and the oldest surviving musical notation dates from this period. The [[Silk Road]] spread musical ideas and instruments; Tang dynasty music was heavily influenced by Central Asian traditions. Religion often drove musical development: the Vedas shaped [[Indian classical music]], the Five Classics of Confucianism laid the basis for Chinese music, and after the 7th century Islamic music dominated Persia and the Arab world. Music of the early Christian Church inaugurated the Western classical tradition, in which [[Medieval music|medieval music]] developed polyphony and staff notation.

Western music history is conventionally divided into the Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), the [[Baroque]] (c. 1600–1750), represented by [[Johann Sebastian Bach]], the Classical period (c. 1750–1820), represented by Haydn, Mozart, and [[Ludwig van Beethoven]], and the Romantic era (c. 1820–1900).

Development of the application of pigments to surfaces, from [[Paleolithic]] cave art to modern abstraction. In Paleolithic times animals were the most common subjects, including the rhinoceros and large Felidae in Chauvet Cave, painted around 31,000 BC. Ancient civilizations used mineral pigments on walls and portable media, as in Egyptian tomb frescoes and Chinese Han dynasty lacquerware. Greek and Roman artists advanced linear perspective, and in the [[Renaissance]] oil binders enabled layered glazing and chiaroscuro, as pioneered by [[Jan van Eyck]]. Romantic painters such as Francisco de Goya, [[John Constable]] and [[J. M. W. Turner]] gave expression to the idea of art for art's sake. Until the early 20th century painting relied mainly on representational motifs, after which [[Abstraction|abstract]] approaches gained favor.

=== KEY: History_of_philosophy_8ceea9
HEADWORD: History of philosophy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Ghazali | Ancient Greece | Avicenna | Buddhism | Confucianism | Marxism | Mulla Sadra | Vedas
ENTRY:
Systematic study of the historical development of philosophical thought.

The field has a historical component, which asks which philosophers held which views and how their contexts shaped them, and a philosophical component, which evaluates the theories for truth and validity. It is controversial how far the field is distinct from philosophy itself; Neo-Kantians such as Wilhelm Windelband argued that philosophy is essentially historical. Mystical and religious traditions are often excluded when their claims do not rest on rational argumentation, though some theorists include myth and proverbial lore.

Western philosophy originated with inquiry into the nature of the cosmos in [[Ancient Greece]], from the Pre-Socratics through Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, whose codification of deductive logic proved foundational. The medieval period focused more on theology. The Renaissance renewed interest in Greek philosophy and brought humanism, and the modern period turned to how philosophical and scientific knowledge is created, in the rationalism of Descartes and the empiricism of Locke and Hume. The Enlightenment used new ideas to challenge traditional authorities. Later developments included German idealism, pragmatism, positivism, formal logic, linguistic analysis, phenomenology, existentialism, and postmodernism.

Arabic–Persian philosophy, strongly influenced by the Greeks, peaked in the Islamic Golden Age. [[Avicenna]] synthesized Islamic faith and Greek philosophy; [[Al-Ghazali]]'s critique contributed to its waning; and [[Mulla Sadra]] developed a metaphysical system based on mysticism in the 17th century. Indian philosophy, rooted in the [[Vedas]], divides into orthodox schools and heterodox schools such as [[Buddhism]] and Jainism. Chinese philosophy centered on right social conduct, government, and self-cultivation, with [[Confucianism]] exploring moral virtues and Daoism the relation of humans to nature; its modern period was shaped by encounters with Western thought, specifically [[Marxism]].

=== KEY: History_of_physics_b641b1

Development of the study of matter and energy, from ancient natural philosophy to quantum theory and cosmology. [[Thales of Miletus]] proposed that every event had a natural cause, and [[Democritus]] and Leucippus formulated early atomism. [[Archimedes]] advanced statics and hydrostatics, formulating the principle of buoyancy. In the Middle Ages [[Ibn al-Haytham]] pioneered experimental optics. The Scientific Revolution of the 17th century, including the discovery of the law of gravity, began physics as an experimental science. [[Isaac Newton]]'s *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* established classical mechanics and universal gravitation. In the 19th century [[James Clerk Maxwell]] unified electricity and magnetism. At the start of the 20th century, [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], relativity and atomic theory transformed the field, as [[Albert Einstein]]'s special relativity revealed time dilation and general relativity described gravity as spacetime curvature.

=== KEY: History_of_political_thought_123ce3

ENTRY:
Development of human thought about government, authority, justice and the organization of society, from ancient China and Greece to modern ideologies. Many histories of Western political thought trace its origins to ancient Greece, where [[Socrates]], [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] are traditionally treated as especially influential. Non-Western traditions are also significant. In China the Hundred Schools of Thought included [[Confucius]], whose ideas of moral example and hierarchical order were influential, and [[Legalism (Chinese philosophy)|Legalism]], which argued that authoritarian discipline was crucial for governing. === KEY: History_of_psychology_7c479c

Development of the scientific study of behavior and mental processes, from ancient philosophy of the soul to cognitive science. In 1879 [[Wilhelm Wundt]] founded the first psychological laboratory dedicated exclusively to psychological research. [[Sigmund Freud]] independently developed psychoanalysis in Vienna. Written record of human religious thought and practice, beginning with the invention of writing about 5,200 years ago (3200 BC).

Religious belief before writing belongs to prehistory. The concept of "religion" itself was formed in the 16th and 17th centuries; sacred texts such as the Bible and the Quran had no word or concept for it in their original languages, and many cultures past and present lack or reject a distinction between the natural and the supernatural.

Evidence for the earliest religious ideas is contested. Some archaeologists regard the apparently intentional burial of Neanderthals and *Homo naledi* as early as 300,000 years ago as proof of religion, but the connection is conjectural. Scientists generally interpret a number of artifacts from the [[Upper Paleolithic]] (50,000–13,000 BC), including the lion man, the Venus figurines, and the ritual burial at Sungir, as representing religious ideas. In the 19th century, Edward Burnett Tylor and Herbert Spencer emphasized [[Animism|animism]]; these theories have been widely criticized, and there is no consensus. [[Göbekli Tepe]], the oldest potentially religious site yet discovered, has circles of T-shaped stone pillars. It was built before the [[Neolithic Revolution]], the beginning of agriculture and animal husbandry around 9000 BC.

The Pyramid Texts of ancient Egypt, dated to between 2400 and 2300 BC, are the oldest known religious texts. The earliest records of Indian religion are the [[Vedas]], composed c. 1500–1200 BC, and some of the [[Upanishads]] date to the mid-1st millennium BC. The [[Dead Sea Scrolls]] are fragmentary texts of the Hebrew Tanakh, and the [[Septuagint]] (300–200 BC) translated it into Greek.

=== KEY: History_of_science_75eaa3
HEADWORD: History of science
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greece | Charles Darwin | Isaac Newton | Islamic Golden Age | Mesopotamia | Molecular biology | Scientific Revolution | Zapotec civilization
ENTRY:
Development of science from ancient times to the present, covering the natural, social, and formal sciences.

Protoscience and natural philosophies such as alchemy and astrology, which existed from the Bronze Age through the Middle Ages, declined after modern sciences emerged in the Scientific Revolution. The earliest roots of scientific practice lie in Ancient Egypt and [[Mesopotamia]] in the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC. Their mathematics, astronomy, and medicine influenced the natural philosophy of [[Ancient Greece]], where formal attempts were made to explain physical events by natural causes. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Greek learning deteriorated in Latin-speaking Western Europe but thrived in the Byzantine Empire and was absorbed into the Arabic-speaking Muslim world during the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. Recovery of Greek works and Islamic inquiries revived natural philosophy in the West from the 10th to 13th century. The [[Zapotec civilization]] of Mesoamerica established the first known traditions of astronomy and mathematics for producing calendars.

The [[Scientific Revolution]] of the 16th and 17th centuries produced a more mechanistic, mathematical science based on a newly defined scientific method; [[Isaac Newton]]'s *Principia Mathematica* (1687) unified mechanics under universal gravitation. The 19th century brought new perspectives on the conservation of energy, the age of Earth, and evolution, as in [[Charles Darwin]]'s *On the Origin of Species* (1859). In the 20th century, genetics and physics founded subdisciplines such as [[Molecular biology|molecular biology]] and particle physics, and after World War II industrial and military concerns and increasingly complex research ushered in "big science."

The relationship between science and religion has been characterized as "conflict," "harmony," "complexity," and "mutual independence." The conflict thesis, postulated c. 1874 by John William Draper, has lost favor among most historians and scientists.

=== KEY: History_of_slavery_81f0bf
HEADWORD: History of slavery
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Americas | Atlantic slave trade | Code of Hammurabi | Human trafficking | Mesopotamia | Neolithic Revolution | Portugal | West Africa
ENTRY:
Development of the institution of owning human beings as property, from ancient [[Mesopotamia]] to the present. Slavery was institutionalized by the time the first civilizations emerged, and it features in the [[Code of Hammurabi]], which refers to it as an established institution. Agriculture after the [[Neolithic Revolution]] offered greater opportunity for mass chattel slavery. Islamic slavery encompassed much of Western and Central Asia, Northern and Eastern Africa, India and Europe from the 7th to the 20th century. Beginning in the 16th century, European merchants, starting mainly with merchants from [[Portugal]], initiated the [[Atlantic slave trade]], mostly purchasing captives from [[West Africa]] kingdoms and transporting them to colonies in the [[Americas]]. The transatlantic trade was eventually curtailed after European and American governments passed legislation abolishing their nations' involvement. In 2019 an estimated 40.3 million people worldwide were subject to some form of slavery, and [[Human trafficking]] remains an international problem.

=== KEY: History_of_sociology_7d0813
HEADWORD: History of sociology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Auguste Comte | Confucius | Herodotus | Karl Marx | Max Weber | Plato | Thucydides | University of Chicago
ENTRY:
Development of sociology as a systematic study of society, from Enlightenment thought to the modern social sciences. Sociology emerged primarily out of Enlightenment thought as a positivist science of society shortly after the French Revolution, in reaction to modernity, capitalism, urbanization and imperialism. [[Auguste Comte]] coined the term in 1838. Sociological perspectives can be found in the thought of ancient writers such as [[Herodotus]], [[Thucydides]] and [[Plato]], and of non-European thinkers such as [[Confucius]]. In the late 19th century sociology took particular interest in the emergence of the modern nation state, including its institutions, units of socialization and means of surveillance. [[Karl Marx]], Herbert Spencer and [[Max Weber]] developed influential approaches, and the first sociology department was established at the [[University of Chicago]] in 1892. Quantitative social research techniques are now common tools for governments and businesses.

Development of human and artificial flight beyond Earth's atmosphere, from early rocketry to commercial spaceflight. Spaceflight became an engineering possibility with [[Robert H. === KEY: History_of_sport_bef902
HEADWORD: History of sport
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Association football | Cricket | Epic of Gilgamesh | Pierre de Coubertin
ENTRY:
Development of organized physical competition from prehistoric activity to modern professional sport. Sport had early links with warfare and entertainment, and competition was used to determine whether individuals were fit for military service. Cave paintings in France appear to depict sprinting in the Upper Paleolithic, and rock art in Egypt shows swimming and archery. Wrestlers are depicted on Sumerian stone slabs dated to around 3000 BC, and the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]] describes belt wrestling. Monuments at Beni Hasan dating to around 2000 BC show that wrestling, weightlifting, long jump, swimming and archery were well developed in [[Ancient Egypt]]. The first Olympic Games were recorded in 776 BC at Olympia, where they were held every four years until 393 AD. [[Pierre de Coubertin]]'s 1896 Olympic revival transformed sport into a global arena. In the 19th century [[Cricket]] and [[Association football]] gained standardized rules, enabling mass participation and commercialization.

=== KEY: History_of_technology_e60f14
HEADWORD: History of technology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cuneiform | Industrial Revolution | Information Age | Iron Age | Neolithic Revolution
ENTRY:
Human invention of tools and techniques, ranging from simple stone tools to genetic engineering and information technology.

The earliest direct evidence of tool use, found in the Great Rift Valley of Ethiopia, dates to 2.5 million years ago, and the Oldowan stone-tool industry dates to at least 2.3 million years ago. Mastery of fire came approximately 1.5 million years ago, and the transition to agriculture in the [[Neolithic Revolution]] around 10,000 BC enabled settled communities. Sumerians developed [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing and a base-60 numerical system by 3300 BC. The Bronze Age (c. 3000–1200 BC) introduced metalworking, which the [[Iron Age]] (from c. 1200 BC) extended. China developed gunpowder, the compass, and movable type printing by the 11th century. The [[Industrial Revolution]] began in Britain around 1760, with James Watt's steam engine of 1776. The telegraph (1837), telephone (1876), and internal combustion engine (1885) followed, then the ENIAC electronic computer (1946), transistors (1947), and integrated circuits (1958), which fueled the [[Information Age]].

Technology is closely tied to the history of science and to economic history, and technological change affects, and is affected by, a society's cultural traditions. Sociologists and anthropologists such as Lewis H. Morgan, Leslie White, and Gerhard Lenski have declared technological progress the primary factor driving civilization. White measured cultural evolution by energy, using the formula P = E/T, and Nikolai Kardashev extended his theory in the Kardashev scale. Economists use productivity as a measure of progress; in developed countries productivity growth has slowed since the late 1970s, and U.S. manufacturing employment fell from over 30% in the 1940s to just over 10% 70 years later. Post-industrial theorists argued that services and information were displacing industry and goods.

=== KEY: History_of_the_Peloponnesian_War_bdd16e

ENTRY:
Historical account of the [[Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BC) by [[Thucydides]], an Athenian historian who also served as a general in the war. The work, apparently unfinished, ends mid-sentence in 411 BC and was divided into eight books by later scholars. It is regarded as one of the earliest scholarly works of history. Thucydides recorded events year by year, each year divided into a summer campaign season and a quieter winter, a method that contrasts with [[Herodotus]]. He used extensive speeches, including the funeral oration of [[Pericles]] in Book 2, which classicists doubt reproduce the words actually spoken. Scholars disagree on bias. J. B. Bury saw an objective, scientific work, while Ernst Badian argued for a strong pro-Athenian bias. Others read the work as literature, a view advanced by W. R. Connor.

Development of theater as an art form over roughly 2,500 years. Theater developed into an autonomous activity distinct from ritual. === KEY: Hokusai_a4ea20
HEADWORD: Hokusai
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Claude Monet | Edo period | Kabuki | Mount Fuji | Ukiyo-e | Vincent van Gogh
ENTRY:
Japanese painter and printmaker of the [[Edo period]], a master of [[Ukiyo-e|ukiyo-e]].

Born in the Katsushika district of Edo, he was probably the son of a mirror-maker to the shogun, and his childhood name was Tokitarō. At 14 he apprenticed to a woodcarver, and at 18 he entered the studio of Katsukawa Shunshō, receiving the name Shunrō. His first prints, a series of pictures of [[Kabuki|kabuki]] actors, appeared in 1779. After Shunshō's death in 1793, Hokusai explored other styles, including European styles known to him through French and Dutch copper engravings, and was expelled from the Katsukawa school by Shunkō, which he later called the motivation for his artistic development. He turned from the courtesans and actors that were the traditional subjects of ukiyo-e to landscapes and the daily life of Japanese people of many social classes. In 1798 he became an independent artist, and by 1800 he had adopted the name Katsushika Hokusai, by which he is best known. He used at least thirty names in his lifetime, more than any other major Japanese artist, and the changes track shifts in his style.

Hokusai's woodblock series *Thirty-Six Views of [[Mount Fuji]]*, a response to a domestic travel boom in Japan, includes *The Great Wave off Kanagawa* and *Fine Wind, Clear Morning*, which secured his fame in Japan and overseas. He also worked in painting and book illustration, and produced over 30,000 paintings, sketches, woodblock prints, and images for picture books. His works strongly influenced [[Vincent van Gogh]] and [[Claude Monet]] during the wave of Japonisme that spread across Europe in the late 19th century.

ENTRY:
Imaging technique for recording and reconstructing a wavefront. A hologram records an interference pattern made by overlaying a reference beam onto the wavefront of interest. When suitably lit, it diffracts light to reproduce the original light field, including depth cues such as [[Parallax|parallax]]. === KEY: Holy_Roman_Empire_c3d433
HEADWORD: Holy Roman Empire
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carolingian Empire | Charlemagne | Frederick Barbarossa | Kingdom of Germany | Napoleon | Otto the Great | Peace of Westphalia | Voltaire
ENTRY:
Decentralized polity of Central and Western Europe, headed by the Holy Roman Emperor (800/962–1806).

The empire developed in the Early Middle Ages, beginning in either 800 or 962, and lasted a millennium. It initially consisted of three kingdoms, [[Kingdom of Germany|Germany]], Italy, and (from 1032) Burgundy, held together by the emperor's overlordship; by the 15th century imperial governance was concentrated in Germany. On December 25, 800, Pope Leo III crowned [[Charlemagne]] as Roman emperor, reviving the title more than three centuries after the fall of the Western Roman Empire in 476. The title lapsed again by 924 and was revived in 962 when Pope John XII crowned Otto of Saxony, known as [[Otto the Great]], who ruled lands first united in the [[Carolingian Empire]]. From 962 until the 13th century the empire was one of the most powerful monarchies in Europe, reaching its greatest territorial extent in the mid-13th century under the House of Hohenstaufen before partially collapsing.

The imperial office was traditionally elective, filled mostly by a handful of predominantly German prince-electors and archbishops. The Habsburgs held the throne almost continuously from 1438. The word *sacrum* ("holy") was used beginning in 1157 under [[Frederick Barbarossa]], and the form "Holy Roman Empire" is attested from 1254. From 1512 it was often styled "Holy Roman Empire of the German Nation." [[Voltaire]] remarked that it was "in no way holy, nor Roman, nor an empire." The [[Peace of Westphalia]] was concluded in 1648.

At its height the empire covered much of what is now the Czech Republic, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Liechtenstein, Switzerland, Slovenia, Germany, and Austria, and large parts of eastern France, northern and central Italy, and western Poland. On August 6, 1806, Emperor Francis II abdicated and dissolved the empire after [[Napoleon]] created the Confederation of the Rhine from German client states loyal to France.

=== KEY: Home_8cf04a
HEADWORD: Home
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Ancient Rome | Cave | House | Indus Valley Civilisation | Knossos | Mesopotamia | Neanderthal
ENTRY:
Space used as a permanent or semi-permanent residence for one or more human occupants, and sometimes companion animals.

A home provides sheltered rooms for sleeping, preparing food, eating, and hygiene, and for work and leisure such as studying and play. Its form may be static, such as a [[House|house]] or apartment; mobile, such as a houseboat, trailer, or yurt; or digital, such as a virtual space. The concept can be considered at scales from the individual dwelling to a town, country, or planet, and has been theorized across disciplines, from psychology to gender and politics. It encompasses exile, yearning, belonging, homesickness, and homelessness.

The earliest human homes were probably natural features such as [[Cave|caves]]. Early human species inhabited caves from at least one million years ago, including *Homo erectus* at Zhoukoudian in China, *Homo heidelbergensis* and [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] at Atapuerca in Europe, and *Homo floresiensis* in Indonesia. Early modern humans in southern Africa regularly used sea caves from about 180,000 years ago, the oldest known site being PP13B at Pinnacle Point. Huts and longhouses have been used for living since the late Neolithic.

By the Bronze Age (c. 3500–1200 BC), communities in [[Mesopotamia]] built permanent mudbrick dwellings; excavations at Uruk and Ubaid reveal single-room and multi-room houses around small courtyards, clustered along straight streets and sharing wells and ovens. In [[Ancient Egypt]], from the Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC), towns such as Amarna and Deir el-Medina had flat-roofed mudbrick houses in dense rows off narrow lanes. The [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] (c. 2600–1900 BC) used standardized fired bricks, and two-story houses at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had private wells and indoor bathrooms with drainage. The Minoan palace at [[Knossos]] had residential quarters with light wells and lustral basins. In [[Ancient Rome]] by the 1st century BC, the affluent lived in domus built around an atrium and peristyle garden, while most people lived in multistory apartment blocks called insulae, often cramped and prone to fire.

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century, European domestic architecture reverted to simple timber-framed or wattle-and-daub huts, while the elite kept stone manor houses with great halls. In the Islamic world from the 8th century the inward-facing courtyard house predominated, and the Chinese siheyuan compound, standardized during the Yuan and Ming dynasties, housed several generations around a courtyard. In Renaissance Florence the Palazzo Medici Riccardi (begun 1444) introduced rusticated façades and symmetrical plans, and masonry chimneys gradually replaced central hearths.

Kirsten Gram-Hanssen argues that the strong tie between home and the physical building is rooted in the 17th-century Enlightenment; before then, a home was more public than private. Edward Coke's declaration that a man's house is his castle and fortress popularized the notion of home as house. The separation of home and work took shape in the 20th century, with home as sanctuary. Stable housing is linked to better health and children's cognitive development, while inadequate housing correlates with higher risks of illness.
