=== KEY: Crime_c967a0
HEADWORD: Crime
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Common law | Criminal law | Genocide | Human rights | Life imprisonment | Murder | Tort | War crime
ENTRY:
Unlawful act punishable by a state or other authority. The term has no simple, universally accepted definition in modern criminal law. The most popular view is that crime is a category created by law: something is a crime if the applicable law declares it so. One proposed definition is that a crime is an act harmful not only to an individual but also to a community, society, or the state, a "public wrong."

Prohibitions on acts such as [[Murder|murder]], rape, and theft exist worldwide, but what counts as an offense is defined by the [[Criminal law|criminal law]] of each jurisdiction. In some [[Common law|common law]] nations no comprehensive criminal code exists, and courts have developed offenses themselves. Usually a crime requires both the act (*actus reus*) and criminal intent (*mens rea*), though strict liability crimes require no proof of intent. Not every violation of law is a crime: breaches of private law, such as [[Tort|torts]] and breaches of contract, are enforced through civil procedure.

Historically, criminal codes often divided offenders by class or caste, and in some tribal societies an entire clan was liable for a crime, leading to feuds lasting generations. Sentences range from community sentences to imprisonment, [[Life imprisonment|life imprisonment]], or in some jurisdictions death. Criminalization raises [[Human rights|human rights]] questions, since it can infringe on autonomy and subject people to unjust punishment.

International criminal law addresses serious offenses such as [[Genocide|genocide]], crimes against humanity, and [[War crime|war crimes]], defined through treaties and international custom. Intentional homicide rates range from under 1 per 100,000 population in many Asian and European countries to over 40 in certain Latin American and African nations. Some criminologists emphasize individual choices weighed against risks and rewards; others stress social and environmental influences.

Peninsula in Eastern Europe on the northern coast of the [[Black Sea]]. It is almost entirely surrounded by the Black Sea and the [[Sea of Azov]] and is linked to mainland [[Ukraine]] by the Isthmus of Perekop. Its population is 2.4 million, and its largest city is [[Sevastopol]]. Historically at the boundary between the classical world and the steppe, Crimea came under the Golden Horde, then the [[Crimean Khanate]], a dependency of the [[Ottoman Empire]]. In 1954 it was transferred to the Ukrainian Soviet Socialist Republic. Russian forces occupied the peninsula in 2014 and Russia annexed it, but most countries recognize it as Ukrainian territory.

=== KEY: Crimean_War_340b85
HEADWORD: Crimean War
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Black Sea | Crimea | Florence Nightingale | Ottoman Empire | Sevastopol
ENTRY:
Conflict of 1853–1856 between Russia and an alliance of the [[Ottoman Empire]], France, the United Kingdom and Sardinia. It arose from the decline of the Ottoman Empire and Russian expansion, and Britain and France sought to preserve the Ottomans for the balance of power. It began with Tsar Nicholas I's demand that the Ottoman Empire's Orthodox subjects be placed under his protection. Britain and France joined in March 1854. Allied forces landed in [[Crimea]] in September 1854 to capture [[Sevastopol]], Russia's main Black Sea naval base, and an 11-month siege followed, with brutal conditions on both sides. The Treaty of Paris, signed March 30, 1856, ended the war and forbade Russia from basing warships in the [[Black Sea]]. The war was among the first to use explosive naval shells, railways and telegraphs. The poor medical conditions it exposed led to demands for professionalized medicine, most famously achieved by [[Florence Nightingale]], who pioneered modern nursing.

=== KEY: Criminal_law_895ef9
HEADWORD: Criminal law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Capital punishment | Code of Hammurabi | Crime | Ur
ENTRY:
Body of law that defines [[Crime|crimes]] and prescribes their punishment. Most criminal law is established by statute and varies by jurisdiction. Unlike civil law, which emphasizes dispute resolution and victim compensation, criminal law includes the punishment and rehabilitation of offenders. Penalties range from fines, probation and house arrest to imprisonment and, in some jurisdictions, [[Capital punishment]] for the most serious crimes. The Code of Ur-Nammu, written around 2100–2050 BC by the king of [[Ur]], is among the earliest written codes, and the [[Code of Hammurabi]] formed the core of Babylonian law. The state's role in dispensing justice through courts became clear in 18th-century Europe, as countries began maintaining police services.

=== KEY: Criminology_c5246b
HEADWORD: Criminology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crime | Criminal law | Deviance (sociology) | Sociology
ENTRY:
Interdisciplinary field that studies [[Crime|crime]] and [[Deviance (sociology)|deviant behavior]]. It draws on [[Sociology|sociology]], political science, economics, psychology, philosophy, biology and law, and examines the nature of crime and criminals, the origins of [[Criminal law]], the causes of crime, social reactions to it, and the work of law enforcement agencies and penal institutions. Modern academic criminology has roots in the 19th-century Italian School of "criminal anthropology." The term was most likely coined in 1885 by Italian law professor Raffaele Garofalo. In the United States the discipline grew substantially in the first quarter of the 20th century, and after the 1960s it became institutionalized outside sociology through standalone criminal justice programs.

=== KEY: Crisis_of_the_Third_Century_18163e

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diocletian | Roman Empire | Zenobia
ENTRY:
Roman government crisis of 235–284, also called the Military Anarchy. Repeated foreign invasions, civil wars and economic disintegration nearly destroyed the [[Roman Empire]]. Emperor Severus Alexander was assassinated by his own troops in 235, and at least 26 claimants to the title of emperor followed. By 268 the empire had split into three competing states: the Gallic Empire, the Palmyrene Empire under [[Zenobia]], and the Italian-centered Roman Empire proper. Currency debasement and the breakdown of trade and productivity further weakened the economy. Aurelian (270–275) reunited the empire and restored some monetary stability. The crisis is said to have ended when [[Diocletian]] defeated Carinus in 285 and restructured the government, economy and military.

=== KEY: Critical_point_thermodynamics_f9e640
HEADWORD: Critical point
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dmitri Mendeleev | Thermodynamics | Vapor
ENTRY:
End point of a phase equilibrium curve in [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]]. The best-known example is the liquid–[[Vapor|vapor]] critical point, the end point of the pressure–temperature curve along which a liquid and its vapor coexist. At this point only one phase exists and the heat of vaporization is zero. Above the critical temperature, a gas cannot be liquefied by pressure alone. Water's critical point lies at 647.096 K (373.946 °C; 705.103 °F) and 22.064 megapascals. Charles Cagniard de la Tour discovered the liquid–vapor critical point in 1822, and [[Dmitri Mendeleev]] named it in 1860.

Treatise by German philosopher [[Immanuel Kant]], published in 1781 with a second edition in 1787. In it Kant seeks to determine the limits and scope of [[Metaphysics]]. Building on [[John Locke]], [[David Hume]], [[René Descartes]] and [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]], he examines the nature of [[Space]] and argues for transcendental idealism, in which objects are known as appearances rather than things in themselves. Large, semiaquatic [[Reptile|reptile]] of the family Crocodylidae, found throughout the tropics in Africa, Asia, the Americas and Australia. === KEY: Crocodylia_c9e704

ENTRY:
Order of semiaquatic, predatory [[Reptile|reptiles]] known as crocodilians. The order appeared 83.5 million years ago in the Late [[Cretaceous]] period. Crocodilians are the closest living relatives of [[Bird|birds]], the two groups being the only known survivors of the Archosauria. The order includes the true [[Crocodile|crocodiles]] (family Crocodylidae), the [[Alligator|alligators]] and caimans (family Alligatoridae), and the [[Gharial|gharial]] and false gharial (family Gavialidae). Crocodilians are ectotherms with thick, scaly skin and a four-chambered heart. They live mainly in the warm, tropical areas of the Americas, Africa, Asia and Oceania, usually in freshwater, and are generally solitary and territorial. Some species, particularly the [[Nile crocodile]], have attacked humans. Hunting, poaching and habitat destruction are the greatest threats to crocodilian populations. Artistic and literary representations of crocodilians have appeared since at least [[Ancient Egypt]].

ENTRY:
Binary operation on two [[Euclidean vector|vectors]] in three-dimensional space. The cross product of linearly independent vectors a and b, written a × b, is a vector perpendicular to both, with a direction given by the [[Right-hand rule|right-hand rule]] and a magnitude equal to the area of the parallelogram the vectors span. === KEY: Crossbow_ecd03a
HEADWORD: Crossbow
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ballista | Shooting sports
ENTRY:
Ranged weapon using a bow-like assembly, called a prod, mounted horizontally on a frame called a tiller, which is held like the stock of a long gun. Crossbows shoot bolts or quarrels. Unlike a bow, a crossbow is spanned and cocked in advance, so the user has unlimited time to aim, and one person can operate a cocked crossbow while others reload. Mechanical devices such as cranequins and windlasses span crossbows of high draw weight, which can give greater precision and let less-trained personnel use them effectively. Reloading takes longer than with a bow. The earliest known crossbows were invented in ancient China in the first millennium BCE, and the medieval European crossbow took many of its names from [[Ballista|ballista]], an ancient Greek siege engine. Firearms have largely replaced crossbows in war, but crossbows remain used in [[Shooting sports|competitive shooting]], hunting and quiet shooting.

=== KEY: Crusader_states_a2e554

Four Catholic polities, also called Outremer, established in the [[Levant]] and southeastern [[Anatolia]] from 1098 to 1291. They were the County of Edessa (1098–1150), the Principality of Antioch (1098–1268), the County of Tripoli (1102–1289) and the [[Kingdom of Jerusalem]] (1099–1291), the most prominent. === KEY: Crusades_ab3e62
HEADWORD: Crusades
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Constantinople | Crusader states | First Crusade | Louis IX of France | Mamluk | Pope Urban II | Saladin | Third Crusade
ENTRY:
Series of military campaigns launched by the papacy between 1095 and 1291 against Muslim rulers for the recovery and defense of the Holy Land. [[Pope Urban II]] proclaimed the [[First Crusade]] at the Council of Clermont in November 1095, calling Christians to reconquer Jerusalem with promises of spiritual reward. Its success, including the capture of Jerusalem in 1099, led to four [[Crusader states]] in the Levant: the Kingdom of Jerusalem, the Principality of Antioch, and the counties of Tripoli and Edessa. Large campaigns required crusade indulgences, military orders, and taxation of clerical income.

The fall of Edessa prompted the Second Crusade, which failed in 1148. [[Saladin]], having united Egypt and Muslim Syria, destroyed the Crusader armies at the Battle of Hattin in 1187. The [[Third Crusade|Third Crusade]] preserved the Crusader states, but Jerusalem remained under Muslim control. The Fourth Crusade was diverted to the Byzantine Empire, culminating in the sack of [[Constantinople]] and the establishment of the Latin Empire in 1204. Jerusalem was regained by negotiation in 1229 during the Sixth Crusade. [[Louis IX of France]] led the Seventh Crusade against Egypt (1248–51) and the Eighth against Tunis (1270); both failed. The [[Mamluk|Mamluks]] captured the last mainland strongholds in 1291.

The crusading movement expanded to campaigns against pagans, Christian dissidents, and other enemies of the papacy, including the Iberian, Albigensian, and Northern Crusades, continuing into the 18th century. Violence on both sides marked the period, including the killing of Jerusalem's inhabitants in 1099 and Saladin's executions of captives. The movement left the military orders of the Templars and Hospitallers and increased East-West exchange of goods and knowledge.

=== KEY: Crust_geology_a92b74
HEADWORD: Crust
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Lithosphere | Mid-ocean ridge
ENTRY:
Outermost solid shell of a planet, dwarf planet or natural satellite. It is usually distinguished from the underlying mantle by its chemical makeup, though on icy satellites it may be defined by its phase. The crusts of Earth, Mercury, Venus, Mars and the Moon formed by igneous processes and were later modified by erosion, impact cratering, volcanism and sedimentation. Secondary crust, formed by partial melting of mantle silicates, is usually basaltic and is the most common crust in the Solar System; on Earth it forms mainly at [[Mid-ocean ridge|mid-ocean spreading centers]]. Earth's crust is less than 1% of the planet's volume and forms the top of the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]]. Earth has continental crust, 30–50 km thick, and oceanic crust, 5–10 km thick and denser. Earth's primary crust has not survived, because erosion and plate tectonics have destroyed rocks older than about 4 billion years.

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arthropod | Barnacle | Cambrian | Crab | Japanese spider crab | Krill | Lobster
ENTRY:
Group of mainly aquatic [[Arthropod|arthropods]], traditionally ranked as the subphylum Crustacea. The name derives from the Latin *crustacea*, "those with shells." Crustaceans include decapods (shrimps, prawns, [[Crab|crabs]], [[Lobster|lobsters]], and crayfish), [[Krill|krill]], isopods, [[Barnacle|barnacles]], copepods, amphipods, and mantis shrimp.

The 67,000 described species range from *Stygotantulus stocki* at 0.1 mm (0.004 in) to the [[Japanese spider crab]], with a leg span of up to 3.8 m (12.5 ft) and a mass of 20 kg (44 lb). Crustaceans have an exoskeleton, which they molt to grow. They are distinguished from insects, myriapods, and chelicerates by biramous (two-parted) limbs and by larval forms such as the nauplius stage. The body has a head, thorax, and abdomen, and head and thorax may fuse into a cephalothorax under a carapace. Because hexapods (insects and entognathans) are now accepted as cladistically crustaceans, the two groups are combined in the clade Pancrustacea, which makes the traditional Crustacea paraphyletic.

Most crustaceans are free-living aquatic animals, but some are terrestrial (woodlice), some parasitic (fish lice), and some sessile (adult barnacles). The fossil record reaches back to the [[Cambrian]]. Krill and copepods may be the animals with the greatest biomass on the planet, and krill form the bottom layer of the Antarctic food chain. More than 7.9 million tons of crustaceans per year are harvested or farmed for human consumption, mostly shrimp and prawns. Some, such as the Chinese mitten crab, are significant invasive species. Their study is carcinology.

=== KEY: Cryosphere_ebea81
HEADWORD: Cryosphere
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Albedo | Glacier | Ice sheet | Permafrost | Sea ice | Snow
ENTRY:
Portions of Earth's surface where water is in solid form, including [[Sea ice|sea ice]], [[Snow|snow]], [[Glacier|glaciers]], [[Ice sheet|ice sheets]], ice on lakes and rivers, and frozen ground such as [[Permafrost|permafrost]]. The cryosphere is one of five components of the climate system, alongside the atmosphere, hydrosphere, lithosphere and biosphere, and it influences climate through feedbacks on surface energy, moisture and [[Albedo|albedo]]. Most of the world's ice volume is in Antarctica, while Northern Hemisphere winter snow and ice cover the largest area, averaging 23% of hemispheric surface area in January. About 10% of Earth's surface is covered by ice, but this is rapidly decreasing. Climate change is causing measurable declines in ice sheets, glaciers, sea ice, permafrost and snow cover.

=== KEY: Cryptography_64ef07
HEADWORD: Cryptography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer science | Encryption | Julius Caesar | Mathematics | National Security Agency | Public-key cryptography
ENTRY:
Practice and study of techniques for secure communication in the presence of adversaries; also called cryptology. Modern cryptography lies at the intersection of [[Mathematics|mathematics]], [[Computer science|computer science]], information security, electrical engineering, and physics. Its core concepts are data confidentiality, data integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation. Applications include electronic commerce, chip-based payment cards, digital currencies, passwords, secure messaging, and military communications.

Before the modern age, cryptography was effectively synonymous with [[Encryption|encryption]]: converting readable plaintext into unintelligible ciphertext that can be read only by reversing the process. A cipher is a pair of algorithms for encryption and decryption, controlled by a secret key. Early techniques included substitution ciphers such as the shift cipher attributed to [[Julius Caesar]]. Rotor cipher machines were developed in World War I, and computers in World War II made methods increasingly complex.

Symmetric systems, the only kind known until the 1970s, use one secret key to encrypt and decrypt. Asymmetric systems use a public key to encrypt and a related private key to decrypt, so parties can communicate without a shared secret. [[Public-key cryptography|Public-key cryptography]] is credited to Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman. In practice, asymmetric systems exchange a key, and a faster symmetric system such as AES then carries the communication. Modern algorithms rest on computational hardness assumptions.

Many governments classify cryptography as a weapon and limit its use or export. Critics have accused agencies such as the [[National Security Agency]] of pressing for deliberate weaknesses in standards, as in the unsuccessful Clipper chip initiative of the 1990s; the debate weighs privacy against national security.

=== KEY: Crystal_a48296
HEADWORD: Crystal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amorphous solid | Ancient Greek | Crystallography | Diamond | Glass | Salt | Snow
ENTRY:
Solid material with highly ordered microscopic structure. Its atoms, molecules or ions form a crystal lattice that extends in all directions, and macroscopic crystals are usually recognizable by flat faces at characteristic angles. The scientific study of crystals is [[Crystallography|crystallography]]. The word derives from the [[Ancient Greek|Ancient Greek]] *krustallos*, meaning both *ice* and *rock crystal*. Examples include [[Snow|snowflakes]], [[Diamond|diamonds]] and [[Salt|table salt]]. Most inorganic solids are polycrystals, made of many microscopic crystals fused together, including most metals, rocks, ceramics and ice. Solids without periodic order, such as [[Glass|glass]], wax and many plastics, are [[Amorphous solid|amorphous]]. Despite its name, lead crystal is a type of glass rather than a crystal.

=== KEY: Crystallization_f2cf87
HEADWORD: Crystallization
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atom | Chemical engineering | Crystal | Liquid | Molecule | Solution (chemistry) | Titanium dioxide
ENTRY:
Process producing a [[Crystal|crystal]], a solid with a uniform pattern of [[Atom|atoms]] or [[Molecule|molecules]]. Crystallization can occur by precipitation from [[Solution (chemistry)|solution]], freezing of a [[Liquid|liquid]] or deposition from a gas, and its results depend on temperature, air pressure, cooling rate and solute concentration. Many compounds have more than one crystal structure, a property called polymorphism; [[Titanium dioxide]] has the phases anatase and rutile. In [[Chemical engineering|chemical engineering]], crystallization separates a pure solid from a solution.

=== KEY: Crystallography_c7eb96
HEADWORD: Crystallography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Greek | DNA | Nucleic acid | Protein | Rosalind Franklin | United Nations | X-ray
ENTRY:
Branch of science devoted to the study of molecular and crystalline structure and properties. The name derives from [[Ancient Greek]] words meaning clear ice and to write. Before the 20th century, crystals were studied by measuring the angles of their faces with a goniometer. Max von Laue performed the first [[X-ray|X-ray]] diffraction experiment in 1912, and modern methods use X-rays, neutrons or electrons. Neutron diffraction is valuable for locating light atoms such as hydrogen. Crystallography has elucidated the structures of [[Protein|proteins]] and [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]]; the structure of [[DNA]] was elucidated in part through X-ray diffraction studies by [[Rosalind Franklin]] and others. In 2012 the [[United Nations]] proclaimed 2014 the International Year of Crystallography.

=== KEY: Cuban_Missile_Crisis_9523d5

=== KEY: Cultural_Revolution_1661a9

Sociopolitical movement in the [[China|People's Republic of China]], 1966–1976. Branch of [[Anthropology|anthropology]] focused on the study of cultural variation among humans. Adoption by a [[Minority group|minority group]] or culture of the values, behaviors, and beliefs of a majority society. Assimilation is opposed to [[Multiculturalism|multiculturalism]], which promotes the coexistence and preservation of multiple cultures. By contrast, cultural integration describes becoming economically and socially integrated while retaining elements of one's original [[Culture|culture]]. === KEY: Culture_6926f2
HEADWORD: Culture
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anthropology | Cicero | Marxism | Multiculturalism | Popular culture | Socialization | Subculture | UNESCO
ENTRY:
Social behavior, institutions, and norms found in human societies, together with the knowledge, beliefs, arts, laws, customs, capabilities, attitudes, and habits of their members. Culture often originates from or is attributed to a specific region or location, and it is a central concept in [[Anthropology|anthropology]], which treats it as the range of phenomena transmitted through social learning.

Humans acquire culture through enculturation and [[Socialization|socialization]], and the diversity of cultures across societies reflects this. In 1871 the anthropologist E. B. Tylor defined it as "that complex whole which includes knowledge, belief, art, morals, law, custom and any other capabilities and habits acquired by man as a member of society." The modern English word rests on a metaphor of the Roman orator [[Cicero]], who wrote of a cultivation of the soul (*cultura animi*); 18th-century German thinkers gave it most of its later meaning and often contrasted culture with civilization. Cultural norms codify acceptable conduct and serve as templates for expectations within a group. Sociological frameworks often divide culture into cognitive (ideas, beliefs, values), normative (rules and expectations), and material (artifacts and technology) components. Cultural universals found in all societies include art, music, dance, ritual, religion, tool use, cooking, shelter, and clothing. The capacity for symbolic culture is often linked to the evolution of behavioral modernity around 50,000 years ago and is often thought to be unique to humans, though some other species show simpler forms of social learning.

The word has several senses. In the humanities it has meant an individual's degree of cultivation in the arts, sciences, education, or manners, and it has been used to distinguish a high culture of social elites from the [[Popular culture|popular]], folk, or low culture of other classes. Mass culture refers to the mass-produced forms of consumer culture that emerged in the 20th century. As a count noun, a culture is the set of customs, traditions, and values of a community such as an ethnic group or nation; the term also describes a [[Subculture|subculture]] or counterculture within a society. [[Multiculturalism]] values peaceful coexistence and mutual respect among cultures.

Cultures change through internal forces both encouraging and resisting change, and through contact between societies. Raimon Panikkar identified 29 ways in which cultural change can occur, including evolution, reform, innovation, revolution, diffusion, borrowing, and syncretism.

Interpretations differ. Cultural relativism holds that cultures cannot easily be objectively ranked, since any evaluation is situated within a given value system. Other researchers argue that cultural practices causally influence economic prosperity, social stability, and individual behavior. Some schools of thought, such as [[Marxism]] and critical theory, argue that culture is used politically by elites to manipulate the proletariat. Organizations such as [[UNESCO]] work to preserve culture and cultural heritage.

=== KEY: Cumulonimbus_cloud_c95473
HEADWORD: Cumulonimbus cloud
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Genus of dense, towering vertical [[Cloud|clouds]], abbreviated Cb. Cumulonimbus typically forms from [[Water vapor|water vapor]] condensing in the lower troposphere and builds upward on powerful buoyant air currents. Above its lower portions the water vapor becomes ice crystals, such as snow and graupel, whose interaction can lead to [[Hail|hail]] and [[Lightning|lightning]]. These clouds may be called thunderheads when they cause thunderstorms, and they can form alone, in clusters or along squall lines. === KEY: Cumulus_cloud_5a0fe5
HEADWORD: Cumulus cloud
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cloud | Convection | Cumulonimbus cloud | Humidity | Latin
ENTRY:
Genus of low-level [[Cloud|clouds]] with flat bases and a puffy, cotton-like appearance. The name derives from the [[Latin|Latin]] *cumulus*, meaning "heap" or "pile." Cumulus clouds form by [[Convection|convection]] as surface-warmed air rises and cools until its relative humidity reaches one hundred percent and water vapor condenses on airborne nuclei. They are generally less than 2,000 m (6,600 ft) in altitude unless they develop into the more vertical *cumulus congestus* form. Normally they produce little or no precipitation, but they can grow into *cumulus congestus* or [[Cumulonimbus cloud|cumulonimbus]] clouds. Their development depends on instability, [[Humidity|humidity]] and temperature gradient. A cumulus cloud can consist of water vapor, supercooled droplets or ice crystals, depending on temperature. In one study, liquid water concentration peaked near the middle of the cloud at up to 1.25 grams of water per kilogram of air. Cumulus clouds generally cool the Earth by reflecting incoming solar radiation.

Unwritten body of community norms treated as binding law. Customary law exists where a legal practice is observed and the relevant actors consider it an opinion of law or necessity (*opinio juris*). Inorganic [[Chemical compound|compound]] containing the cyano group, a carbon atom triple-bonded to a nitrogen atom (C≡N). The name comes from Greek *kyanos*, "dark blue," reflecting the compound's historical association with Prussian dark blue pigment. The cyanide anion is extremely toxic and causes cyanide poisoning, binding to iron in cytochrome c oxidase and halting aerobic respiration. Soluble salts such as sodium cyanide and potassium cyanide are also highly toxic. Organic compounds with the group, called [[Organic compound|nitriles]], generally do not release cyanide ions, but cyanohydrins do. [[Hydrogen cyanide|Hydrogen cyanide]] is a highly volatile toxic liquid produced on a large scale industrially. Cyanides occur in certain seeds and fruit stones, such as bitter almonds, apricots and peaches, and in [[Cassava|cassava]] roots, where cyanogenic glycosides defend plants against [[Herbivore|herbivores]]. Cyanide is also used in [[Mining|mining]] to extract gold and silver from ore.

=== KEY: Cyanobacteria_f85ab5
HEADWORD: Cyanobacteria

ENTRY:
Phylum of photosynthetic [[Prokaryote|prokaryotes]], informally called blue-green algae. Cyanobacteria are the first organisms known to have produced oxygen: they split water, release oxygen as a byproduct and use the energy to fix [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] into carbohydrates. Over billions of years they changed the early atmosphere, contributing to the [[Great Oxidation Event|Great Oxidation Event]] in the early [[Proterozoic|Proterozoic]] eon. Their photosynthetic pigments include chlorophyll, carotenoids and phycobilins, and their internal membranes, called thylakoids, perform photosynthesis. Plant and algal [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] are thought to descend from cyanobacteria acquired through endosymbiosis. Cyanobacteria first appeared in the middle [[Archean|Archean]] eon and are probably the most numerous taxon ever to have existed on Earth. Many species fix [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]] and live in soils or in symbiosis with plants and [[Lichen|lichens]]. Some produce toxins that can harm humans and animals.

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Transdisciplinary study of circular causal processes such as feedback and [[Recursion|recursion]], in which the outcomes of actions return as inputs for subsequent actions. The field is named after the steering of a ship, in which the rudder is adjusted in response to its observed effect, forming a feedback loop. The term derives from the [[Ancient Greek|Ancient Greek]] *kybernētēs*, "steersman." Cybernetics arose from exchanges among disciplines in the 1940s and was consolidated at meetings such as the Macy conferences. Early focuses included neural networks, [[Information theory|information theory]] and self-organization. In his 1948 book, Norbert Wiener characterized cybernetics as concerned with "control and communication in the animal and the machine." Its scope later broadened to design, management, sociology and the creative arts. Its ideas about purposeful, adaptive systems are sometimes seen as a precursor to [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]].

HEADWORD: Cyberpunk
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Artificial intelligence | Science fiction
ENTRY:
Subgenre of [[Science fiction|science fiction]] set in a dystopian future and characterized by a focus on "lowlife and high tech." Its settings pair advanced technology, including [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]] and cyberware, with societal collapse and decay. The genre drew on the 1960s–70s New Wave movement, and William Gibson's *Neuromancer* (1984) helped establish it. The Japanese strand began in 1982 with Katsuhiro Otomo's manga *Akira*, whose 1988 anime film popularized it. Early films include Ridley Scott's *Blade Runner* (1982).

=== KEY: Cyborg_a6bd53
HEADWORD: Cyborg
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Android (robot) | Human | Mammal
ENTRY:
Being with both organic and biomechatronic body parts. The term, a portmanteau of *cybernetic* and *organism*, was coined in 1960 by Manfred Clynes and Nathan S. Kline. Unlike [[Android (robot)|androids]] or biorobots, a cyborg is a living organism whose function has been restored, or whose abilities have been enhanced, by an artificial component that relies on feedback, such as a prosthesis, implant or artificial organ. Cyborgs are commonly thought of as [[Mammal|mammals]], including [[Human|humans]], but the term can apply to any organism.
