Division of naked-seeded, dioecious plants that superficially resemble palms. Cycads are seed plants with a stout, woody, usually unbranched [[Trunk (botany)|trunk]] crowned by large, hard, evergreen, usually pinnate (feather-shaped) leaves. Individual plants are either male or female, and both sexes bear strobili, somewhat like conifer cones, that hold pollen or seeds. Cycads appeared around 300 million years ago and reached their greatest diversity in the [[Mesozoic]]. Four species are on the brink of extinction, and seven have fewer than 100 plants left in the wild. In [[Vanuatu]] the plant symbolizes peace and appears on the national flag.

=== KEY: Cyclone_b92323
HEADWORD: Cyclone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Atmospheric pressure | Extratropical cyclone | Jet stream | Jupiter | Mars | Tornado | Tropical cyclone | Typhoon
ENTRY:
Large-scale air mass rotating around a center of low [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]]. Cyclones rotate counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and clockwise in the Southern Hemisphere, with winds spiraling inward. The name comes from the Greek *kýklos*, meaning "circle," and was coined by Henry Piddington, who published 40 papers on tropical storms from Calcutta between 1836 and 1855. Cyclones have also been observed on [[Mars]], [[Jupiter]] and Neptune. [[Extratropical cyclone|Extratropical cyclones]] form along baroclinic zones and last two to six days, steered by the subtropical [[Jet stream|jet stream]]. Cold fronts typically feature narrow bands of thunderstorms, while warm fronts are usually preceded by stratiform precipitation and fog. [[Tropical cyclone|Tropical cyclones]] are warm-core systems fueled by latent heat from thunderstorm activity. They are called hurricanes in the Atlantic and northeastern Pacific, [[Typhoon|typhoons]] in the northwestern Pacific, and cyclones in the Indian and South Pacific oceans. Mesocyclones over land can lead to [[Tornado|tornado]] formation.

=== KEY: Cygnus_X_1_c2f6b4
HEADWORD: Cygnus X-1
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Astronomical unit | Black hole | Cygnus (constellation) | Stephen Hawking | X-ray
ENTRY:
High-mass [[X-ray|X-ray]] binary in the constellation [[Cygnus (constellation)|Cygnus]], containing a stellar [[Black hole|black hole]]. Discovered in 1964 during a rocket flight, it is one of the strongest X-ray sources detectable from Earth and was the first such source widely accepted to be a black hole. The system pairs a blue supergiant variable star with the black hole, about 0.2 [[Astronomical unit|AU]] apart. A stellar wind from the supergiant feeds an accretion disk around the black hole, where matter heated to millions of degrees emits the observed X-rays. A pair of relativistic jets, arranged perpendicular to the disk, carries part of the infalling material's energy into interstellar space. In 1975 physicist [[Stephen Hawking|Stephen Hawking]] wagered that Cygnus X-1 was not a black hole, hoping to lose. He conceded the bet in 1990, after observational data had strengthened the case for a black hole.

=== KEY: Cyrillic_script_01bbd2

ENTRY:
[[Writing system|Writing system]] used for many languages across [[Eurasia|Eurasia]], including Slavic, Turkic and Mongolic languages. As of 2019, around 250 million people used it as the official script for their national languages, with Russia accounting for about half. When Bulgaria joined the [[European Union]] in 2007, Cyrillic became the third official script of the EU, after the Latin and Greek alphabets. The Early Cyrillic alphabet was developed in the 9th century at the Preslav Literary School in the [[First Bulgarian Empire]], probably by disciples of [[Cyril and Methodius]], and is named in honor of Saint Cyril. It spread among other [[Slavs|Slavs]] and non-Slavic Romanians, and came to dominate Glagolitic in the 12th century.

=== KEY: Cyrus_the_Great_b2ca64
HEADWORD: Cyrus the Great
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Babylon | Babylonian captivity | Isaiah | Lydia
ENTRY:
Persian king, founder of the [[Achaemenid Empire]] (r. 559–530 BC). Also known as Cyrus II of Persia, he came from Anshan, in Persis. Around 550 BC he revolted against the Median king Astyages. He defeated [[Lydia]] in 546 BC and captured [[Babylon]] in 539 BC with minimal resistance, gaining control of Anatolia and the Fertile Crescent. His conquests created what became the largest empire in history up to that time. It reached its greatest extent later, under Darius the Great.

Cyrus allegedly died in battle against the Massagetae along the Syr Darya, according to Herodotus; Xenophon says he instead returned to the capital, Pasargadae. His son Cambyses II succeeded him and conquered Egypt, Nubia, and Cyrenaica.

Greek writers praised his tolerance toward the customs and religions of conquered peoples. The Cyrus Cylinder records his restoration of local cults and the repatriation of exiles, including the Jews. Some modern assessments hold that this was pragmatic toleration for administrative efficiency rather than ideological benevolence. In Judaism he is exalted for ending the [[Babylonian captivity]]; the Hebrew Bible describes the return to Judah and the rebuilding of the Temple in Jerusalem. According to the Book of [[Isaiah]], he was anointed by Yahweh and designated "messiah," the only non-Jewish figure so revered.

Cyrus is credited with developing a central administration to govern the empire's satraps, a model said to have influenced later states. Later Persian empires viewed the Achaemenid era as an ideal to emulate, and his tomb at Pasargadae remains a site of reverence for many Iranians.

=== KEY: Cytoplasm_811ec4
HEADWORD: Cytoplasm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Calcium | Cell (biology) | Cell membrane | Glycolysis | Mitochondria | Photosynthesis | Ribosome
ENTRY:
Material within a [[Cell (biology)|cell]], enclosed by the [[Cell membrane|cell membrane]] and excluding the nucleus in eukaryotes. Its main components are the cytosol, a gel-like substance, the cell's internal structures and various inclusions. About 80% water and usually colorless, the cytosol suspends larger structures such as [[Ribosome|ribosomes]], [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]], plastids, lipid droplets and vacuoles. Many cellular activities take place in the cytoplasm, including [[Glycolysis|glycolysis]], [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] and cell division. === KEY: Cytoskeleton_f80f36
HEADWORD: Cytoskeleton
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cell (biology) | Cell membrane | Cell nucleus | Cytoplasm | Eukaryote | Mitosis | Muscle contraction | Protein
ENTRY:
Dynamic network of [[Protein|protein]] filaments in the [[Cytoplasm|cytoplasm]] of all [[Cell (biology)|cells]], including those of bacteria and archaea. In [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]], it extends from the [[Cell nucleus|nucleus]] to the [[Cell membrane|cell membrane]] and has three main components: microfilaments, intermediate filaments and microtubules. Its primary function is to give the cell its shape and mechanical resistance, and it can contract, allowing cells to migrate. It takes part in cell signaling, endocytosis, the segregation of chromosomes during [[Mitosis|mitosis]], and the intracellular transport of vesicles and organelles. Microfilaments are made of actin, the most abundant cellular protein, and during [[Muscle contraction|muscle contraction]] myosin motors exert force on parallel actin filaments. Tropomyosin inhibits this interaction until troponin, responding to rising calcium, releases the inhibition. Nikolai K. Koltsov proposed the cytoskeleton in 1903, and it was found in prokaryotes in 1992.

=== KEY: DNA_396c98
HEADWORD: DNA
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chromosome | Francis Crick | James Watson | Polymer | Protein | RNA | Rosalind Franklin
ENTRY:
Deoxyribonucleic acid, a [[Polymer|polymer]] of two polynucleotide chains coiled in a double helix, which carries the genetic instructions for the development, functioning, growth, and reproduction of all known organisms and many viruses. DNA and [[RNA|ribonucleic acid]] (RNA) are the two nucleic acids found in all life.

Each strand is a series of nucleotides, each made of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), or thymine (T). The strands are antiparallel and held together by hydrogen bonds, which pair A with T and C with G. The double helix has a pitch of 34 ångströms (3.4 nm). The sequence of bases encodes genetic information; because both strands carry it, it can be replicated when they separate. Under the genetic code, RNA copies of DNA (transcription) specify the sequence of amino acids in [[Protein|proteins]] (translation).

In eukaryotes, DNA is organized into [[Chromosome|chromosomes]], stored mostly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Prokaryotes store DNA in the cytoplasm in circular chromosomes. The human genome comprises about 3 billion base pairs on 23 pairs of chromosomes; only about 1–2% consists of protein-coding genes, approximately 20,000 in total.

Friedrich Miescher first isolated DNA in 1869. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty showed that it is responsible for heredity in bacteria. In 1953 [[James Watson]] and [[Francis Crick]] proposed the double-helix model, building on X-ray data from [[Rosalind Franklin]] and Maurice Wilkins; it was published in *Nature*.

=== KEY: DNA_profiling_ab92f1
HEADWORD: DNA profiling
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Twin
ENTRY:
Technique for determining an individual's [[DNA]] characteristics, also called DNA fingerprinting. In forensic investigations it compares suspects' profiles with DNA evidence. Modern techniques are highly reliable, but they give only a fallible probabilistic estimate of a match. British geneticist Sir Alec Jeffreys discovered the technique in 1984 at the University of Leicester, after realizing that some regions of DNA contain highly variable repetitive sequences unique to each individual. Although 99.9% of human DNA sequences are the same in every person, the variable regions allow individuals to be distinguished unless they are [[Twin|identical twins]]. DNA profiling is also used in paternity testing, to establish immigration eligibility, and in genealogical and medical research. Its first forensic use, in the murders of two teenagers in Narborough, Leicestershire, led to the conviction of Colin Pitchfork in January 1988.

=== KEY: DNA_replication_5f00f2
HEADWORD: DNA replication
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: DNA | Enzyme | Polymerase chain reaction
ENTRY:
Process by which a cell makes exact copies of its [[DNA]]. It occurs in all organisms and is essential to inheritance, cell division and the repair of damaged tissues. Because each original strand serves as a template, every replicated molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand. Replication usually begins at origins of replication, where [[Enzyme|enzymes]] called helicases unwind the double helix and replication forks grow in both directions. DNA polymerase adds nucleotides that complement the template strand. Proofreading and error-checking mechanisms ensure near-perfect fidelity, with an error rate as low as one in 10⁹ nucleotides. Replication occurs during the S (synthesis) stage of interphase. It can also be performed *in vitro*, as in the [[Polymerase chain reaction|polymerase chain reaction]] (PCR).

=== KEY: Dam_f497ee
HEADWORD: Dam
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hydropower | Irrigation | Reservoir | Roman Empire
ENTRY:
Structure built across a watercourse to impound water or restrict its flow, usually creating a [[Reservoir|reservoir]]. Dams are classified into four basic types: gravity dams, of concrete or masonry, which resist the water by their weight; embankment dams, large earthworks of rock, clay, sand, soil, or gravel; buttress dams, with a sloped concrete face supported by triangular buttresses; and arch dams, whose curved concrete wall transfers the water's weight to the valley walls. Natural dams include rockslide, volcanic, and beaver dams.

Dams serve [[Irrigation|irrigation]], [[Hydropower|hydropower]], water supply, flood management, recreation, inland navigation, and fish farming. About 20% of the world's arable land is irrigated with water from reservoirs behind dams. Many include locks, fish ladders, spillways, and outlets. The embankment dam is the most common type; it is cheaper than concrete and can sit on softer soils, but it is inherently permeable, and seepage can cause failure by "piping."

An early dam was Jawa Dam in modern Jordan, built around 3000 BC. The Hittite Empire built several dams in modern Turkey between the 17th and 13th centuries BC, and in the 1st century AD the [[Roman Empire]] began building masonry gravity dams. From the late 18th century, dam design became an engineering discipline rooted in science, and in the 20th century concrete and heavy machinery enabled many large projects. Over 58,000 large dams now operate worldwide.

Failures cause flooding and loss of life, as in the 1975 collapse of the Banqiao Dam in China. Dams can also disrupt ecosystems, trap sediment, and alter aquatic habitats. A dam may be removed if it is unsafe, no longer serves its purpose, or blocks fish migration or sediment flow.

The game is commonly played in the [[British Isles]] and enjoyed around the world. In 1908 darts was declared a game of skill, allowing it in pubs, after a Leeds landlord was prosecuted for playing it on licensed premises. Before [[World War I]], dartboards were made of elm, which was easily damaged. From 1935 they were made of compressed sisal fibers bound with a metal ring, a more durable design.
