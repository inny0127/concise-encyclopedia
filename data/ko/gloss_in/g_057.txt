=== KEY: Hair_c2ad21
HEADWORD: Hair
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Camouflage | Cuticle | Hairstyle | Mammal | Melanin | Protein | Stem cell | Whiskers
ENTRY:
Protein filament that grows from follicles in the dermis of the skin, one of the distinguishing characteristics of [[Mammal|mammals]]. Human skin, apart from areas such as the palms and soles, produces thick terminal hair and fine vellus hair.

The word refers to two structures. The follicle, which lies in the dermis, contains [[Stem cell|stem cells]] that regrow the hair after it falls out and also help regrow skin after a wound. The shaft, the part above the skin, consists of dead, keratinized cells and has no biochemical activity. Keratin, a [[Protein|protein]], makes up most of its volume, notably alpha-keratin. The shaft has three layers: the outer [[Cuticle|cuticle]], of overlapping flat cells; the cortex, which holds the keratin bundles and is the main source of mechanical strength; and the medulla, an open, unstructured center that is not always present. The shape of the follicle determines the shape of the fiber: straight hair has round fibers, while oval fibers are generally wavy or curly. Arrector pili muscles make hairs stand up, producing goose bumps.

All natural hair colors come from two types of [[Melanin|melanin]]. Eumelanin dominates in brown and black hair and pheomelanin in red hair; blond hair has little pigmentation, and gray hair results when melanin production decreases or stops. Hair grows in cycles of active growth (anagen), regression (catagen), and rest (telogen). In mammals it insulates, protects the skin, aids sensation through [[Whiskers|whiskers]], and may serve in [[Camouflage|camouflage]].

Attitudes toward hair, including [[Hairstyle|hairstyles]] and hair removal, vary across cultures and periods, and hair often signals a person's beliefs or social position, such as age, gender, or religion.

=== KEY: Haitian_Revolution_4cca30
HEADWORD: Haitian Revolution
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Slave revolt and war of independence in the French colony of Saint-Domingue, 1791–1804. Its leaders included [[Toussaint Louverture|Toussaint Louverture]], a former enslaved man who emerged as the most prominent general. The revolt began on August 21, 1791, and the colony became independent as Haiti on January 1, 1804. It is one of the only known slave rebellions to lead to a state free from slavery, though not from forced labor, and ruled by former enslaved people. It was the largest slave uprising since [[Spartacus|Spartacus]]'s revolt against the [[Roman Republic|Roman Republic]] nearly 1,900 years earlier. The revolution reshaped debates over slavery, strengthened the abolitionist cause and sparked revolts in nearby colonies. France did not officially recognize Haiti's independence until 1825, and Haiti was required to pay an indemnity of 150 million francs, a heavy financial burden.

=== KEY: Haitian_Vodou_af5d30

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
African diasporic religion that developed in Haiti between the 16th and 19th centuries. It arose through [[Syncretism|syncretism]] between traditional religions of West and Central Africa and [[Catholic Church|Catholicism]], and it absorbed influences from [[Freemasonry|Freemasonry]]. Vodou has no central authority. It teaches a creator divinity, Bondye, and spirits called lwa, who are equated with Catholic saints. Many Vodouists took part in the [[Haitian Revolution|Haitian Revolution]], after which the Catholic Church left for several decades and Vodou became Haiti's dominant religion. Most Haitians practice both Vodou and Catholicism. The religion has been described as one of the world's most misunderstood.

Collective body of Jewish religious law, derived from the [[Torah|Written and Oral Torah]]. Halakha is based on biblical commandments, [[Talmud|Talmudic]] and rabbinic law, and customs compiled in works such as the Shulchan Aruch and the Mishneh Torah. Because halakha is developed by various authorities rather than one official voice, communities may give different answers to halakhic questions.

ENTRY:
Time required for a quantity to reduce to half its initial value, symbol t½. Commonly used in nuclear physics to describe how quickly unstable atoms undergo [[Radioactive decay|radioactive decay]], the term also applies to any exponential decay, such as the biological half-life of drugs. [[Ernest Rutherford|Ernest Rutherford]] discovered the principle in 1907 and used it to date rocks by the decay of [[Radium|radium]] to lead-206. === KEY: Hall_effect_5354dd

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Voltage produced across a conductor, transverse to a current flowing in it and to a magnetic field applied perpendicular to the current. Edwin Hall discovered the effect in 1879 while working on his doctoral degree at [[Johns Hopkins University|Johns Hopkins University]] in [[Baltimore|Baltimore]], [[Maryland|Maryland]]. === KEY: Halley_s_Comet_f00ab8
HEADWORD: Halley's Comet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Edmond Halley | European Space Agency | Giotto | Isaac Newton
ENTRY:
Short-period comet visible to the naked eye from Earth, officially designated 1P/Halley. It returns every 74 to 79 years, last appearing in 1986 and next due in mid-2061. Its returns were recorded from at least 240 BC, but in 1705 the English astronomer [[Edmond Halley|Edmond Halley]] used [[Isaac Newton|Isaac Newton]]'s laws of gravity to show that several sightings were of the same comet. In 1986 the [[European Space Agency|European Space Agency]]'s [[Giotto|Giotto]] mission became the first spacecraft to observe a comet in detail. Its results supported Fred Whipple's "dirty snowball" model, which predicted that the comet would be a mixture of volatile ices and dust. They also showed that the surface is largely dusty, non-volatile material, with only a small portion of it icy.

ENTRY:
Sensory perception in the absence of an external stimulus, with a compelling sense of reality. Hallucinations can occur in any sense, and auditory hallucinations are the most common type; they are very common in [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]]. Optical phenomenon of rings, arcs and spots of light in the sky, produced by ice crystals. === KEY: Haloalkane_799182
HEADWORD: Haloalkane
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bromine | Chlorine | Fluorine | Iodine | Ozone depletion
ENTRY:
Organic compound derived from an alkane in which one or more hydrogen atoms are replaced by halogens. Haloalkanes, also called alkyl halides, have the general formula RX, where R is an alkyl group and X is [[Fluorine|fluorine]], [[Chlorine|chlorine]], [[Bromine|bromine]] or [[Iodine|iodine]]. They serve as flame retardants, refrigerants, propellants, solvents and pharmaceuticals. Chlorofluorocarbons, a widely used class, were shown to lead to [[Ozone depletion|ozone depletion]], and haloalkanes containing chlorine, bromine or iodine threaten the ozone layer. Chloro-, bromo- and iodoalkanes have higher melting and boiling points than their parent alkanes. Haloalkanes were known for centuries; chloroethane was produced in the 15th century, and systematic synthesis developed in the 19th century. Substantial amounts are biogenic, produced by living organisms.

Group of chemically related elements in group 17 of the periodic table. The halogens are [[Fluorine|fluorine]], [[Chlorine|chlorine]], [[Bromine|bromine]], [[Iodine|iodine]] and the radioactive [[Astatine|astatine]] and [[Tennessine|tennessine]], although some authors exclude tennessine, whose chemistry is unknown. Iodine was discovered in 1811 by Bernard Courtois, and astatine was produced in 1940.

=== KEY: Halting_problem_50514f
HEADWORD: Halting problem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alan Turing | Algorithm
ENTRY:
Decision problem in computability theory. It asks whether a given computer program, run on a given input, will eventually halt or will continue to run forever. [[Alan Turing|Alan Turing]] proved in 1937 that no general [[Algorithm|algorithm]] can solve the problem for all program–input pairs, making it undecidable. The proof constructs a "pathological" program that does the opposite of whatever a supposed halting decider predicts about it. Specific programs can often be shown to halt, and tools such as the T2 Temporal Prover have established that certain device-driver routines always return.

=== KEY: Ham_f0777d

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
[[Pork|Pork]] from a leg cut that has been preserved by dry or wet curing, with or without smoking. Traces of cured ham appear among the Etruscans of the 6th and 5th centuries BC, and [[Cato the Elder|Cato the Elder]] wrote about salting hams around 160 BC. === KEY: Hamiltonian_mechanics_ef82ee
HEADWORD: Hamiltonian mechanics
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Reformulation of [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] based on momenta rather than velocities. Introduced by [[William Rowan Hamilton|Sir William Rowan Hamilton]] in 1833, it replaces the generalized velocities of [[Lagrangian mechanics|Lagrangian mechanics]] with generalized momenta, though both describe the same physical phenomena. The Hamiltonian is the energy function of the system, which in simple cases is the sum of [[Kinetic energy|kinetic]] and [[Potential energy|potential energy]]. Hamilton's equations describe the motion as first-order differential equations in phase space. A spherical pendulum, a mass moving without friction on a sphere under gravity, is a standard worked example. The approach has a close relationship with symplectic geometry and serves as a link between classical and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].

=== KEY: Hammurabi_c71a8f

Sixth [[Amorites|Amorite]] king of [[Babylon]] (r. c. 1792–c. 1750 BC), also spelled Hammurapi. He succeeded his father, Sin-muballit.

During his reign he conquered the kingdoms of Larsa, Eshnunna, and Mari, bringing almost all of [[Mesopotamia]] under Babylonian rule. Babylon rose under him to the status of a leading kingdom of the Middle East, and the foundations were laid for it to become one of the major cities of ancient history. The most common translation of his name is "the ancestor is great," though the meaning is debated; an alternative Amorite reading is "the ancestor heals."

His reign is among the best documented in Mesopotamia. The sources are mostly [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] texts on clay tablets and stone, written in Babylonian and Sumerian, including royal inscriptions and correspondence unearthed at Larsa and Mari. The Mari archives cover the 18th to 32nd years of his reign (1774 to 1761 BC), the best-understood period.

His son Samsu-iluna succeeded him but failed to maintain the empire. Later tradition de-emphasized Hammurabi's military achievements and made his role as the ideal lawgiver the main part of his legacy. Archaeologists rediscovered him in the late 19th century, and he has since been seen as an important figure in the history of law.

Imperial dynasty of China (202 BC–AD 220), founded by Liu Bang after the collapse of the [[Qin dynasty]]. It is divided into the Western Han (202 BC–AD 9), with its capital at Chang'an (modern [[Xi'an]]), and the Eastern Han (AD 25–220), interrupted by the Xin dynasty (AD 9–23) of the usurping regent Wang Mang. Advances included papermaking, rudders for ships, negative numbers, raised-relief maps, hydraulic-powered armillary spheres, and seismometers.

The [[Xiongnu]] defeated the Han in 200 BC, leading to a policy of marriage alliance and tribute. From 133 BC Emperor Wu campaigned against them, taking the Hexi Corridor and the Tarim Basin and helping to establish the [[Silk Road]]. After AD 92, palace [[Eunuch|eunuchs]] became involved in violent court power struggles with consort clans. Taoist societies led the Yellow Turban Rebellion. After Emperor Ling died (r. 168–189), officers massacred the eunuchs, and military governors became warlords who divided the empire.

=== KEY: Happiness_edccf2
HEADWORD: Happiness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jeremy Bentham | South America
ENTRY:
Positive mental state ranging from contentment to intense joy, closely linked to well-being and life satisfaction.

There is no single accepted definition, and usage differs by culture and context. The word is used mainly in two ways: for the current experience of an emotion such as pleasure or joy (Daniel Kahneman: "what I experience here and now"), and for an appraisal of life satisfaction (Ruut Veenhoven: "overall appreciation of one's life as-a-whole"). Subjective well-being combines both, and the Greek term *eudaimonia* is variously translated as happiness, flourishing, or blessedness. Results depend on the definition: Nordic countries often score highest on surveys of subjective well-being, while [[South America|South American]] countries score higher on surveys of current positive experience. Philosopher Michela Summa distinguishes joy, bound to the present moment, from happiness, which presupposes an evaluation of one's life.

In 1780 the English utilitarian philosopher [[Jeremy Bentham]] proposed measuring happiness to judge how well a government performed. Today it is measured mainly by self-report surveys, which are prone to cognitive biases such as the peak–end rule; memories of felt emotions can be inaccurate, and people predict their future emotions poorly. Scales include the four-item Subjective Happiness Scale (1999), the 20-item Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (1988), and the Cantril ladder, on which respondents rate their lives from 0 to 10 and which is used in the World Happiness Report.

Twin studies estimate the heritability of happiness at 30–50% of variance, and people adapt quickly to life events, a process called the hedonic treadmill.

=== KEY: Harmonic_oscillator_6e2b8d

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Physical system in which a restoring force is proportional to displacement from equilibrium. Harmonic oscillators occur widely in nature, and any mass subject to a force in stable equilibrium acts as one for small vibrations. They are exploited in devices such as [[Clock|clocks]]. If the restoring force is the only force acting, the system undergoes simple harmonic motion, with sinusoidal oscillations of constant amplitude and a frequency independent of amplitude. Stringed instrument whose strings run at an angle to its soundboard and are plucked with the fingers. Harps are usually wooden and triangular, and some have multiple rows of strings and pedals. The earliest harps and lyres were found in [[Sumer]], c. 2500 BCE, with harps excavated from royal tombs in [[Ur]]. Angular harps appeared in the [[Iraq]]–[[Iran]] region around 1900 BCE, and harps later spread across Europe and the Americas, where they remain part of [[Folk music|folk music]]. Historically, strings were made of sinew from [[Tendon|tendons]], gut, plant fiber or silk, and later of nylon and wire. The modern double-action pedal harp, developed in the early 19th century by Sébastien Érard, has 47 strings.
