=== KEY: Cape_Horn_306c00
HEADWORD: Cape Horn
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chile | Drake Passage | Panama Canal | Tierra del Fuego
ENTRY:
Southernmost headland of the [[Tierra del Fuego]] archipelago, on Hornos Island in southern [[Chile]]. Cape Horn marks the northern boundary of the [[Drake Passage|Drake Passage]] and where the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans meet. Dutch navigators Willem Schouten and Jacob Le Maire first rounded it in 1616, naming it Kaap Hoorn after the city of Hoorn in the Netherlands. For decades it was a major milestone on the clipper route, and its waters are hazardous owing to strong winds, large waves, currents and icebergs. The opening of the [[Panama Canal]] in August 1914 greatly reduced the need to round the cape. Sailing around it remains one of the major challenges in yachting, and several ocean yacht races sail around the world via the Horn.

=== KEY: Capillary_223ca5
HEADWORD: Capillary
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Smallest type of blood vessel, 5 to 10 micrometres in diameter, forming part of the microcirculation. Its thin wall is made of simple squamous endothelial cells, and it is the site where [[Oxygen|oxygen]], [[Carbon dioxide]], [[Glucose]] and water pass between the blood and the surrounding interstitial fluid. === KEY: Capital_economics_7c377d
HEADWORD: Capital
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Factory
ENTRY:
Durable produced goods used as productive inputs for further production of goods and services. Capital goods, such as machinery in a [[Factory]], differ from intermediate goods such as raw materials because they provide productive services over many production cycles. Economists classify capital as a factor of production alongside labor and land, a view dating from classical economics. The capital stock comprises tangible and intangible assets held by an individual, company or nation, and its precise definition and productivity have long been debated. In Marxian critique, constant capital refers to capital goods, variable capital to wages paid for labor, and fictitious capital to stocks and bonds.

=== KEY: Capital_punishment_3bf42a
HEADWORD: Capital punishment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Code of Hammurabi | Genocide | Great Purge | Murder | OECD | Universal Declaration of Human Rights
ENTRY:
State-sanctioned killing of a person as punishment for actual or supposed misconduct, also known as the death penalty. Crimes punishable by death, called capital crimes, commonly include [[Murder|murder]], terrorism, war crimes and [[Genocide|genocide]]. The [[Code of Hammurabi]], around 1750 BCE, prescribed death for offenses such as false accusation and adultery. Executions after the show trials of the [[Great Purge]] in the Soviet Union, 1936–1938, were an instrument of political repression. As of 2023, only two of the 38 [[OECD]] member countries, the United States and Japan, allowed capital punishment. Although most countries have abolished it, over half of the world's population lives where it is retained. Amnesty International holds that it breaches the right to life protected by the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]]. Supporters argue it is justified for the gravest crimes, where life imprisonment cannot ensure permanent incapacitation.

=== KEY: Capitalism_201179
HEADWORD: Capitalism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adam Smith | Globalization | Industrial Revolution | Karl Marx | Laissez-faire | Neoliberalism | Pierre-Joseph Proudhon
ENTRY:
Economic system based on private ownership of the means of production and its use to obtain profit. Its elements include private property, the profit motive, capital accumulation, competitive markets, and wage labor.

Analysts recognize forms including [[Laissez-faire|laissez-faire]], free-market, state, and welfare capitalism, which differ in the degree of free markets, public ownership, and regulation. Most existing economies are mixed economies combining free markets with state intervention and, in some cases, planning.

The modern form emerged from agrarianism in England and from mercantilist practices in European countries between the 16th and 18th centuries. [[Adam Smith]]'s *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) is cited as its intellectual foundation, and the [[Industrial Revolution]] of the 18th century made it the dominant mode of production, characterized by factory work and a complex division of labor. Through [[Globalization|globalization]] it spread across the world in the 19th and 20th centuries. It was largely unregulated by the state in the 19th century, became more regulated after World War II through Keynesianism, and saw a return of less regulated capitalism termed [[Neoliberalism|neoliberalism]] from the 1980s.

The word *capitalist*, meaning an owner of capital, dates to the mid-17th century. The term *capitalism* in its modern sense is attributed to Louis Blanc in 1850 and [[Pierre-Joseph Proudhon]] in 1861; [[Karl Marx]] wrote of the capitalist mode of production in *Das Kapital* (1867).

Assessments differ. Supporters credit capitalist economies with sustained growth in GDP per capita and a decline in extreme poverty from over 90% of the world population in the 19th century to under 10%. Critics point to wealth disparities and recurrent business cycles, which defenders attribute to policy distortions such as subsidies and regulations favoring incumbents.

=== KEY: Car_e9989d
HEADWORD: Car
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air pollution | Carbon dioxide | Carl Benz | Climate change | Ford Model T | Fossil fuel | Internal combustion engine | Traffic collision
ENTRY:
Wheeled motor vehicle, usually four-wheeled, designed mainly to carry people on roads. Most definitions say a car seats 1–8 people and transports passengers rather than cargo; more than 1.6 billion were in use worldwide as of 2025.

The French inventor Nicolas-Joseph Cugnot built the first steam-powered road vehicle in 1769, a three-wheeled dray that hauled artillery at walking pace. In 1808 the Swiss inventor François Isaac de Rivaz built the first internal-combustion automobile. The modern car, a practical and marketable vehicle for everyday use, dates from 1886, when the German inventor [[Carl Benz]] patented his Benz Patent-Motorwagen; by the end of the 19th century he was selling 500 a year of later versions. The 1901 Oldsmobile Curved Dash and the 1908 [[Ford Model T]] are widely considered the first mass-produced and mass-affordable cars. Cars quickly replaced horse-drawn carriages in the U.S., while demand in Europe and elsewhere did not rise until after World War II. Use is still increasing rapidly, especially in China and India.

Most cars in the early 2020s are propelled by an [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engine]] burning [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]]. Electric cars, which competed with petrol and steam cars early in the 20th century, became commercially available again in the 2000s and widespread in the 2020s.

Cars bring personal mobility and independence and support large industries, but [[Traffic collision|traffic collisions]] are the largest cause of injury-related deaths worldwide. As of 2024, cars produce 10% of global [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] emissions, contributing to [[Climate change|climate change]]. Other costs include [[Air pollution|air pollution]], congestion, and urban sprawl.

=== KEY: Carbohydrate_bc7dca
HEADWORD: Carbohydrate
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amino acid | Cellulose | Chitin | Glycogen | Nucleic acid | RNA | Starch | Sucrose
ENTRY:
Organic compound that is a sugar (saccharide), a polysaccharide such as starch, or a derivative of these, and one of the major families of biomolecules. For many carbohydrates, such as simple sugars, the carbon-to-hydrogen-to-oxygen atomic ratio is 1:2:1, giving the empirical formula CH₂O and molecular formulas of the form Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ. The other major families are [[Amino acid|amino acids]], fats, and [[Nucleic acid|nucleic acids]].

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates and cannot be hydrolyzed to smaller ones. Disaccharides such as sucrose and polysaccharides or oligosaccharides such as starch and cellulose are classified by degree of polymerization.

Carbohydrates have numerous roles in living organisms. Polysaccharides store energy, as [[Starch|starch]] and [[Glycogen|glycogen]] do, and give structure, as [[Cellulose|cellulose]] does in plants and [[Chitin|chitin]] in arthropods and fungi. The 5-carbon sugar ribose is the backbone of [[RNA]] and a component of the coenzymes ATP, FAD, and NAD.

In nutrition, starch is abundant in cereals, potatoes, and foods made from flour. Sugars in the human diet are mainly table sugar ([[Sucrose|sucrose]], extracted from sugarcane or sugar beets), lactose in milk, and glucose and fructose, which occur in honey and many fruits.

Sugar cane was first grown in New Guinea and was mass-cultivated in India, reaching Europe around the 13th century. Constantin Kirchhoff discovered in 1811 that grape sugar (glucose) forms when starch is boiled with acid. The German chemist Carl Schmidt proposed the term "carbohydrate" in 1844, and the French physiologist Claude Bernard discovered glycogen in 1856. Emil Fischer received the 1902 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on sugars.

=== KEY: Carbon_dioxide_a1578a
HEADWORD: Carbon dioxide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbohydrate | Carbonate | Climate change | Fossil fuel | Greenhouse gas | Infrared | Photosynthesis | Welding
ENTRY:
Chemical compound of one carbon atom double-bonded to two oxygen atoms, formula CO₂; a colorless, odorless gas at room temperature. The molecule is linear and centrosymmetric. Solid carbon dioxide, dry ice, exists below −78.5 °C at atmospheric pressure.

It is a trace gas in Earth's atmosphere at 428 parts per million (ppm), about 0.043%, as of July 2025, up from pre-industrial levels of 280 ppm, about 0.028%. Burning of [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] is the main cause of the increase, which is the primary cause of [[Climate change|climate change]]. Transparent to visible light, CO₂ absorbs [[Infrared|infrared]] radiation and so acts as a [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]]. About half of excess emissions are absorbed by land and ocean carbon sinks, which can saturate and are volatile.

As the source of carbon in the carbon cycle, atmospheric CO₂ is the primary carbon source for life. Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria use sunlight to make [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]] from CO₂ and water by [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], releasing oxygen; aerobic organisms release CO₂ by respiration, and it is also released by decay and combustion. It is soluble in water, where it reversibly forms carbonic acid, a weak acid, though most dissolved CO₂ remains as CO₂ molecules. Dissolved CO₂ forms [[Carbonate|carbonate]] and mainly bicarbonate, which causes ocean acidification as atmospheric levels rise.

Nearly all CO₂ produced by humans goes into the atmosphere. Less than 1% of annual production is put to commercial use, mostly in the fertilizer industry and in enhanced oil recovery. Other uses include food and beverage production, cooling, fire suppression, greenhouse plant growth, and inert atmospheres for [[Welding|welding]].

=== KEY: Carbon_fbebe0
HEADWORD: Carbon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Carbon nanotube | Diamond | Graphene | Graphite | Isotope | Organic chemistry | Periodic table | Radiocarbon dating
ENTRY:
Nonmetallic chemical element, symbol C, atomic number 6, in group 14 of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. Its name comes from Latin *carbo* 'coal'. It is tetravalent, its atoms able to form up to four covalent bonds through four valence electrons, and it has been known since antiquity.

Carbon makes up about 0.025 percent of Earth's crust and is the 15th most abundant element there. It is the fourth most abundant element in the universe by mass, after hydrogen, helium, and oxygen, and the second most abundant in the human body by mass (about 18.5%) after oxygen. Three [[Isotope|isotopes]] occur naturally: ¹²C and ¹³C are stable, while ¹⁴C is a radionuclide with a half-life of 5,700 years, used in [[Radiocarbon dating|radiocarbon dating]]. Its abundance, the diversity of its organic compounds, and its unusual ability to form polymers at Earth temperatures make it the common element of all known life.

Carbon bonds together in many ways, producing allotropes that include [[Graphite|graphite]], [[Diamond|diamond]], amorphous carbon, and fullerenes. Their properties differ widely. Graphite is opaque, black, soft enough to streak paper, and a good electrical conductor. Diamond is highly transparent, the hardest naturally occurring material known, and a poor electrical conductor. Diamond, [[Carbon nanotube|carbon nanotubes]], and [[Graphene|graphene]] have the highest thermal conductivities of all known materials. All allotropes are solid under normal conditions and chemically resistant. Carbon has no melting point at atmospheric pressure, since its triple point lies at 10.8 ± 0.2 megapascals and 4,600 ± 300 K; it sublimes at about 3,900 K (3,630 °C).

The most common oxidation state in inorganic compounds is +4, and +2 occurs in carbon monoxide. Carbon forms over a hundred million described compounds and is the basis of [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]].

=== KEY: Carbon_monoxide_231082
HEADWORD: Carbon monoxide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Combustion | Hemoglobin | Neurotransmitter | Smelting | Stove
ENTRY:
Colorless, odorless, poisonous and flammable gas, CO, consisting of one carbon atom and one oxygen atom joined by a triple bond. It is the simplest carbon oxide, produced by the partial [[Combustion|combustion]] of carbon-containing fuels in [[Stove|stoves]], heating systems and vehicles. It is one of the most acutely toxic indoor air contaminants, and carbon monoxide poisoning is the most common type of fatal air poisoning in many countries. CO binds to [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]] approximately 200–250 times more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin and impairing oxygen delivery. In mammals, low concentrations act as an endogenous [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitter]]. Industrially, it is used in producing drugs, fragrances and fuels, and as a reducing agent in [[Smelting|smelting]] iron ore.

=== KEY: Carbon_nanotube_181d8c
HEADWORD: Carbon nanotube
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cobalt | Composite material | Electronics | Graphene | Graphite | IBM | Semiconductor | Young's modulus
ENTRY:
Cylindrical allotrope of carbon with a diameter on the nanometer scale. Single-walled nanotubes (SWCNTs) have diameters of about 0.5–2.0 nanometers and can be idealized as a [[Graphene|graphene]] sheet rolled into a hollow cylinder. === KEY: Cardinality_6eab36
HEADWORD: Cardinality
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Property of sets in mathematics that measures their size, roughly the number of objects they contain, which may be infinite. Two [[Set (mathematics)|sets]] have the same cardinality if their objects can be paired one-to-one, with none left unpaired. For finite sets, cardinality equals the usual count of elements. Infinite sets are measured by the [[Aleph number|Aleph numbers]], which extend the process of counting. The [[Natural number|natural numbers]] have cardinality ℵ₀, and a set is countably infinite if it can be paired one-to-one with them. The [[Real number|real numbers]] are uncountable, a result proved by diagonal arguments, which show that there are different sizes of infinity. [[Georg Cantor|Cantor]] introduced cardinality formally to mathematics in the late 19th century. Whether the reals have cardinality ℵ₁ is the continuum hypothesis, which is neither provable nor disprovable in standard set theories such as [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory]].

=== KEY: Cardiovascular_disease_a6c2a2

ENTRY:
Any disease involving the heart or blood vessels; abbreviated CVD. It includes [[Coronary artery disease|coronary artery disease]], [[Heart failure|heart failure]], hypertensive heart disease, valvular heart disease (such as rheumatic heart disease), cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, congenital heart disease, aortic aneurysms, peripheral artery disease, and venous thrombosis.

Mechanisms vary by disease. Coronary artery disease, [[Stroke|stroke]], and peripheral artery disease involve [[Atherosclerosis|atherosclerosis]], which may be caused by [[Hypertension|high blood pressure]], smoking, [[Obesity|obesity]], high cholesterol, [[Diabetes|diabetes mellitus]], poor diet, lack of exercise, excessive alcohol consumption, and poor sleep. Geopolitical region in the middle of the Americas, centered on the [[Caribbean Sea]] in the North Atlantic Ocean of islands, cays, reefs, banks, and adjoining mainland coasts. It largely overlaps the West Indies, a term often used interchangeably.

German polymath active as a mathematician, astronomer, geodesist, and physicist. A child prodigy from a family of low social status in Brunswick, he studied with the Duke of Brunswick's support. From 1807 until his death he was director of the Göttingen Observatory and professor of astronomy.

He received the Lalande Prize in 1809 and the Copley Medal in 1838. He withheld incomplete work, delaying the spread of many discoveries. A reluctant teacher, he nonetheless taught [[Richard Dedekind]] and [[Bernhard Riemann]].

Cartoons are divided into gag cartoons, which include editorial cartoons, and comic strips. Sir John Tenniel, illustrator of *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, contributed over two thousand cartoons to *Punch* over more than 50 years. In the early 20th century the term also came to refer to animated films.

American [[Cable television|cable television]] network and flagship property of The Cartoon Network, Inc. Launched on October 1, 1992, it was the first 24-hour single-genre cable channel with animation as its main theme, and it primarily broadcasts animated series for children. The evening block [[Adult Swim]] targets young adults. The network reached a peak of 100 million households in 2011 and was available in approximately 66 million U.S. pay television households as of November 2023. It was later brought under common ownership with its long-time rival [[Nickelodeon]].

Process of shaping material by scraping away portions of it with [[Tool|tools]]. The technique applies to any material solid enough to hold a form when pieces are removed, yet soft enough to be scraped with available tools. Unlike methods using malleable materials such as [[Clay|clay]], [[Fruit|fruit]] and melted [[Glass|glass]], which are shaped while soft and then harden, carving generally requires much more work. Relief carving cuts a design into a flat surface to make it three-dimensional; chip carving and whittling remove small pieces from a solid block, usually of wood; sculptural carving shapes a lifelike form. Stone and wood carving is a means of making [[Sculpture|sculpture]]. Carved figures date to the Upper Paleolithic era, including the ivory Venus of Hohle Fels, carved around 40,000 years ago in what is now Germany. Durable stone was carved for long-lasting reliefs in [[Ancient Egypt]].
