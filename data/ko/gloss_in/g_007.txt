=== KEY: Anger_811b20
HEADWORD: Anger
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrenaline | Aggression | Blood pressure | Body language
ENTRY:
Intense emotional state involving a strong, uncomfortable, and non-cooperative response to a perceived provocation, hurt, or threat.

Anger is also called rage; wrath is its extreme counterpart. It is often accompanied by increased heart rate, elevated [[Blood pressure|blood pressure]], and raised levels of the stress hormones [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]] and noradrenaline, and some view it as triggering part of the fight-or-flight response. It is expressed in facial expressions, [[Body language|body language]], physiological responses, and at times public acts of [[Aggression|aggression]]. Anger reduces self-monitoring, so an angry person may misjudge the cause of the feeling.

Psychologists recognize three types: hasty and sudden anger, tied to self-preservation and shared with other animals; settled and deliberate anger, a reaction to perceived deliberate harm or unfair treatment; and dispositional anger, related to character traits such as irritability and sullenness.

Modern psychologists generally regard anger as a normal emotion with functional value for individual survival and mutual cooperation. It can mobilize resources and determination toward correcting wrongs, promoting social justice, and redressing grievances. Strong or uncontrolled anger impairs the processing of information and cognitive control, and can harm personal well-being, health, and relationships. Anger is distinct from aggression: it can activate aggression but is neither a necessary nor a sufficient condition for it. Philosophers and writers since the earliest times have warned against uncontrolled fits of anger, though they disagreed about its intrinsic value; modern psychologists also note possible ill effects of suppressing it. Management approaches aim to reduce emotional intensity and physiological arousal through cognitive reappraisal, relaxation techniques, and problem-solving.

=== KEY: Angle_45f4ce
HEADWORD: Angle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circle | Degree (angle) | Euclid | Geometry | Radian | Right angle | Rotation
ENTRY:
Figure formed by two rays that share a common endpoint, and the measure of the opening between them.

In [[Geometry|geometry]], each ray is a side of the angle and the shared point is its vertex. The word denotes both the geometric figure and its size, or angular measure, which is a real number. There is no universally agreed definition: an angle may be taken as the opening between the rays, the area of the plane between them, or the amount of rotation about the vertex from one ray to the other. Measurement is closely linked to [[Circle|circles]] and [[Rotation|rotations]] and is often visualized by the arc of a circle centered at the vertex.

Angles are written with the symbol ∠ and one or three points, as in ∠BAC, with the vertex always in the middle; the measure is m∠BAC. Signed angles indicate direction of rotation: positive for anti-clockwise, negative for clockwise.

The most common units are the [[Degree (angle)|degree]] (°), the [[Radian|radian]] (rad), and the turn. A full angle measures 1 turn or 360°; in radians it is 2π rad, approximately 6.28 rad.

Named types are the zero angle (0°), acute (less than 90°), [[Right angle|right]] (90°, or π/2 rad), obtuse (between 90° and 180°), straight (180°, or π rad), reflex (between 180° and 360°), and full (360°, or 2π rad). Adjacent angles share a vertex and a side. Vertical angles, formed opposite each other when two lines cross, are always equal. Angle pairs summing to a right angle are complementary. Early definitions appear in [[Euclid]]'s *Elements* (c. 300 BC).

=== KEY: Angular_momentum_b05a33
HEADWORD: Angular momentum
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth | Gyroscope | Neutron star | Noether's theorem | Quantum mechanics | Sun | Torque
ENTRY:
Rotational analogue of linear momentum and a conserved physical quantity. Angular momentum has both direction and magnitude, and for a system with zero net external torque the total angular momentum is constant. Bicycles, flying discs, rifled bullets and [[Gyroscope|gyroscopes]] owe useful properties to its conservation, which is also why hurricanes form spirals and [[Neutron star|neutron stars]] rotate rapidly. For a point particle it is the cross product of position and linear momentum, so it depends on the chosen origin. [[Torque]] is the rate of change of angular momentum, analogous to force. The orbital angular momentum of the [[Earth]] with respect to the [[Sun]] is about 2.66 × 10⁴⁰ J⋅s, while its rotational angular momentum is about 7.05 × 10³³ J⋅s. In [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]], angular momentum is represented by operators, and its magnitude and projections are quantized. Its conservation follows from the rotational symmetry of space, through [[Noether's theorem]].

=== KEY: Animal_161e7c
HEADWORD: Animal
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aristotle | Bilateria | Cambrian explosion | Carl Linnaeus | Ediacaran | Ernst Haeckel | Insect | Zoology
ENTRY:
Multicellular, eukaryotic organism of the biological kingdom Animalia.

Animals consume organic material, breathe oxygen, have muscle cells, can reproduce sexually, and grow from a hollow sphere of cells, the blastula, during embryonic development. With few exceptions they are able to move. They form a clade, arising from a single common ancestor. Unlike plants and algae, animals cannot produce their own food; they ingest organic material and digest it internally. Their cells are surrounded by an extracellular matrix of collagen and elastic glycoproteins, which can be calcified to form shells, bones, and spicules.

More than 1.5 million living species have been described, of which around 1.05 million are [[Insect|insects]], over 85,000 are molluscs, and around 65,000 are vertebrates; as many as 7.77 million species may exist. Body lengths range from 8.5 μm (0.00033 in) to 33.6 m (110 ft). The scientific study of animals is [[Zoology|zoology]]; the study of their behavior is ethology.

The kingdom is divided into five major clades: Porifera, Ctenophora, Placozoa, Cnidaria, and [[Bilateria]]. Most species belong to Bilateria, whose members have bilaterally symmetric, cephalized bodies. Most bilaterians are protostomes (arthropods, molluscs, flatworms, annelids, nematodes) or deuterostomes (echinoderms, hemichordates, chordates, including the vertebrates).

Nearly all animals reproduce sexually: haploid gametes produced by meiosis, the small motile spermatozoa and the larger non-motile ova, fuse to form zygotes, which develop by mitosis into a blastula. Some reproduce asexually by fragmentation, budding, or parthenogenesis, as in aphids. Ecologically, animals are grouped as carnivores, herbivores, omnivores, fungivores, scavengers or detritivores, and parasites, and they form complex food webs.

Animals first appear in the fossil record in the [[Ediacaran]] period. Most modern phyla appeared as marine species in the [[Cambrian explosion]], which began around 539 million years ago, and most classes in the Ordovician radiation 485.4 million years ago. A set of 6,331 groups of genes common to all living animals may derive from a common ancestor that lived about 650 million years ago.

[[Aristotle]] divided animals into those with blood and those without. [[Carl Linnaeus]] created the first hierarchical classification in 1758 with *Systema Naturae*; Lamarck expanded it to 14 phyla by 1809. In 1874 [[Ernst Haeckel]] divided the kingdom into multicellular Metazoa (now synonymous with Animalia) and the single-celled Protozoa, no longer counted as animals. Modern classification relies on molecular phylogenetics.

Humans use animals for food (meat, eggs, dairy), for materials (leather, fur, wool), as pets, and as working animals. Dogs were the first domesticated animal. Animals appear in cave art, totems, mythology, religion, literature, heraldry, politics, and sports.

General appearance of an animal, resulting from light reflected or emitted by its surfaces. Coloration has evolved for several reasons. === KEY: Animal_husbandry_1d2735
HEADWORD: Animal husbandry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Climate change | Columbian exchange | Horse | Livestock | Neolithic Revolution | Ruminant | Selective breeding
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Agriculture|agriculture]] concerned with raising captive or domesticated animals for valuable products, labor, or services.

It involves day-to-day management, feeding, nutrition and health, [[Selective breeding|selective breeding]]. The animals are primarily domesticated terrestrial mammals (cattle, sheep, goats, horses, pigs) and birds (mainly chickens, ducks, and geese), collectively known as [[Livestock|livestock]].

Husbandry was important in the transition from nomadic hunter-gatherers to agrarian and pastoralist societies. Dogs were first tamed more than 17,500 years ago, before the [[Neolithic Revolution]], which began around 13,000 BC with the first crops and tame animals. Old World livestock reached the New World in the [[Columbian exchange]]. In the 18th century's British Agricultural Revolution, breeders such as Robert Bakewell improved breeds for meat, milk, and wool.

Animals also provide services. [[Horse|Horses]], donkeys, and mules were widespread in transport, logistics, warfare, and law enforcement until motor vehicles diminished their value in the early 20th century.

Modern production systems are adapted to available land. Intensive farming, such as high-density feedlots and broiler houses holding thousands of chickens, is superseding subsistence farming in more developed regions, while on poorer soil animals are kept extensively and forage widely. [[Ruminant|Ruminants]] such as cattle and sheep are adapted to eat grass; pigs and poultry cannot digest the cellulose in forage and need high-protein feed. Animal agriculture at modern scale is linked to [[Climate change|climate change]], ocean acidification, and biodiversity loss.

=== KEY: Animal_locomotion_849fd1
HEADWORD: Animal locomotion
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buoyancy | Cephalopod | Echinoderm | Jellyfish | Natural selection | Spider
ENTRY:
Methods by which animals move from one place to another. Some locomotion is self-propelled, such as running, swimming, jumping, flying, hopping, soaring and gliding. Passive locomotion relies on the environment or other animals, as in sailing by some [[Jellyfish|jellyfish]], kiting by [[Spider|spiders]], or riding on other animals. Animals move to find food, mates or suitable habitat, or to escape predators, and [[Natural selection]] has shaped their methods. Migratory animals such as the Arctic tern typically use locomotion that costs little energy per unit distance, while animals that must escape predators often use costly but very fast movement. In water, a body less dense than water relies on [[Buoyancy|buoyancy]], and most fish generate thrust by oscillating the body. [[Cephalopod|Cephalopods]] travel fast by jet propulsion. On the seabed, [[Echinoderm|echinoderms]] move with tube feet, while crabs typically walk sideways.

=== KEY: Animal_migration_ab87f8
HEADWORD: Animal migration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circadian rhythm | Goose | Salmon | Wildebeest
ENTRY:
Relatively long-distance movement of animals, usually on a seasonal basis. It is the most common form of migration in ecology and occurs in birds, mammals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, insects and crustaceans. Causes include local climate, food availability, season and mating. To count as true migration, the movement must be an annual or seasonal occurrence, or a major habitat change in an animal's life. Examples include Northern Hemisphere birds flying south for winter, [[Wildebeest|wildebeest]] moving annually to seasonal grazing, and young [[Salmon|Atlantic salmon]] leaving the river of their birth. Migration may be obligate, when all individuals migrate, or facultative. Birds may use a [[Circadian rhythm|circadian rhythm]] together with annual clocks to orient in time and space. Before its causes were understood, folklore held that [[Goose|barnacle geese]] grew from goose barnacles.

=== KEY: Animal_rights_55d4a0
HEADWORD: Animal rights
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Argentina | Suffering
ENTRY:
Philosophy holding that many or all sentient animals have moral worth independent of their utility to humans. Advocates argue that animals' basic interests, such as avoiding [[Suffering|suffering]], deserve the same consideration as similar human interests. In a narrower sense, animal rights are fundamental rights to life, liberty and freedom from torture that may not be overridden by aggregate welfare. Many advocates oppose assigning moral value on the basis of species membership alone. Some legal scholars support extending basic legal rights and personhood to nonhuman animals, most often great apes; [[Argentina]] granted captive orangutans basic human rights in 2014. Critics such as Roger Scruton argue that only humans have duties, and therefore only humans have rights. Utilitarian critics argue that animals may be used as resources so long as there is no unnecessary suffering.

=== KEY: Animation_d6b6b6
HEADWORD: Animation
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Computer animation | Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (film) | Traditional animation | Walt Disney | Who Framed Roger Rabbit
ENTRY:
Filmmaking technique in which pictures are created or manipulated and played in sequence to create the illusion of moving images.

In traditional animation, images are drawn or painted by hand on transparent celluloid sheets (cels) and photographed. Most animation is either [[Traditional animation|traditional]] or [[Computer animation|computer animation]] made with computer-generated imagery (CGI); stop motion, including claymation, is also prominent. Animation is contrasted with live action, though many films combine the two. Computer animation is usually based on programming paths between key frames to move digitally created figures through digitally created environments.

The illusion has traditionally been attributed to persistence of vision and later to the phi phenomenon and beta movement, though the exact neurological causes remain uncertain. A rapid succession of minimally different images with unnoticeable interruptions produces a stroboscopic effect.

An animated cartoon is an animated film, usually short, in an exaggerated style inspired by comic strips and gag cartoons. In 1988 the hybrid film *[[Who Framed Roger Rabbit]]* popularized the term "toon" for cartoonishly drawn characters.

The word comes from Latin *animātiō*, "bestowing of life." Puppets, automata, shadow puppets, and magic lantern projections preceded cinema. Joseph Plateau's phenakistiscope (1833) introduced stroboscopic animation, later applied in the zoetrope (1866), flip book (1868), and praxinoscope (1877). Émile Cohl's *Fantasmagorie* (1908) is cited as the first fully hand-drawn animated film, and Quirino Cristiani's *El Apóstol* (1917), now lost, as the earliest animated feature. [[Walt Disney]]'s *[[Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (film)|Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs]]* (1937) was the first commercially successful full-length cel-animated color feature. Synchronized sound arrived in the 1920s and computer-generated imagery from the 1990s.

=== KEY: Animism_d8ea08
HEADWORD: Animism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Indigenous peoples | Religion
ENTRY:
Worldview holding that natural phenomena, including non-human organisms, landforms and inanimate objects, possess spiritual essences, souls or personhood. It assumes that animals, plants, rivers, weather systems and other natural entities have some degree of agency, life and will. The term is used in the anthropology of [[Religion|religion]] for the beliefs of many [[Indigenous peoples]], in contrast to later organized religions. The term is an anthropological construct, and many indigenous peoples have no word for animism or even for religion. === KEY: Antarctica_44b439
HEADWORD: Antarctica
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Earth's southernmost and least-populated continent, lying almost entirely south of the [[Antarctic Circle]] and surrounded by the Southern Ocean.

It contains the geographic [[South Pole]] and is the fifth-largest continent, with an area of 14,200,000 sq km (5,500,000 sq mi), about 40% larger than Europe. Most of it is covered by the [[Antarctic ice sheet]], with an average thickness of 1.9 km (1.2 mi). About 70% of the world's freshwater reserves are frozen there; if melted, they would raise global sea levels by almost 60 m (200 ft).

It is the coldest, driest, and windiest continent on average, with the highest average elevation. It is mainly a polar desert, with annual precipitation of over 200 mm (8 in) along the coast and far less inland. The lowest temperature recorded on Earth, −89.2°C (−128.6°F), was measured there; coastal regions can exceed 10°C (50°F) in summer. Native animals include mites, nematodes, [[Penguin|penguins]], and seals; vegetation is mostly lichen or moss.

The ice shelves were probably first seen in 1820 during a Russian expedition led by Fabian Gottlieb von Bellingshausen and Mikhail Lazarev. The first confirmed landing was by a Norwegian team in 1895, and [[Roald Amundsen]] first reached the geographic South Pole in 1911.

The continent is governed under the 1959 Antarctic Treaty System, which prohibits military activity, mining, nuclear explosions, and nuclear waste disposal. About 5,000 people live at research stations in summer and around 1,000 in winter. Human activity affects the continent through pollution, [[Ozone depletion|ozone depletion]], and climate change.

The name derives from Greek *antarktikos*, "opposite to the Arctic"; it was adopted in the 1890s, with first use attributed to the cartographer John George Bartholomew.

=== KEY: Anthropology_4636f4
HEADWORD: Anthropology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archaeology | Biological anthropology | Cultural anthropology | Franz Boas | Linguistic anthropology | On the Origin of Species | Social anthropology
ENTRY:
Scientific study of humanity, concerned with human behavior, biology, cultures, societies, and language, in both the present and the past.

The discipline crosses biology and sociology. It is traditionally divided into four subfields. [[Social anthropology]] studies patterns of behavior, and [[Cultural anthropology|cultural anthropology]] studies cultural meaning, including norms and values; the term sociocultural anthropology is commonly used today. [[Linguistic anthropology]] studies how language influences social life. [[Biological anthropology]] studies the biology and evolution of humans and their close primate relatives. [[Archaeology]], called the "anthropology of the past," examines physical remains; it is generally regarded as a branch of anthropology in North America and Asia, but in Europe as an independent discipline or a subfield of history or paleontology.

The term *anthropologia* appeared in Renaissance Germany in the works of Magnus Hundt and Otto Casmann, from Greek *ánthrōpos*, "human," and *lógos*, "study." In 1647 the Bartholins of the University of Copenhagen divided anthropology into anatomy and psychology. Anthropology grew out of the comparative methods of the early 19th century, and the publication of [[On the Origin of Species|Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*]] in the late 1850s encouraged applying evolution to the social sciences. Paul Broca, who discovered the speech center of the brain, founded the Société d'Anthropologie de Paris in 1859.

The four-field approach was pioneered in the United States, while European traditions are more specialized. [[Franz Boas]] advanced rigor in fieldwork and rejected racial determinism. Achievements include reconstructions of human dispersal from Africa. The field has faced controversies over ethics in participant observation, such as informed consent in studies of isolated groups. Debate continues over the relative weight given to cultural and biological explanations of human behavior.

=== KEY: Anti_reflective_coating_d90485

Optical coating applied to lenses, other optical elements and [[Solar cell|solar cells]] to reduce reflection. === KEY: Antibiotic_c63ed4
HEADWORD: Antibiotic
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander Fleming | Bacteria | Infection | Penicillin | Plasmid | Pneumonia | World Health Organization | World War II
ENTRY:
Antimicrobial substance active against [[Bacteria|bacteria]].

Antibiotics are the most important type of antibacterial agent against bacterial [[Infection|infections]] and are widely used in their treatment and prevention. They may kill bacteria or inhibit their growth. A limited number also have antiprotozoal activity, but they are not effective against viruses or fungi.

In normal medical usage, antibiotics such as [[Penicillin|penicillin]] are produced naturally by one microorganism fighting another, whereas non-antibiotic antibacterials such as sulfonamides and antiseptics are fully synthetic; both are included in antimicrobial chemotherapy. In current usage it applies to any medication that kills bacteria or inhibits their growth, whether or not a microorganism produced it.

Chemical analyses of Nubian skeletons from northern Sudan, dated AD 350–550, show high levels of tetracycline, probably from beer brewed with *Streptomyces*. Later evidence from Egypt, China, Serbia, Greece, and Rome shows moldy bread applied to infections. Synthetic antibacterial chemotherapy began in Germany with Paul Ehrlich in the late 1880s. [[Alexander Fleming]] discovered modern penicillin in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain developed it therapeutically in the early 1940s, enabling widespread use in [[World War II]]. Prontosil, the first sulfonamide and first systemically active antibacterial drug, was developed by a team led by Gerhard Domagk in 1932 or 1933 at Bayer Laboratories.

Antibiotics sharply reduced mortality from [[Pneumonia|pneumonia]], sepsis, and wound infections. Their overuse in healthcare, agriculture, and veterinary medicine has driven bacterial resistance through mutation and [[Plasmid|plasmid]] transfer. The [[World Health Organization]] calls antimicrobial resistance a "serious threat" that is "happening right now in every region of the world." Nearly 5 million deaths a year are associated with it; deaths directly attributable to it numbered 1.27 million in 2019.

=== KEY: Antibody_0dc28f

Large [[Protein|protein]], also called an immunoglobulin, used by the [[Immune system|immune system]] to identify and neutralize antigens such as those on [[Bacteria|bacteria]] and viruses. Each antibody recognizes one or more specific antigens. The paratope at the tip of each branch of the Y-shaped molecule binds a particular epitope, allowing antibodies to tag an antigen for attack by immune cells or to neutralize it directly, for example by blocking a part of a [[Virus|virus]] needed to invade a host cell. Antibodies are generally considered part of the adaptive immune system, and they are a manifestation of immunological memory.

Country in the [[Lesser Antilles]], comprising [[Antigua]], Barbuda and dozens of smaller islands (pop., 2024: 102,634). Its total area is 440 sq km (170 sq mi), and the country is mostly flat. Most of the population lives on Antigua, and the most populous city is St. John's. The country became independent from Britain on November 1, 1981, and is a [[Constitutional monarchy|constitutional monarchy]] with [[Charles III]] as head of state. It is a [[Unitary state|unitary state]] in which Barbuda has been administered by the Barbuda Council since 1976. The Labour Party has dominated national politics since 2014. Antigua and Barbuda is a high-income country and the most developed in the Caribbean by the [[Human Development Index]]. Tourism generates nearly 60% of GDP.

Ancient Greek hand-powered orrery, the oldest known analog computer. It could predict astronomical positions and [[Eclipse|eclipses]] decades in advance and track the four-year cycle of the [[Ancient Olympic Games]]. Its fragments were recovered in 1901 from a shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera and are held by the National Archaeological Museum in Athens. Estimates of its construction date range from about 87 BC to 205 BC, and the shipwreck is dated to approximately 70–60 BC. Its surviving bronze gears enabled it to model the Moon's irregular orbit, a motion a motion studied by the astronomer [[Hipparchus]], who may have been consulted in its construction.
