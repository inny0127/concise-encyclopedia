=== KEY: Alcoholic_beverage_4914ab
HEADWORD: Alcoholic beverage
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alcoholism | Central nervous system | Distillation | Rum | Vodka | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Drink containing ethanol, produced by yeast fermentation of sugars from grains, fruits, or other plant materials. Alcoholic beverages are typically divided into beers, wines, and spirits, with alcohol content usually between 3% and 50% by volume; fermented drinks such as beer and wine generally run from 4% to 15%, while distilled spirits such as [[Rum|rum]] and [[Vodka|vodka]] often exceed 40%. Production begins with making sugars available, then fermentation, and for spirits, [[Distillation|distillation]].

The oldest verifiable brewery, in a burial cave near Haifa, Israel, contained residue of 13,000-year-old beer, probably used in ritual feasts. Residues in pottery jars from Jiahu, in China's Henan province, show that a fermented drink of grape, hawthorn fruit, honey, and rice was made about 7000–5600 BC. The earliest evidence of winemaking, from about 6000 BC, comes from Georgia in the South Caucasus, and the earliest clear chemical evidence of barley beer, about 3500–3100 BC, from Godin Tepe in the Zagros Mountains of Iran. In Egypt wine was reserved for royalty and the elite, while beer was the drink of common people; Sumerian society regulated alcohol and used it in offerings to the gods.

Alcohol is a depressant of the [[Central nervous system|central nervous system]]. Low doses cause euphoria, reduced anxiety, and sociability; higher doses cause drunkenness, stupor, unconsciousness, or death. Long-term use can lead to [[Alcoholism|alcoholism]], physical dependence, cardiovascular disease, and several cancers, and alcohol is classified as a group 1 carcinogen. In 2023 the [[World Health Organization]] stated that health risk begins "from the first drop." Alcohol use is linked to about 2.6 million deaths a year.

About a third of humans drink alcohol, averaging about 5.5 liters of pure alcohol a year per adult. The industry exceeded $1.5 trillion in 2017. Most countries regulate production, sale, and consumption, and a few ban it; the temperance movement campaigns against it.

=== KEY: Alcoholism_9d3d0d
HEADWORD: Alcoholism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benzodiazepine | Cirrhosis | Dementia | Immune system | Pancreas | Support group | World Health Organization
ENTRY:
Continued drinking of alcohol despite the problems it causes. Also called alcohol use disorder, it is characterized by increased tolerance, physical dependence and withdrawal symptoms when drinking stops. Heavy long-term use can damage the brain, heart, liver, [[Pancreas|pancreas]] and [[Immune system|immune system]], and can cause [[Cirrhosis|cirrhosis]], [[Dementia|dementia]] and Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome. The [[World Health Organization]] estimated that 283 million people had alcohol use disorders in 2016. Genetics and environment are each thought to contribute equally, and a person with a parent or sibling with the disorder is 3–4 times more likely to develop it. Treatment may include [[Benzodiazepine|benzodiazepines]] during withdrawal, acamprosate or naltrexone to reduce cravings, and [[Support group|support groups]] such as Alcoholics Anonymous, which a 2020 review linked to higher abstinence than other clinical interventions.

=== KEY: Aldebaran_73a29b
HEADWORD: Aldebaran
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Red giant star, the brightest in the constellation [[Taurus (constellation)|Taurus]]. Aldebaran lies about 67 [[Light-year|light-years]] away and is typically the 14th-brightest star in the night sky. It is over six billion years old and has evolved off the [[Main sequence|main sequence]]; its radius is about 45 times that of the Sun, while its mass is about equal. Its name derives from the Arabic for 'the follower', because it seems to follow the [[Pleiades]]. It lies along the line of sight to the [[Hyades (star cluster)|Hyades cluster]] but is much older and unrelated to it. Aldebaran forms a [[Binary star|binary]] system with a much fainter companion, Aldebaran B.

=== KEY: Aldehyde_05261d
HEADWORD: Aldehyde
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Acetaldehyde | Carboxylic acid | Ethanol | Formaldehyde | Glucose | Ketone | Methanol
ENTRY:
Organic compound containing the functional group R–CH=O. The aldehyde carbon is bonded to oxygen by a double bond and to hydrogen and one other substituent, which makes the group polar. [[Formaldehyde|Formaldehyde]] and [[Acetaldehyde|acetaldehyde]] are soluble in water, and the volatile aldehydes have pungent odors. Large-scale production relies on the hydroformylation of alkenes and on the oxidation of [[Methanol|methanol]] and [[Ethanol|ethanol]]. Most sugars, including [[Glucose|glucose]], are derivatives of aldehydes. Aldehydes also contribute to the pleasant odors of essential oils, including cinnamaldehyde and vanillin. They are readily oxidized to [[Carboxylic acid|carboxylic acids]], which distinguishes them from [[Ketone|ketones]].

=== KEY: Alder_7480aa
HEADWORD: Alder
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Andes | Birch | Central America | Nitrogen | Photosynthesis
ENTRY:
Genus of about 35 species of mostly deciduous trees and shrubs in the birch family, Betulaceae. The genus *Alnus* is found throughout the north temperate zone, with a few species reaching [[Central America]] and the northern and southern [[Andes]]. Alders bear male and female catkins on the same plant. Unlike [[Birch|birches]], their female catkins are woody and open to release seeds, much like conifer cones. The largest species, red alder (*A. rubra*) of the west coast of North America and black alder (*A. glutinosa*) of Europe, reach over 30 m (100 ft). Alders form root nodules with the nitrogen-fixing bacterium *Frankia alni*, which absorbs nitrogen from the air and makes it available to the tree, receiving sugars from [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] in return. Red alder stands supply between 130 and 320 kilograms per hectare (120 and 290 pounds per acre) of [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]] to the soil each year.

Infinite cardinal number used to measure the size of infinite sets, introduced by [[Georg Cantor]]. The smallest is ℵ₀ (aleph-null), the [[Cardinality|cardinality]] of the [[Natural number|natural numbers]] and of any countably infinite set. The next is ℵ₁, the smallest cardinality greater than ℵ₀. === KEY: Aleppo_369bf1
HEADWORD: Aleppo
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amorites | Cairo | Constantinople | Ottoman Empire | Silk Road | Suez Canal | Syrian civil war
ENTRY:
City in northern Syria, capital of Aleppo Governorate (pop., 2021: 2,098,000). It is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world, possibly occupied since the sixth millennium BC. It is first mentioned in cuneiform tablets from the late third millennium BC, when it was part of the [[Amorites|Amorite]] state of Yamhad. Its location at one end of the [[Silk Road]] made it a trading center between the Mediterranean and Mesopotamia. For centuries it was the third-largest city of the [[Ottoman Empire]], after [[Constantinople]] and [[Cairo]]. The opening of the [[Suez Canal]] in 1869 diverted much trade to sea, starting a slow decline. After World War I, Aleppo lost its northern hinterland to Turkey. In the [[Syrian civil war]], many parts of the city suffered massive destruction, and an estimated 31,000 people were killed in Aleppo during the conflict.

Italian chemist and physicist, pioneer of electricity. Volta is credited with inventing the [[Electric battery|electric battery]] in the form of the voltaic pile, which he reported in a two-part letter to the president of the Royal Society in London, published in 1800. The device showed that electricity could be generated chemically, disproving the view that it came only from living beings, and it helped found [[Electrochemistry|electrochemistry]]. The SI unit of electric potential, the [[Volt|volt]], is named after him.

=== KEY: Algae_ac9b27
HEADWORD: Algae
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charophyta | Cyanobacteria | Diatom | Kelp | Plankton | Red algae | Seaweed
ENTRY:
Diverse group of photosynthetic organisms that excludes the land plants. Algae range from microscopic unicellular microalgae, including [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] and phytoplankton, to [[Seaweed|seaweeds]], multicellular macroalgae that may grow up to 50 m (160 ft) in length. Most are aquatic, especially marine, and most are [[Plankton|plankton]] carried passively by water. Freshwater forms include [[Charophyta]] such as the filamentous *Spirogyra*. Most algae are single-celled and lack roots, leaves, or stems.

Algae are polyphyletic, having multiple evolutionary origins. Chlorophytes, [[Red algae|red algae]], and glaucophytes have primary chloroplasts derived directly from endosymbiont cyanobacteria, while [[Diatom|diatoms]], euglenoids, and brown algae have secondary chloroplasts derived indirectly from endosymbiont red or green algae. Algae contain chlorophyll a as their primary photosynthetic pigment, and their photosynthetic machinery, ultimately derived from cyanobacteria, produces oxygen by splitting water molecules. Most are photoautotrophs and the main primary producers of aquatic ecosystems; some are mixotrophs, and some unicellular algae are heterotrophs or parasites. Oceanic phytoplankton generate roughly half of Earth's atmospheric oxygen. Fossilized filamentous algae from the Vindhya basin have been dated to 1.6 to 1.7 billion years ago. Only the reds, browns, and some chlorophytes form three-dimensional multicellular bodies; the brown [[Kelp|kelps]] reach 50 m. Certain species form harmful blooms that deplete oxygen and release toxins.

Seaweed farming has existed for thousands of years and is strongly rooted in East Asian food cultures. Modern uses include cattle feed, bioremediation, algae fuels, and medical and scientific applications. The study of algae is called phycology.

=== KEY: Algebra_8f4e7c
HEADWORD: Algebra
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstract algebra | Al-Khwarizmi | Algebraic structure | Arithmetic | Brahmagupta | Elementary algebra | Linear algebra | Variable (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics that studies abstract systems, known as [[Algebraic structure|algebraic structures]], and the manipulation of expressions within those systems. Algebra generalizes [[Arithmetic|arithmetic]] by introducing variables and algebraic operations other than the standard arithmetic operations, such as addition and multiplication.

An algebraic structure is a non-empty set of mathematical objects, such as the integers, together with operations defined on that set. Arithmetic studies operations in a particular domain of numbers, such as the real numbers. Elementary algebra is the first level of abstraction above it; abstract algebra is a higher level, not limited to a particular domain; and universal algebra is more abstract still, investigating the characteristics of algebraic structures in general.

[[Elementary algebra]], also called school algebra, is the oldest and most basic form. It uses [[Variable (mathematics)|variables]], symbols for unspecified or unknown quantities, to express general laws, such as the commutative property of multiplication, a × b = b × a. Algebraic expressions combine variables and numbers, as in 5x + 3. An equation states that two expressions are equal. Identity equations, such as 2x + 5x = 7x, are true for all values of the variables; conditional equations are true only for some values, as x² = 4 is true if x is either 2 or −2. Elementary algebra seeks the values for which a statement is true by transforming equations to isolate variables. [[Linear algebra]] is a closely related field that investigates linear equations and systems of them, and supports applications in data analysis and quantum mechanics.

[[Abstract algebra]] studies algebraic structures, which consist of a set of objects with one or several operations. It allows objects other than numbers and non-arithmetic operations, and distinguishes types of structures, such as groups, rings, and fields, by the number of operations they use and the laws, called axioms, they follow. Universal algebra and category theory provide general frameworks for studying classes of structures.

Algebraic methods were first studied in the ancient period to solve specific problems in fields like geometry; Babylonian clay tablets from around 1800 BC solved quadratic equations by geometric methods. [[Brahmagupta]] established rules for zero, negative quantities, and operations on variables. In the 9th century the Persian mathematician Muhammad ibn Musa [[Al-Khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]] systematized algebra as an independent discipline, distinct from geometry. The word comes from the Arabic *al-jabr*, which originally referred to bonesetting; he used it in the title of his treatise *The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing*. The word entered English in the 16th century. Equations were described in words and abbreviations until the 16th and 17th centuries, when a rigorous symbolic formalism developed. In the mid-19th century algebra broadened beyond a theory of equations to cover diverse operations and structures.

=== KEY: Algebraic_number_field_b1dcc1
HEADWORD: Algebraic number field
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic number theory | Field (mathematics) | Rational number
ENTRY:
Finite-degree extension of the [[Rational number|rational numbers]]. A [[Field (mathematics)|field]] K containing ℚ, and of finite dimension as a vector space over ℚ, is called a number field; that dimension is its degree. The study of such fields is the central topic of [[Algebraic number theory]], which uses algebraic methods to reveal hidden structures behind the rational numbers. The rationals themselves are the smallest and most basic example. The Gaussian rationals, ℚ(i), with elements a + bi for rational a and b, are the first historically nontrivial example and have dimension two over ℚ. For any square-free integer d, the quadratic field ℚ(√d) is obtained by adjoining the square root of d. The cyclotomic field ℚ(ζₙ), formed by adjoining a primitive n-th root of unity, has dimension φ(n) over ℚ, where φ is the Euler totient function. Unlike the integers, the ring of algebraic integers in a number field need not be a unique factorization domain.

=== KEY: Algebraic_number_theory_57ad9e
HEADWORD: Algebraic number theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstract algebra | Carl Friedrich Gauss | Diophantine equation | Diophantus | Fermat's Last Theorem | Number theory | Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet | Pierre de Fermat
ENTRY:
Branch of [[Number theory|number theory]] that applies the techniques of [[Abstract algebra|abstract algebra]] to the integers, the rational numbers and their generalizations. Questions are expressed through algebraic objects such as algebraic number fields, their rings of integers, finite fields and function fields. Properties such as unique factorization and the behavior of ideals can resolve questions such as the existence of solutions to [[Diophantine equation|Diophantine equations]]. Its origins lie in the work of the 3rd-century Alexandrian mathematician [[Diophantus]]. [[Pierre de Fermat]] conjectured [[Fermat's Last Theorem]] in 1637, and no successful proof was published until 1995; the unsolved problem stimulated the field's development in the 19th century. [[Carl Friedrich Gauss|Gauss]]'s *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae*, written in 1798 and published in 1801, brought earlier results into a systematic framework. In papers of 1838 and 1839, [[Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet|Dirichlet]] proved the first class number formula for quadratic forms.

=== KEY: Algebraic_structure_19fc5a
HEADWORD: Algebraic structure
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstract algebra | Category theory | Field (mathematics) | Vector space
ENTRY:
Nonempty set with operations obeying specified axioms. An algebraic structure consists of an underlying set, called the carrier set, a collection of operations on it, typically binary operations such as addition and multiplication, and a finite set of identities, called axioms, that these operations must satisfy. Structures can be built on others: a [[Vector space|vector space]] involves a [[Field (mathematics)|field]] of scalars and an operation of scalar multiplication. Common axioms include commutativity, x∗y = y∗x, and associativity, (x∗y)∗z = x∗(y∗z). The study of algebraic structures is commonly called [[Abstract algebra|abstract algebra]]. Universal algebra formalizes the general theory, and [[Category theory|category theory]] studies structures together with the homomorphisms between them.

=== KEY: Algebraic_topology_b992d8
HEADWORD: Algebraic topology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abstract algebra | Circle | Euclidean space | Fundamental group | Homology (mathematics) | Homotopy | Topological space
ENTRY:
Branch of mathematics using tools from [[Abstract algebra|abstract algebra]] to study topological spaces. Its basic goal is to find algebraic invariants that classify [[Topological space|topological spaces]] up to homeomorphism, though usually most classify them up to [[Homotopy|homotopy]] equivalence. Homotopy groups record the basic shape, or holes, of a space; the first, the [[Fundamental group]], records information about loops. [[Homology (mathematics)|Homology]] associates a sequence of abelian groups to a space, and cohomology, its algebraic dual, assigns invariants with a more refined structure. Results often concern global aspects of manifolds, such as Poincaré duality. In knot theory, a knot is an embedding of a [[Circle]] in three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]], and two knots are equivalent if one can be deformed into the other without cutting. Algebraic topology also gives a convenient proof that any subgroup of a free group is free.

HEADWORD: Algebraic variety
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic geometry | Complex number | Fundamental theorem of algebra
ENTRY:
Solution set of a system of polynomial equations, the central object of [[Algebraic geometry|algebraic geometry]]. Classically, it is defined over the real or [[Complex number|complex numbers]]. Definitions differ: some require irreducibility, meaning the set is not the union of two smaller closed sets in the Zariski topology, and call non-irreducible sets algebraic sets. Varieties of dimension one are algebraic curves, and those of dimension two are algebraic surfaces. The [[Fundamental theorem of algebra|fundamental theorem of algebra]] links algebra and geometry, since a monic polynomial in one variable is determined by its roots in the complex plane. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz generalizes this, establishing a correspondence between ideals of polynomial rings and algebraic sets. In modern scheme theory, an algebraic variety over a field is an integral scheme of finite type over that field.

Country in the Maghreb region of North Africa; capital and largest city, [[Algiers]]. Officially the People's Democratic Republic of Algeria, it borders Tunisia, Libya, Niger, Mali, Mauritania, Western Sahara, Morocco, and the Mediterranean Sea. It spans 2,381,741 sq km (919,595 sq mi), the largest area of any African country, and has a population of 47 million, concentrated in the fertile, mountainous north; the [[Sahara]] covers most of the country. Algeria is a semi-presidential republic; critics cite strong military influence and restrictions on dissent. It has the highest [[Human Development Index]] in continental Africa, the third-largest economy in Africa, and the continent's largest military budget. Hydrocarbons account for roughly 30% of GDP, 60% of budget revenues, and nearly 95% of exports.

1954–1962 war of Algerian independence from [[France]]. === KEY: Algorithm_4afa80
HEADWORD: Algorithm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Khwarizmi | Al-Kindi | Computer science | Donald Knuth | Euclidean algorithm | Geoffrey Chaucer | Heuristic | Sieve of Eratosthenes
ENTRY:
Finite sequence of rigorous instructions, used in mathematics and [[Computer science|computer science]] to solve a class of problems or perform a computation.

An algorithm can be expressed in a finite amount of space and time and in a well-defined formal language. The instructions proceed from an initial state through a finite number of well-defined states, eventually producing output and terminating; randomized algorithms incorporate random input. By contrast, a [[Heuristic|heuristic]] is a problem-solving approach that may not guarantee correct or optimal results. A program is an algorithm only if it stops eventually. In *The Art of Computer Programming*, [[Donald Knuth]] specified five properties: finiteness, definiteness, input, output, and effectiveness.

Step-by-step procedures are recorded in Babylonian mathematics (around 2500 BC), Egyptian mathematics (around 1550 BC), and Indian, Greek, Chinese, and Arabic mathematics. A Sumerian tablet from Shuruppak, c. 2500 BC, describes the earliest division algorithm. The [[Euclidean algorithm]] was first described in Euclid's *Elements* (c. 300 BC), and the [[Sieve of Eratosthenes]] in Nicomachus's *Introduction to Arithmetic*. In the 9th century [[Al-Kindi]] gave the first description of cryptanalysis by frequency analysis, the earliest codebreaking algorithm.

The word derives from the Latinized name of the Persian scholar [[Al-Khwarizmi|al-Khwarizmi]], who around 825 AD wrote a book on Indian computation; its 12th-century Latin translations began *Dixit Algoritmi*, "Thus spoke Al-Khwarizmi." In English, *algorism* meant place-value calculation and occurs c. 1225; [[Geoffrey Chaucer|Chaucer]] used a variant in the late 14th century. Under the influence of Greek *arithmos*, "number," the Latin became *algorithmus* in the 15th century, and Thomas Hood used *algorithm* in English by 1596.

=== KEY: Ali_7a9b46

Fourth Rashidun caliph (r. 656–661) and first [[Shia Islam|Shia]] Imam, cousin and son-in-law of the prophet [[Muhammad]]. Ali was raised in Muhammad's household and was among the first to accept his teachings; the order relative to [[Abu Bakr]] is debated among Shia and Sunni scholars. In 622 he stayed behind in Mecca as Muhammad's decoy when Muhammad escaped to Medina, where Muhammad swore a pact of brotherhood with him and gave him his daughter [[Fatima]] in marriage.

Shia Muslims hold that it invested Ali with Muhammad's religious and political authority; Sunni Muslims regard it as a statement of friendship. After Muhammad died in 632, a group of Muslims met in Ali's absence and appointed Abu Bakr as leader. Ali withdrew from public life under Abu Bakr and [[Umar]], and later criticized [[Uthman]].

After Uthman was assassinated in 656, the people of Medina urged Ali to lead, and after initial hesitation he accepted. Talha, Zubayr, and Muhammad's widow [[Aisha]], who sought to avenge Uthman, were defeated at the Battle of the Camel in 656. Mu'awiya fought him at the inconclusive Battle of Siffin in 657, and the ensuing arbitration alienated the Kharijites, whom he crushed in 658. In 661, the Kharijite Ibn Muljam assassinated him. His son Hasan abdicated that year, and Mu'awiya founded the [[Umayyad Caliphate]].

Sunni Muslims regard Ali as the last of the rightly guided caliphs; Shia Muslims venerate him as the first Imam and rightful successor to Muhammad. His shrine in Najaf, Iraq, is a major Shia pilgrimage destination.

=== KEY: Alkali_metal_dd8141

ENTRY:
Group of highly reactive metallic elements in Group 1 of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. The alkali metals are [[Lithium|lithium]], sodium, potassium, rubidium, cesium and [[Francium|francium]]. They are soft, shiny metals that readily lose their outermost electron to form +1 ions, and their similar properties show clear group trends. They tarnish rapidly in air and must be stored under oil; cesium is the most reactive metal. All of them react with water, the heavier ones more vigorously. Sodium is the most abundant in nature, while francium is so radioactive that only minute traces occur. Rubidium and cesium are used in [[Atomic clock|atomic clocks]], and cesium clocks form the basis of the second. [[Sodium chloride|Table salt]] has been used since antiquity, and lithium is used in batteries. Sodium, potassium and possibly lithium are essential elements.

Any of six chemical elements in group 2 of the periodic table. Calcium and magnesium are among the most abundant elements in the Earth's crust.

Unsaturated [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbon]] with one or more carbon–carbon double bonds. Allegory in Book VII of [[Plato]]'s *Republic*. Plato presents it to compare the effect of [[Education|education]] and its lack on human nature. Prisoners chained since childhood face a cave wall and see only shadows cast by objects carried past a fire behind them; they take the echoing voices of the carriers to belong to the shadows. A prisoner freed from the cave comes to understand the true source of the images, and eventually the sunlit world outside. [[Socrates]] narrates the allegory in a dialogue with Glaucon.

=== KEY: Allergy_392226
HEADWORD: Allergy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adrenaline | Aspirin | Asthma | Atopic dermatitis | Penicillin | Pollen
ENTRY:
Exaggerated immune response in which the body mistakenly identifies an ordinarily harmless substance, the allergen, as a threat. Allergic reactions give rise to hay fever, allergic conjunctivitis, allergic [[Asthma|asthma]], [[Atopic dermatitis|atopic dermatitis]], food allergies, and anaphylaxis. Symptoms include an itchy rash, sneezing, coughing, a runny nose, shortness of breath, or swelling. Common allergens include [[Pollen|pollen]], certain foods, metals, insect stings, medications, and latex.

Allergies develop through genetic and environmental factors. In the reaction, immunoglobulin E antibodies (IgE) bind to an allergen and then to a receptor on mast cells or basophils, which release inflammatory chemicals such as histamine. Insect stings, foods, and medicines such as [[Aspirin|aspirin]] and [[Penicillin|penicillin]] can produce anaphylaxis, a systemic reaction that may affect the skin, digestive, respiratory, and circulatory systems and can include low blood pressure, coma, and death.

Diagnosis is typically based on medical history. Skin or blood tests may help, but positive results do not necessarily indicate a significant allergy. Treatment includes avoiding known allergens and using medications such as steroids and antihistamines. Severe reactions call for injectable [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]] (epinephrine). Allergen immunotherapy, which gradually exposes people to larger amounts of allergen, is useful for some allergies, such as hay fever and reactions to insect bites; its use in food allergy is unclear.

In the developed world about 20% of people are affected by allergic rhinitis, food allergy affects 10% of adults and 8% of children, and about 20% have or have had atopic dermatitis. Depending on the country, about 1–18% of people have asthma, and anaphylaxis occurs in 0.05–2%. Rates of many allergic diseases appear to be increasing. Clemens von Pirquet first used the word "allergy" in 1906.

Military coalition that opposed the [[Axis powers]] from 1939 to 1945. Its principal members were the 'Big Four': the [[United Kingdom]], the [[Soviet Union]], the [[United States]] and the [[Republic of China (1912–1949)|Republic of China]]. Formally called the [[United Nations]] from 1942, when 26 countries signed the Declaration by United Nations, the coalition was joined by many more countries. The Allies became the basis of the United Nations after the war.

=== KEY: Alligator_2f397f
HEADWORD: Alligator
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Large reptile in the genus *Alligator*, order Crocodilia. Two species survive: the American alligator (*A. mississippiensis*) and the Chinese alligator (*A. sinensis*). Alligators first appeared in the late [[Eocene]] about 37 million years ago. Alligators are native only to the United States and China. The American alligator ranges across the southeastern United States, with the largest populations in [[Florida]] and [[Louisiana]], each with over a million animals. Adult American alligators average 360 kg (790 lb) and 4 m (13 ft), and the largest recorded, found in Louisiana, measured 5.84 m (19.2 ft). The Chinese alligator is smaller, rarely exceeding 2.1 m (7 ft). Alligators commonly live up to 50 years. Southern Florida is the only place where alligators and [[Crocodile|crocodiles]] coexist.

=== KEY: Alligator_snapping_turtle_6b7ee5
HEADWORD: Alligator snapping turtle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alligator | Chicago | Common snapping turtle | United States
ENTRY:
Large species of freshwater turtle, the largest in North America. Its name refers to its powerful jaws and to the shell ridges that resemble [[Alligator|alligator]] skin; it is also called the loggerhead snapper. It is endemic to freshwater habitats of the [[United States]], from the Florida Panhandle west to East Texas. It is often associated with the [[Common snapping turtle]], which is not closely related. Three rows of spikes on the carapace flatten with age. A specimen at the Shedd Aquarium in [[Chicago]] weighed 113 kg (249 lb). Populations have declined through commercial harvesting and habitat degradation, and the species is classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN.
