Family of small ray-finned fishes, Gasterosteidae, with a Holarctic distribution in fresh, brackish and marine waters. === KEY: Stiffness_f0144f
HEADWORD: Stiffness
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Elastic modulus | Force | Newton (unit) | Radian
ENTRY:
Resistance of an object to deformation in response to an applied [[Force|force]]; its inverse is flexibility or compliance. For a body with a single degree of freedom, stiffness is the force divided by the displacement it produces along the same direction, k = F/δ, usually defined under quasi-static conditions. In SI units it is measured in [[Newton (unit)|newtons]] per meter (N/m), while rotational stiffness is measured in newton-meters per [[Radian|radian]]. Stiffness is a property of a structure or component and depends on its physical dimensions, whereas the [[Elastic modulus|elastic modulus]] is a property of the constituent material. Shear and torsional stiffness are further measures.

=== KEY: Stoicism_0f0821
HEADWORD: Stoicism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Athens | Marcus Aurelius | Virtue
ENTRY:
Hellenistic philosophy and practical guide to living, founded by Zeno of Citium in [[Athens]] around 300 BCE. Stoics believed the universe operates according to reason, or *logos*, and held that [[Virtue|virtue]] is the highest good and sufficient for a well-reasoned life. The philosophy is traditionally divided into logic, physics and ethics. It has since been revived in Renaissance Neostoicism and has inspired modern cognitive therapies.

=== KEY: Stoma_f2d0b4
HEADWORD: Stoma
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Liverwort | Monocotyledon | Photosynthesis | Transpiration
ENTRY:
Pore in the epidermis of plant leaves and stems, bordered by a pair of guard cells that regulate its opening. Carbon dioxide used in [[Photosynthesis]] and oxygen used in respiration pass through stomata by diffusion, while water vapor leaves through them in [[Transpiration]]. Plants therefore cannot take in carbon dioxide without losing water. Stomata occur in the sporophyte of most land plants, though not in [[Liverwort|liverworts]]. Most tree species have them only on the lower leaf surface, whereas [[Monocotyledon|monocotyledons]] such as onion, oat and maize often have about the same number on both surfaces. Many desert plants that use crassulacean acid metabolism open their stomata at night, when water evaporates more slowly, to reduce water loss. Size varies by species, but lengths range from 10 to 80 μm end to end.

=== KEY: Stone_Age_7c3801
HEADWORD: Stone Age
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australopithecus | Bronze Age | Chalcolithic | Ethiopia | Homo erectus | Mesolithic | Neolithic | Paleolithic
ENTRY:
Broad prehistoric period during which stone was widely used to make tools with an edge, a point, or a percussion surface. It lasted roughly 3.4 million years and ended between 4000 BC and 2000 BC with the advent of metalworking, and it spans over 99% of human history. The smelting of copper marks the end of the period; in Western Asia this occurred by about 3000 BC, when bronze became widespread, ushering in the [[Bronze Age]].

Tools were made by modern humans, earlier *Homo* species, and possibly [[Australopithecus]] and *Paranthropus*. The Stone Age is the first period of the three-age system used in archaeology, followed by the Bronze Age and Iron Age. It is commonly divided into the [[Paleolithic]], a transitional [[Mesolithic]] with finer tools, and the [[Neolithic]], when people first moved from hunter-gatherer life to settled towns and villages as agriculture spread. The Neolithic usually overlaps with the [[Chalcolithic]] ("Copper") era.

The Stone Age coincides with the evolution of the genus *Homo*. The cradle of the genus appears to be the East African Rift System, especially in [[Ethiopia]]. [[Homo erectus]] found an ecological niche as a tool-maker on the grasslands of the rift.

The oldest indirect evidence of stone tool use is animal bones with tool marks, 3.4 million years old, from the Lower Awash Valley in Ethiopia. The oldest stone tools, from Lomekwi 3 in West Turkana, Kenya, are 3.3 million years old. In July 2018 scientists reported what may be, if confirmed, the oldest known stone tools outside Africa, at 2.12 million years old, in China.

Prehistoric [[Megalith|megalithic]] monument on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England. Added to the list of [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Sites in 1986, it is one of the most famous landmarks in the United Kingdom.

=== KEY: Storm_fdcf3c
HEADWORD: Storm
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Blizzard | Dust storm | Great Red Spot | Lightning | Low-pressure area | Thunderstorm | Tornado | Tropical cyclone
ENTRY:
Disturbed state of the natural environment or of the atmosphere of an astronomical body. A storm may be marked by strong wind, tornadoes, hail, thunder and lightning, heavy precipitation, or wind carrying material through the air, among other forms of severe weather. Its forms include the [[Thunderstorm]], the [[Blizzard|blizzard]], the ice storm, the [[Tropical cyclone|tropical cyclone]], and the [[Dust storm|dust storm]].

Storms form when a center of low pressure develops within a surrounding system of high pressure. This combination of opposing forces can create winds and storm clouds such as cumulonimbus. Small, localized [[Low-pressure area|low-pressure areas]] formed by hot air rising off hot ground produce smaller disturbances such as dust devils and whirlwinds.

Types are defined by their conditions. A blizzard combines gale-force winds, snow accumulating at a rate of at least 5 cm (2 in) per hour, and very cold conditions. Ice storms, among the most dangerous winter storms, need only 8 mm (0.31 in) of ice accumulation to start downing power lines and tree limbs. Thunderstorms occur throughout the world, most often in tropical rainforest regions, and individual storm clouds can measure 2–10 km across. A [[Tornado]] is a violent whirlwind storm occurring on land.

Storms can harm lives and property through storm surge, flooding, [[Lightning|lightning]], wildfires, and wind shear. Systems with significant rainfall and duration help relieve drought, and heavy snowfall enables recreation such as skiing. Storms also occur on other bodies; the [[Great Red Spot]] of Jupiter and the dust storms of Mars are examples. The English word comes from Proto-Germanic *sturmaz*, meaning "noise, tumult."

Strait connecting the [[Atlantic Ocean]] to the [[Mediterranean Sea]] and separating [[Europe]] from [[Africa]]. Navigable sea route in southern Chile separating mainland [[South America]] from the [[Tierra del Fuego]] archipelago. === KEY: Stroke_8e763d
HEADWORD: Stroke
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Air pollution | Aneurysm | Aspirin | Atrial fibrillation | Brain | CT scan | Diabetes | Smoking
ENTRY:
Medical condition in which blood flow to part of the brain is reduced or blocked, causing cell death; also called cerebrovascular accident. The two main types are ischemic, due to lack of blood flow, and hemorrhagic, due to bleeding. About 87% of strokes are ischemic. Both types cause parts of the [[Brain|brain]] to stop functioning properly.

Signs include facial drooping, inability to walk, move, or feel on one side of the body, problems understanding or speaking, dizziness, or loss of vision. A hemorrhagic stroke may also bring a severe headache. If symptoms last less than 24 hours, the event is a transient ischemic attack (TIA), or mini-stroke. Symptoms of a stroke can be permanent, and long-term complications may include pneumonia and loss of bladder control.

High blood pressure is the biggest risk factor. Others include [[Smoking|smoking]], obesity, [[Diabetes|diabetes]], [[Atrial fibrillation|atrial fibrillation]], and [[Air pollution|air pollution]]. An ischemic stroke is typically caused by blockage of a blood vessel. A hemorrhagic stroke is caused by bleeding into the brain or into the space between its membranes, sometimes from a ruptured [[Aneurysm|aneurysm]]. Diagnosis rests on a physical exam and imaging such as a [[CT scan]].

Prevention includes reducing risk factors, surgery to open narrowed carotid arteries, anticoagulant medication in atrial fibrillation, and sometimes [[Aspirin|aspirin]] or statins. A stroke is a medical emergency. Ischemic strokes detected within three to four-and-a-half hours may be treated with clot-breaking medication, while hemorrhagic strokes sometimes benefit from surgery.

In 2023, 13.2 million strokes were recorded worldwide. Strokes were the second biggest cause of death, and some 105 million people were alive after a past stroke. Two thirds of cases occurred in those over 65.

=== KEY: Strong_interaction_5f11ab
HEADWORD: Strong interaction
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetism | Gluon | Murray Gell-Mann | Quantum chromodynamics | Quark | Standard Model | Weak interaction
ENTRY:
One of the four known fundamental interactions, binding quarks into hadrons and nucleons into atomic nuclei. Also called the strong force or strong nuclear force, it is studied in nuclear and particle physics. It confines [[Quark|quarks]] into protons, neutrons, and other hadrons; between nucleons it is called the nuclear force, or residual strong force.

Most of the mass of a proton or neutron results from strong-interaction energy; the individual quarks provide only about 1% of a proton's mass. At 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer), the strong force is approximately 100 times as strong as [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]], 10⁶ times as strong as the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]], and 10³⁸ times as strong as gravitation.

In 1964, [[Murray Gell-Mann]], and separately George Zweig, proposed that baryons and mesons are composed of elementary particles, which Gell-Mann called quarks. The strong attraction between nucleons was then understood as a side effect of a more fundamental force binding quarks together. The theory of [[Quantum chromodynamics]] (QCD), part of the [[Standard Model]], describes this force. Quarks carry a "color charge," unrelated to visible color. The force carrier is the [[Gluon|gluon]], a massless gauge boson that itself carries color charge, unlike the electromagnetic photon.

The force acts at two ranges. Below about 0.8 fm, gluons hold quarks together in hadrons; this is color confinement. Up to about 3 fm, mesons carry the force and bind nucleons into nuclei. Nuclear fusion accounts for most energy production in the Sun and other stars, and the nuclear force's energy is partly released in nuclear power and nuclear weapons.

The term is the counterpart to [[North Africa]], which is grouped with the Middle East and North Africa and with the [[Arab world]]. The Comoros, Djibouti, Mauritania, and Somalia, and sometimes Sudan, belong to the Arab League yet are geographically considered part of sub-Saharan Africa. Mauritania and Niger include only a band of the Sahel.

Since around 3900 BCE the harsh climate of the sparsely populated Sahara has separated the two regions, a barrier interrupted only by the [[Nile]] in Sudan, where navigation was blocked by the Sudd and the river's cataracts.

Critics have called the concept a racist construction that divides Africa, arguing that it ignores historical and cultural connections and trade among North, West, and East Africa. Medieval Arabic writers used *bilād as-sūdān* ("Land of the Blacks") for the Sudan region and Zanj for the [[Swahili]] coast of the southeast.

Early hominids adopted bipedal locomotion 10 million to 5 million years ago, as savanna spread. By 3 million years ago, several australopithecine species lived in Southern, East, and Central Africa.

=== KEY: Subatomic_particle_fb7bae
HEADWORD: Subatomic particle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electron | Elementary particle | Neutron | Proton | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Particle smaller than an atom. According to the [[Standard Model]] of particle physics, it is either a composite particle, such as a [[Proton|proton]] or [[Neutron|neutron]], made of other particles, or an [[Elementary particle|elementary particle]], such as an [[Electron|electron]], with no substructure. Particle physics and nuclear physics study these particles and their interactions.

The Standard Model's elementary particles are six flavors of quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom); six leptons (electron, electron neutrino, muon, muon neutrino, tau, tau neutrino); twelve gauge bosons, comprising the photon, the three W and Z bosons of the weak force, and the eight gluons of the strong force; and the Higgs boson. All have been found experimentally, the latest being the top quark (1995), tau neutrino (2000), and Higgs boson (2012).

Particles divide by spin into bosons, with integer spin, and fermions, with half-integer spin. All elementary fermions have spin 1/2 and are split into quarks, which carry color charge and feel the strong interaction, and leptons, which do not. Gauge bosons have spin 1, and the Higgs boson is the only elementary particle with spin zero. Photons and gluons have zero rest mass, while the W, Z, and Higgs bosons are massive, at approximately 80 GeV/c², 90 GeV/c², and 125 GeV/c² respectively.

Hadrons are composites of quarks bound by gluons, which never occur singly because of color confinement. Baryons contain an odd number of quarks, almost always 3. Mesons contain an even number, almost always one quark and one antiquark; pions and kaons are best known. Except for the proton and neutron, all hadrons are unstable and decay in microseconds or less.

=== KEY: Substance_chemistry_6cf1f1
HEADWORD: Substance
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Benzene | Chemical compound | Diamond | Mixture | Sugar
ENTRY:
Unique form of matter with constant chemical composition and characteristic properties. A chemical substance may be a single element or a [[Chemical compound|chemical compound]]. Substances that can be combined without reacting form a [[Mixture|mixture]]; if a mixture is separated to isolate one substance to a desired degree, that substance is chemically pure.

A substance can exist in different physical states, such as solid, liquid, gas, or plasma, without changing composition, and moves between them as temperature or pressure changes. Some substances can be combined or converted into new ones by chemical reactions; those that cannot are inert. Pure water is an example: its composition is always two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom (H₂O), a 2:1 ratio in every molecule, and it tends to boil near 100 °C (212 °F). Other examples are [[Diamond|diamond]], a form of the element carbon; table salt (NaCl), an ionic compound; and refined [[Sugar|sugar]] (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁), an organic compound.

Fields use the term differently. In inorganic chemistry, non-stoichiometric compounds such as palladium hydride violate the requirement of constant composition. In geology, inorganic solid substances of uniform composition are minerals, and combinations of two or more are rocks. In law, the term can include mixtures with a defined composition or manufacturing process.

The concept became firmly established in the late 18th century through work by Joseph Proust on pure compounds such as basic copper carbonate. He deduced that all samples of a compound have the same proportions by mass of their elements, now called the law of constant composition. Isomers, which have the same composition but different atomic arrangements, long troubled researchers; the structure of [[Benzene|benzene]] was eventually described by Friedrich August Kekulé.

=== KEY: Substance_dependence_72a4b5

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Need for a drug, the discontinuation of which produces withdrawal symptoms. Also called drug dependence, it is a biopsychological condition in which a person's functioning depends on repeated consumption of a psychoactive substance. Withdrawal, the body's reaction to abstinence, can involve physical symptoms, emotional and motivational symptoms, or both, and it promotes continued use. === KEY: Substitution_reaction_75844c

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Chemical reaction in which one functional group in a compound is replaced by another. Also called single displacement or single substitution, it is of prime importance in [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]], where reactions are classified as electrophilic or nucleophilic depending on the reagent. === KEY: Sugar_f0dc7a
HEADWORD: Sugar
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christopher Columbus | New World | Sucrose | Sugar beet | Sugarcane
ENTRY:
Class of sweet-tasting, soluble carbohydrates, many used in food. Simple sugars, or monosaccharides, include glucose, fructose, and galactose. Compound sugars, or disaccharides, are two bonded monosaccharides: [[Sucrose|sucrose]] (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose), and maltose (two glucose molecules). White sugar is almost pure sucrose. Longer saccharide chains, such as starch, are not regarded as sugars.

Sugars occur in the tissues of most plants. Honey and fruits are abundant natural sources. Sucrose is especially concentrated in [[Sugarcane|sugarcane]] and [[Sugar beet|sugar beet]], whose combined world production in 2016 was about two billion tonnes. Lactose occurs only in milk.

Sugar was first produced from sugarcane in the Indian subcontinent. The English word derives from Sanskrit *śarkarā*, through Persian *shakar*, Arabic *sukkar*, and Medieval Latin *succarum*; the Arabs introduced sugar into Europe through Sicily and Spain. In the mid-15th century it was introduced into Madeira and the Canary Islands and mass-produced there. [[Christopher Columbus]] brought it to the [[New World]], and sugar industries existed in Cuba and Jamaica by the 1520s. Beet sugar, the start of the modern sugar industry, was first produced industrially in 1801 in Cunern, Prussia. Demand drove colonization and industrialization of underdeveloped lands and was intimately associated with slavery. World consumption rose more than 100 times from 1850 to 2000.

Globally, a person consumes about 24 kg (53 lb) of sugar a year, up to 50 kg (110 lb) in the Americas and under 20 kg (44 lb) in Africa. Added sugar is associated with increased risk of obesity, diabetes, cardiovascular disorders, and tooth decay. In 2015 the World Health Organization recommended that free sugars be under 10% of total energy intake, and below 5% ideally.

=== KEY: Suharto_01ac85
HEADWORD: Suharto
PERSON: yes

ENTRY:
Indonesian military general and second president, 1967–1998. After troops under his command countered an attempted coup on September 30 and October 1, 1965, the army carried out a nationwide anti-communist purge. Suharto became acting president in 1967 and president the following year. When he took power, [[Inflation|inflation]] exceeded 650%; his economic advisers adopted free-market policies, and prices were stable by 1969. In 1975 he ordered the invasion of East Timor, followed by a 23-year occupation. His New Order regime was an [[Authoritarianism|authoritarian]] state built around a [[Cult of personality|cult of personality]], and it drew Western backing during the [[Cold War]]. GDP grew by about 7% a year, and in 1986 the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization awarded him the Ceres Medal for rice self-sufficiency. Suharto resigned in May 1998 amid the 1997 Asian financial crisis. He is praised for economic growth and stability and condemned for corruption and human rights abuses.

=== KEY: Sui_dynasty_db1e48
HEADWORD: Sui dynasty

ENTRY:
Chinese imperial dynasty that ruled from 581 to 618. The Sui reunified China after the [[Northern and Southern dynasties]] era, ending centuries of division since the War of the Eight Princes. Founded by [[Emperor Wen of Sui|Emperor Wen]], it laid much of the institutional foundation for the [[Tang dynasty]], which succeeded it. Reforms included an equal-field land system, a Five Departments and Six Boards administration, and standardized coinage; the Sui also promoted [[Buddhism]]. The capital moved from Daxing (modern [[Xi'an]]) to [[Luoyang]] in 605. Massive projects included the [[Grand Canal (China)|Grand Canal]], which linked Luoyang with the south, and the reconstruction of Luoyang, built with conscripted labor. Campaigns against Goguryeo in Korea failed by 614. Popular revolts followed, and Emperor Yang was assassinated in 618. The dynasty lasted only 37 years.

=== KEY: Suicide_a704d9
HEADWORD: Suicide
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mental disorder | Psychotherapy
ENTRY:
Act of intentionally causing one's own death. Risk factors include [[Mental disorder|mental disorders]], neurodevelopmental disorders, physical disorders, and substance abuse. Some suicides are impulsive acts driven by stress, relationship problems, or harassment and bullying. People who have attempted suicide before are at higher risk.

Prevention efforts reported as effective include limiting access to methods such as firearms, drugs, and poisons; treating mental disorders and substance abuse; careful media reporting; improving economic conditions; and [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]], primarily dialectical behavior therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy. Crisis hotlines, such as 988 in North America and 13 11 14 in Australia, are common, but their effectiveness has not been well studied.

Suicide accounts for more than 740,000 deaths per year, an estimated 1.1% of deaths worldwide in 2021, and is the third-leading cause of death among 15- to 29-year-olds. Nearly three-quarters (73%) occur in low- and middle-income countries. Rates are generally higher among men than women, from 1.5 times higher in the developing world to 3.5 times higher in the developed world, and are generally highest over age 70. An estimated 10 to 20 million non-fatal attempts occur each year.

Assisted suicide, in which one person helps another by providing advice or the means, is legal in many countries. Views on suicide have been shaped by religion, honor, and the meaning of life, and most religions disapprove of it. In samurai-era Japan, seppuku was respected as a way of making up for failure or as protest. Suicide and attempted suicide, once illegal, are no longer so in most Western countries but remain criminal offenses in some. Some advocacy groups and style guides recommend "died by suicide" over "committed suicide," arguing the latter implies a crime.
