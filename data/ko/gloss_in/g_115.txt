=== KEY: Ring_system_b68fc1
HEADWORD: Ring system
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Jupiter | Neptune | Ordovician | Roche limit | Saturn | Uranus
ENTRY:
Disk or torus of solid chunks of ice, rock and dust orbiting an astronomical object. Ring systems are also called planetary rings, and giant ring systems such as Saturn's may contain moonlets. All four giant planets, [[Jupiter]], [[Saturn]], [[Uranus]] and [[Neptune]], have ring systems, but Saturn's is the largest and most visible. Rings have also been detected around minor planets through occultations. Thicker planetary rings have been proposed to form from material inside the [[Roche limit]], from debris of a moon disrupted by a large impact, or from a moon torn apart by tidal stresses. Fainter rings can form when meteoroids strike moons. Some studies theorize that the Earth may have had a ring system during the mid-late [[Ordovician]] period. Phobos, a moon of Mars, is predicted to break up and form a ring in about 50 million years.

=== KEY: Ring_theory_4ef27b
HEADWORD: Ring theory
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algebraic geometry | Algebraic number theory | Commutative algebra | Commutative ring | Fermat's Last Theorem | Module (mathematics)
ENTRY:
Branch of algebra concerned with rings, algebraic structures in which addition and multiplication have properties similar to those of the integers. Ring theory studies the structure of rings, their representations (or, equivalently, [[Module (mathematics)|modules]]), and special classes such as group rings and division rings. [[Commutative ring|Commutative rings]] are much better understood than noncommutative ones. [[Algebraic geometry]] and [[Algebraic number theory]] supply many examples of commutative rings and have driven the development of [[Commutative algebra]], a major area of modern mathematics. Many results belong to all three fields: Hilbert's Nullstellensatz is fundamental to algebraic geometry, while [[Fermat's Last Theorem]] is stated in elementary arithmetic but proved with both algebraic number theory and algebraic geometry. Since the 1980s, noncommutative geometry and quantum groups have led to a better understanding of noncommutative rings, especially noncommutative Noetherian rings.

=== KEY: Rings_of_Saturn_2af9e4
HEADWORD: Rings of Saturn
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Christiaan Huygens | Galileo Galilei | Moons of Saturn
ENTRY:
Ring system around Saturn, the most extensive of any planet in the Solar System. It is made almost entirely of water ice with a trace of rocky material. [[Galileo Galilei]] first observed the rings in 1610 without recognizing them, and in 1655 [[Christiaan Huygens]] described them as a disk. Many gaps are cleared by embedded moons or lie at destabilizing resonances with [[Moons of Saturn]], while others remain unexplained. There is no consensus on when the rings formed: models suggested an early origin, but newer Cassini data indicate a more recent one.

=== KEY: Ritual_94f57e
HEADWORD: Ritual
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cult | Eric Hobsbawm | Marriage | Obsessive–compulsive disorder | Psychology | Religion | Worship
ENTRY:
Repeated, structured sequence of actions or behaviors that alters the internal or external state of an individual, group, or environment, regardless of conscious understanding, emotional context, or symbolic meaning. Rituals are a feature of all known human societies and also occur in non-human species, such as elephant mourning or corvid object-leaving. They include the [[Worship|worship]] rites and sacraments of organized [[Religion|religions]] and [[Cult|cults]], rites of passage, atonement and purification rites, oaths of allegiance, coronations, presidential inaugurations, [[Marriage|marriages]], and funerals; even hand-shaking and saying "hello" may be termed rituals. The English word derives from Latin *ritualis*, "that which pertains to rite (*ritus*)," and is first recorded in English in 1570.

Definitions in ritual studies conflict. Kyriakidis describes ritual as an outsider's, or "etic," category for activity that seems irrational or illogical to the observer; an insider, or "emic," performer may use the term to acknowledge that it can look so to the uninitiated. Catherine Bell argues that rituals are characterized by formalism, traditionalism, invariance, rule-governance, sacral symbolism, and performance. [[Eric Hobsbawm]] and Terrence Ranger have argued that many rituals presented as ancient, such as those of the British monarchy, took their actual form in the late nineteenth century.

In [[Psychology|psychology]], ritual is sometimes used in a technical sense for a repetitive behavior used to neutralize or prevent anxiety; it can be a symptom of [[Obsessive–compulsive disorder|obsessive–compulsive disorder]], though such behaviors are generally isolated activities. Some researchers report that rituals regulate emotion, enhance performance under stress, and strengthen group cohesion, and that secular forms, such as athletic pre-game routines or diplomatic protocols, share structural features with religious ones.

=== KEY: River_149529
HEADWORD: River
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aquifer | Dam | Drainage basin | Erosion | Meander | Water cycle
ENTRY:
Natural stream of fresh water that flows on land or inside caves toward another body of water at a lower elevation, such as an ocean, lake, or another river. A river may run dry before reaching its end or flow only in certain seasons. Rivers are regulated by the [[Water cycle|water cycle]]; water enters them through precipitation, runoff down a slope, melting of glaciers or snow, or seepage from [[Aquifer|aquifers]]. The headwaters are the smaller streams that feed a river, and all land that feeds it makes up its [[Drainage basin|drainage basin]], or watershed, usually separated from others by a ridge. Because groundwater feeds rivers where their elevation is lower than the water table, rivers can keep flowing during drought.

Rivers flow downhill under gravity and merge at confluences, where a tributary joins a larger river. They seldom run straight but [[Meander|meander]]. They carve rock and soil into canyons and valleys by [[Erosion|erosion]] of their beds, and where the flow slows, sediment, or alluvium, forms deltas and islands.

Rivers have sustained human and animal life for millennia, including the first civilizations, and provide food, transportation, drinking water, and recreation. Humans have engineered rivers to prevent flooding, irrigate crops, drive water wheels, and produce hydroelectricity from [[Dam|dams]]. People associate rivers with life and fertility, and with religious, political, social, and mythological meanings. Rivers are threatened by pollution, climate change, and human activity; dams, canals, and levees have eliminated habitats, caused the extinction of some species, and reduced the alluvium carried downstream. Regulation of pollution, dam removal, and sewage treatment have helped improve water quality and restore habitats.

=== KEY: River_delta_488b76
HEADWORD: River delta
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dam | Levee | Mississippi River | Nile
ENTRY:
Typically triangular landform formed where a river deposits its sediment as it enters a sea, lake, reservoir or estuary. The name comes from the shape of the [[Nile]] Delta, which approximates the triangular Greek letter delta (Δ). As a river expands into standing water it slows, and sediment settles out to build the delta. Over time a channel builds a deltaic lobe, such as the bird's-foot of the [[Mississippi River|Mississippi]], and the river may breach its natural [[Levee|levees]] to take a shorter, steeper course. Deltas are major agricultural and population centers, can provide coastal defense, and are important carbon sinks on geologic timescales. Many face threats from [[Dam|dams]] that reduce sediment supply, land subsidence and sea-level rise.

=== KEY: Riverboat_6a8c31
HEADWORD: Riverboat
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diesel engine | Ferry | Mississippi River | Missouri River | Ohio River
ENTRY:
Watercraft designed for inland navigation on lakes, rivers and artificial waterways. Riverboats are generally built as work boats for freight or passengers, or as luxury tour craft, and include dredgers, survey boats and fireboats. Because they do not have to withstand high winds or large waves, they are usually less sturdy than ocean vessels and can be built from light composite materials. Their size is limited by river width, depth and bridge height, and early paddle steamers on the [[Mississippi River]] could operate in water under two meters deep. A [[Ferry|ferry]] crosses a river, while a riverboat travels along it carrying passengers or cargo for revenue. The most famous riverboats operated on the [[Ohio River]] and [[Missouri River]]. Modern riverboats are usually screw-driven and powered by pairs of [[Diesel engine|diesel engines]].

=== KEY: Rivet_6cbf17
HEADWORD: Rivet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Bronze Age | Steel
ENTRY:
Semi-permanent mechanical fastener with a head on one end. Installation flattens the tail of the shaft, called the shop head or buck-tail, into a shape like the head, creating a second head. Because of these two heads a rivet supports tension loads, but it is much more capable of supporting shear loads. Solid rivets are among the oldest and most reliable fasteners, found at [[Bronze Age]] sites, and [[Ancient Egypt|Egyptian]] spearheads from the Naqada culture, dated between 4400 and 3000 BC, have rivet holes. High-strength bolts have largely replaced structural [[Steel]] rivets, partly because skilled workers are costly; riveting once required four skilled workers. Rivets may be hammered by hand, driven with pneumatic hammers, or squeezed with machines.

=== KEY: Road_d11aed
HEADWORD: Road
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Achaemenid Empire | Bactria | Controlled-access highway | India | OECD | Susa | Urban design
ENTRY:
Thoroughfare from one place to another, primarily used for movement of traffic, many of them paved. The words road and street are commonly considered interchangeable, but the distinction matters in [[Urban design|urban design]]: streets serve primarily local access, and stroads combine the features of streets and roads. Types include parkways, avenues, [[Controlled-access highway|controlled-access highways]] (freeways, motorways, and expressways), tollways, local roads, public roads, and private roads. Primary features include lanes, sidewalks (pavement), roadways (carriageways), medians, shoulders, verges, bike paths (cycle paths), and shared-use paths.

Definitions vary. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development ([[OECD]]) defines a road as a line of communication using a stabilized base other than rails or air strips, open to public traffic, primarily for road motor vehicles; this includes bridges, tunnels, junctions, interchanges, and toll roads but not cycle paths. The 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic defines a road as the entire surface of any way or street open to public traffic. In the United Kingdom, the Highways Act 1980 treats a road as any length of highway or other road to which the public has access, including bridges.

Many early roads were simply recognizable routes. By about 10,000 BC human travelers used rough pathways. The world's oldest known paved road was built in Egypt between 2600 and 2200 BC. The Sweet Track, a timber causeway in England built in winter 3807 BC or spring 3806 BC, is one of the oldest engineered roads discovered. In 500 BC, Darius I the Great began an extensive road system for the [[Achaemenid Empire]], including the Royal Road connecting Sardis to [[Susa]]; the system reached as far east as [[Bactria]] and [[India]].

=== KEY: Road_transport_3418ad

ENTRY:
Movement of people and goods on roads by bicycle, automobile, bus, truck, or by animals such as horses or oxen. === KEY: Robot_5d1eca
HEADWORD: Robot
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Machine, especially one programmable by computer, capable of automatically carrying out a complex series of actions. A robot may be guided by an external or internal control device. Robots may be humanoid, but most are task-performing machines prioritizing function over aesthetics. They range from humanoids such as Honda's ASIMO to industrial robots, medical operating robots, swarm robots, UAV drones such as the General Atomics MQ-1 Predator, and microscopic nanorobots. === KEY: Rock_geology_08258b
HEADWORD: Rock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earth's crust | Granite | Igneous rock | Metamorphic rock | Mineral | Petrology | Plate tectonics | Sedimentary rock
ENTRY:
Naturally occurring solid mass or aggregate of minerals or mineraloid matter, also called stone. Rocks are categorized by the [[Mineral|minerals]] included, their chemical composition, and how they formed. They make up the Earth's outer solid layer, the [[Earth's crust|crust]], and most of its interior, except for the liquid outer core and pockets of magma in the asthenosphere. [[Petrology]] studies the character and origin of rocks, and mineralogy their mineral components; the field may extend to planetary geology.

Silicate minerals account for about one-third of all known mineral species and about 95% of the earth's crust. Rocks are grouped into three classes, [[Igneous rock|igneous]], [[Sedimentary rock|sedimentary]], and [[Metamorphic rock|metamorphic]], and can be transformed from one type to another, as described by the rock cycle.

Igneous rocks form when magma cools in the crust or lava cools at the surface or seabed. Plutonic (intrusive) rocks cool slowly within the crust, as with [[Granite|granite]]; volcanic (extrusive) rocks reach the surface, forming pumice or basalt. Sedimentary rocks form by diagenesis and lithification of sediments produced by the weathering, transport, and deposition of existing rocks, as with limestone and sandstone. Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are transformed by high pressures and temperatures without significant melting, as with marble from limestone or slate from shale.

Humans have used rocks since the earliest times, from Stone Age tools to buildings and infrastructure, and mining extracts rocks and the metals within them. The study of rocks became a formal science in the 19th century; the discovery of radioactive decay in 1896 allowed radiometric dating, and understanding of [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]] developed in the second half of the 20th century.

=== KEY: Rocket_15e8bf
HEADWORD: Rocket
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Apollo 11 | Escape velocity | Hyder Ali | Jet engine | Napoleonic Wars | Rocket engine | Song dynasty | Wernher von Braun
ENTRY:
Elongated flying vehicle that accelerates by expelling high-speed exhaust and carries all of its own propellant.

A [[Rocket engine|rocket engine]] produces thrust by reaction to exhaust expelled at high speed. Unlike [[Jet engine|jet engines]], rockets need no oxygen from the air, so they can fly in the vacuum of space and operate more efficiently outside the atmosphere. Multistage rockets can reach [[Escape velocity|escape velocity]] from Earth. Chemical rockets, the most common high-power type, burn fuel with an oxidizer. The name comes from the Italian *rocchetta*, "bobbin" or "little spindle", after the shape.

Gunpowder rockets evolved in medieval China under the [[Song dynasty|Song dynasty]] by the 13th century, and the Mongols carried the technology to the Middle East and Europe in the mid-13th century. The Mysorean rockets of the late 18th century, developed under [[Hyder Ali]], were the first successful iron-cased rockets. Sir William Congreve's British rocket of 1804, based directly on them, was fielded in the [[Napoleonic Wars|Napoleonic Wars]]; together the innovations raised effective range from 100 to 2,000 yards (91 to 1,829 m).

Modern rocketry rests on Konstantin Tsiolkovsky's 1903 theory of liquid-propellant rockets and Robert Goddard's 1926 launch of the first liquid-fueled rocket, which reached 12.5 m. The German V-2, developed by [[Wernher von Braun]], reached 80 km during World War II. Postwar rockets launched Sputnik 1 in 1957 and, in 1969, the [[Apollo 11]] mission to the Moon. Rockets are now used for fireworks, missiles, ejection seats, launch vehicles, human spaceflight, and space exploration.

=== KEY: Rocket_engine_3d2552

Because it carries its own oxidizer, it can operate in a [[Vacuum|vacuum]], unlike most jet engines. Vehicles propelled by rocket engines include [[Missile|missiles]], artillery shells, ballistic missiles and space vehicles. Compared with other jet engines, rocket engines typically have the highest thrust but the lowest specific impulse, making them the least propellant-efficient, although some types, such as Hall-effect thrusters, are very efficient. Black powder, discovered by ninth-century Chinese alchemists, led to fire arrows, among the first rocket engines to leave the ground. Konstantin Tsiolkovsky first wrote about liquid-fueled rocket engines in the later 19th century.

=== KEY: Rocky_Mountains_4db62d
HEADWORD: Rocky Mountains
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: British Columbia | Colorado | Fur trade | Glacier | Lewis and Clark Expedition | Mackenzie River | Sierra Nevada
ENTRY:
Mountain range, western North America, the largest mountain system on the continent.

The Rockies stretch 3,000 mi (4,800 km) in a straight line from northern Western Canada to New Mexico in the southwestern United States, varying in width from 110 to 480 km (70 to 300 miles). They are the easternmost portion of the North American Cordillera and older than the Cascade Range and [[Sierra Nevada]], which lie farther west. Definitions of the northern end differ between Canada and the United States; it lies in northern [[British Columbia]] or farther north near the Alaska–Yukon border.

The system comprises dozens of subranges divided by high basins and wide tablelands. Most of the highest summits are in [[Colorado]]; Mount Elbert, at 4,401 m (14,440 ft), is the highest peak, and Mount Robson, at 3,954 m (12,972 ft), is highest in the Canadian Rockies. The Continental Divide of the Americas runs through the range, separating waters flowing to the Atlantic and Pacific. In Alberta, rivers feed the basin of the [[Mackenzie River]], which empties into the Beaufort Sea.

The Rockies formed 55 million to 80 million years ago during the Laramide orogeny, when plates began sliding at a shallow angle beneath the North American plate. Tectonic activity, erosion, and [[Glacier|glaciers]] have since sculpted the peaks and valleys. Humans began inhabiting the range at the end of the last ice age. The [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] and Sir Alexander Mackenzie explored it, and minerals and the [[Fur trade|fur trade]] drove early economic exploitation, though the population has never been dense. Parks protect much of the range, a popular destination for hiking and skiing.

=== KEY: Rodent_7dc618
HEADWORD: Rodent
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brown rat | Capybara | Eocene | House mouse | Lagomorpha | Madagascar
ENTRY:
Mammals of the order Rodentia, characterized by a single pair of continuously growing incisors in each jaw.

Rodents make up about 40% of all mammal species and are native to every major landmass except Antarctica. The name comes from Latin *rodens*, "gnawing". Most are small, with robust bodies, short limbs, and long tails, and most eat plant material. Enamel covers only the front of each incisor, so the softer dentine behind wears away faster and leaves a chisel-like edge; the animals must gnaw constantly to keep the teeth from growing so long that they pierce the skull. Rodents have no canines.

Size ranges from most species weighing under 100 g (3.5 oz) to the [[Capybara|capybara]], the largest, at up to 66 kg (146 lb). Rodents live in nearly every terrestrial habitat and can be arboreal, burrowing, leaping, or semiaquatic. Many are social.

The fossil record begins in the Paleocene of Asia, and rodents diversified greatly in the [[Eocene]]. They reached South America and [[Madagascar]] from Africa and, until humans arrived, were the only terrestrial placental mammals to colonize Australia. Rodentia and [[Lagomorpha]] (rabbits, hares, and pikas) are sister groups forming the clade Glires; lagomorphs are distinguished by an extra pair of upper incisors.

Humans use rodents for food, clothing, pets, and laboratory research. The [[Brown rat]], the black rat, and the [[House mouse]] are serious pests that spoil stored food and spread disease, and introduced rodents are often invasive. Familiar rodents include mice, rats, squirrels, beavers, guinea pigs, and hamsters.

American songwriting team of composer Richard Rodgers and lyricist-dramatist Oscar Hammerstein II. Their first collaboration, *Oklahoma!* (1943), integrated song and dance into the plot and helped launch the "golden age" of musical theater. Five of their Broadway shows, *Oklahoma!*, *Carousel*, *South Pacific*, *The King and I* and *The Sound of Music*, were outstanding successes, as was the television broadcast of [[Cinderella]] (1957). Their works earned 34 Tony Awards, 15 [[Academy Awards|Academy Awards]], two [[Pulitzer Prize|Pulitzer Prizes]] and two [[Grammy Awards|Grammy Awards]]. Most of their shows have been revived frequently around the world, by professional and amateur companies alike. Their partnership has been called the greatest of the 20th century.
