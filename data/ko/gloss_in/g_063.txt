=== KEY: Hubble_s_law_436dd5
HEADWORD: Hubble's law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Big Bang | Edwin Hubble | Expansion of the universe | Georges Lemaître | Physical cosmology | Redshift
ENTRY:
Observation in [[Physical cosmology|physical cosmology]] that galaxies recede from Earth at speeds proportional to their distance. A galaxy's recessional velocity is typically measured from its [[Redshift|redshift]]. Officially the Hubble–Lemaître law, it is attributed to [[Edwin Hubble]]'s work published in 1929, after [[Georges Lemaître]] had concluded in 1927 that the universe might be expanding. The proportionality constant is the Hubble constant, measured in km/s/Mpc: a galaxy 1 megaparsec (3.09×10¹⁹ km) away recedes at 70 km/s. Hubble combined Vesto Slipher's redshift velocities with Henrietta Swan Leavitt's distance methods. The law is considered the first observational basis for the [[Expansion of the universe|expansion of the universe]] and supports the [[Big Bang]] model.

=== KEY: Human_behavior_a39846
HEADWORD: Human behavior
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ethology | Neurology | Philosophy | Psychology | Social control | Social norm | Sociology | Twin
ENTRY:
Potential and expressed capacity of individuals or groups, mentally, physically, and socially, to respond to internal and external stimuli throughout life. Behavior is driven by environmental and genetic factors and, in part, by thoughts and feelings, which reveal attitudes and values.

The term covers many domains. Social behavior concerns actions directed at others and is shaped by interaction, culture, ethics, politics, and conflict; its acceptability depends on [[Social norm|social norms]] and is regulated by various means of [[Social control|social control]]. Cognitive behavior concerns obtaining and using knowledge, including learning, creativity, and belief. Physiological behavior maintains the body, and economic and ecological behavior concern work and interaction with the environment.

The study of human behavior is interdisciplinary. The social sciences, including [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Sociology|sociology]], and [[Ethology|ethology]], approach it alongside [[Neurology|neurology]] and evolutionary biology, which examine how the nervous system controls behavior and how the human mind evolved. [[Philosophy]] and literature treat the subject outside formal science, with philosophy of mind considering free will and the malleability of behavior. Methods include questionnaires, interviews, and experiments, and [[Twin|twin]] studies are a common way of separating genetic from environmental influences.

The nature versus nurture debate, over whether behavior is predominantly affected by genetic or environmental factors, is a fundamental division in the field. Twin and meta-analytic studies estimate average heritability across traits at 49%, while researchers increasingly emphasize complex interactions among biological, psychological, social, cultural, and environmental factors. The relative weight of deterministic biological causes against volitional or cultural ones remains a subject of controversy.

=== KEY: Human_body_b4bb53
HEADWORD: Human body
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Anatomy | Cell (biology) | Coronary artery disease | Heart | Organ (biology) | Physiology | Tissue (biology)
ENTRY:
Physical structure of the human organism, composed of many types of cells that together form tissues, then organs, and then organ systems. The external body consists of a head, neck, torso (including the thorax and abdomen), arms, hands, legs, and feet. The internal body includes organs, teeth, bones, muscles, tendons, ligaments, blood vessels and blood, and lymphatic vessels and lymph. It is studied through [[Anatomy|anatomy]], [[Physiology|physiology]], histology, and embryology by health professionals, physiologists, anatomists, and artists. Many systems interact to maintain homeostasis, keeping substances such as sugar, iron, and oxygen in the blood at safe levels.

The body is composed of elements including hydrogen, oxygen, carbon, calcium, and phosphorus. The adult male body is about 60% water, some 42 liters, of which about 23 liters lies inside cells. At maturity there are roughly 30 trillion [[Cell (biology)|cells]] and 38 trillion bacteria in the body, according to one estimate. Each cell sustains on average tens of thousands of instances of DNA damage per day, which if not repaired correctly may lead to mutations.

There are four main types of [[Tissue (biology)|tissue]]: lining cells (epithelia), connective tissue, nerve tissue, and muscle tissue. [[Organ (biology)|Organs]] are structured collections of cells with a specific function, and most sit within cavities such as the abdomen and pleura. The [[Heart|heart]] lies in the thoracic cavity between the lungs and slightly to the left. It has two atria and two ventricles. [[Coronary artery disease]] is the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for 16% of all deaths. Eleven major organ systems are commonly distinguished, among them the skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine, and digestive systems.

=== KEY: Human_brain_1458ca
HEADWORD: Human brain
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alzheimer's disease | Cerebellum | Cerebral cortex | Cerebrum | Neuron | Stroke
ENTRY:
Central organ of the human nervous system. With the spinal cord, it forms the central nervous system and consists of the [[Cerebrum]], brainstem and [[Cerebellum]]. The cerebrum has two hemispheres, each covered by a gray matter [[Cerebral cortex|cerebral cortex]] and divided into four lobes. The frontal lobe is associated with executive functions such as self-control, planning and reasoning, while the occipital lobe is dedicated to vision. Language is associated with the left hemisphere and visual-spatial ability with the right. The brain contains more than 86 billion [[Neuron|neurons]] and a roughly equal number of other cells. It is protected by the skull and cerebrospinal fluid, and is susceptible to [[Stroke]], Parkinson's disease, [[Alzheimer's disease|Alzheimer's disease]] and multiple sclerosis.

=== KEY: Human_c1bb19
HEADWORD: Human
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Agriculture | Australopithecus | Bipedalism | Carl Linnaeus | Fertile Crescent | Hominidae | Homo habilis | Primate
ENTRY:
Species of [[Primate|primate]] (*Homo sapiens*, "wise man"), the only extant member of the genus *Homo*, characterized by [[Bipedalism|bipedalism]], minimal body hair, and large, complex brains. It is the most abundant and widespread primate species, with over 8.3 billion individuals occupying almost all regions of Earth.

Humans are highly social, living in structures from families and kinship networks to political states. Their social interactions have established values, norms, and rituals. Curiosity and the desire to understand and influence the environment have motivated art, science, philosophy, religion, mythology, and other fields of knowledge. A large, highly developed prefrontal cortex supports episodic memory, flexible facial expressions, self-awareness, theory of mind, introspection, imagination, and mental time travel. Cumulative preservation and advancement of knowledge across generations is known in anthropology as the ratchet effect.

Humans are omnivorous, have used fire to cook food, and can survive up to eight weeks without food and three or four days without water. They sleep on average seven to nine hours per day. Childbirth carries a high risk of complications and death, and offspring are helpless at birth and typically cared for by both parents. Any two humans are on average over 99% genetically similar, with the most diverse populations in Africa; the genetic difference between males and females is around 1% to 2%. On average, males have greater body strength, and females a higher body fat percentage, longer life span in almost every population, and menopause, after which they are infertile. Gender roles have varied historically.

The species name was coined by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1735. Humans belong to the great apes ([[Hominidae]]); the lineage split from chimpanzees and bonobos around 4–8 million years ago, during which chromosome 2 formed from two others, leaving 23 pairs of chromosomes against 24 in other apes. The genus *Homo* evolved from [[Australopithecus]]. The earliest named species include [[Homo habilis]] and *Homo rudolfensis*, by 2.3 million years ago, and *H. erectus*, which evolved 2 million years ago and was the first to leave Africa. *Homo sapiens* emerged in Africa at least 300,000 years ago and migrated out in at least two waves, gradually replacing or interbreeding with archaic humans. Humans began exhibiting behavioral modernity about 160,000–70,000 years ago, and possibly earlier.

Early humans were hunter-gatherers until [[Agriculture|agriculture]] and animal domestication led to permanent settlement in the [[Fertile Crescent]] and other cradles of civilization. Food surpluses enabled denser populations, governance, and writing. Humans have visited the Moon, and industrialization has contributed significantly to pollution and to the ongoing mass extinction of other forms of life.

=== KEY: Human_cannibalism_14524a
HEADWORD: Human cannibalism
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Holodomor | Lesser Antilles
ENTRY:
Practice of humans eating the flesh or organs of other humans. Anatomically modern humans, Neanderthals and Homo antecessor practiced it to some extent in the Pleistocene. It occurred in ancient Egypt and Rome and during severe famines. The word cannibal derives from the Island Caribs of the [[Lesser Antilles]]. Cannibalism is documented in Fiji, the Amazon Basin, the Congo and among the Māori of New Zealand. Consuming body parts or blood for medicine was popular in early modern Europe, peaking in the 17th century. Starvation led to cannibalism in cases such as the [[Holodomor]] (1932–1933). Reports by outsiders, especially during colonialism, require critical examination. Cannibalism was still practiced in Papua New Guinea as of 2012, for cultural reasons.

=== KEY: Human_evolution_ed4f71
HEADWORD: Human evolution
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Australopithecus | Bipedalism | Chimpanzee | Ethiopia | Homo habilis | Lucy (hominid) | Neanderthal | Primate
ENTRY:
Evolutionary process by which *Homo sapiens* arose from earlier primates. Over this history humans gradually developed traits such as [[Bipedalism|bipedalism]], dexterity, and complex language. Modern humans interbred with archaic humans, so their evolution was not linear but weblike.

[[Primate|Primates]] diverged from other mammals about 85 million years ago, in the Late Cretaceous, with the earliest fossils appearing over 55 million years ago in the Paleocene. One of the oldest known primate-like mammals, *Plesiadapis*, came from North America and another, *Archicebus*, from China. Fossils from the Faiyum depression southwest of Cairo record the population that gave rise to all extant primates, including the great apes. Orangutans diverged from the African great apes about 14 million years ago.

Hominins parted from the gorilla lineage between 8 and 9 million years ago. The australopithecines, which include the extinct biped ancestors of humans, separated from the genus *Pan*, containing chimpanzees and bonobos, 4–7 million years ago. Human DNA is 98–99% similar to that of the [[Chimpanzee|chimpanzee]]. The earliest potential hominins, such as *Sahelanthropus* from Chad, date to 7–6 million years ago, and *Ardipithecus ramidus* from [[Ethiopia]], about 4.4 million years ago, combined climbing with upright walking. [[Australopithecus]] flourished from 4 to 2 million years ago; its fully bipedal forms include *A. afarensis*, represented by the skeleton known as [[Lucy (hominid)|Lucy]], discovered in 1974.

The genus *Homo* is evidenced by [[Homo habilis]] over 2 million years ago. *Homo erectus*, from about 1.9 million years ago, had larger brains, used fire, and spread from Africa to Eurasia. *Homo heidelbergensis* gave rise to the [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] and to *Homo sapiens*. Anatomically modern humans emerged about 300,000 years ago, and non-African populations carry 1–4% Neanderthal DNA.

=== KEY: Human_eye_af8005
HEADWORD: Human eye
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Circadian rhythm | Lens | Visual system
ENTRY:
Sensory organ of the [[Visual system|visual system]] that responds to visible light. The eye is approximately spherical and typically about 24 mm (0.94 in) in diameter. The cornea provides most of its optical power, and the iris controls how much light enters through the pupil. The crystalline [[Lens]] focuses the remaining light onto the retina. The retina contains approximately 120 million rods and 6 million cones. Rods respond to low-intensity light and produce grayscale images, while cones respond to high-intensity light and perceive color. Photosensitive ganglion cells help regulate the hormone melatonin and [[Circadian rhythm|circadian rhythm]]. Signals reach the brain via the optic nerve.

=== KEY: Human_genome_a248c9

=== KEY: Human_history_86a8d2
HEADWORD: Human history
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Africa | Globalization | Hunter-gatherer | Industrial Revolution | Islam | Mesopotamia | Neolithic Revolution | Renaissance
ENTRY:
Record of humankind from prehistory to the present, studied through history, archaeology, anthropology, linguistics, and genetics. Researchers divide it into periods in a variety of ways to give an accessible overview.

Humans evolved in [[Africa]] from great apes through the hominin lineage, which arose 7–5 million years ago. Unlike other primates, hominins developed bipedalism, and they began using rudimentary stone tools c. 3.3 million years ago, marking the start of the Paleolithic. The genus *Homo* evolved from *Australopithecus*; the earliest named species, *Homo habilis*, evolved by 2.3 million years ago with a 50% increase in brain size. *H. erectus*, about 2 million years ago, was the first hominin to leave Africa and spread across Eurasia, and hominins used fire by 400,000 years ago at the latest. Later species included the Neanderthals in Europe and the Denisovans in Siberia, which interbred with *Homo sapiens* and other hominins. *Homo sapiens* evolved in Africa around 300,000 years ago and was anatomically modern by 125,000 years ago. By 100,000 years ago, humans buried their dead and wore jewelry. As nomadic [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherers]], they left cave paintings and sculptures in ivory, stone, and bone. The dominant scholarly view is that all modern non-Africans descend from a single group that left Africa 70,000–50,000 years ago. Humans reached Australia 65,000 years ago, Europe 45,000 years ago, and the Americas 21,000 years ago, and by the end of the Ice Age 12,000 years ago had settled nearly all ice-free land.

Beginning around 10,000 BC, the [[Neolithic Revolution]] brought agriculture, which arose independently in at least 11 centers of origin. Wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were domesticated in [[Mesopotamia]] by at least 8500 BC, and rice in the Yangtze Valley around 8000–7000 BC. Farming allowed permanent settlements and created a need for accounting and writing. Early civilizations emerged in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Peru, the Indus Valley, and China, marking the start of the ancient period in the 4th millennium BC. They supported regional empires and transformative religious and philosophical ideas. Hinduism originated in the late Bronze Age, followed by the belief systems of the Axial Age: Buddhism, Confucianism, Greek philosophy, Jainism, Judaism, Taoism, and Zoroastrianism. Christianity began later as an offshoot of Judaism.

The post-classical period, c. 500–1500, saw the rise of [[Islam]], the flourishing of China under the Tang and Song dynasties, and the Byzantine, Islamic caliphate, and Mongol empires. Gunpowder and the printing press were invented. In the early modern period, c. 1500–1800, European powers explored and colonized regions worldwide, and Europe experienced the [[Renaissance]], the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the Enlightenment. By the 18th century, accumulated knowledge and technology brought about the [[Industrial Revolution]], which ushered in the modern period around 1800. Growing productive power increased international trade and colonization, linked civilizations in the process of [[Globalization|globalization]], and cemented European dominance through the 19th century. The last 250 years, which included two world wars, have brought rapid growth in population, agriculture, industry, commerce, scientific knowledge, technology, communications, military capabilities, and environmental degradation.

=== KEY: Human_migration_fa0fd8
HEADWORD: Human migration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Colonization | Emigration | Immigration | Invasion | Late antiquity | Migration Period | Natural disaster | Refugee
ENTRY:
Movement of people from one place to another with the intention of settling, permanently or temporarily, in a new location. It often covers long distances and crosses national borders (external migration), but internal migration, within a single country, is the dominant form worldwide. Four major forms are recognized: [[Invasion|invasion]], conquest, [[Colonization|colonization]], and [[Emigration|emigration]] and [[Immigration|immigration]]. Seasonal nomadic movement, tourism, pilgrimage, and commuting are not usually counted, since they involve no intention to settle.

Migrants who leave for reasons such as better jobs or health care are distinguished from people forcibly displaced, for example by a [[Natural disaster|natural disaster]] or civil disturbance. People who flee persecution and request shelter abroad are asylum seekers and, if approved, become [[Refugee|refugees]]. Movements of entire ethnic groups, as in the [[Migration Period]] of [[Late antiquity|late antiquity]], contrast with differential migration, which involves only part of a population group.

Statistics come from sources such as the World Bank's *Migration and Remittances Factbook* (editions in 2008, 2011, and 2016) and the International Organization for Migration's *World Migration Report* (ten editions since 1999). In 2013, 38% of migrants had moved from developing countries to other developing countries and 23% between high-income countries. From 2000 to 2013, the migrant population grew by an average of 2.3% a year in developing regions and 2.1% in developed regions. Almost half of migrants are women. International migrants numbered 304 million in 2024, equal to 3.7% of the world population.

Some studies conclude that migration is the most direct route out of poverty. Policy debates continue: supporters stress remittances and labor for aging populations, while critics cite strains on public services and wage pressure on low-skilled workers in some contexts.

=== KEY: Human_nature_56cbfc

ENTRY:
Fundamental dispositions and characteristics that humans are said to have naturally, including ways of thinking, feeling and acting. According to Aristotle, the philosophical study of human nature began with [[Socrates]], who turned philosophy toward how humans should best live. === KEY: Human_rights_2557ec
HEADWORD: Human rights
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Freedom of speech | International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights | International Criminal Court | John Locke | Magna Carta | Mali Empire | The Holocaust | Universal Declaration of Human Rights
ENTRY:
Moral principles or norms, often protected by national and international law, held to belong to every person simply by virtue of being human. Such rights are considered inherent and inalienable. They include civil, political, economic, social, and cultural rights, among them the right to life, [[Freedom of speech|freedom of speech]], protection against enslavement, and the right to education.

Ancient peoples lacked the modern conception of universal rights, but the idea of natural rights emerged from the medieval natural law tradition, shaped by early Christian thinkers such as Augustine. The *Kouroukan Fouga*, the 13th-century constitution of the [[Mali Empire]] in West Africa, included a "right to life and to the preservation of physical integrity." The [[Magna Carta]] (1215) influenced later documents, including the English Bill of Rights (1689) and the U.S. Bill of Rights (1791). [[John Locke]] named natural rights as "life, liberty, and estate (property)," and the revolutions in the United States (1776) and France (1789) brought them into political discourse.

The modern framework took shape after World War II, in response to atrocities including [[The Holocaust|the Holocaust]]. The UN General Assembly adopted the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] on December 10, 1948. Though non-binding, it inspired treaties such as the [[International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights]] and the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, both in force from 1976, and the [[International Criminal Court]] was established in 2002.

Universality is widely accepted, but debate persists over which rights take precedence, how they are implemented, and how they apply across cultures. Cultural relativists argue that individual rights may be inappropriate for societies that prioritize communal identity, and some empirical analyses find limited effect of treaty ratification on state compliance.

=== KEY: Human_sacrifice_fea642
HEADWORD: Human sacrifice
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Aztecs | Iron Age
ENTRY:
Ritual killing of one or more humans, usually to please or appease gods, rulers or ancestors. Many societies practiced it from prehistoric times, and it became less common across Africa, Europe and Asia in the [[Iron Age]]. === KEY: Human_settlement_c19f57
HEADWORD: Human settlement
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: City | Holocene | Megalopolis | Pleistocene | Suburb | Urban planning | Village
ENTRY:
Place where people live and carry out social, economic, political, and cultural activities. The term covers a broad range of inhabited environments, from hamlets, [[Village|villages]], towns, and [[City|cities]] to metropolitan regions and larger settlement systems, and from individual dwellings to extensive urban regions. Settlements are created by human activity rather than natural processes, typically involve built infrastructure and the modification of surrounding environments, and develop over long periods through the cumulative actions of many individuals.

For most of the c. 300,000 years since *Homo sapiens* first appeared, people lived in mobile hunter-gatherer groups rather than permanent communities. Permanent settlements arose independently in multiple regions between roughly 10,000 and 5,000 years ago, during the transition from the [[Pleistocene]] to the [[Holocene]]. Early settlements were small, often modest hamlets, and formed through gradual changes in subsistence and social coordination rather than abrupt change. In Mesopotamia, villages are dated to around 8000–7000 BC. By about 7,000 years ago permanent settlements existed in several regions, with higher population density, more durable resource management, and more complex social and economic systems. By about 5,000 years ago, cities with substantial populations had emerged, with formal governance to coordinate larger populations. Industrialization accelerated urban growth and contributed to the rise of large metropolitan areas and megacities.

Settlements may be classified by function, scale, form, or administrative status. Major categories are urban, [[Suburb|suburban]], rural, and temporary settlements; examples include refugee camps and post-disaster housing. Spatial patterns are described as nucleated, linear, dispersed, or isolated. Settlement hierarchies rank places by size and range of services, from homesteads and hamlets to conurbations, megacities, and [[Megalopolis|megalopolises]], with the hypothetical ecumenopolis at the top.

Theories of settlement include central place theory, which explains patterns through the provision of goods and services; ekistics, which treats settlements as parts of a comprehensive system of human habitation; and settlement scaling theory, which relates population size to settlement characteristics. Settlements are also a major subject of [[Urban planning|urban planning]] and sustainability research. The share of the world's population living in urban areas was 58% as of 2025 and is projected to reach 68% by 2050, bringing housing shortages, slums, and infrastructure demands in developing regions. UN-Habitat promotes sustainable development and equitable access to water and sanitation, including through the New Urban Agenda.

=== KEY: Human_sexuality_0b75ec
HEADWORD: Human sexuality
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Birth control | Bisexuality | Homosexuality | Mammal | Puberty | Sexual orientation | Sexually transmitted infection | Twin
ENTRY:
Way people experience and express themselves through sexual activities, involving biological, psychological, physical, erotic, emotional, social, and spiritual feelings and behaviors. Because the term has varied across historical contexts, it lacks a precise definition. Its biological aspects concern human reproductive functions, including the sexual response cycle, and interest in sexual activity normally increases at [[Puberty|puberty]]. Humans, like other [[Mammal|mammals]], are primarily grouped into male or female sex, and the hypothalamus is the most important part of the brain for sexual functioning.

[[Sexual orientation]] is a person's pattern of sexual interest, or lack of it, in the opposite or same sex. No single theory of its cause has gained widespread support. Researchers cite moderate genetic influences found in [[Twin|twin]] studies, prenatal hormonal effects on brain organization, and the fraternal birth order effect as evidence for innate causes, and describe the evidence for hypothesized social causes as weak. Cross-cultural surveys report that about 90–97% of people identify as exclusively heterosexual, 1–4% as [[Homosexuality|homosexual]], and 1–3% as [[Bisexuality|bisexual]], with figures varying by measurement method and culture. Hypotheses for why genes associated with homosexuality persist include kin selection and sexually antagonistic genes.

Several psychological theories, including social learning theory and social role theory, predict that men will be more approving of casual sex and have more partners than women. Observed differences in the number of partners are modest, and sexual satisfaction and attitudes toward masturbation show little to no difference between the sexes.

Sociocultural aspects include history and religious belief, and some cultures have been described as sexually repressive. The study of sexuality also covers identity within social groups, [[Sexually transmitted infection|sexually transmitted infections]], and [[Birth control|birth control]].

=== KEY: Human_skeleton_ef3c8f

ENTRY:
Internal framework of the body. Around 270 bones are present at birth, and some fuse together, leaving about 206 by adulthood, not counting accessory bones. Philosophical stance centered on the agency and potential of human beings, seen as the starting point for moral and philosophical inquiry. === KEY: Humanistic_psychology_a77fc4

Psychological perspective of the early- to mid-20th century, developed as a response to psychoanalysis and [[Behaviorism|behaviorism]]. Otto Rank and [[Carl Rogers]] centered the individual in therapy, and [[Abraham Maslow]] built on their work to establish a "third force" in psychology in the 1950s. === KEY: Humanitarianism_a89e7c
HEADWORD: Humanitarianism
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Beliefs and practices centered on the value of human life, aimed at reducing suffering through assistance to others. Unlike charity and philanthropy, which apply to both domestic and international projects, humanitarianism tends to involve helping populations affected by international emergencies such as wars and disasters. It overlaps with [[Human rights|human rights]] advocacy, development assistance and domestic philanthropy. Academic disciplines studying human society and culture, using mainly critical, speculative or interpretive methods. The humanities include [[Philosophy|philosophy]], religion, [[History|history]], language arts such as [[Literature|literature]] and [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]], the performing arts and the visual arts. During the [[Renaissance]], the term referred to the study of classical literature and language, as opposed to divinity. The Latin phrase *studia humanitatis* covered grammar, literature, rhetoric, history and moral philosophy. Historically the humanities have been distinguished from the [[Social science|social sciences]] by their methods and objectives. The humanities take an idiographic approach that focuses on the unique and context-specific, while the social sciences seek general laws through empirical and quantitative analysis, a distinction first conceptualized by Wilhelm Windelband. The distinction is not absolute, since some social scientists use narrative inquiry and historical methods. Proponents argue the humanities foster self-reflection, civic responsibility and cultural continuity.

=== KEY: Humidity_ddab80
HEADWORD: Humidity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dew | Dew point | Evaporation | Fog | Precipitation | Water vapor
ENTRY:
Concentration of [[Water vapor|water vapor]] in the air, indicating the likelihood of [[Precipitation|precipitation]], [[Dew|dew]] or [[Fog|fog]]. Humidity depends on temperature and pressure. The same amount of water vapor gives a higher relative humidity in cool air than in warm air, and the [[Dew point|dew point]] is the temperature at which air reaches saturation. Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapor per volume of air, in grams per cubic meter. Relative humidity, often expressed as a percentage, indicates the present amount relative to the maximum at the same temperature. Specific humidity is the ratio of water vapor mass to total moist air mass. High humidity impairs heat exchange for animals that rely on sweating, because it reduces the rate of [[Evaporation|evaporation]] from skin surfaces; a heat index table or a humidex can estimate the effect.

=== KEY: Humour_3b8c71
HEADWORD: Humor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Al-Farabi | Aristotle | Arthur Schopenhauer | Averroes | Avicenna | Latin | Plato | Socrates
ENTRY:
Tendency of experiences to provoke laughter and provide amusement. The word derives from the humoral medicine of the ancient Greeks, which taught that the balance of bodily fluids, or "humors" ([[Latin]] *humor*, "body fluid"), controlled health and emotion. People of all ages and cultures respond to humor, but what a person finds funny depends on geography, culture, maturity, education, intelligence, and context. Young children may favor physical slapstick such as *Tom and Jerry* cartoons, while satire requires an understanding of social meaning and context.

Theories include psychological accounts, most of which regard humor-induced behavior as healthy; spiritual accounts, which may treat humor as a "gift from God"; and views that see it as an unexplainable mystery. The benign-violation theory, endorsed by Peter McGraw, holds that humor occurs only when something seems wrong or threatening but simultaneously seems acceptable or safe. Other theories stress incongruity (pleasure in resolving unexpected patterns), superiority (amusement at others' failings), and relief (discharge of pent-up energy); incongruity is described as the best supported empirically.

Western humor theory begins with [[Plato]], who in the *Philebus* has [[Socrates]] hold that the essence of the ridiculous is an ignorance in the weak, who cannot retaliate when ridiculed. [[Aristotle]], in the *Poetics*, suggested that an ugliness that does not disgust is fundamental to humor. In ancient India, Bharata Muni's *Natya Shastra* defined humor (*hāsyam*) as one of the nine *rasas*, or emotional responses. After the *Poetics* was translated into Arabic, writers including [[Al-Farabi]], [[Avicenna]], and [[Averroes]] linked comedy to Arabic poetic forms such as *hija* (satirical poetry) and called it the "art of reprehension." [[Arthur Schopenhauer]] lamented the use of the German loanword *Humor* for any type of comedy.

Person living in a community that obtains most or all of its food from wild plants and animals. === KEY: Hunting_bdf2e4
HEADWORD: Hunting
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Animal rights | Fur | Homo erectus | Invasive species | Old English | Taxidermy
ENTRY:
Human practice of seeking, pursuing, capturing, or killing wild or feral animals. The most common motives are to obtain meat and products such as [[Fur|fur]], bone, horn, and antler, and to pursue recreation or [[Taxidermy|taxidermy]]. Hunting is also done to remove predators dangerous to people or livestock, to eliminate pests, for trade and tourism, and for conservation against overpopulation and [[Invasive species|invasive species]], a practice called culling. Recreationally hunted species, usually mammals and birds, are called game. Fishing is not commonly classed as hunting, nor is pursuing animals without intent to kill them, as in wildlife photography or birdwatching.

Hunting arose in *[[Homo erectus]]* or earlier. The oldest undisputed evidence dates to the Early Pleistocene, consistent with the emergence of *Homo erectus* about 1.7 million years ago. It became embedded in many cultures and was once an important part of rural economies, classified as primary production alongside forestry, agriculture, and fishery.

Modern game laws distinguish lawful hunting from poaching, the unauthorized killing, trapping, or capture of animals. Advocates argue that regulated hunting is a necessary part of wildlife management, helping keep animal populations within the carrying capacity of their environment and funding reserves and conservation programs through licensing fees and dedicated taxes. Excessive hunting has also contributed heavily to the endangerment, extirpation, and extinction of many animals. Some [[Animal rights|animal rights]] and anti-hunting activists regard it as a cruel and unnecessary blood sport, and practices such as canned hunts and trophy tours are considered unethical even by some hunters. Hunting of marine mammals, including whales and pinnipeds, remains controversial.

The English noun *hunt* is dated to the early 12th century and derives from the verb, which in [[Old English]] was *huntian*, "to chase game."

ENTRY:
Nearest open star cluster to the Sun, in the constellation [[Taurus (constellation)|Taurus]]. Its distance of about 153 light-years (47 parsecs) was measured directly by parallax using the Hipparcos satellite and the [[Hubble Space Telescope]], making it an important rung on the [[Cosmic distance ladder|cosmic distance ladder]]. The cluster's hundreds of stars share the same age, origin and motion, and it is estimated to be about 625 million years old. Its brightest stars form a V shape, but [[Aldebaran]] is much closer, at 65 light-years, and is not a member. Epsilon Tauri has a gas giant exoplanet candidate, the first planet found in any open cluster.

=== KEY: Hybrid_biology_c0ed1d
HEADWORD: Hybrid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Neanderthal | Wheat
ENTRY:
Offspring of two organisms from different varieties, subspecies, species or genera. Each cell contains genetic material from both parents, unlike a chimera, in which cells come from different organisms. Hybrids are not always intermediate between their parents; they can show hybrid vigor, growing larger or taller than either. Roughly 25% of plants and 10% of animals form hybrids with at least one other species. Hybridization is fundamental to plant evolution, and crop [[Wheat|wheat]] arose through hybrid speciation with its number of chromosomes doubled. Human-mediated crosses of wild and domesticated species include beefalo and wolfdogs, while [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] and modern humans are thought to have interbred as recently as 40,000 years ago.

Indian soldier and sultan of Mysore, de facto ruler from 1761. He rose to commander-in-chief under the Wodeyar rajas, came to dominate the titular monarch Krishnaraja Wodeyar II, and then governed as Chief Minister. He was the primary military leader against the [[East India Company]] in the first and second Anglo-Mysore wars. He allied with the French, who helped raise his artillery and arsenal. He left his eldest son, [[Tipu Sultan]], a kingdom bordered by the Krishna River and the Arabian Sea.

=== KEY: Hyderabad_02a342

ENTRY:
Capital and largest city of the Indian state of [[Telangana]]. Located on the Deccan Plateau along the Musi River, it had 6.9 million residents within city limits at the 2011 census and 9.7 million in its metropolitan region. Muhammad Quli Qutb Shah founded it in 1591, and the [[Mughal Empire|Mughals]] annexed it in 1687. As imperial capital of the Asaf Jahi Nizams from 1769 to 1948, it was the center of a distinctive culture. Until the 19th century it was known as the "City of Pearls" for its pearl industry. Since the 1990s it has become a hub of pharmaceuticals, biotechnology and information technology, with the HITEC City special economic zone attracting multinationals. [[UNESCO]] lists its cuisine as a creative city of gastronomy.

Second-largest city in [[Sindh]], Pakistan, after [[Karachi]]. === KEY: Hydrazine_c7d8bc
HEADWORD: Hydrazine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ammonia
ENTRY:
Colorless, flammable inorganic liquid with an [[Ammonia|ammonia]]-like odor, N₂H₄. It is highly hazardous unless handled in solution, such as hydrazine hydrate. Its largest use is as a precursor to blowing agents for polymer foams, and it is also used to make pharmaceuticals and agrochemicals. Hydrazine is a long-term storable propellant for spacecraft and an oxygen scavenger in power plant steam cycles, where it reduces corrosion. Approximately 120,000 tons of hydrazine hydrate were manufactured worldwide per year as of 2000.

=== KEY: Hydrocarbon_8e3f28
HEADWORD: Hydrocarbon
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alkane | Alkene | Alkyne | Methane | Petroleum
ENTRY:
Organic compound consisting entirely of hydrogen and carbon. Hydrocarbons are generally colorless and hydrophobic, and occur as gases such as [[Methane|methane]] and propane, liquids such as hexane and benzene, low-melting solids such as paraffin wax, or polymers such as polyethylene. Saturated hydrocarbons contain only single bonds and include [[Alkane|alkanes]], while [[Alkene|alkenes]] and [[Alkyne|alkynes]] contain double or triple bonds, and aromatic hydrocarbons contain at least one aromatic ring. In the fossil fuel industries the term refers to petroleum, natural gas and coal. Combustion of hydrocarbons supplies most of the world's energy, and [[Petroleum]] is the dominant raw material for commodity chemicals such as solvents and polymers. Most human-generated greenhouse gas emissions are carbon dioxide from burning fossil fuels or methane from natural gas handling and agriculture.

=== KEY: Hydrochloric_acid_e93ec8
HEADWORD: Hydrochloric acid
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Abu Bakr al-Razi | Aqua regia | Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac | Nitric acid
ENTRY:
Aqueous solution of hydrogen chloride (HCl), a strong acid also called muriatic acid. A colorless solution with a distinctive pungent smell, it is a component of the gastric acid in the digestive systems of most animals, including humans. It is an important laboratory reagent and industrial chemical. Alchemists called it spirits of salt, and the name hydrochloric acid was coined by French chemist [[Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac]] in 1814. The Persian physician [[Abu Bakr al-Razi]] produced hydrogen chloride gas in the early tenth century, but the acid itself was isolated only later. A mixture of [[Nitric acid|nitric acid]] and hydrochloric acid, known as [[Aqua regia]], dissolves gold.

Semi-synthetic [[Opioid|opioid]] used to treat pain and as a cough suppressant. Usually taken by mouth, it is combined with acetaminophen or [[Ibuprofen|ibuprofen]] for severe pain. === KEY: Hydroelectricity_781ba8
HEADWORD: Hydroelectricity
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dam | Greenhouse gas | Reservoir
ENTRY:
Electricity generated from hydropower, including stations with a [[Dam|dam]] and reservoir. Hydropower supplies 15% of the world's electricity, almost 4,210 TWh in 2023, more than all other renewable sources combined and more than nuclear power. It can provide large amounts of low-carbon electricity on demand, and a station with a [[Reservoir|reservoir]] can raise or lower output within seconds or minutes. Once built, it produces no direct waste and almost always emits considerably less [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]] than fossil fuel plants, although flooding lowland rainforest can release substantial amounts. Construction can displace populations, destroy arable land and disrupt river ecosystems. China added 24 GW in 2022, nearly three-quarters of global hydropower capacity additions.
