=== KEY: Swamp_2b6f48
HEADWORD: Swamp
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brackish water | Marsh | Wetland
ENTRY:
Forested [[Wetland|wetland]], a transition zone in which both land and water shape the environment. Freshwater swamps form along large rivers and lakes, depending on rainwater and seasonal flooding, while saltwater swamps occur along tropical and subtropical coasts. The water may be fresh, [[Brackish water|brackish]] or seawater. The two main types are swamp forests and shrub swamps. Unlike [[Marsh|marshes]], which are dominated by herbaceous plants, swamps are dominated by water-tolerant woody vegetation such as shrubs, bushes and trees. Swamps filter sediment, nutrients and pollutants from water, and their vegetation holds soil in place. Farmers historically drained swamps for cropland, yet two thirds of global fish and shellfish are commercially harvested and depend on wetlands.

=== KEY: Swimming_7b7a04
HEADWORD: Swimming
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Buoyancy | Competitive swimming | Infection | Olympic Games | Viscosity
ENTRY:
Self-propulsion of a person through water, usually for recreation, sport, exercise, or survival.

Swimmers move by coordinating limb and body movements to achieve [[Buoyancy|buoyancy]] and hydrodynamic thrust, combining forward thrusting actions of the head and torso with arm strokes and leg kicks. The human body has an average relative density of 0.98 compared with water, so it floats, though buoyancy varies with body composition, lung inflation, muscle and fat content, and the salinity of the water. The density and [[Viscosity|viscosity]] of water create resistance that strokes use for propulsion but that also produces drag, so faster swimmers reduce resistance by holding a horizontal position, rolling the body, and extending the arms.

Four main strokes are used in competition and recreation: the front crawl (freestyle), breaststroke, backstroke, and butterfly. The front crawl is widely regarded as the fastest.

Swimming is consistently among the top public recreational activities, and swimming lessons are compulsory in the educational curriculum in some countries. It offers health benefits, including cardiovascular health, muscle strength, and flexibility, and is low-impact because water supports the body's weight. Risks include panic and exhaustion, which can cause drowning, and exposure to [[Infection|infection]] or hostile aquatic fauna; most facilities employ lifeguards.

Cave paintings from approximately 7000 years ago depict swimming, and organized practice existed in ancient Egypt around 2500 BC. [[Competitive swimming]] gained prominence in 19th-century Europe and has featured in every modern Summer [[Olympic Games]], beginning in 1896. Races cover distances from 50 to 1500 m. American swimmer Michael Phelps holds the record for most Olympic medals, with 28, including 23 golds.

=== KEY: Symbiogenesis_c315d2
HEADWORD: Symbiogenesis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Chloroplast | Cyanobacteria | Eukaryote | Mitochondria | Plastid | Prokaryote
ENTRY:
Evolutionary theory, also called the endosymbiotic theory, on the origin of [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] from [[Prokaryote|prokaryotic]] ancestors. It holds that [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] are endosymbionts descended from free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by another prokaryote. Mitochondria appear to derive from Alphaproteobacteria, and chloroplasts from [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]]. Mitochondria and [[Plastid|plastids]] contain their own chromosomes and reproduce by binary fission, and some of these chromosomes are circular DNA like those of bacteria. The idea that chloroplasts were once independent organisms dates to the 19th century, when Andreas Schimper espoused it. The Russian botanist Konstantin Mereschkowski articulated the theory in 1905 and 1910, and Lynn Margulis substantiated it with microbiological evidence in 1967.

=== KEY: Symbiosis_a4bb83
HEADWORD: Symbiosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Algae | Ancient Greek | Chloroplast | Eukaryote | Mitochondria | Pollination | Symbiogenesis
ENTRY:
Close and long-term biological interaction between two organisms of different species. The term comes from [[Ancient Greek|Greek]] roots meaning "living together," and in 1879 Heinrich Anton de Bary defined it as "the living together of unlike organisms." Relationships may be obligate, with one or both partners dependent on the other, or facultative. In mutualism both partners benefit; in commensalism one benefits and the other is unaffected; in parasitism one benefits at the other's expense. Ectosymbiosis occurs on the host's surface, as with head lice, and endosymbiosis within its tissues, as with [[Algae|algae]] in reef-building corals. Symbiosis has driven evolution: mycorrhiza, insect [[Pollination|pollination]] and nitrogen-fixing bacteria in legume root nodules are major examples. [[Symbiogenesis]] is thought to have helped create [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] when bacteria became [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]].

Harmonious balance of proportion or, in mathematics, invariance of an object under transformations such as translation, reflection, rotation or scaling. Symmetries that preserve a given property of an object form a [[Group (mathematics)|group]]. Invariance of a mathematical object under a set of operations or transformations, found in many branches of mathematics. For a structured object, a symmetry is a mapping of the object onto itself that preserves its structure. For a set with no additional structure, symmetries are bijections, which give rise to [[Permutation|permutation]] groups. For points in a plane with a metric, symmetries are [[Distance|distance]]-preserving bijections called isometries. In calculus, an even function satisfies f(x) = f(−x), and an odd function has rotational symmetry about the origin. The integral of an odd function from −A to A is zero, provided it is integrable. In [[Linear algebra|linear algebra]], a symmetric matrix equals its transpose, so only square matrices can be symmetric. A real symmetric matrix represents a self-adjoint operator on a real [[Inner product space|inner product space]].

=== KEY: Symmetry_physics_6541ef
HEADWORD: Symmetry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetism | General relativity | Group theory | Reflection (physics) | Spacetime | Special relativity | Speed of light | Standard Model
ENTRY:
Physical or mathematical feature of a system that is preserved under some transformation. Transformations may be continuous, such as the rotation of a circle, or discrete, such as the [[Reflection (physics)|reflection]] of a bilaterally symmetric figure. Symmetries are described by [[Group theory|symmetry groups]], and they can simplify problems and explain conservation laws. [[Speed of light|The speed of light]] is the same in all frames of reference, a symmetry described in [[Special relativity]] by the Poincaré group. Other examples include the invariance of physical laws under coordinate changes in [[General relativity]] and the gauge invariances of [[Electromagnetism]] and the [[Standard Model]]. Symmetries may be global, applied the same way at every point of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], or local, applied differently at each point.

New Zealand filmmaker and actor. His features *Boy* (2010) and *Hunt for the Wilderpeople* (2016) were each the top-grossing film entirely funded and produced in New Zealand. He directed, wrote and starred in the black comedy *Jojo Rabbit* (2019), which won the Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay, and directed the [[Marvel Cinematic Universe]] film *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017). He co-wrote and co-directed the horror comedy *What We Do in the Shadows* (2014) and co-created the series *Reservation Dogs*. [[Time (magazine)|Time]] named him one of the 100 most influential people in the world in 2022.
