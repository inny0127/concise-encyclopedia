=== KEY: 0_cfcd20
HEADWORD: 0
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Arabic | Fibonacci | Integer | Maya civilization | Number | Positional notation | Sanskrit | Tennis
ENTRY:
Number representing an empty quantity, and the digit that marks an empty place in written numerals. Adding zero to or subtracting zero from any [[Number|number]] leaves it unchanged, so 0 is the additive identity of the [[Integer|integers]] and of the rational, real, and complex numbers. Multiplying any number by 0 gives 0. Division by zero is generally regarded as undefined in arithmetic, because no real number multiplied by 0 produces 1. Any number to the power of 0 equals 1, except that 0⁰ is sometimes regarded as undefined. Zero is the smallest nonnegative integer and the largest nonpositive one; it is even, neither positive nor negative, neither prime nor composite, and is usually shown as the origin of a number line.

As a digit in [[Positional notation|place-value notation]], 0 shows that a position adds nothing to a number's value: in decimal notation, 205 means two hundreds, no tens, and five ones. The same principle holds in bases other than ten, such as binary and hexadecimal. This use derives from Indian mathematics, where the earliest recorded use is dated to the 3rd or 4th century in the Bakhshali manuscript. It was transmitted to Europe by medieval Islamic mathematicians and popularized by [[Fibonacci]], and it was used independently by the [[Maya civilization]].

The English word *zero* came through French and Italian from the Venetian *zevero*, a contraction of *zefiro*, itself borrowed from [[Arabic]] *ṣifr*. That word meant "empty" and came to mean zero when used to translate the [[Sanskrit]] *śūnya*. Some sports have their own words for a zero score, such as "love" in [[Tennis|tennis]] and "duck" in cricket.

=== KEY: 100_metres_48e291
HEADWORD: 100 meters
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Florence Griffith Joyner | Track and field | Usain Bolt
ENTRY:
Sprint race in [[Track and field|track and field]] competitions, the shortest common outdoor running distance. It has been contested at the Summer Olympics since 1896 for men and since 1928 for women, and it is the shortest sprint at the Games. The inaugural World Championships were held in 1983. Starts follow three commands, "on your marks," "set" and the starter's pistol, and sprinters typically reach top speed between 50 and 60 meters. A reaction time under 0.100 second is a false start, and since the 2010 season a false-starting athlete is disqualified at once. The men's world record of 9.58 seconds was set by [[Usain Bolt]] of Jamaica in 2009; the women's record of 10.49 seconds was set by [[Florence Griffith Joyner]] of the United States in 1988.

ENTRY:
Time convention dividing the day into two 12-hour periods, a.m. (from Latin *ante meridiem*, "before midday") and p.m. (*post meridiem*, "after midday"). It can be traced to [[Mesopotamia]] and [[Ancient Egypt]], and reached its modern form in the 16th century. It remains common in the [[United States]], the [[United Kingdom]] and [[Canada]], where "12 p.m." usually means noon and "12 a.m." midnight. The [[24-hour clock]] is used in specialized fields such as aviation and medicine.

=== KEY: 1556_Shaanxi_earthquake_006bf5

ENTRY:
Earthquake that struck Huaxian, Shaanxi Province, [[China]], during the [[Ming dynasty]]. It occurred in the early morning of January 23, 1556. Most residents lived in *yaodong*, artificial caves dug into loess cliffs, which collapsed on those sleeping inside. Modern estimates put direct deaths at roughly 100,000, while more than 700,000 people either migrated or died of famine and plague, a total reduction of 830,000 in the imperial household registration. Landslides and ground ruptures were widespread, and the shaking reduced the height of the Small Wild Goose Pagoda in [[Xi'an]] by three levels. Chinese records call it the Jiajing Great Earthquake, after the [[Jiajing Emperor]] who reigned at the time.

=== KEY: 1755_Lisbon_earthquake_4152db
HEADWORD: 1755 Lisbon earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Lisbon | Portuguese Empire | Seismology | Voltaire
ENTRY:
Catastrophic earthquake, tsunami and fires that almost completely destroyed [[Lisbon]], Portugal. The quake struck on the morning of November 1, 1755, the Christian feast of All Saints, and lasted several minutes. About 40 minutes later a tsunami surged up the Tagus River, and fires started by candles knocked over in homes and churches burned for hours. Estimates place the death toll in Lisbon at 30,000 to 40,000, with perhaps 10,000 more in Morocco. Destruction was widespread across southern Portugal, particularly the Algarve. The disaster disrupted the [[Portuguese Empire]] and heightened political tensions in Portugal. European Enlightenment philosophers, including [[Voltaire]], debated its implications for theodicy. As the first earthquake studied scientifically over a large area, it contributed to the birth of modern [[Seismology]] and earthquake engineering.

Volcanic eruption in the Sunda Strait, then part of the [[Dutch East Indies]]. The volcanic island of Krakatoa erupted from May to October 1883. ENTRY:
Series of floods along the Yangtze and Huai rivers in central China, from June to August 1931. [[Drought]], followed by heavy snowmelt and a summer of repeated cyclones, pushed the Yangtze to the highest level since records began in the mid-19th century, and about 180,000 square kilometers were inundated. Global oil supply shock caused by an embargo that the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) imposed on states that had supported Israel in the [[Yom Kippur War]]. === KEY: 1976_Tangshan_earthquake_3d648c
HEADWORD: 1976 Tangshan earthquake
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: 1556 Shaanxi earthquake | Hebei | Modified Mercalli intensity scale
ENTRY:
Destructive earthquake in the industrial city of Tangshan, [[Hebei]] Province, China. It struck in the early hours of July 28, 1976, with maximum intensity XI on the [[Modified Mercalli intensity scale|Mercalli scale]]. Within minutes, 85 percent of the city's buildings collapsed or were rendered unusable, and most highway and railway bridges were destroyed or seriously damaged. The official count was 242,469 deaths, but historians accept that at least 300,000 people died, making it the deadliest earthquake in recorded history, excluding the famine deaths of the [[1556 Shaanxi earthquake]]. Ground shaking was felt across Beijing and Tianjin, and aftershocks continued for months. Reconstruction later made Tangshan a model of post-disaster urban planning.

Student-led demonstrations in [[Beijing]], China, that ended with a military crackdown on June 3 and 4, 1989. ENTRY:
First positive [[Integer|integer]] and multiplicative identity. It is the first [[Natural number|natural number]] after 0 and the unit of counting. Multiplying any number by 1 leaves it unchanged, any power of 1 equals 1, and by modern convention 1 is neither prime nor composite, although it meets the naive definition of a prime. Its symbol evolved from ancient [[Sumer|Sumerian]] and Babylonian symbols to the modern Arabic numeral. In binary, 1 represents the "on" state on which [[Computing|digital computing]] is based. Benford's law makes 1 the most common leading digit in many real-world datasets, at about 30 percent.

Global financial crisis centered on U.S. mortgage-backed securities, 2007–09. === KEY: 20th_century_classical_music_8cf88b

ENTRY:
Western art music composed from 1901 to 2000. The century had no dominant style. [[Claude Debussy]] led Impressionism in France, while Gustav Mahler and Richard Strauss extended post-Romantic symphonic writing. In Vienna, [[Arnold Schoenberg]] developed atonality and then the twelve-tone technique, which his followers Alban Berg and Anton Webern extended. After World War I, [[Igor Stravinsky]], [[Sergei Prokofiev]] and [[Maurice Ravel]] wrote neoclassical works that drew on earlier forms. Serialism, electronic music, *musique concrète* and aleatory techniques were all developed during the century. [[Minimalism]] emerged later, and [[Jazz|jazz]] and folk music became important influences on many composers.

=== KEY: A_priori_and_a_posteriori_e412f3
HEADWORD: A priori and a posteriori
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epistemology | Immanuel Kant
ENTRY:
Two categories of knowledge, justification or argument in [[Epistemology|epistemology]], distinguished by whether they depend on sensory experience. A proposition is known or justified a priori if it can be known independently of any experience beyond what is needed to understand it. It is known a posteriori if its justification depends on empirical evidence. That it is sunny in London today is known a posteriori; that it is either sunny or not sunny there can be known a priori. Mathematics and formal logic rely predominantly on a priori justification, while most sciences involve a posteriori justification. The distinction became prominent through *Critique of Pure Reason* by [[Immanuel Kant]], who related it to the analytic–synthetic distinction. W. V. O. Quine argued that the analytic–synthetic distinction is illegitimate, challenging the analytic explanation of a priori knowledge.

=== KEY: Abacus_d8a292

LINK TARGETS: Calculator
ENTRY:
Hand-operated calculating device in which beads slide on rods or in grooves within a frame. Also called a counting frame, it was used from ancient times in the Near East, Europe, China and Russia, and it supports addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and square and cube roots. Each rod usually represents one digit in a base-ten positional system. The Sumerian abacus appeared between 2700 and 2300 BC. === KEY: Abbasid_Caliphate_680f01

Third Islamic caliphate, ruled from Iraq by the Abbasid dynasty, with [[Baghdad]] as its capital for most of its history. The dynasty descended from Abbas ibn Abd al-Muttalib, an uncle of Muhammad, and came to power in 750, when the Abbasid Revolution overthrew the [[Umayyad Caliphate]]. The second caliph, al-Mansur, founded Baghdad in 762, and the reign of [[Harun al-Rashid]] (r. 786–809) marked the height of Abbasid power. Baghdad became a center of science and culture, home to the [[House of Wisdom]]. From the 9th century provinces grew autonomous, and by the 10th century the caliphs were figureheads while the Buyids and Seljuk Turks held real power. The caliphate effectively ended in 1258, when the [[Mongols]] sacked Baghdad. A surviving branch of the dynasty was reinstated in Cairo by the [[Mamluk]] sultans in 1261, but it had no political power.
