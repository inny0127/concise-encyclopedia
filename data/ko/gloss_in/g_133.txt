=== KEY: Telecommunications_ba075f
HEADWORD: Telecommunications
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander Graham Bell | Guglielmo Marconi | Internet | Radio | Samuel Morse | Telephone | Television
ENTRY:
Transmission of information over a distance by electrical or electronic means, typically through cables, radio waves, or other technologies. Channels may be divided for multiplexing, letting one medium carry several concurrent sessions. Long-distance technologies of the 19th, 20th, and 21st centuries generally use electric power and include the electrical telegraph, [[Telephone|telephone]], [[Television|television]], and [[Radio|radio]]. The term was coined in 1904 by the French engineer and novelist Édouard Estaunié, who defined it as "remote transmission of thought through electricity". Slow methods such as postal mail are excluded from the definition.

Earlier methods included smoke signals, beacons, and homing pigeons; in 1849 Paul Julius Reuter ran a pigeon service carrying stock prices between Aachen and Brussels until the telegraph gap closed. In 1792 Claude Chappe built the first fixed visual telegraph line, between Lille and Paris; the last commercial line was abandoned in 1880. On July 25, 1837, William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone demonstrated the first commercial electrical telegraph. [[Samuel Morse]] independently developed a version and a code that was an important advance. Many inventors contributed to the telephone, including [[Alexander Graham Bell]]. [[Guglielmo Marconi]], who won the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics, led a revolution in wireless communication in the first decade of the 20th century.

Since the 1960s, digital technologies have supplemented voice with data, and the limits of metallic media prompted optical fiber. Mobile networks evolved from 1G analog to 5G. The [[Internet]] is independent of any given medium and has widened global access to services. In the United States, the AT&T monopoly was broken up in 1984; debates persist over natural monopoly in network deployment and the need for antitrust oversight.

=== KEY: Telegraphy_5ca46b
HEADWORD: Telegraphy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Samuel Morse | Telephone | World War II
ENTRY:
Long-distance transmission of messages using symbolic codes known to sender and recipient, rather than a physical exchange of objects. The earliest true telegraph in wide use was the optical Chappe telegraph, invented by Claude Chappe in the late 18th century and used extensively in France during the Napoleonic era. Electric telegraphs began replacing optical systems in the mid-19th century. The [[Samuel Morse|Morse]] system, developed in the United States, became the international standard in 1865, using a modified code developed in Germany. Heliographs, which used reflected sunlight, were standard military equipment as late as [[World War II]]. Telegrams became popular once prices fell, but the [[Telephone|telephone]] drove the telegraph into decline from 1920 onward.

Automatic collection and transmission of measurements from remote points to receiving equipment for monitoring. The word derives from Greek roots meaning "far off" and "measure." Today telemetry monitors [[Spacecraft|spacecraft]], patients' vital signs and wildlife movements.

=== KEY: Telephone_b2a1e8
HEADWORD: Telephone
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander Graham Bell | Microphone | Mobile phone | Patent | Smartphone
ENTRY:
Telecommunications device that enables two or more users to converse when too far apart to be easily heard directly. A telephone converts sound, typically the human voice, into electronic signals that travel to another telephone, which reproduces the sound. Its essential elements are a [[Microphone|microphone]] (transmitter) and an earphone (receiver), usually built into a handset; transmission is possible in both directions simultaneously. Calls are placed by entering a telephone number on a keypad or dial. The word derives from Ancient Greek *tēle* ('far') and *phōnē* ('voice').

Credit for the electric telephone is frequently disputed: Charles Bourseul, Antonio Meucci, Johann Philipp Reis, Elisha Gray, and [[Alexander Graham Bell]], among others, have all been credited. In 2002 the U.S. House of Representatives passed a resolution acknowledging Meucci's work. Bell received the first U.S. [[Patent|patent]] for the device on March 7, 1876, and made the first successful call to his assistant Thomas Watson three days later. His patent rested on the finding that a fluctuating current reproduced sound best.

The first commercial line, in 1877, linked Boston and Somerville, Massachusetts, and nearly 600,000 telephones were in use across the Bell system by 1900. Direct connections between customers were impractical beyond a few, so manually operated switchboards replaced them; exchanges were eventually joined into an automated worldwide public switched telephone network. On April 3, 1973, Martin Cooper of Motorola made the first handheld [[Mobile phone|mobile phone]] call, and commercial cell phones followed in 1983. Convergence with computing produced the [[Smartphone|smartphone]], now the dominant type of telephone.

=== KEY: Telescope_399bee
HEADWORD: Telescope
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Electromagnetic spectrum | Galileo Galilei | Hubble Space Telescope | Isaac Newton | James Webb Space Telescope | Optical telescope | Radio telescope
ENTRY:
Instrument for observing distant objects by detecting their emission, absorption, or reflection of electromagnetic radiation. The term originally meant an [[Optical telescope|optical telescope]], using lenses, curved mirrors, or both, but now covers instruments that detect many regions of the [[Electromagnetic spectrum|electromagnetic spectrum]]. The word was coined in 1611 by Giovanni Demisiani, from Ancient Greek *tēle* ('far') and *skopein* ('to look or see').

The first practical telescopes were refractors with glass lenses, invented in the Netherlands at the beginning of the 17th century. The earliest record is a 1608 patent application by the spectacle maker Hans Lipperhey; the actual inventor is unknown. [[Galileo Galilei]] built his own version in 1609 and observed Jupiter's moons, the phases of Venus, and the Moon's surface. In 1668 [[Isaac Newton]] built the first practical reflecting telescope, which uses mirrors. Refractors are limited to about 1 meter (39 inches), so most large research telescopes built since the turn of the 20th century are reflectors; the largest have objectives over 10 meters, and 30–40 m designs are under development.

The 20th century brought [[Radio telescope|radio telescopes]] in the 1930s (the first purpose-built one operated in 1937) and infrared telescopes in the 1960s. Because the atmosphere is opaque to most of the spectrum, X-ray and far-infrared observation requires orbit. The [[Hubble Space Telescope]] detects visible, ultraviolet, and near-infrared light, and the Kepler Space Telescope discovered thousands of exoplanets. The [[James Webb Space Telescope]], an infrared instrument, launched on an Ariane 5 from Kourou, French Guiana, on December 25, 2021, and orbits the L2 Lagrange point.

=== KEY: Television_1bf5d9
HEADWORD: Television
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cathode ray tube | John Logie Baird | World War II
ENTRY:
Telecommunication medium for transmitting moving images and sound; the term also denotes the receiving set. Television is a mass medium for advertising, entertainment, news, and sports. The word combines Ancient Greek *tēle* ('far') and Latin *visio* ('sight'); Constantin Perskyi used it in 1900 at the International Congress of Electricity in Paris.

In 1884 Paul Nipkow patented in Berlin the Nipkow disk, a spinning disk with a spiral of holes that scanned an image line by line. Karl Ferdinand Braun developed the [[Cathode ray tube|cathode ray tube]] in 1897. In 1926 [[John Logie Baird]] gave the first public demonstration of a working television system in London, using mechanical scanning; the next year Philo Farnsworth transmitted the first fully electronic image in San Francisco. The BBC began regular electronic broadcasts in 1936.

After [[World War II]], black-and-white broadcasting became popular in the United Kingdom and the United States, and in the 1950s television was the primary medium for influencing public opinion. Color broadcasting was introduced in the mid-1960s in the U.S. and most other developed countries. Signals were first sent by terrestrial transmitter, then also by cable, satellite, and, since the 2000s, the Internet, and transmission shifted from analog to digital. High-definition formats (1080p, 1080i, 720p) supplemented standard definition. In 2013, 79% of the world's households owned a television set. Flat-panel LCD, OLED, and plasma displays replaced CRT screens, and flat screens decisively overtook CRT in the early 2010s. Smart televisions with Internet functions became dominant by the late 2010s, and streaming services such as Netflix expanded access.

=== KEY: Temperate_climate_32f0cf

PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Humid subtropical climate | Köppen climate classification | Mediterranean climate | Ocean current | Tropical climate
ENTRY:
Main climate class in the middle latitudes, between the tropics and the polar regions. Temperate climates occur roughly 23.5° to 66.5° north and south of the Equator. They have wider temperature ranges and more distinct seasons than [[Tropical climate|tropical climates]], which usually differ mainly in precipitation. Latitude, [[Ocean current|ocean currents]], prevailing winds, continentality and altitude all shape them. The [[Köppen climate classification|Köppen classification]] defines a temperate (C) climate as having a mean temperature above −3 °C (26.6 °F) but below 18 °C (64.4 °F) in its coldest month. Subtypes include [[Humid subtropical climate|humid subtropical climates]], with long, hot, humid summers, and [[Mediterranean climate|Mediterranean]] climates, with dry summers and wet winters.

=== KEY: Temperature_ee7a8e
HEADWORD: Temperature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Absolute zero | Celsius | Fahrenheit | International System of Units | Kelvin | Kinetic energy | Thermometer
ENTRY:
Physical quantity expressing relative hotness or coldness, reflecting the average [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] of the particles in a system in classical thermodynamics and kinetic theory. Temperature is important across natural science, including physics, chemistry, Earth science, astronomy, medicine, biology, and metallurgy. It affects the phase and density of materials, the rate of chemical reactions, thermal radiation, and the speed of sound, which in a gas is proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature.

Temperature is measured with a [[Thermometer|thermometer]], calibrated in scales that historically relied on various reference points. The [[Celsius]] scale (°C), used in most of the world, sets 0 °C at the freezing point of water and 100 °C at its boiling point at sea-level atmospheric pressure. The [[Fahrenheit]] scale (°F) is in common use in the United States, with water freezing at 32 °F and boiling at 212 °F. The [[Kelvin]] scale (K) is an absolute scale used predominantly for science; an increment of one degree Celsius equals one kelvin, and the scales differ by an exact offset of 273.15. The kelvin is one of the seven base units of the [[International System of Units]], defined since May 2019 through particle kinetic theory and statistical mechanics.

[[Absolute zero]], 0 K, equals −273.15 °C or −459.67 °F. Under the third law of thermodynamics it can be approached but never reached; the lowest temperature attained experimentally is 38 pK, or 38 trillionths of a kelvin. Matter there still has quantum-mechanical zero-point energy. Negative absolute temperatures exist and are hotter than any positive temperature.

=== KEY: Tendon_3bed10

ENTRY:
Tough band of dense fibrous [[Connective tissue|connective tissue]], also called a sinew, that connects muscle to [[Bone|bone]]. It transmits the mechanical forces of muscle contraction to the skeleton while withstanding tension. Tendons, like [[Ligament|ligaments]], are made of collagen, but ligaments connect bone to bone while tendons connect muscle to bone. The adult human body has about 4,000 tendons. Collagen makes up 60–85% of a normal tendon's dry mass, mostly type I, and its fibers run parallel and are grouped into fascicles. The whole tendon is enclosed by a fascia, and the space between it and the tendon tissue is filled with the paratenon, a fatty loose connective tissue.

=== KEY: Terrorism_848361

ENTRY:
Use of violence against non-combatants to achieve political or ideological aims, generally intended to instill fear and to reach beyond the immediate victims. No definition has gained universal agreement, partly because the term is politically and emotionally charged. Suicide attacks increased from the 1980s, typified by the [[September 11 attacks]] in 2001.

Left-wing and right-wing organizations, nationalist and religious groups, revolutionaries, and ruling governments have all used terrorism. Attacks often target crowded public places such as airports, shopping centers, and nightlife venues, aiming to undermine confidence in security measures and prompt policy changes. The Global Terrorism Database, maintained by the University of Maryland, College Park, recorded more than 61,000 incidents of non-state terrorism, causing at least 140,000 deaths, between 2000 and 2014.

=== KEY: Textile_922c88
HEADWORD: Textile
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Cotton | Industrial Revolution | Knitting | Polyester | Silk | Weaving | Wool
ENTRY:
Any of various fiber-based materials, including fibers, yarns, filaments, threads, and fabrics. The word comes from Latin *textilis* ('woven'). Textiles were originally woven, but other methods developed later, notably [[Knitting|knitting]] and non-woven processes such as felting. Fibers, which may be natural, synthetic, or both, are twisted or laid out into yarn, and yarns are made into fabric by [[Weaving|weaving]], knitting, crocheting, knotting, tatting, or braiding. Natural fibers include [[Cotton|cotton]], [[Wool|wool]], and [[Silk|silk]]; [[Polyester|polyester]] is synthetic. Consumer textiles, such as clothing and furnishings, prioritize aesthetics and comfort; technical textiles, such as geotextiles and medical textiles, prioritize function. Cotton or blend garments can last twenty years or more with regular use and care.

Textiles rarely survive across millennia, so prehistoric evidence consists mostly of spinning and weaving tools. The earliest spinning tool was the spindle, to which a whorl was eventually added; the spinning wheel came later, though historians are unsure whether in China or India. The first clothes, probably animal skins, were worn at least 70,000 years ago. Dyed flax fibers found in a cave in the Republic of Georgia, dated to 34,000 BC, suggest that textile-like materials were made in the Paleolithic.

The textile industry grew out of craft and was sustained by craft guilds. During the [[Industrial Revolution]] it became increasingly mechanized; with the spinning jenny, invented in the United Kingdom in 1765, textile production became the first economic activity to be industrialized. For plain weave, twill, and satin weave, ancient and modern methods differ little. Textile manufacture is the oldest industrial art.

The Beatles are the best-selling music act of all time, with estimated sales of over 600 million units worldwide, and hold the records for most number-one singles (20) and albums (19) on the U.S. Billboard charts. Their accolades include nine [[Grammy Awards]], and they entered the [[Rock and Roll Hall of Fame]] in 1988, their first year of eligibility.

ENTRY:
Only novel by American writer and poet [[Sylvia Plath]], first published in London in 1963 under the pseudonym Victoria Lucas. Semi-autobiographical, it follows Esther Greenwood, a student from the [[Boston]] suburbs whose summer internship at a [[New York City]] magazine is followed by a mental breakdown. Plath died by [[Suicide]] a month after the novel's first British publication. It was published under her name in 1966, and it was not published in the United States until 1971, in line with the wishes of her husband Ted Hughes and her mother. It has been translated into more than 40 languages.

American [[Sitcom|sitcom]] created by [[Chuck Lorre]] and Bill Prady for [[CBS]], broadcast from 2007 to 2019 for 12 seasons and 279 episodes. Filmed before a live audience, it centers on Leonard Hofstadter, an experimental physicist, and his roommate Sheldon Cooper, a theoretical physicist who researches [[Quantum mechanics]] and [[String theory]] at the California Institute of Technology. Their neighbor Penny is a waitress and aspiring actress. Jim Parsons won four Emmy Awards for playing Sheldon. The show spawned the prequel *Young Sheldon* (2017–2024) and other spin-offs.

1998 crime comedy film written and directed by the [[Coen brothers]], starring [[Jeff Bridges]] as Jeffrey Lebowski, known as The Dude, a Los Angeles slacker who is mistaken for a millionaire with the same name and drawn into a kidnapping scheme. Loosely inspired by [[Raymond Chandler]], the neo-noir film received mixed reviews on its release. Its eccentric characters, comedic dream sequences and eclectic soundtrack later earned it a cult following. In 2014 it was selected for preservation in the National Film Registry by the [[Library of Congress]].
