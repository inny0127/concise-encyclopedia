=== KEY: Sediment_56a7c9
HEADWORD: Sediment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Erosion | Sandstone | Weathering
ENTRY:
Loose solid particles transported and deposited on a planetary surface by water, wind, ice or gravity. Sediment forms through [[Weathering|weathering]] and [[Erosion|erosion]] of existing rocks. Sand and silt carried in river water settle on the seabed, and if buried, they may become [[Sandstone|sandstone]] and siltstone through lithification. Most sediment is moved by water, but wind forms desert dunes and loess, and glaciers deposit moraine and till. Grain size is measured on the log base 2 Phi scale, from colloid to boulder. Shape is described by form, roundness and surface texture, and composition by parent rock, mineral makeup and chemistry. Stronger flows lift and drag particles more, while larger or denser particles are more likely to fall through the flow.

=== KEY: Sedimentary_basin_b85377
HEADWORD: Sedimentary basin
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Coal | Natural gas | Petroleum | Sediment | Sedimentary rock
ENTRY:
Region-scale depression of Earth's crust in which long-term subsidence allows a thick sequence of [[Sediment|sediment]] to accumulate and become [[Sedimentary rock|sedimentary rock]]. Basins form when deformation of the lithosphere, usually linked to plate tectonics, creates space for sediment. Mechanisms include thinning of the crust, loading by sediment or volcanic and tectonic forces, and changes in the density of the underlying lithosphere. Once formed, the weight of accumulating sediment accentuates subsidence. More than 600 basins have been identified worldwide, ranging from tens of square kilometers to over a million, with sedimentary fills from 1 to almost 20 kilometers thick. Almost all the world's [[Natural gas|natural gas]], [[Petroleum|petroleum]] and [[Coal|coal]] is found in sedimentary rock, making basins economically important.

=== KEY: Sedimentary_rock_10661e
HEADWORD: Sedimentary rock
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Conglomerate (geology) | Limestone | Sandstone | Sediment | Sedimentary basin
ENTRY:
Rock formed by the cementation of [[Sediment|sediment]], particles of mineral or organic matter that accumulate at Earth's surface. Sedimentation may also occur when dissolved minerals precipitate from water. Sedimentary rocks cover much of the continents but make up a small share of the crust's volume, forming a thin veneer over igneous and metamorphic rock. They are deposited in layers called strata and are often found in large [[Sedimentary basin|sedimentary basins]]. Clastic rocks are made of cemented fragments such as [[Conglomerate (geology)|conglomerate]] and [[Sandstone|sandstone]], while biochemical and chemical rocks include [[Limestone|limestone]] and coal. Their layers preserve evidence of Earth's history, including paleoclimate and the history of life, and they are sources of fossil fuels, drinking water and ores.

=== KEY: Seed_dba005
HEADWORD: Seed
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Embryo | Fern | Flowering plant | Fruit | Gymnosperm | Moss
ENTRY:
Plant structure containing an embryo and stored nutrients in a protective coat called a testa. Seeds are the product of the ripened ovule, after the embryo sac is fertilized by sperm from pollen to form a zygote, from which the [[Embryo|embryo]] develops. Seed formation is the defining part of reproduction in seed plants, which now dominate land habitats from forests to grasslands. [[Fern|Ferns]] and [[Moss|mosses]] have no seeds.

In [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] the ovary ripens into a [[Fruit|fruit]] that contains the seed and disseminates it. Many structures called seeds are actually dry fruits. In stone fruits such as the peach, a hardened layer, the endocarp, surrounds the actual seed. Nuts are one-seeded, hard-shelled fruits, such as the acorn or hazelnut. In [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperms]], no special structure encloses the seeds, which begin development "naked" on the bracts of cones.

Flowering-plant seeds have three genetically distinct parts: the embryo; the endosperm, normally triploid; and the seed coat, derived from maternal tissue of the ovule. Development begins with double fertilization, in which two male gametes fuse with the egg cell and the central cell to form the primary endosperm and the zygote. The endosperm becomes the food the young plant consumes until its roots develop after germination.

The first land plants evolved around 468 million years ago and reproduced by spores. The earliest seed-bearing plants were gymnosperms, which arose in the late Devonian period (416 million to 358 million years ago). Seed ferns followed in the Carboniferous period (359 to 299 million years ago), with ovules borne in a cupule of enclosing branches.

=== KEY: Seismic_magnitude_scales_9c589f
HEADWORD: Seismic magnitude scales
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Amplitude | Earthquake
ENTRY:
Scales for the overall strength, or size, of an [[Earthquake|earthquake]]. They are distinct from seismic intensity scales, which rate the severity of ground shaking at a given location. Magnitude is usually determined from seismic waves recorded on a seismogram, using measures such as timing, orientation, [[Amplitude|amplitude]], frequency or duration. Magnitude is approximately related to the released seismic energy. Different scales exist because earthquakes differ, the available information differs, and magnitudes serve different purposes.

=== KEY: Seismology_d6bce1
HEADWORD: Seismology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Earthquake | Geology | Plate tectonics | Tsunami | Zhang Heng
ENTRY:
Scientific study of [[Earthquake|earthquakes]] and the propagation of elastic waves through planets. The field also covers the environmental effects of earthquakes, such as [[Tsunami|tsunamis]], and seismic sources including volcanoes and [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Paleoseismology uses [[Geology|geology]] to infer information about past earthquakes, and a seismogram records Earth's motion over time. Scholarly interest in earthquakes dates to antiquity; in 132 CE [[Zhang Heng]] of China's Han dynasty designed the first known seismoscope. In 1906 Richard Dixon Oldham found the first clear evidence that Earth has a central core. In 1910 Harry Fielding Reid proposed the "elastic rebound theory," which remains the foundation for modern tectonic studies.

ENTRY:
Process of choosing which animals or plants reproduce in order to develop particular traits. Also called artificial selection, it is often combined with [[Inbreeding|inbreeding]], linebreeding and outcrossing. Domesticated animals are known as breeds, and domesticated plants as varieties or cultivars. Robert Bakewell established it as a scientific practice during Britain's Agricultural Revolution in the 18th century. It is now common in agriculture and experimental biology.

ENTRY:
Principle of international law that a people has the right to form its own political entity. The principle does not specify how the decision is made or what the outcome should be, whether independence, federation or autonomy, and no right to secession is recognized under international law. The concept emerged with 19th-century nationalism. [[Woodrow Wilson]] and others proclaimed a general principle of self-determination during and after [[World War I]], and it was included in the 1941 Atlantic Charter. === KEY: Self_esteem_34678e
HEADWORD: Self-esteem
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hume | Loneliness | Shame | William James
ENTRY:
Confidence in one's own worth, abilities or morals. It encompasses beliefs about oneself, such as "I am worthy," as well as emotional states such as triumph, pride and [[Shame|shame]]. Psychologists usually regard it as an enduring personality trait, although short-term variations also exist. High self-esteem is associated with academic achievement, relationship satisfaction, happiness and lower rates of criminal behavior, while low self-esteem has been linked to anxiety, [[Loneliness|loneliness]] and vulnerability to substance abuse. The concept was first expressed by the Scottish Enlightenment thinker [[David Hume]] in the 18th century, and [[William James]] identified it as a distinct psychological construct. In the mid-1960s Morris Rosenberg defined self-esteem as a feeling of self-worth and developed the Rosenberg self-esteem scale, the most widely used such scale in the social sciences. Self-esteem typically declines from ages 4–8 and peaks around ages 60–70.

=== KEY: Semantics_760627

ENTRY:
Study of meaning in language. Semantics examines what linguistic meaning is and how words and complex expressions acquire it. It distinguishes sense, the ideas and concepts associated with an expression, from reference, the object to which it points. It contrasts with [[Syntax]], which studies the rules for grammatical sentences, and with [[Pragmatics]], which investigates how people use language. === KEY: Semiconductor_548c00
HEADWORD: Semiconductor
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Diode | Electronics | Germanium | Integrated circuit | Light-emitting diode | Silicon | Transistor
ENTRY:
Material with electrical conductivity between that of a conductor and an insulator. Its conductivity can be modified by adding impurities, a process called doping, and by electric fields or light. Raising the temperature increases conductivity, contrary to the behavior of a metal. Examples include [[Silicon|silicon]], [[Germanium|germanium]], gallium arsenide, and elements near the "metalloid staircase" of the periodic table. After silicon, gallium arsenide is the second most common semiconductor and is used in laser diodes, solar cells, and microwave-frequency integrated circuits.

For most semiconductors the band gap, the energy needed to lift an electron from the valence band to the conduction band, ranges from about 0.5 to 3 eV; silicon's is 1.1 eV, germanium's 0.67 eV, gallium arsenide's 1.43 eV, and silicon carbide's 3.2 eV.

Adding a small amount, of the order of 1 in 10⁸, of pentavalent atoms (antimony, phosphorus, arsenic) or trivalent atoms (boron, gallium, indium) to silicon produces doped, or extrinsic, semiconductors. Group V dopants act as donors, creating free electrons ("n-type"); Group III dopants act as acceptors, creating free holes ("p-type"). Where regions of different doping meet in one crystal they form a p–n junction. The behavior of charge carriers at such junctions is the basis of the [[Diode|diode]], the [[Transistor|transistor]], and most modern [[Electronics|electronics]]. Devices are used for amplification, switching, and energy conversion. In certain semiconductors excited electrons relax by emitting light, as in the [[Light-emitting diode|light-emitting diode]].

The first practical electronic application was the 1904 cat's-whisker detector, a primitive diode used in early radio receivers. Quantum physics led to the transistor in 1947 and the [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuit]] in 1958.

=== KEY: Semiconductor_device_7c1e37
HEADWORD: Semiconductor device
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Boron | Diode | Integrated circuit | MOSFET | Semiconductor | Vacuum tube
ENTRY:
Electronic component that relies on the electronic properties of [[Semiconductor|semiconductor]] materials such as silicon or germanium. Devices are made as discrete components or as [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]] containing from hundreds to billions of devices on a single wafer.

=== KEY: Semiotics_382934
HEADWORD: Semiotics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Charles Sanders Peirce | Ferdinand de Saussure | Pragmatics | Semantics
ENTRY:
Study of signs and of how meaning is created and communicated through them. It is usually divided into syntactics, which addresses how signs are organized; [[Semantics]], which addresses the relation between signs and their meanings; and [[Pragmatics]], which addresses the relation between signs and their users. Signs can be sounds, images, written marks or gestures, and they include icons, which resemble their referents; indexical signs, which have a direct physical link such as smoke signaling fire; and conventional symbols, such as most words. [[Ferdinand de Saussure]] and [[Charles Sanders Peirce]] established semiotics as an independent field in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Because it is interdisciplinary, it has been applied to literature, art and media, as well as to biosemiotics, which examines sign processes in animals and plants.

=== KEY: Sense_564107
HEADWORD: Sense
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brain | Central nervous system | Cognitive psychology | Pain | Perception | Peripheral nervous system | Umami
ENTRY:
Biological system used by an organism to gather information about its surroundings by detecting stimuli, a process called sensation. Sense organs collect stimuli such as sound or smell and transduce them into a form the [[Brain|brain]] can interpret. Sensation and [[Perception|perception]] are fundamental to nearly every aspect of an organism's cognition, behavior, and thought. Some cultures traditionally identified five human senses (sight, smell, touch, taste, and hearing), but many more are now recognized, and non-human organisms use still more.

Receptor cells, such as mechanoreceptors, photoreceptors, chemoreceptors, and thermoreceptors, convert stimuli into action potentials. Signals travel through cranial and spinal nerves of the [[Central nervous system|central]] and [[Peripheral nervous system|peripheral nervous systems]] to the sensory cortices of the brain, where they are processed and perceived.

Senses are divided into external (exteroception) and internal (interoception) systems. Human external senses rely on the eyes, ears, skin, nose, and mouth. Internal senses include proprioception (body position), equilibrioception, and nociception ([[Pain|pain]]), and further signals produce hunger, thirst, suffocation, and nausea.

Sensory modality refers to how information is encoded. Listing modalities, which can number as many as 17, involves dividing the major senses into submodalities. Touch can be separated into light pressure, deep pressure, vibration, itch, pain, temperature, and hair movement; taste into sweet, salty, sour, bitter, spicy, and [[Umami|umami]]. Multimodality integrates senses into one perceptual experience, so that one sense can influence how another is perceived.

Other mammals generally have a stronger sense of smell than humans. Some animals detect electrical and magnetic fields, air moisture, or polarized light, or perceive through echolocation. Psychophysics, neurobiology, [[Cognitive psychology|cognitive psychology]], and cognitive science study the senses.

=== KEY: Sensor_06b185

Device that measures a physical quantity and converts it into an electrical signal. === KEY: Sensory_nervous_system_c6c106

ENTRY:
Principle dividing a state's government into distinct branches, usually legislation, execution and adjudication. The principle is closely linked to checks and balances, in which branches limit one another. Separation of judicial power from the other branches is the norm in modern democracies. === KEY: Series_mathematics_34238f
HEADWORD: Series
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Archimedes | Augustin-Louis Cauchy | Calculus | Carl Friedrich Gauss | Isaac Newton | Mathematical analysis | Taylor series | Zeno's paradoxes
ENTRY:
Sum of infinitely many terms added one after another. The terms may be numbers, functions, matrices, or anything else that can be added, written a₁ + a₂ + a₃ + ⋯. Series, also called infinite series, are a major part of [[Calculus|calculus]] and [[Mathematical analysis|mathematical analysis]]. They are used in most areas of mathematics, including combinatorics through generating functions, and in physics, computer science, statistics, and finance.

An infinite sequence of additions cannot be performed in finite time, so a value is assigned through a limit. The finite sums of the first n terms are called partial sums. If their limit exists as n tends to infinity, the series is convergent, or summable, and the limit is its sum; otherwise it is divergent. The same notation denotes both the process of adding and, for a convergent series, its result. [[Taylor series]] approximate smooth functions by polynomials with infinitely many terms.

Among the Ancient Greeks, the idea that a potentially infinite summation could produce a finite result was considered paradoxical, most famously in [[Zeno's paradoxes]]. [[Archimedes]] and other Greek mathematicians nonetheless applied infinite series, for instance in the quadrature of the parabola. The concept of a limit resolved the mathematical side of Zeno's paradoxes during the 17th century, especially through the early calculus of [[Isaac Newton]]. In the 19th century [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]], [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]], and others made the resolution rigorous, using the completeness of the real numbers and the notions of absolute and conditional convergence.

=== KEY: Service_economics_09d32a
HEADWORD: Service
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bank | Goods | Government | Insurance | Lawyer | Mechanic | School
ENTRY:
Intangible act or use for which a consumer, company, or [[Government|government]] is willing to pay. Examples include work done by barbers, doctors, [[Lawyer|lawyers]], accountants, [[Mechanic|mechanics]], [[Bank|banks]], and [[Insurance|insurance]] companies. Public services are those that a society as a whole pays for, such as hospitals, elementary [[School|schools]], or libraries. Unlike [[Goods|goods]], which involve the transfer of tangible products, services give value to the customer through the provider's effort rather than ownership of an object.

Services are usually described by four characteristics. They are intangible: they are not manufactured, transported, or stocked, and cannot be stored for future use. They are inseparable, being produced and consumed simultaneously, so the consumer is part of the delivery. They are perishable: resources assigned to a period go unused if no one consumes the service then, as with an empty airline seat after departure. They are variable, or heterogeneous: each delivery differs in time, place, circumstances, or resources, and many services are modified for each consumer.

These traits make quality hard to keep consistent, since many services depend on variable human activity rather than a precisely determined process, and demand varies by season, time of day, and business cycle. Exceptions include utilities. A service can be specified through standard attributes, such as the benefits to the consumer, the delivery point, the times at which it is available, a fulfillment target, a delivery duration, and a price.

Services have become the dominant sector in modern economies, accounting for approximately 67% of global GDP and over 50% of employment worldwide, with higher shares in developed nations. Services often show slower productivity growth than goods, a point linked to debates such as Baumol's cost disease.

=== KEY: Set_mathematics_f555d2
HEADWORD: Set
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: David Hilbert | Empty set | Georg Cantor | Natural number | Russell's paradox | Set theory | Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory
ENTRY:
Collection of distinct things, called elements or members, typically mathematical objects such as numbers, symbols, points in space, lines, other geometric shapes, variables, functions, or other sets. Sets cannot be mathematically defined, since they form a primitive notion and are instead characterized by axioms. Since the first half of the 20th century, all mathematical objects have ultimately been defined in terms of sets, so [[Set theory|set theory]] serves as the foundation of mathematics.

Before the end of the 19th century, sets were not studied specifically and were not clearly distinguished from sequences. Most mathematicians treated infinity as potential, the result of an endless process, and were reluctant to consider infinite sets. The study of infinite sets began with [[Georg Cantor]]. It produced counterintuitive results: the number line has strictly more elements than there are [[Natural number|natural numbers]]. Assuming a set of all sets led to [[Russell's paradox]], which triggered a foundational crisis in mathematics. One resolution, [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory]], has been generally adopted as a foundation. [[David Hilbert]] prophesied the wide use of sets in mathematics, saying: "No one will drive us from the paradise that Cantor created for us."

A set may be specified by listing its elements or by giving a property that characterizes them. Roster notation, introduced by Ernst Zermelo in 1908, lists elements between braces, and a set does not change if elements are repeated or reordered. By the axiom of extensionality, two sets are equal if and only if they have the same elements, so only one [[Empty set|empty set]] exists. A set is finite if its elements can be put in one-to-one correspondence with the first n natural numbers, and infinite otherwise.

=== KEY: Set_theory_cf9d70

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Branch of mathematical logic that studies sets, collections of unique objects. As a foundational system, set theory underpins the whole of mathematics and provides a framework for a mathematical theory of infinity. It has applications in [[Computer science|computer science]], philosophy, and formal semantics. Contemporary research ranges from the structure of the [[Real number|real number]] line to the consistency of large cardinals.

Its Alcázar palace complex, cathedral and General Archive of the Indies form a [[World Heritage Site|UNESCO World Heritage Site]].

=== KEY: Sewage_treatment_228125
HEADWORD: Sewage treatment
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Nitrogen | Pathogen | Phosphorus | Sanitation | Water pollution
ENTRY:
Process of removing contaminants from municipal wastewater. It aims to produce an effluent suitable for discharge to the environment or for reuse, thereby preventing [[Water pollution|water pollution]] from raw sewage. Secondary treatment uses aerobic or anaerobic biological processes to reduce organic matter, measured as biological oxygen demand. Tertiary treatment adds polishing and nutrient removal, reducing [[Nitrogen|nitrogen]] and [[Phosphorus|phosphorus]] and disinfecting against [[Pathogen|pathogenic organisms]]. A quaternary step can remove micropollutants such as pharmaceuticals, as implemented at full scale in Sweden. High-income countries treat about 74% of their sewage, but developing countries treat an average of just 4.2%. Treatment is part of [[Sanitation|sanitation]].

HEADWORD: Sewing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Embroidery | Sewing machine | Silk Road
ENTRY:
Craft of fastening textiles together with a needle and thread. One of the oldest textile arts, it originated in the Paleolithic era, when early humans in Europe and Asia made fur and leather garments with bone, antler or ivory needles and thread of sinew or catgut. For thousands of years all sewing was done by hand, and before the 19th century most of it repaired clothing. Decorative [[Embroidery|embroidery]] was valued in many cultures, and its stitches spread along trade routes such as the [[Silk Road]]. The [[Sewing machine|sewing machine]], invented in the 19th century, mechanized stitching and enabled mass garment production in the 20th century. Hand sewing remains common in high-quality tailoring, haute couture and custom dressmaking.

=== KEY: Sewing_machine_be183d
HEADWORD: Sewing machine
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clothing industry | Industrial Revolution | Thread (textiles)
ENTRY:
Machine used to stitch fabric and other materials together with [[Thread (textiles)|thread]]. Invented during the first [[Industrial Revolution]] to reduce manual sewing in clothing companies, it greatly improved efficiency in the [[Clothing industry|clothing industry]]. The first machine is generally credited to Englishman Thomas Saint, who designed one in 1790 but probably never built it. The first practical, widely used machine was invented by the French tailor Barthélemy Thimonnier, whose machine sewed straight seams with a chain stitch. American inventor Elias Howe patented a lockstitch machine in 1846. Home machines are designed for one person to sew individual items, while industrial machines are larger, faster and more specialized. Early machines were powered by a flywheel handle or a foot treadle, and electric machines came later.
