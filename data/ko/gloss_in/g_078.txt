=== KEY: Latin_America_dfcd11
HEADWORD: Latin America
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Brazil | Mexico | Napoleon III | Romance languages
ENTRY:
Cultural region of the Americas, with [[Romance languages]] predominantly spoken, chiefly Spanish and Portuguese. Defined by culture rather than geography, it includes [[Mexico]], most countries of Central America, South America and the Caribbean, and [[Brazil]]. English-, Dutch- and other Germanic-speaking countries such as Belize, Guyana, Jamaica and Suriname are usually excluded. The term was introduced at a conference in Paris in 1856, when the Chilean politician Francisco Bilbao coined it for countries sharing a cultural and linguistic heritage. It gained wider use in the 1860s under [[Napoleon III]], whose government sought to justify France's intervention in the Second Mexican Empire.

=== KEY: Latin_American_literature_b29a18
HEADWORD: Latin American literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bartolomé de las Casas | Gabriel García Márquez | Isaac Newton | Juana Inés de la Cruz | Latin America | Octavio Paz | Pablo Neruda
ENTRY:
Oral and written literature of [[Latin America]] in Spanish, Portuguese and indigenous languages. Pre-Columbian cultures were largely oral, although the Maya produced glyphic texts and the Aztecs produced codices, and the *Popol Vuh* was recorded after European arrival. Colonial writers included [[Bartolomé de las Casas]], whose *Brief Account of the Destruction of the Indies* debated the ethics of colonization, and [[Juana Inés de la Cruz]], whose scientific writings included exchanges with [[Isaac Newton]]. Nineteenth-century novels, often called "foundational fictions", sought national identity. The 20th-century Latin American Boom brought magical realism to worldwide attention, most notably through [[Gabriel García Márquez]]'s *One Hundred Years of Solitude*. Jorge Luis Borges, [[Pablo Neruda]] and [[Octavio Paz]], the last two both Nobel laureates, exemplify its range.

=== KEY: Latin_alphabet_3f05dd
HEADWORD: Latin alphabet
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Egyptian hieroglyphs | Latin | Latin script | Middle Ages | Phoenician alphabet
ENTRY:
Set of letters used by the ancient Romans to write [[Latin]], later augmented with lowercase letters for Medieval Latin. It evolved from the Etruscan alphabet, which came from Greek and ultimately from the [[Phoenician alphabet|Phoenician]] alphabet, itself derived from [[Egyptian hieroglyphs]]. The modern 26-letter inventory added W in the [[Middle Ages]] and separated I from J and V from U in the early modern era. The [[Latin script]] built on this set, often with diacritics or extra letters, is the most widely used writing system, serving over 3,000 languages spoken by about 70% of the global population.

=== KEY: Latin_literature_405725
HEADWORD: Latin literature
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Baruch Spinoza | Cicero | Francis Bacon | Horace | Isaac Newton | Ovid | Thomas Aquinas | Virgil
ENTRY:
Writings in the Latin language, including essays, histories, poems and plays. Formal Latin literature began in 240 BC, when the first Latin stage play was performed in Atella, a town in Campania south of Rome. Early authors adapted Greek works: Livius Andronicus translated Homer's *Odyssey* into Latin, and Quintus Ennius wrote the historical epic *Annals*. Plautus and Terence wrote comedies, and 20 of the 26 surviving early Latin comedies are by Plautus. Cato the Elder wrote the first Latin history of Rome. The golden age, traditionally dated from 81 BC to AD 17, produced [[Cicero]], [[Virgil]], [[Ovid]] and [[Horace]]. Latin remained the lingua franca of Western and Central Europe through the Middle Ages, and European writers after the fall of the Roman Empire, including [[Thomas Aquinas]], [[Francis Bacon]], [[Baruch Spinoza]] and [[Isaac Newton]], also wrote in Latin.

=== KEY: Latin_script_888a78
HEADWORD: Latin script
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: ASCII | Greek alphabet | International Phonetic Alphabet | Roman Empire | Unicode
ENTRY:
Writing system based on the letters of the classical Latin alphabet, also called the Roman script. Its ancestor was a form of the [[Greek alphabet]] used in the ancient Greek city of Cumae, which the Etruscans altered and the ancient Romans later changed. The script is the basis of the [[International Phonetic Alphabet]] and the basis for the largest number of alphabets of any writing system. It is the standard method of writing the languages of Western and Central Europe, most of sub-Saharan Africa, the Americas and Oceania, as well as many other languages. Its spread followed the [[Roman Empire]] from the Italian Peninsula to the lands around the Mediterranean Sea. The 26 most widespread letters form the ISO basic Latin alphabet, which matches the English alphabet. The American Standard Code for Information Interchange ([[ASCII]]) included the 26 × 2 uppercase and lowercase letters, and later [[Unicode]] standards extended the set to other letters.

=== KEY: Latitude_3b0eb7
HEADWORD: Latitude
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Equator | Geodesy | Longitude | North Pole | South Pole
ENTRY:
Geographic coordinate specifying the north-south position of a point on the surface of the Earth or another celestial body. It is given as an angle from −90° at the [[South Pole]] to 90° at the [[North Pole]], with 0° at the [[Equator]]. Lines of constant latitude, called parallels, run east-west as circles parallel to the equator. Latitude and [[Longitude]] are used together as a coordinate pair. On its own, the term normally refers to geodetic latitude, the angle between the perpendicular to an ellipsoidal reference surface and the plane of the equator. Because there are many reference ellipsoids, a precise latitude is not unique without a specified coordinate reference system. Precise measurement requires an understanding of the Earth's gravitational field, the subject of [[Geodesy]], and latitude is determined in celestial navigation with the meridian altitude method.

=== KEY: Launch_vehicle_5e11b0
HEADWORD: Launch vehicle
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Low Earth orbit | NASA | Outer space | Satellite | SpaceX | Spacecraft
ENTRY:
Rocket-powered vehicle designed to carry a payload, such as a crewed [[Spacecraft|spacecraft]] or [[Satellite|satellites]], from Earth's surface or lower atmosphere to [[Outer space|outer space]]. The most common form is a multistage rocket shaped like a ballistic missile, and most launch vehicles operate from a launch pad. An orbital launch vehicle must lift its payload at least to the boundary of space, about 150 km (93 mi), and accelerate it to a horizontal velocity of at least 7,814 m/s (17,480 mph). Practical orbital vehicles use chemical propellants such as solid fuel, liquid hydrogen, kerosene or liquid oxygen. [[NASA|NASA]] classifies vehicles by [[Low Earth orbit|low Earth orbit]] payload capacity, from small-lift vehicles below 2,000 kilograms to super-heavy-lift vehicles above 50,000 kilograms. Expendable vehicles are designed for one-time use, while reusable vehicles such as the [[SpaceX]] Falcon 9 are recovered and launched again.

=== KEY: Laundry_51f0cc
HEADWORD: Laundry
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Clothing | Industrial Revolution | Washing machine
ENTRY:
Washing of [[Clothing|clothing]] and other textiles, along with their drying and ironing. Laundry has been part of history since humans began to wear clothes, and it has traditionally been highly gendered, with women responsible in most cultures. Early laundering took place in watercourses, where flowing water carried away stains and smells, and the cloth was rubbed, twisted or slapped against flat rocks. Villages across Europe later built communal wash-houses, often with two basins, one for washing and one for rinsing, which became meeting places for women. The [[Industrial Revolution]] led to mechanized solutions, notably the [[Washing machine|washing machine]] and later the tumble dryer. Laundry is still done both at home and by commercial establishments; a self-service laundry is called a launderette in British English or a laundromat in North American English.

=== KEY: Lava_1d18fe
HEADWORD: Lava
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ketchup | Viscosity | Volcano
ENTRY:
Molten rock expelled from the interior of a terrestrial planet or moon onto its surface. Lava may erupt at a [[Volcano|volcano]] or through a fracture in the crust, on land or underwater, usually at temperatures of 800 to 1,200 °C (1,470 to 2,190 °F). A lava flow is an outpouring of lava during an effusive eruption, whereas an explosive eruption produces a mixture of volcanic ash and other fragments called tephra. The [[Viscosity|viscosity]] of most molten lava is about that of [[Ketchup|ketchup]], roughly 10,000 to 100,000 times that of water, yet lava can flow great distances because exposed lava quickly forms a crust that insulates the liquid beneath. Silica content largely determines viscosity: felsic lavas have a silica content greater than 63% and are extremely viscous, so they usually erupt explosively. The word comes from Italian, where it originally described a stream caused by sudden rain.

=== KEY: Law_81588d
HEADWORD: Law
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Civil law (legal system) | Common law | Criminal law | Hammurabi | Justice | Religious law | Thomas Aquinas
ENTRY:
System of rules created and enforced by governmental or societal institutions to regulate behavior; its precise definition is a matter of longstanding debate. It has been variously described as a science and as the art of justice, and it shapes politics, economics, history, and society while mediating relations between people. The word comes from Old Norse *lǫg*, attested in Old English as *lagu*.

State-enforced laws can be made by a legislature, resulting in statutes; by the executive through decrees and regulations; or by judges' decisions, which form precedent in [[Common law|common law]] jurisdictions. A constitution, written or tacit, and the rights it encodes may influence their creation. Legal systems differ between jurisdictions, a subject of comparative law. In [[Civil law (legal system)|civil law]] jurisdictions a legislature or other central body codifies and consolidates the law, while in common law systems judges may make binding case law. [[Religious law]], such as Sharia or Halakha, is used in some religious communities and states and has historically influenced secular law. Customary law rests on communal practice.

The scope of law is commonly divided into two domains. Public law concerns government and society, including constitutional, administrative, and [[Criminal law|criminal]] law; private law deals with disputes between parties in areas such as contracts, property, and torts. The distinction is stronger in civil law countries and less pronounced in common law jurisdictions.

The philosophy of law is known as jurisprudence. Normative jurisprudence asks what law should be, analytic jurisprudence what law is. John Austin called law "the command of a sovereign, backed by the threat of a sanction"; H. L. A. Hart described it as a system of primary and secondary rules; Ronald Dworkin, as an "interpretive concept"; and Joseph Raz, as an "authority." On morality, natural law thinkers such as [[Thomas Aquinas]] and Hugo Grotius held that law reflects moral principles, whereas positivists such as Jeremy Bentham and Austin argued that real law is separate from morality. Hans Kelsen, in 1934, held that law is separate from morality yet endowed with "normativity."

Law's history is closely linked to the development of civilization. Ancient Egyptian law, dating as far back as 3000 BC, was based on the concept of Ma'at. By the 22nd century BC the Sumerian ruler Ur-Nammu had formulated the first law code, and around 1760 BC King [[Hammurabi]] codified Babylonian law and inscribed it in stone. Law also raises issues of equality, fairness, and [[Justice|justice]], and its application is debated in terms of legitimacy and the balance between state power and individual liberty.

=== KEY: Law_of_large_numbers_20659c
HEADWORD: Law of large numbers
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Expected value | Monte Carlo method
ENTRY:
Mathematical law of probability theory stating that the average of results from a large number of independent random samples converges to the true value. A casino may lose money on a single spin of a roulette wheel, but its earnings tend toward a predictable percentage over many spins. The law applies only when many observations are considered: a streak of one outcome is not immediately balanced by the others. The [[Expected value|expected value]] of a six-sided die roll is 3.5, and the average of many rolls approaches 3.5 as more dice are rolled. Similarly, the proportion of heads in many fair coin flips approaches 1⁄2. The law underlies the [[Monte Carlo method]], which relies on repeated random sampling. The average of results from some heavy-tailed distributions, such as the Cauchy distribution, does not converge. The law is used in statistics, probability theory, economics and insurance.

=== KEY: Law_of_war_b20b72
HEADWORD: Law of war
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Augustine of Hippo | Code of Hammurabi | Geneva Conventions | International Criminal Court
ENTRY:
Part of international law that regulates the conditions for initiating war and the conduct of hostilities. It includes *jus ad bellum*, which governs the right to use force and includes the crime of aggression, and *jus in bello*, the law applicable during wartime. Modern rules address declarations of war, the treatment of prisoners of war, the principles of distinction, military necessity and proportionality, and the prohibition of weapons that cause unnecessary or excessive suffering. The first traces of such law appear in the [[Code of Hammurabi|Code of Hammurabi]] of 1750 BC, and [[Augustine of Hippo|Augustine of Hippo]] wrote on the doctrine of just war. The four [[Geneva Conventions|Geneva Conventions]] of 1949 are the foundational modern instruments, and violations can be prosecuted before the [[International Criminal Court|International Criminal Court]].

=== KEY: Laws_of_thermodynamics_2b1368
HEADWORD: Laws of thermodynamics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Absolute zero | Entropy
ENTRY:
Set of physical laws defining quantities such as temperature, energy and entropy in systems at thermodynamic equilibrium. The zeroth law defines thermal equilibrium and forms the basis for temperature: if two systems are each in equilibrium with a third, they are in equilibrium with each other. The first law states that a system's internal energy changes with work and heat in accordance with the conservation of energy. The second law states that the sum of the [[Entropy|entropies]] of interacting systems never decreases, so heat does not spontaneously pass from a colder body to a warmer one. The third law states that a system's entropy approaches a constant value as temperature approaches [[Absolute zero|absolute zero]]. The first and second laws prohibit perpetual motion machines of the first and second kinds.

=== KEY: Layoff_6daa99
HEADWORD: Layoff
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Euphemism
ENTRY:
Involuntary termination of an employee's job for business reasons, usually as a cost-cutting measure. Originally, *layoff* meant a temporary interruption in work and pay; it now usually means the permanent elimination of a position, so *temporary layoff* is used for the older sense. Other [[Euphemism|euphemisms]] for permanent layoffs include downsizing, rightsizing and reduction in force (RIF). Downsizing became popular in companies during the 1980s and early 1990s as a way to cut costs and deliver better shareholder value. The U.S. Department of Labor defines a mass layoff as 50 or more workers laid off from the same company around the same time. Layoffs imply economic forces beyond anyone's control, while firings imply misconduct or failure. A study of 391 downsizing announcements by S&P 100 firms from 1990 to 2006 found that stock prices rose after layoff announcements, by more at firms that had laid off workers before.

=== KEY: Leap_year_cfbf55
HEADWORD: Leap year
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Gregorian calendar | Julian calendar | Julius Caesar | Leap second
ENTRY:
Calendar year containing an added day, or in lunisolar calendars an added month. The solar year lasts slightly less than 365¼ days, so calendars need extra time to stay aligned with the seasons. [[Julius Caesar]] reformed the Roman calendar in 45 BC, adding a leap day every 4 years to create the [[Julian calendar]]. The [[Gregorian calendar]], introduced in 1582 by Pope Gregory XIII, refined the rule: years divisible by 100 are leap years only if also divisible by 400, so 2000 was a leap year but 1900 was not. Lunisolar calendars, traditional across much of Asia, add a leap month instead. The term probably comes from the way a fixed date skips a weekday, advancing two days rather than one, in the year after a leap day. From 1972 to 2016, [[Leap second|leap seconds]] were also added to UTC.

=== KEY: Learning_8af0f5
HEADWORD: Learning
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ivan Pavlov | John B. Watson | Lev Vygotsky | Memory | Pedagogy | Play (activity)
ENTRY:
Process of acquiring new understanding, knowledge, behavior, skills, values, attitudes, and preferences. The ability to learn is possessed by humans, other animals, and some machines, and there is evidence for some kind of learning in certain plants. Some learning is immediate, induced by a single event such as being burned by a hot stove, but much skill and knowledge accumulates from repeated experiences. Its effects often last a lifetime.

Human learning begins at birth, may start before it, and continues until death through interaction with the environment. Habituation has been observed as early as 32 weeks into gestation, indicating that the nervous system is developed enough for learning and [[Memory|memory]] to occur very early. The subject is studied in educational psychology, neuropsychology, cognitive science, and [[Pedagogy|pedagogy]].

Researchers distinguish several kinds. Non-associative learning is a relatively permanent change in the strength of response to a single stimulus due to repeated exposure; it includes habituation, in which an innate response diminishes when a stimulus is repeated, and sensitization, in which a response is progressively amplified. Associative learning includes classical conditioning, studied by [[Ivan Pavlov]], and operant conditioning, which links voluntary behavior to consequences. Behaviorism was pioneered by figures such as [[John B. Watson]] and B. F. Skinner, and observational learning was theorized by Albert Bandura. Learning that an aversive event cannot be avoided may result in learned helplessness.

[[Play (activity)|Play]] has been approached as a form of learning in which children experiment with the world and learn rules; [[Lev Vygotsky]] held it to be the first form of learning language and communication. Evolutionary research suggests that learning is favored over innate behavior in environments that are neither completely stable nor entirely unpredictable.

=== KEY: Least_squares_19fc02

ENTRY:
Method of finding a best-fit model by minimizing the sum of squared residuals, the differences between observed and predicted values. === KEY: Lens_443b8e
HEADWORD: Lens
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Microscope | Pliny the Elder | Refraction | Telescope
ENTRY:
Transmissive optical device that focuses or disperses a light beam by means of [[Refraction|refraction]]. A simple lens consists of a single piece of transparent material; a compound lens consists of several simple lenses (elements), usually arranged along a common axis. Lenses are made from materials such as glass or plastic and are ground, polished, or molded to shape. Unlike a prism, a lens can focus light to form an image.

Lenses are used in [[Telescope|telescopes]], binoculars, and cameras, and in glasses that correct defects of vision such as myopia. The word comes from Latin *lēns*, the name of the lentil, because a double-convex lens is lentil-shaped.

Scholars disagree on how widely lenses were used in antiquity. The Nimrud lens, a rock crystal artifact dated to the 7th century BCE, may or may not have served as a magnifying or burning glass. The oldest certain reference to a burning glass is in Aristophanes' play *The Clouds* (424 BCE), and [[Pliny the Elder]] confirmed that burning glasses were known in the Roman period. Between the 11th and 13th centuries "reading stones," primitive plano-convex lenses made by cutting a glass sphere in half, were used. Spectacles were invented in northern Italy in the second half of the 13th century.

Spectacle makers' experiments led to the compound optical [[Microscope|microscope]] around 1595 and the refracting telescope in 1608, both in the Netherlands. Because no single-element lens could bring all colors to a focus, the compound achromatic lens was invented by Chester Moore Hall in England in 1733, an invention also claimed by John Dollond in a 1758 patent. Fresnel lenses, which use less material through concentric annular sectioning, were first fully implemented in a lighthouse in 1823.

=== KEY: Lenticular_galaxy_4e8ad5
HEADWORD: Lenticular galaxy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Elliptical galaxy | Galaxy morphological classification | Interstellar medium | Spiral galaxy | Star formation
ENTRY:
Type of galaxy intermediate between [[Elliptical galaxy|elliptical]] and [[Spiral galaxy|spiral]] galaxies, denoted S0. It has a large-scale disk but no large-scale spiral arms. Lenticular galaxies have used up or lost most of their [[Interstellar medium|interstellar matter]], so they have very little ongoing [[Star formation|star formation]] and consist mainly of aging stars, though they may retain significant dust in their disks. Their featureless disk does not fit the spiral classes, and their spherical bulge does not fit the elliptical ones, so they are subdivided by dust content (S01 to S03) or by a central bar (SB01 to SB03). Lenticular and elliptical galaxies share spectral features and scaling relations, and both are considered passively evolving early-type galaxies in the local universe. They are often viewed as a poorly understood transition state between spiral and elliptical galaxies in [[Galaxy morphological classification|morphological classification]].

He found that for a polyhedron with no holes, vertices plus faces minus edges equals 2, now known as the [[Euler characteristic]].

He introduced much of modern mathematical terminology and notation, including the notion of a function. He popularized the Greek letter π for the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, first used the notation f(x), the letter *i* for the imaginary unit, Σ for summation, and Δ for finite differences, and gave the current definition of the constant [[E (mathematical constant)|e]], now known as Euler's number.

In physics he reformulated [[Newton's laws of motion]] in his two-volume *Mechanica* to better explain the motion of rigid bodies, formulated the partial differential equations for the motion of inviscid fluid, and laid the mathematical foundations of potential theory.

His 866 publications and his correspondence were collected in the *Opera Omnia Leonhard Euler*. [[Pierre-Simon Laplace]] said, "Read Euler, read Euler, he is the master of us all." Euler is regarded as arguably the most prolific contributor in the history of mathematics and science, and the greatest mathematician of the 18th century.

Soviet politician and General Secretary of the [[Communist Party of the Soviet Union]] from 1964 to 1982. He also served as [[Head of state|head of state]] from 1960 to 1964 and again from 1977 to 1982. Brezhnev took part in removing [[Nikita Khrushchev]] in 1964, shared power in a collective leadership, and by the end of the 1960s had become the dominant figure in the Soviet leadership. He pursued détente with the [[United States]] while achieving nuclear parity, and military interventions expanded Soviet influence in the Middle East and Africa. The 1968 Warsaw Pact invasion of [[Czechoslovakia]] enforced the Brezhnev Doctrine of limited sovereignty over socialist states. His rule saw increased repression and censorship, and an era of stagnation marked by shrinking consumer goods and declining growth. [[Mikhail Gorbachev]] later denounced his government for inefficiency, yet polls in post-Soviet Russia have shown consistently high approval of his rule.
