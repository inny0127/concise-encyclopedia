=== KEY: Psychology_1d5b12
HEADWORD: Psychology
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Behaviorism | Hippocrates | Immanuel Kant | John B. Watson | Neuroscience | Psychotherapy | Wilhelm Wundt | William James
ENTRY:
Scientific study of the mind and behavior. Its subject matter includes the behavior of humans and nonhumans, conscious and unconscious phenomena, and mental processes such as thoughts, feelings, and motives. It is an academic discipline of broad scope that crosses the boundaries between the natural and social sciences. A professional practitioner or researcher is called a psychologist, and some are also classed as behavioral or cognitive scientists. Biological psychologists seek to understand the emergent properties of brains, linking the field to [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], while others, as social scientists, study the behavior of individuals and groups. Research covers perception, cognition, attention, emotion, intelligence, motivation, brain functioning, personality, interpersonal relationships, and the unconscious mind. Research psychologists use empirical methods to infer causal and correlational relationships between psychosocial variables. Psychological knowledge is applied to the assessment and treatment of mental health problems, and many psychologists practice [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] in clinical, counseling, or school settings. Others conduct research, usually in academic settings, work in industrial and organizational settings, or study human development, aging, sports, health, forensic science, education, and the media.

The word derives from the Greek *psyche*, "spirit" or "soul," and *-logia*, "study." In its Latin form *psychiologia* it was first used by the Croatian humanist Marko Marulić between 1510 and 1520; the earliest known English use is by Steven Blankaart in 1694. In 1890 [[William James]] defined psychology as "the science of mental life, both of its phenomena and their conditions." In 1913 [[John B. Watson]] contested this, asserting a methodological [[Behaviorism|behaviorist]] view of psychology as a purely objective experimental branch of natural science whose goal is "the prediction and control of behavior."

The ancient civilizations of Egypt, Greece, China, India, and Persia all engaged in philosophical study of the mind. [[Hippocrates]] theorized that mental disorders had physical rather than supernatural causes. In Germany, Christian Wolff identified psychology as its own science, writing *Psychologia Empirica* in 1732 and *Psychologia Rationalis* in 1734, while [[Immanuel Kant]] rejected the idea of an experimental psychology. The discipline is commonly dated as an independent science from 1879, when [[Wilhelm Wundt]] established the first experimental psychology laboratory, at the University of Leipzig. Behaviorism, which prioritized observable responses over internal states, was followed in the mid-20th century by the cognitive revolution, which brought information-processing models. Cognitive-behavioral therapy is an evidence-based intervention for disorders such as depression and anxiety. Since the 2010s a replication crisis has been documented: a 2015 Open Science Collaboration project reproduced 36% of 100 studies, prompting reforms such as preregistration and open data. A 2010 analysis by Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan noted that most research participants come from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic societies, raising questions about generalizing results.

=== KEY: Psychopathy_c78049
HEADWORD: Psychopathy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Criminology | Psychosis
ENTRY:
Personality construct characterized by impaired empathy and remorse, persistent antisocial behavior, and bold, disinhibited and egocentric traits. These traits are often masked by superficial charm and immunity to stress. The American psychiatrist Hervey M. Cleckley influenced early diagnostic criteria for antisocial personality disorder in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders. Canadian psychologist Robert D. Hare later re-popularized the construct in [[Criminology|criminology]] with his Psychopathy Checklist. Although no psychiatric or psychological organization has sanctioned a formal diagnosis of this kind, assessments are used in criminal justice settings in some nations. A 2023 umbrella review and meta-analysis found a pooled correlation of r = 0.284 between the construct and dangerousness, including violent recidivism and self-reported aggression. It is categorically different from [[Psychosis|psychosis]].

=== KEY: Psychosis_5c3fc0
HEADWORD: Psychosis
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Bipolar disorder | Delusion | Dopamine | Hallucination | Hippocrates | Psychopathy | Psychotherapy | Schizophrenia
ENTRY:
Inability to distinguish what is real from what is not, characterized by [[Delusion|delusions]], [[Hallucination|hallucinations]] and disorganized thoughts or speech. Psychosis describes a person's state or symptoms rather than a specific mental illness, and it is unrelated to [[Psychopathy|psychopathy]]. Common causes of chronic psychosis include [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]], [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]] and brain damage, usually from alcoholism. Acute psychosis can be triggered by severe distress, sleep deprivation, some medications, or drug use and withdrawal. Treatment may include antipsychotic medication, [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapy]] and social support, and early treatment appears to improve outcomes. [[Dopamine|Dopamine]], along with other neurotransmitters, is known to play an important role, although the condition is not well understood at the neurological level. About 3% of people in the United States develop psychosis at some point in their lives. A similar condition was described by [[Hippocrates|Hippocrates]] in the 4th century BCE.

=== KEY: Psychotherapy_3fc302
HEADWORD: Psychotherapy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Mental disorder | Pseudoscience
ENTRY:
Use of psychological methods, usually through regular personal interaction, to help a person change behavior, increase happiness and overcome problems. Also called talk therapy, it aims to improve well-being and mental health, to resolve troublesome behaviors, beliefs, compulsions, thoughts or emotions, and to improve relationships. There are hundreds of techniques, most involving one-to-one sessions, though some are conducted with groups, couples or families. Psychotherapists may be psychiatrists, psychologists, mental health nurses, clinical social workers or licensed counselors. Some types are evidence-based for treating [[Mental disorder|mental disorders]], while others have been criticized as [[Pseudoscience|pseudoscience]]. A 2022 umbrella review of 102 meta-analyses found that effect sizes for both psychotherapies and medications were generally small. Because many forms produce similar outcomes, theories hold that common factors, such as the therapeutic relationship, are key drivers of efficacy.

=== KEY: Pteridophyte_a3cba8
HEADWORD: Pteridophyte
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Fern | Lycophyte
ENTRY:
Vascular plant with xylem and phloem that reproduces by spores rather than flowers or seeds. Because pteridophytes produce neither flowers nor seeds, they are sometimes called cryptogams, meaning their means of reproduction is hidden. [[Fern|Ferns]], horsetails and [[Lycophyte|lycophytes]] such as clubmosses and quillworts are all pteridophytes, but they do not form a monophyletic group, because ferns and horsetails are more closely related to seed plants than to lycophytes. As a result, *Pteridophyta* is no longer a widely accepted taxon, although the term remains in common use. Pteridophytes have free-living gametophyte and sporophyte phases that are independent at maturity. Ferns account for nearly 90% of extant pteridophyte diversity, and fossils date back more than 400 million years.

=== KEY: Pterodactylus_590826
HEADWORD: Pterodactylus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Quetzalcoatlus
ENTRY:
Genus of extinct pterosaurs, thought to contain only one species, *Pterodactylus antiquus*, the first pterosaur to be named and identified as a flying reptile. Fossils have mainly been found in the Solnhofen limestone of Bavaria, Germany, dating from the Late Jurassic, about 150.8 to 148.5 million years ago. A small generalist carnivore, it probably ate a variety of invertebrates and vertebrates. Its wing membrane stretched from an elongated fourth finger to its hind limbs, and it lived earlier than the much larger [[Quetzalcoatlus|Quetzalcoatlus]] and Pteranodon, which lived in the Late Cretaceous.

=== KEY: Pteropus_b38870
HEADWORD: Pteropus
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Crop | Megabat
ENTRY:
Genus of [[Megabat|megabats]], among the largest bats in the world, commonly known as fruit bats or flying foxes. At least 60 extant species live in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Australia, East Africa and oceanic islands of the Indian and Pacific Oceans. They eat fruit and other plant matter, navigate with keen eyesight and smell rather than echolocation, and are mostly nocturnal. Most species produce only one offspring per year, so populations are vulnerable to overhunting, and six flying fox species have been made extinct in modern times. Flying foxes are often persecuted for damaging [[Crop|crops]], yet they benefit forests by dispersing seeds and pollinating plants. They are reservoirs of disease agents including Hendra virus and Australian bat lyssavirus, and Nipah virus, also carried by flying foxes, has over 100 attributed fatalities.

=== KEY: Pterosaur_62aa57
HEADWORD: Pterosaur
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Dinosaur | Quetzalcoatlus
ENTRY:
Extinct order of warm-blooded flying reptiles, the earliest vertebrates known to have evolved powered flight. They lived from the Late Triassic to the end of the Cretaceous, about 228 to 66 million years ago. Their wings were formed by a membrane of skin, muscle and other tissues stretching from the ankles to a dramatically lengthened fourth finger. Basal pterosaurs were smaller, with toothed jaws and typically long tails, while later pterodactyloids had narrower wings, reduced tails and long necks with large heads. Their bodies were covered in hair-like filaments called pycnofibers, which may be homologous to the down feathers of [[Dinosaur|dinosaurs]]. Sizes ranged from very small anurognathids to giants such as [[Quetzalcoatlus|Quetzalcoatlus]] and Hatzegopteryx, which reached wingspans of at least nine meters. Although often called flying dinosaurs, pterosaurs were not dinosaurs; they were more closely related to birds and dinosaurs than to crocodiles.

=== KEY: Pterygota_1280b7
HEADWORD: Pterygota
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Beetle | Fly | Hymenoptera | Lepidoptera | Odonata
ENTRY:
Subclass of insects that includes all winged insects and groups that lost their wings secondarily. It comprises 99.9% of all insects; the excluded orders are the primitively wingless Archaeognatha (jumping bristletails) and Zygentoma (silverfishes and firebrats). The oldest known representatives appeared during the mid-Carboniferous, around 328–324 million years ago, and the group rapidly diversified. Claims of earlier origins in the Silurian or Devonian, based on molecular clock estimates, are likely analytical artifacts. Traditionally divided into the infraclasses Paleoptera and Neoptera, the Paleoptera are now strongly suspected of being paraphyletic. Its orders include [[Beetle|beetles]], [[Fly|flies]], [[Lepidoptera|butterflies and moths]], [[Hymenoptera|ants and bees]] and [[Odonata|dragonflies and damselflies]].

=== KEY: Ptolemaic_Kingdom_a75353
HEADWORD: Ptolemaic Kingdom
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Alexandria | Cleopatra | Ptolemy I Soter
ENTRY:
Hellenistic Greek kingdom based in Egypt, founded in 305 BC by the Macedonian general [[Ptolemy I Soter|Ptolemy I Soter]]. Its capital was [[Alexandria|Alexandria]], a city founded by [[Alexander the Great|Alexander the Great]] that became a major center of Greek culture, learning and trade. The Ptolemies were the longest and final dynasty of ancient Egypt; they adopted the title of pharaoh and Egyptian customs, such as sibling marriage, while a Greek elite dominated the military and government. From the mid-3rd century BC, Ptolemaic Egypt was the wealthiest of Alexander's successor states, and under Ptolemy III it was the greatest power in the Mediterranean. Dynastic strife and foreign wars weakened the kingdom from the mid-2nd century BC. Under [[Cleopatra|Cleopatra VII]], Egypt was drawn into a Roman civil war and was conquered by Rome in 30 BC, becoming the last independent Hellenistic state.

=== KEY: Ptolemy_I_Soter_8eddc6
HEADWORD: Ptolemy I Soter
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alexander the Great | Library of Alexandria | Ptolemaic Kingdom | Seven Wonders of the Ancient World
ENTRY:
Macedonian Greek general, historian and pharaoh of Egypt (r. 305–282 BC). A trusted companion of [[Alexander the Great|Alexander the Great]], he was one of the seven bodyguards and held an independent command in the campaigns. After Alexander's death in 323 BC he took control of Egypt, and by 305 BC he had declared himself king, founding the [[Ptolemaic Kingdom|Ptolemaic Kingdom]]. He retrieved Alexander's body as it was en route to Macedon and placed it in Memphis, from where it was later moved to Alexandria. He also commanded the construction of the [[Library of Alexandria|Library of Alexandria]] and the Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the [[Seven Wonders of the Ancient World|Seven Wonders of the Ancient World]]. He also wrote a history of Alexander's campaigns.

=== KEY: Ptolemy_d12eb0
HEADWORD: Ptolemy
PERSON: yes
LINK TARGETS: Alexandria | Astrology | Catholic Church | Optics
ENTRY:
Greco-Roman writer on astronomy, geography, astrology and music theory, who lived and worked in [[Alexandria|Alexandria]] under Roman rule. His treatise on astronomy, the *Almagest*, provided mathematical models and tables for celestial motion, including a catalog of 1,022 stars, and its geocentric model remained the standard astronomical reference in Europe and the Islamic world until the 17th century. His *Geography* discussed maps and the geographic knowledge of the Greco-Roman world, using latitude and longitude to map the known world. The *Tetrábiblos* attempted to adapt horoscopic [[Astrology|astrology]] to Aristotelian natural philosophy. He also wrote on [[Optics|optics]] and harmonics. The [[Catholic Church|Catholic Church]] promoted his work, which was copied and studied through the Middle Ages.

=== KEY: Puberty_2ea377
HEADWORD: Puberty
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Adolescence | Ejaculation | Hormone
ENTRY:
Process of physical change through which a child's body matures into an adult body capable of sexual reproduction. It is initiated by hormonal signals from the brain to the gonads, which produce [[Hormone|hormones]] that drive growth of the brain, bones, muscle, skin, breasts and sex organs. On average, females begin puberty at age 10.5 and complete it at 15–17; males begin at 11.5–12 and complete it at 16–17. The landmark for females is menarche, the onset of menstruation, at about age 12.5; for males, [[Ejaculation|first ejaculation]] occurs on average at age 13. Puberty that starts earlier or later than usual is called precocious or delayed puberty. The word describes physical changes, while the psychosocial transition from child to adult is termed [[Adolescence|adolescence]]. Females reach reproductive maturity about four years after the first physical changes, whereas males keep growing for about six years after theirs.

=== KEY: Pubic_hair_fd4fbe
HEADWORD: Pubic hair
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Estrogen | Puberty
ENTRY:
Terminal body hair found in the genital area and pubic region of adolescent and adult humans. It is heavier, longer and coarser than the fine vellus hair of childhood and develops during [[Puberty|puberty]] in response to rising androgens in males and [[Estrogen|estrogens]] in females, an onset known as pubarche. The Tanner scale quantifies its development in five stages. Many cultures regard it as erotic, and in some cultures removal, especially of female pubic hair, is considered part of personal hygiene.

=== KEY: Public_3d067b
HEADWORD: Public
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Community | John Dewey | Market (economics) | Public relations
ENTRY:
Group of individual people concerned with a common issue, as the term is used in public relations and communication science. The public, or general public, is the totality of such groupings. The word derives from Latin *publicus*, from *populus*, meaning people. In public relations, [[John Dewey|John Dewey]] defined a public as a group of people who, facing a similar problem, recognize it and organize to address it. James E. A public is a subset of an organization's [[Public relations|stakeholders]] and, unlike a [[Market (economics)|market]], does not necessarily have an exchange relationship. The concept has often been blurred with audience, market segment, [[Community|community]] and constituency.

=== KEY: Public_administration_83148d
HEADWORD: Public administration
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Max Weber | Political science | Woodrow Wilson
ENTRY:
Implementation of public policies, which are proposed or decided actions to solve social and economic problems. It generally occurs through government programs in the public sector, but also through nonprofit organizations and businesses that supply goods and services to government. It is closely related to [[Political science|political science]] and is taught in public policy schools as a subfield of it. Carroll and Zuck called [[Woodrow Wilson|Woodrow Wilson]]'s essay "The Study of Administration" the beginning of public administration as a specific and influential field of study. The mid-20th century saw the rise of [[Max Weber|Max Weber]]'s theory of bureaucracy, which brought substantive interest in the field's theoretical aspects. The discipline is also recognized as applicable to private businesses and nonprofit organizations.

=== KEY: Public_domain_675db4
HEADWORD: Public domain
PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Creative works to which no exclusive intellectual property rights apply, so anyone may use them without permission. Rights may have expired, been forfeited or waived, or may never have applied. Works by [[William Shakespeare|William Shakespeare]], [[Ludwig van Beethoven|Ludwig van Beethoven]] and [[Miguel de Cervantes|Miguel de Cervantes]] are public domain because they predate [[Copyright|copyright]] or their terms have expired. === KEY: Public_health_8b4110
HEADWORD: Public health
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Epidemiology | Sanitation | Vaccination
ENTRY:
Science of preventing disease, prolonging life and promoting health through the organized efforts and informed choices of society, organizations, communities and individuals. It is an interdisciplinary field drawing on [[Epidemiology|epidemiology]], biostatistics, social sciences and health services management. Its work includes surveillance of cases and health indicators and promotion of healthy behavior, such as hand-washing, [[Vaccination|vaccination]], ventilation, smoking cessation and condom distribution. Great Britain led early development in the 19th century, with work on [[Sanitation|sanitation]], including the Liverpool and London sewerage systems, and on infectious diseases through vaccination and quarantine. In developing countries, public health infrastructure is still forming, and poor maternal and child health, malnutrition and poverty are major concerns. Developed nations face risks such as childhood obesity.

=== KEY: Public_key_cryptography_2ca927
HEADWORD: Public-key cryptography
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Transport Layer Security
ENTRY:
Cryptographic system that uses pairs of related keys: a public key that can be openly distributed and a private key that must be kept secret. Key pairs are generated with algorithms based on one-way functions. Before the mid-1970s, all cipher systems used symmetric keys shared by both parties, which became unmanageable as the number of participants grew. The main types are digital signature and public-key encryption. Public key algorithms underpin standards such as [[Transport Layer Security|Transport Layer Security]] (TLS), SSH, S/MIME and PGP, usually combined with symmetric cryptography in hybrid systems because public-key methods can be too slow for many purposes.

=== KEY: Public_policy_ad0981
HEADWORD: Public policy
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Economics | Public administration
ENTRY:
Set of laws, regulations, guidelines and other actions that a government implements or proposes to address social issues. It is often implemented through programs in education, health care, employment, finance, [[Economics|economics]] and transportation, and its implementation is known as [[Public administration|public administration]]. Scholars define it either as the sum of a government's direct and indirect activities or by its stated goals. A popular model is the policy cycle, first discussed by political scientist Harold Lasswell in *The Decision Process* (1956), with the stages of agenda setting, policy formulation, legitimation, implementation and evaluation. Policies are made by many actors, including elected politicians, party leaders, civil servants, judges and pressure groups. Common instruments include regulations, subsidies, taxes and spending plans.

=== KEY: Public_relations_149d03
HEADWORD: Public relations
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Advertising | Company | Journalism | Social media | Website
ENTRY:
Practice of managing information from an organization to the public in order to influence perception. Public relations (PR) differs from publicity in that PR is controlled internally, whereas publicity is contributed by external parties. PR seeks media exposure, often without direct payment, known as earned media, which distinguishes it from paid [[Advertising|advertising]]. PR and [[Journalism|journalism]] share close media relations, but journalism reports events with objectivity, while PR presents developments in ways that support the interests of the organization it represents. Practitioners write press releases, arrange interviews for spokespeople, manage [[Company|company]] reputation and crises, and produce content for [[Social media|social media]] and [[Website|websites]]. The aim is to inform the public, customers, investors and employees, and to persuade them to maintain a favorable view of the organization.

=== KEY: Public_transport_e2e941
HEADWORD: Public transport
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Airline | Aswan | Greek mythology | High-speed rail | Japan | Train
ENTRY:
Transport made available for use by the general public, typically operating on fixed routes and schedules for a standardized fare. Common modes include city buses, trams, rapid transit, passenger trains and ferries. Intercity service is provided mainly by coaches, intercity [[Train|rail]] and [[Airline|airlines]], with [[High-speed rail]] networks under development in several regions. Systems vary widely by region. In [[Japan]], many urban networks are run by private companies, often integrated with real estate development. In North America, mass transit is usually operated by municipal or regional transit authorities funded by fares, local taxes and government subsidies. The earliest public transport was water transport: ferries appear in [[Greek mythology]], and in ancient Egypt canals carried freight around the [[Aswan]] cataract.

=== KEY: Publishing_9fb1f5
HEADWORD: Publishing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Comic book | E-commerce | ISBN | Johannes Gutenberg | Magazine | Newspaper | Peer review | Video game
ENTRY:
Process of making information, literature, music, software, and other content available to the public, in physical or digital form, for sale or free of charge. Traditionally the term meant the creation and distribution of printed works such as books, zines, [[Comic book|comic books]], [[Newspaper|newspapers]], and [[Magazine|magazines]]. Digital systems have extended it to e-books, websites, social media, music, and [[Video game|video game]] publishing. It has evolved from a small, ancient form limited by law or religion into a large-scale modern industry. The development of movable-type printing by [[Johannes Gutenberg]] around 1440 drastically reduced costs and widened access to books.

The commercial industry ranges from multinational conglomerates such as News Corp, Pearson, Penguin Random House, and Thomson Reuters to thousands of small independent publishers. Its divisions include trade publishing of fiction and non-fiction, educational publishing, and academic and scientific publishing. "Publisher" can mean a publishing company, an organization, or an individual who leads a company, imprint, periodical, or newspaper.

Newspapers publish current reports by journalists and are usually subsidized with advertising. Academic and technical journals usually require submissions to undergo [[Peer review|peer review]]. Book publishing covers fiction and non-fiction in print, e-book, and audiobook form, and each book has a registered [[ISBN]], standardized in 1966; self-publishing is made possible by small-run digital printing and online platforms. A university press is run by a university, and Oxford University Press is the largest in the world. Print catalogs have evolved into searchable product databases known as [[E-commerce|e-commerce]]. In 2024 the U.S. industry reported $32.5 billion in aggregate revenue, and the global books market was approximately $151 billion, with print still dominating digital formats.

Capital and largest city of Puebla state, Mexico. Founded in 1531 as a planned Spanish city between the indigenous settlements of Tlaxcala and Cholula, it lies on the main route between [[Mexico City]] and the Atlantic port of [[Veracruz (city)|Veracruz]]. Its history and architecture, ranging from Renaissance to Mexican Baroque, led to its designation as a [[World Heritage Site]] in 1987. The city is known for mole poblano, chiles en nogada and Talavera pottery, but its economy is mostly industrial. It has the world's largest Volkswagen factory outside Germany, and many suppliers to Volkswagen and Audi plants have opened factories in the metropolitan area.

=== KEY: Puerto_Rico_f76257

Caribbean archipelago and unincorporated territory of the [[United States]], officially the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico. Genus of three species of small pelagic [[Seabird|seabirds]] in the [[Auk|auk]] family, *Fratercula*. Puffins feed mainly by diving and breed in large colonies on coastal cliffs or offshore islands. === KEY: Pulley_ba4190
HEADWORD: Pulley
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Ancient Egypt | Archimedes | Chain | Hero of Alexandria | Mesopotamia | Rope
ENTRY:
Wheel on an axle or shaft that guides a taut [[Rope|rope]], [[Chain|chain]] or belt, changing its direction or transferring power. A grooved pulley wheel is also called a sheave. The earliest evidence comes from [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] in the Twelfth Dynasty (1991–1802 BC) and from [[Mesopotamia]] in the early 2nd millennium BC. [[Hero of Alexandria]] identified the pulley as one of six simple machines used to lift weights. A block and tackle, which combines pulleys, gives a mechanical advantage equal to the number of rope sections supporting the moving block. Plutarch recounts that [[Archimedes]] used compound pulleys to draw a fully laden ship toward him as if it were gliding through water. Pulleys also drive belts and chains that transmit power between shafts.

=== KEY: Pulmonology_812def

ENTRY:
Medical specialty concerned with diseases of the respiratory tract, also called pneumology, respirology or respiratory medicine. Pulmonology is a branch of [[Medicine|internal medicine]] closely related to intensive care, since pulmonologists often manage patients who need mechanical ventilation. They treat conditions such as [[Pneumonia]], [[Asthma]], [[Tuberculosis]], [[Emphysema]] and complicated chest infections, and work closely with [[Cardiology]] and cardiothoracic surgery departments. Rotating [[Neutron star|neutron star]] that emits beams of [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]] from its magnetic poles. Group of rock-forming inosilicate minerals with single chains of silica tetrahedra. They are found in many igneous and metamorphic rocks, and pyroxene and [[Feldspar|feldspar]] are the major minerals in [[Basalt|basalt]], andesite and gabbro. The upper mantle is composed mainly of [[Olivine|olivine]] and pyroxene. Clinopyroxenes crystallize in the monoclinic system and orthopyroxenes in the orthorhombic system. The name comes from the Ancient Greek words for fire and stranger, because the crystals appear in volcanic lava and were once assumed to be impurities in the glass, though they formed before the lava erupted.

=== KEY: Pythagorean_theorem_d5f776

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Fundamental relation in [[Euclidean geometry]] between the three sides of a right triangle. The square of the hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle, equals the sum of the squares on the other two sides, written a² + b² = c². The theorem is named after [[Pythagoras]], but the relation is older: the Mesopotamian tablet Plimpton 322, written around 1800 BC, contains entries that can be read as 15 Pythagorean triples, and Old Babylonian mathematicians used the rule widely. The theorem has been proved in many ways, possibly more than any other mathematical theorem.

=== KEY: Qin_Shi_Huang_aae201

ENTRY:
Founder of the Qin dynasty and first emperor of China (r. 221–210 BC). Born Ying Zheng, he became king of Qin with the help of the merchant Lü Buwei. By 221 BC he had conquered all the other [[Warring States period|warring states]] and unified China. Rather than keep the title of king, he created the title *huángdì* ("emperor"), combining the names of the mythical Three Sovereigns and legendary Five Emperors; it was used by monarchs in China and other countries for the next two millennia.

His generals added the Yue lands of Hunan and Guangdong in campaigns south of Chu and took the Ordos Plateau from the [[Xiongnu]] in Inner Asia, although the Xiongnu later rallied. With his minister [[Li Si]] he enacted economic and political reforms standardizing the diverse practices of earlier states, including weights, measures, currency, axle widths, and script, and replaced feudal lords with appointed officials. His public works included the incorporation of state walls into a single [[Great Wall of China]], a national road system, and a city-sized mausoleum guarded by the life-sized [[Terracotta Army]].

He is traditionally said to have banned and burned many books and executed scholars. He has often been portrayed as a tyrant and strict Legalist, characterizations stemming partly from the assessments of the succeeding [[Han dynasty]] and of [[Sima Qian]]. Since the mid-20th century scholars have questioned this evaluation and debated the nature of his policies. He died in 210 BC during his fifth tour of eastern China, and the [[Qin dynasty]] collapsed in 206 BC amid revolts. The sinologist Michael Loewe wrote that the achievements of his reign marked "the start of an epoch that closed in 1911."

=== KEY: Quadratic_equation_a879bc

PERSON: no

ENTRY:
Second-degree polynomial equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0. The name comes from the Latin *quadratus*, "square". The values of x that satisfy it are called roots or zeros. With [[Real number|real]] coefficients, it has two distinct real roots, one repeated real root, or two complex conjugate roots. Minimum amount of any physical property involved in an interaction. A quantized property can take only discrete values, which are integer multiples of one quantum (plural quanta). A [[Photon|photon]] is a single quantum of light, and the energy of an [[Electron|electron]] bound within an [[Atom|atom]] is quantized, which keeps matter stable. Branch of physical chemistry that applies [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] to chemical systems to predict the physical and chemical properties of molecules and materials. Quarks carry three kinds of charge, called color by loose analogy with the three colors perceived by humans; the name comes from the Greek *chrōma*, "color." Color confinement keeps quarks bound within [[Hadron|hadrons]] such as the proton, neutron and pion. Asymptotic freedom, the weakening of interactions at high energies, was discovered in 1973 by David Gross and Frank Wilczek, and independently by David Politzer; the three shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics. Chiral symmetry breaking gives hadrons masses far above those of their quarks. Yoichiro Nambu, awarded the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physics, elucidated the phenomenon in 1960.

=== KEY: Quantum_computing_c89ffe
HEADWORD: Quantum computing
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Quantum superposition | Qubit
ENTRY:
Computer that represents and processes information using quantum states. It exploits phenomena such as [[Quantum superposition|superposition]], interference and entanglement, and can potentially complete some calculations exponentially faster than classical computers. Its basic unit is the [[Qubit]]. A large-scale machine could break widely used encryption schemes and aid physical simulations, but current hardware is largely experimental and decoherence introduces errors. Global government investment reached $10 billion by April 2025. As of 2026, demonstrations of quantum advantage are best understood as scientific milestones rather than evidence for near-term deployment.

=== KEY: Quantum_electrodynamics_bd0abb
HEADWORD: Quantum electrodynamics
PERSON: no
LINK TARGETS: Hans Bethe | Paul Dirac | Photon | Quantum chromodynamics
ENTRY:
Relativistic quantum field theory of electromagnetism, describing how light and matter interact. It covers all phenomena involving electrically charged particles that interact by exchanging [[Photon|photons]], and it is the quantum counterpart of classical electromagnetism. Richard Feynman called it "the jewel of physics" for its extremely accurate predictions, such as the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron and the Lamb shift in hydrogen. [[Paul Dirac]] formulated the first quantum theory of radiation and matter interaction in the 1920s and coined the term. Infinities in higher-order calculations were handled by renormalization; [[Hans Bethe]]'s 1947 computation of the Lamb shift introduced the idea. Tomonaga, Schwinger and Feynman shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics. QED remains the model for later field theories, including [[Quantum chromodynamics]].
