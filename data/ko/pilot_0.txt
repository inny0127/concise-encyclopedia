=== KEY: Abraham_Lincoln_2f7cfa
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: yes
HEADWORD: Lincoln, Abraham
ENTRY:
American lawyer and statesman, 16th president of the United States (1861–65). Born in a one-room log cabin in [[Kentucky]], he grew up on the frontier in Kentucky, Indiana, and [[Illinois]] and was largely self-educated. He became a lawyer, Illinois state legislator, and a one-term U.S. representative. Angered by the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854, which opened territories to slavery, he became a leader of the new [[Republican Party (United States)|Republican Party]] and won national attention in the 1858 Senate campaign debates with Stephen A. Douglas.

Lincoln won the 1860 presidential election as the first Republican president, and most slave states began to secede. A month after he took office, Confederate forces attacked Fort Sumter, beginning the [[American Civil War]] against the Confederate States. He closely supervised Union strategy. In April 1861 he suspended the writ of [[Habeas corpus|habeas corpus]]; Chief Justice Roger Taney held in *Ex parte Merryman* that only Congress could do so. Critics, then and later, saw his expansion of executive power as overreach, while supporters regarded it as necessary to save the Union. The war claimed over 620,000 lives.

On January 1, 1863, Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation, declaring slaves in states "in rebellion" free; it was a war measure and did not cover Union border states. On November 19, 1863, he delivered the [[Gettysburg Address]]. He promoted the Thirteenth Amendment, which abolished chattel slavery in 1865. Re-elected in 1864, he favored a lenient Reconstruction.

On April 14, 1865, five days after the Confederate surrender at Appomattox, Lincoln was shot by [[John Wilkes Booth]] at Ford's Theatre in Washington, D.C., and died the next day, the first U.S. president to be assassinated. He is ranked in popular and scholarly polls among the greatest American presidents.

=== KEY: Adrenaline_abe1a4
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Adrenaline
ENTRY:
Hormone and medication, also known as epinephrine. Normally produced by the [[Adrenal gland|adrenal glands]] and a small number of neurons in the medulla oblongata, it plays an essential role in the fight-or-flight response, increasing blood flow to muscles, increasing [[Heart|heart]] output by acting on the SA node, dilating the pupils and raising [[Blood sugar level|blood sugar]]. It binds to alpha and beta receptors. As a medication, it treats anaphylaxis, [[Cardiac arrest|cardiac arrest]] and superficial bleeding, and inhaled adrenaline may ease croup. It can be used for [[Asthma|asthma]] when other treatments are not effective, and it lowers intraocular pressure in open-angle [[Glaucoma|glaucoma]] by increasing outflow of aqueous humor. Common side effects include shakiness, [[Anxiety|anxiety]] and sweating. The adrenal medulla contributes over 90% of circulating adrenaline, and a small number of neurons use it as a [[Neurotransmitter|neurotransmitter]].

=== KEY: Adult_5a600f
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Adult
ENTRY:
Fully grown animal; in humans, a person who has attained legal and social independence. Biologically, an adult is an organism that has reached sexual maturity and is capable of reproduction. Historically and across cultures, human adulthood was determined chiefly by the onset of [[Puberty|puberty]], and the passage from child to adult was often marked by a coming-of-age test or ceremony. A person may be physically mature by about age 15 or 16 yet not be an adult in law until a later age.

Legally, an adult has reached the age of majority, when parents lose their rights and responsibilities regarding the person. The typical age is 18, although it varies by country, and in some places is 20 or 21. The age of majority may be set separately from the minimum ages for contracts, [[Marriage|marriage]], [[Voting|voting]], employment, military service, [[Firearm|firearms]], driving, alcohol, smoking, gambling, and other activities. In the U.S., for example, one cannot vote or join the armed forces until 18, and many legal and financial responsibilities begin at 21. Most modern societies set legal adulthood by age alone, without requiring a demonstration of maturity.

Definitions of adulthood are often inconsistent. A person may be biologically adult and behave maturely but still be treated as a child under the law, while another may be legally an adult yet lack maturity and responsibility. Media claims since 2005 that the brain reaches full maturity at age 25 rest on a 2004–05 imaging study of participants no older than 21; later studies show prefrontal cortex maturation continuing past age 30. Whether any single age of full mental maturity exists remains debated.

=== KEY: Ancient_Egypt_b7e128
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Ancient Egypt
ENTRY:
Bronze Age civilization along the lower [[Nile]] in northeastern Africa, and a cradle of civilization.

It emerged around 3150 BC (by conventional chronology), when Upper and Lower Egypt were united by Menes, whom most Egyptologists believe to be the same person as [[Narmer]]. Its history comprised stable kingdoms, the Old, Middle, and New, separated by Intermediate Periods of instability. The New Kingdom was the pinnacle of Egyptian power, extending its rule over much of [[Nubia]] and a considerable portion of the [[Levant]]. Egypt then slowly declined and was invaded or conquered by the Hyksos, Kushites, Assyrians, Persians, Greeks, and Romans. The end of ancient Egypt is variously placed at 332 BC, during the wars of [[Alexander the Great]], or at 30 BC, when the Greek-ruled [[Ptolemaic Kingdom]] fell to Rome.

Success rested on the Nile's predictable flooding and controlled irrigation, which produced surplus crops and supported dense population. A bureaucracy of scribes, religious leaders, and administrators served the [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]], who was believed to embody the god Horus on earth. Predynastic cultures with firm control of agriculture and animal husbandry had developed by about 5500 BC; the Naqada culture grew over about 1,000 years from small farming communities into a powerful civilization.

Achievements include quarrying, surveying, and construction techniques behind monumental pyramids, temples, and obelisks; mathematics, as in the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus; medicine; irrigation; the first known planked boats; faience and glass technology; and the earliest known peace treaty, with the Hittite Empire. Religion centered on preparation for the afterlife through mummification. Egyptian art and architecture were widely copied, and its ruins inspired travelers and writers for millennia.

=== KEY: Ancient_Egyptian_architecture_02f3ef
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Ancient Egyptian architecture
ENTRY:
Set of changing styles of building in ancient Egypt, most of whose surviving examples are religious and funerary structures. Because wood was scarce, the main materials were sun-dried mud brick and stone, chiefly limestone, with sandstone and granite used in considerable quantities. From the Old Kingdom onward stone was generally reserved for tombs and temples, while brick was used for palaces, fortresses and town walls. Monumental buildings used post-and-lintel construction, with thick sloping walls and closely spaced [[Column|columns]], and many were aligned astronomically. Although the [[Arch]] was developed in the fourth dynasty, monumental buildings did not adopt it. Column capitals were carved to resemble plants such as the [[Papyrus|papyrus]], and walls bore painted carvings. The best-known examples are the [[Pyramid|pyramids]] and the [[Sphinx]]. The Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, built by [[Ramesses II]], has columns up to 20 meters high. Egyptian motifs later influenced architecture elsewhere, first in the Greek Orientalizing period and again in 19th-century Egyptomania.

=== KEY: Capitalism_201179
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Capitalism
ENTRY:
Economic system based on private ownership of the means of production and its use to obtain profit. Its elements include private property, the profit motive, capital accumulation, competitive markets, and wage labor.

Analysts recognize forms including [[Laissez-faire|laissez-faire]], free-market, state, and welfare capitalism, which differ in the degree of free markets, public ownership, and regulation. Most existing economies are mixed economies combining free markets with state intervention and, in some cases, planning.

The modern form emerged from agrarianism in England and from mercantilist practices in European countries between the 16th and 18th centuries. [[Adam Smith]]'s *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) is cited as its intellectual foundation, and the [[Industrial Revolution]] of the 18th century made it the dominant mode of production, characterized by factory work and a complex division of labor. Through [[Globalization|globalization]] it spread across the world in the 19th and 20th centuries. It was largely unregulated by the state in the 19th century, became more regulated after World War II through Keynesianism, and saw a return of less regulated capitalism termed [[Neoliberalism|neoliberalism]] from the 1980s.

The word *capitalist*, meaning an owner of capital, dates to the mid-17th century. The term *capitalism* in its modern sense is attributed to Louis Blanc in 1850 and [[Pierre-Joseph Proudhon]] in 1861; [[Karl Marx]] wrote of the capitalist mode of production in *Das Kapital* (1867).

Assessments differ. Supporters credit capitalist economies with sustained growth in GDP per capita and a decline in extreme poverty from over 90% of the world population in the 19th century to under 10%. Critics point to wealth disparities and recurrent business cycles, which defenders attribute to policy distortions such as subsidies and regulations favoring incumbents.

=== KEY: Chemical_compound_13fc34
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Chemical compound
ENTRY:
Substance composed of many identical molecules containing atoms from more than one [[Chemical element|chemical element]] held together by [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]]. A molecule consisting of atoms of only one element is therefore not a compound. A compound can be transformed into a different substance by a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]], in which bonds between atoms may be broken, formed, or both.

Four major types are distinguished by how the atoms are bonded: molecular compounds, held together by covalent bonds; ionic compounds, held together by ionic bonds; intermetallic compounds, held together by metallic bonds; and coordination complexes, held together by coordinate covalent bonds. Non-stoichiometric compounds, whose proportions may be reproducible but are not integral, as in palladium hydride, PdHₓ (0.02 < x < 0.58), form a disputed marginal case.

A [[Chemical formula|chemical formula]] specifies the number of atoms of each element in a molecule of a compound, using standard chemical symbols with numerical subscripts. Water, for example, consists of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom, with the formula H₂O. Many compounds have a unique CAS number assigned by the Chemical Abstracts Service. Globally, more than 350,000 chemical compounds (including mixtures of chemicals) have been registered for production and use.

The term "compound", with a meaning similar to the modern one, has been used at least since 1661, when [[Robert Boyle|Robert Boyle]]'s *The Sceptical Chymist* was published. In his *Logick* (1724), the English minister and logician Isaac Watts contrasted element with compound in clear, modern terms.

=== KEY: Climate_96ebd2
LEVEL: 2  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Climate
ENTRY:
Long-term weather pattern of a region, typically averaged over 30 years.

More rigorously, climate is the mean and variability of meteorological variables over a span from months to millions of years. The variables most commonly measured are temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, wind, and precipitation. In a broader sense, climate is the state of the whole climate system: the [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]], hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere and the interactions among them. The word derives from Ancient Greek *klima*, 'inclination'. The standard averaging period of 30 years was defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), whose technical commission designated 1901–30 as the reference period for standard climate normals in 1934. The 1961–90 normals serve as the baseline reference period. The popular phrase "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get" summarizes the difference between climate and weather.

A location's climate is affected by its latitude, longitude, terrain, altitude, land use, and nearby bodies of water and their currents. Over historical time spans these determinants are nearly constant and change only over millions of years through processes such as [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Others are more dynamic: [[Thermohaline circulation|thermohaline circulation]] of the ocean warms the northern Atlantic Ocean by 5 °C (9 °F) compared with other ocean basins, other ocean currents redistribute heat regionally, and vegetation affects heat absorption, water retention, and rainfall. Changes in atmospheric greenhouse gases, particularly [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and [[Methane|methane]], determine how much solar energy the planet retains.

Climates are classified by average and typical variables, most commonly temperature and precipitation. The most widely used scheme is the [[Köppen climate classification]], first developed in 1899, which was designed to identify climates associated with certain biomes. The Thornthwaite system, in use since 1948, adds evapotranspiration, and the Bergeron and Spatial Synoptic systems focus on the origin of air masses. Originally, climes were defined in Ancient Greece by a location's latitude. A common shortcoming of all such schemes is that they draw distinct boundaries, while real climates change gradually.

Paleoclimatology studies past climates. Because few direct observations exist before the 19th century, it relies on proxy evidence: sediments, ice cores, pollen, and rocks, as well as tree rings and coral. Climate models are mathematical models of past, present, and future climates.

Small migratory ground-nesting game bird of the pheasant family Phasianidae.

=== KEY: Communism_868f00
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Communism
ENTRY:
Political and economic ideology whose goal is a communist society: a classless order based on common ownership of the means of production, distribution, and exchange, with products allocated according to need. Such a society would have no private property or social classes and, ultimately, no money or state. The word derives from French *communisme*, from [[Latin]] *communis*, 'common'.

Communists often seek voluntary self-governance but disagree on the means. A libertarian socialist approach stresses revolutionary spontaneity and workers' self-management; an authoritarian socialist, party-driven approach aims to establish a socialist state expected to wither away. These share the analysis that under capitalism the proletariat, who sell their labor power, and the bourgeoisie, who own the means of production, stand in an exploitative relationship that only social revolution can resolve.

Modern communism grew out of the 19th-century European socialist movement. In 1848 [[Karl Marx]] and Friedrich Engels offered a new definition in *The Communist Manifesto*. Communist governments espousing Marxism–Leninism came to power first in the Soviet Union with the 1917 Russian Revolution, and after World War II in Eastern Europe, Asia, and elsewhere.

For much of the 20th century more than one-third of the world's population lived under Communist governments, characterized by one-party rule, state control of the economy, and suppression of opposition. Supporters and critics assess the record differently: some regimes achieved rapid industrialization or literacy gains, while critics point to repression, forced labor, and famines. Mass killings under communist regimes and their mortality figures remain controversial and debated.

After the [[Dissolution of the Soviet Union]] in 1991, many governments abolished Communist rule. Remaining Communist governments include China, Cuba, Laos, North Korea, and Vietnam.

=== KEY: Conifer_7997c9
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Conifer
ENTRY:
Group of woody, mostly evergreen [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperm]] plants that bear cones. There are over 600 living species. Each tree produces separate male and female cones, and the seeds are usually dispersed by wind. Conifers first appeared more than 300 million years ago in the [[Carboniferous|Carboniferous]] and dominated land plants during the Mesozoic, until [[Flowering plant|flowering plants]] took over many ecosystems in the [[Cretaceous|Cretaceous]]. Conifers dominate the [[Taiga|taiga]] of the Northern Hemisphere, where conical shapes shed snow and waxy needle-shaped leaves limit water loss. The tallest living tree is a coast redwood, 116.07 m tall, and the oldest non-clonal living tree is a Great Basin bristlecone pine, 4,700 years old. As of 2025, 94 conifer species were listed as endangered. Conifers are of great economic value for timber and paper production.

=== KEY: Conscription_ceefaa
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Conscription
ENTRY:
Compulsory enlistment in national service, mainly [[Military|military]], enforced by law. Conscription dates to antiquity, but the modern system of near-universal conscription of young men began with the [[French Revolution]] in the 1790s. Most European nations later copied it in peacetime, with men serving one to eight years on active duty before transferring to the reserve. Conscription is controversial on grounds including conscientious objection, opposition to particular wars and sexism, since often only men are subject to the draft. [[Norway]] and [[Sweden]] became the first nations to conscript women on the same legal terms as men in the 2010s, and [[Denmark]] ruled in 2025 to implement a similar system. Many states that have abolished conscription keep the power to reinstate it in wartime. The [[United Kingdom]] abolished conscription in 1960, and it has not been enforced in the [[United States]] since 1973.

=== KEY: Dielectric_9d5ce2

HEADWORD: Dielectric
ENTRY:
Electrical insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field. Unlike a conductor, a dielectric has no loosely bound, free electrons to carry current. In an electric field its charges shift slightly from their equilibrium positions, producing an internal field that reduces the overall field. Dielectric properties govern the storage and dissipation of electric and magnetic energy, and they are important in electronics, optics and solid-state physics. === KEY: El_Ni_o_Southern_Oscillation_115a9e
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: El Niño–Southern Oscillation
ENTRY:
Global climate phenomenon of fluctuating sea surface temperatures over the tropical [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific Ocean]]. Its warming phase is called El Niño and its cooling phase La Niña, and the Southern Oscillation is the accompanying shift in [[Atmospheric pressure|surface air pressure]]. The phases recur irregularly about every two to seven years and are not easily predictable. El Niño causes short-term spikes in global average surface temperature, lasting about one year, while La Niña causes short-term cooling. The phenomenon disrupts weather across the [[Tropics|tropics]] and beyond, bringing intense storms to some places and droughts to others. The countries most affected are developing countries bordering the Pacific that depend on agriculture and fishing. The term was first recorded in a climate context in 1892, when Captain Camilo Carrillo reported to a geographical congress in [[Lima]] that Peruvian sailors had named the warm current after the Christ Child, as it typically appeared around [[Christmas]].

=== KEY: Electron_configuration_d9e95a
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Electron configuration
ENTRY:
Distribution of electrons among the atomic or molecular orbitals of an atom or molecule. For example, the configuration of neon is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, meaning that the 1s, 2s and 2p subshells hold two, two and six electrons. Each electron is described by four quantum numbers, and the [[Pauli exclusion principle]] states that no two electrons can share the same set of four. The Aufbau principle dictates that electrons fill orbitals from the lowest energy level upward, producing the ground state, the lowest-energy configuration. An electron can move to a different configuration by emitting or absorbing a photon. Electron configuration explains the structure of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]], the nature of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]] and molecular geometry, and the behavior of lasers and semiconductors.

=== KEY: Epidemiology_7113ad
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Epidemiology
ENTRY:
Study of the distribution and determinants of disease and other health conditions in human populations. It is a cornerstone of [[Public health|public health]], identifying risk factors for disease and targets for [[Preventive healthcare|preventive healthcare]], and it shapes policy. Major areas include disease causation, transmission, outbreak investigation, surveillance and the comparison of treatment effects in clinical trials. Epidemiologists rely on biology, [[Statistics|statistics]], the social sciences and engineering. In 1854 [[John Snow]] traced a [[Cholera|cholera]] outbreak in London's Broad Street to a contaminated water pump, demonstrating waterborne transmission. Descriptive studies characterize cases by time, place and person, while analytic studies test hypotheses through cohort, case-control and randomized controlled trials. The term appears to have been first used to describe the study of epidemics in 1802 by the Spanish physician Joaquín de Villalba.
