South African anti-apartheid revolutionary and politician, president of South Africa (1994–99), the country's first black head of state and the first elected in a fully representative democratic election.

A Thembu royal family member, he worked as a lawyer in [[Johannesburg]]. He joined the [[African National Congress]] (ANC) in 1943 and co-founded its Youth League in 1944. After the National Party government established [[Apartheid|apartheid]], a system of racial segregation, he and the ANC committed themselves to its overthrow. Though initially committed to non-violent protest, in 1961 he co-founded the militant Umkhonto we Sizwe and led a sabotage campaign. Arrested in 1962, he was sentenced to [[Life imprisonment|life imprisonment]] in the Rivonia Trial.

Mandela served 27 years in prison, mainly on Robben Island. President F. W. de Klerk released him in 1990, and the two negotiated an end to apartheid and organized the 1994 multiracial election, which the ANC won. They shared the 1993 Nobel Peace Prize. Mandela's coalition government promulgated a new constitution, created the Truth and Reconciliation Commission to investigate past abuses, and pursued land reform, poverty reduction, and expanded healthcare. He was Secretary-General of the [[Non-Aligned Movement]] from 1998 to 1999, declined a second term, and was succeeded by Thabo Mbeki. He then campaigned against poverty and [[HIV/AIDS]].

Mandela was controversial for much of his life. Critics on the right denounced him as a communist terrorist; some on the radical left thought him too eager to reconcile with apartheid's supporters; others have criticized civilian casualties from Umkhonto we Sizwe bombings and his ties to [[Fidel Castro]] and [[Muammar Gaddafi]]. He received more than 250 honors.

=== KEY: Early_modern_period_d9a376
FILE: data/ko/tr_out/t_098.json

ENGLISH:
Historical period, c. 1500–c. 1800, with divisions based primarily on the history of Europe and the broader concept of modernity. No exact date marks its beginning or end, and its extent varies with the area of history studied. It is generally considered to have started at the beginning of the 16th or 17th century and to have ended in the 18th or 19th. In a European context it follows the [[Middle Ages]] and precedes the advent of modernity, though there is no universal agreement on these boundaries. Proposed starting points include the start of the [[Renaissance]], the printing revolution of the 1440s, the Fall of Constantinople in 1453, the [[Age of Discovery]], and the start of the [[Reformation]]. The transition into the [[Late modern period]] in the late 18th century was marked by the Industrial Revolution, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution, along with Napoleon's rise to power.

The concept grew from the traditional European division into ancient, medieval, and modern periods, made since the time of Petrarch. The term was first proposed to denote a distinct period by the medieval historian Lynn Thorndike in his 1926 work *A Short History of Civilization*; it spread through economic history in the 1940s and 1950s and became widely known among scholars during the 1990s. Attempts to apply it beyond Europe began by the 1980s, with scholars using it for societies such as Tokugawa Japan. Historian Jerry H. Bentley defined the early modern world as the era of about 1500 to 1800 in which cross-cultural interactions increasingly linked peoples throughout the world. He identified the connection of the world by sea, global biological exchanges, and the creation of an "early capitalist global economy" as its three main processes. Some historians have criticized the periodization as Eurocentric and as underemphasizing contemporaneous developments in non-Western regions, such as the Mughal and Ottoman empires.

Feudalism declined in Europe, and religious unity under the Roman Catholic Church ended. The Reformation provoked a backlash that expanded the Inquisition and led to the European wars of religion, including the Thirty Years' War (1618–48), which ended with the Peace of Westphalia. European colonization of the Americas began, and with it the [[Atlantic slave trade]]. The period also saw the Commercial Revolution, the [[Scientific Revolution]], the Golden Age of Piracy, and the rise of mercantilism. Voyages included those of Christopher Columbus in 1492 and Ferdinand Magellan from 1519 to 1522; Nicolaus Copernicus's heliocentric model dates to 1543 and Isaac Newton's *Principia Mathematica* to 1687. Russia reached the Pacific coast in 1647, and Western Europe greatly surpassed China in technology and per capita wealth, a development known as the Great Divergence.

Scientific study of the life of the past, mainly but not exclusively through [[Fossil|fossils]]. Paleontologists use fossils to classify organisms, measure geologic time, and assess the interactions between prehistoric organisms and their environment. Paleontological observations are known from at least the 6th century BC, but the science's foundation dates to the work of [[Georges Cuvier]] in 1796, who demonstrated evidence for [[Extinction|extinction]] and showed that the life of the past was not necessarily the same as that of the present. The French word *paléontologie* was introduced in 1822. [[Charles Darwin]] later popularized the concept of [[Evolution|evolution]]; evolution and extinction are understood as complementary processes that shaped the history of life.

The field overlaps most with [[Geology|geology]] and [[Biology|biology]], and also with ecology, chemistry, physics, and mathematics. Invertebrate paleontology has traditionally been tied to geology and biostratigraphy, areas with both commercial and academic drivers, while vertebrate paleontology has been closer to biology. Fieldwork, laboratory preparation, and comparative anatomy remain core components. Other subfields include micropaleontology, paleobotany, paleobiology, and paleoecology. Methods include stratigraphy, radiometric dating, and cladistics, and biostratigraphy is used to reconstruct the geologic time scale. Theoretical analysis introduced in the 1950s and 1960s led to more focused fields studying changing geography and climate, phylogenetic relationships, and how fossilization occurs and what biases affect the fossil record.

Paleontology draws attention in mass media comparable to that given to astrophysics and global health. Public interest can be traced to the mythologies of indigenous peoples and to the interpretation of fossils as the bones of dragons or giants. Prehistoric life inspires toys, television, film, computer games, and tourism, with budgets that often exceed funding within the field itself.

Sunnis hold that [[Muhammad]] appointed no successor and that his closest companion [[Abu Bakr]] (r. 632–634) rightfully succeeded him as caliph of the Muslim community, chosen at the meeting of Saqifa. This contrasts with the view of [[Shia Islam]], which holds that Muhammad appointed [[Ali|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] (r. 656–661) as his successor. Sunnis nonetheless revere Ali, together with Abu Bakr, Umar (r. 634–644), and Uthman (r. 644–656), as the four *rashidun* ("rightly guided" caliphs), and honor the *sahaba*, *tabi'in*, and *tabi al-tabi'in* as the *salaf* (predecessors).

The Arabic term is pre-Islamic, originally meaning "the right path that has always been followed," and gained political significance after the murder of Uthman. During the second Civil War (680–92) it acquired connotations critical of Shia doctrines. Its use as a short group name is relatively recent; Ibn Taymiyyah probably used it first, and scholars such as Muhammad Rashid Rida later popularized it.

In creed, the tradition upholds the six pillars of *iman* (faith) and comprises the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools of *kalam* (theology) and the textualist Athari school. Sunni Islam is sometimes called "Orthodox Islam," though some scholars, such as John Burton, argue that there is no such thing as "Orthodox Islam."

ENGLISH:
Military construction designed for the defense of territory in warfare, also used to establish rule in a region during peacetime. The word derives from [[Latin]] *fortis* ("strong") and *facere* ("to make"). Fortification is usually divided into permanent and field fortification, with an intermediate semipermanent branch. Castles differ from the generic fort in being the residence of a monarch or noble and commanding a specific defensive territory.

Walls have been necessary for cities from early history. The walled town of Sesklo in Greece dates from 6800 BC, and Solnitsata in Bulgaria, from 4700 BC, held 350 people in two-story houses behind a stone wall. [[Uruk]] in Sumer is one of the world's oldest known walled cities, and the Egyptians built frontier fortresses and mud brick city walls. A prehistoric stone wall of 3200 BC, 4 m thick and 4 m tall, surrounded the Ness of Brodgar temple in Scotland. In [[Mycenaean Greece]], large "cyclopean" stone walls were fitted without mortar, and Athens built the Long Walls to its seaport at Piraeus. The [[Celts]] of Central Europe built large fortified settlements called oppida.

The [[Castle|castle]] emerged in the 9th century in the [[Carolingian Empire]], with Roman forts and hill forts as its main antecedents. [[Cannon|Cannons]] of the 14th century made medieval-style fortifications largely obsolete. Fortifications in the age of black powder became much lower, with ditches and earth ramparts to absorb the energy of cannon fire. Explosive shells of the 19th century disrupted the intricate arrangements of star forts, and steel-and-concrete fortifications were common in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The advances in modern warfare since World War I have made large-scale fortifications obsolete in most situations.

Account of how living and extinct organisms evolved, from the first life on Earth to the present.

Earth formed about 4.54 ± 0.05 billion years ago, and life emerged before 3.7 billion years ago. The earliest clear evidence of life is biogenic carbon and stromatolite fossils from 3.7 billion-year-old rocks in western Greenland. Possible "remains of biotic life" were reported from 4.1 billion-year-old rocks in Western Australia in 2015, and microfossils from the Nuvvuagittuq Belt may be as old as 4.28 billion years, though such fossils may have non-biological origins. How life began remains hypothetical.

Microbial mats of bacteria and archaea dominated the early Archean eon. Photosynthesis by [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] around 3.5 billion years ago released oxygen that led to the [[Great Oxidation Event]] around 2.4 billion years ago. The earliest evidence of [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] dates from 1.85 billion years ago, likely resulting from symbiogenesis between archaea and proteobacteria, and multicellular organisms appeared around 1.7 billion years ago. Bilaterian animals were present by 555 million years ago, and animal life diversified in the [[Cambrian explosion]], with early vertebrates by about 525 million years ago. Land plants evolved from green algae, with fossils from about 470 million years ago in the Ordovician.

Synapsids, including the ancestors of mammals, dominated the Permian. The [[Permian–Triassic extinction event]] 252 million years ago killed most complex species, and dinosaurs later dominated the Jurassic and Cretaceous periods. The [[Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event]] 66 million years ago killed off the non-avian dinosaurs, after which mammals increased rapidly in size and diversity. Only 1.75–1.8 million species have been named, though one estimate suggests Earth may have 1 trillion, and living species are under one percent of all that have existed.

=== KEY: Black_hole_05b337

ENGLISH:
Astronomical body so compact that its gravity prevents anything, including light, from escaping.

[[Albert Einstein]]'s theory of [[General relativity|general relativity]], which describes gravitation as the curvature of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], predicts that any sufficiently compact mass will form a black hole. The boundary of no escape is the [[Event horizon|event horizon]]; crossing it traps an object but produces no locally detectable change. General relativity also predicts a central singularity, where the curvature of spacetime is infinite.

The idea of a body from which light could not escape was proposed in the late 18th century by John Michell, whose letter of 1784 considered a star of the same density but 500 times the radius of the Sun, and independently by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1796. The first widely accepted black hole was [[Cygnus X-1]], proposed through studies between 1971 and 1974.

Black holes typically form when massive stars collapse at the end of their life cycle, often in a supernova, and can grow by absorbing surrounding mass. Supermassive black holes of millions to billions of solar masses exist at the centers of most galaxies; the radio source [[Sagittarius A*]] in the Milky Way contains one of about 4.3 million solar masses.

Black holes are detected through their effects on nearby matter and light. Infalling plasma forms a glowing accretion disk, which in extreme cases powers a quasar. Merging black holes emit gravitational waves, and the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadows of M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime predicts that event horizons emit [[Hawking radiation]], causing very slow mass loss.

Organ forming the center of the nervous system in all vertebrate and most invertebrate animals, consisting of nervous tissue and typically located in the head.

The brain receives information from the sensory nervous system, processes it (thought, [[Cognition|cognition]], and intelligence), and coordinates motor control and the endocrine system. It acts on the body by generating patterns of muscle activity and by driving the secretion of hormones, allowing rapid and coordinated responses to the environment. In humans, the main regions are the cerebrum, cerebellum, and brainstem; the cerebrum has two hemispheres and four lobes: frontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital.

Brains are composed primarily of [[Neuron|neurons]] and glial cells. Glial cells provide structural and metabolic support, insulation, and guidance of development. Neurons send signals to specific target cells, sometimes over long distances, by means of axons, which are usually myelinated and carry rapid electrical pulses called action potentials. The human cerebral cortex contains approximately 14–16 billion neurons and the cerebellum an estimated 55–70 billion; another account gives about 86 billion neurons in total. The human brain weighs about 1.4 kg (3 lb) and consumes roughly 20% of the body's oxygen and energy while making up about 2% of body mass. ENGLISH:
Application of conceptual knowledge to achieve practical goals, especially in a reproducible way. The word also denotes the products of such efforts, both tangible, such as tools and machines, and intangible, such as software. Technology plays a critical role in science, engineering, and everyday life.

The term dates to the early 17th century, when it meant "systematic treatment"; it derives from Greek *tekhnē* ('craft, art') and *-logia* ('study, knowledge'). From the 19th century, continental Europeans used *Technik* (German) or *technique* (French) for a "way of doing" and *Technologie* for the academic study of the "methods of arts and crafts"; English translated both as technology. In the 20th century, with scientific progress and the Second Industrial Revolution, it came to mean the systematic use of knowledge to practical ends.

The earliest known technology is the stone tool. Hominids learned to make the first stone tools about 2 mya (million years ago) by hammering flakes off a pebble, and the practice was refined 75 kya (thousand years ago) into pressure flaking. Evidence points to continuous human use of fire at least 1.5 mya; Charles Darwin described its discovery as "possibly the greatest ever made by man". Fire allowed cooking, which improved nutrient value. The cooking hypothesis holds that cooking promoted growth in hominid brain size, though some researchers find the evidence inconclusive. Hearths dated to 790 kya may have intensified socialization and contributed to the emergence of language. Evidence of clothing dates to 90–120 kya and of shelter to 450 kya.

The [[Neolithic Revolution]] accelerated innovation and social complexity. Polished stone axes allowed large-scale forest clearance and farming, and agriculture fed larger populations. Increased labor specialization, trade, war, and the need for collective action such as irrigation are thought to have contributed to the first cities, such as Uruk, and the first civilizations, such as Sumer. Writing became the basis for history, libraries, schools, and scientific research. Furnaces and bellows enabled smelting of metals; native copper was probably used from about 10 kya, alloys such as bronze and brass appeared about 4,000 BC, and iron alloys such as steel date to around 1,800 BC.

The earliest record of a ship under sail is a Nile boat dating to around 7,000 BC. The Sumerians in Mesopotamia diverted the Tigris and Euphrates by canals and levees for irrigation. The wheel was invented independently in Mesopotamia, the Northern Caucasus, and Central Europe; estimates range from 5,500 to 3,000 BC, with most experts favoring closer to 4,000 BC. Drawings of wheeled carts date from about 3,500 BC.

Later inventions, including the [[Printing press|printing press]], telephone, and the [[Internet]], lowered barriers to communication and ushered in the knowledge economy. Technology contributes to economic development and prosperity but can also cause pollution, resource depletion, and technological unemployment resulting from [[Automation|automation]]. Philosophical and political debates continue over its role, its ethics, and ways to mitigate its downsides.

이후의 발명품인 [[Printing press|인쇄기]], 전화, [[Internet|인터넷]]은 의사소통의 장벽을 낮추고 지식 경제의 도래를 알렸다. 기술은 경제 발전과 번영에 기여하지만 오염, 자원 고갈, [[Automation|자동화]]로 인한 기술적 실업도 일으킬 수 있다. 기술의 역할과 윤리, 단점을 완화하는 방법을 둘러싼 철학적, 정치적 논쟁이 계속되고 있다.
