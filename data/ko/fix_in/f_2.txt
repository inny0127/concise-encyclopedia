ENGLISH:
Comic performer who uses physical [[Comedy|comedy]], usually in distinctive makeup and [[Costume|costume]]. The most ancient known clowns come from Egypt's Fifth Dynasty, around 2400 BC, when the roles of [[Priest|priest]] and clown were traditionally held by the same people. Many indigenous American cultures have ritual clowns; among the Lakota and Dakota, the Heyoka is a backward clown who does everything in reverse. The [[Circus|circus]] clown developed from comic roles in [[Theatre|theater]] and variety shows in the 19th to mid-20th centuries, with outlandish costumes, colorful wigs and exaggerated footwear. Joseph Grimaldi, an English pantomime actor, expanded the role in the early 1800s and created the whiteface makeup design used by many modern Western clowns.

ENGLISH:
Hard, lustrous metal; [[Chemical element|chemical element]] with symbol Co and atomic number 27. Cobalt is ferromagnetic and is protected from oxidation by a passivating oxide film. It is used primarily in [[Lithium-ion battery|lithium-ion batteries]] and in magnetic, wear-resistant and high-strength [[Alloy|alloys]]. Cobalt-based blue pigments have been used since antiquity to color glass, paints and jewelry. Its name derives from an ore that 16th-century German silver miners considered a nuisance, possibly named after a goblin. Today cobalt is mostly produced as a by-product of [[Copper|copper]] and [[Nickel|nickel]] mining. In 2024, production exceeded 300,000 tons, with the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) accounting for more than 80%. Cobalt is the active center of cobalamins, also known as [[Vitamin B12|vitamin B12]], which is essential for all animals.

Public sculpture by artist Anish Kapoor in Millennium Park, [[Chicago]]. Constructed between 2004 and 2006, the [[Stainless steel|stainless steel]] sculpture is nicknamed "The Bean" for its shape. Its 168 plates are welded together into a highly polished surface with no visible seams, which reflects and distorts the city's skyline and passing clouds. Kapoor's design was inspired by liquid [[Mercury (element)|mercury]]. Visitors can walk around and under its 12-foot (3.7 m) arch, where a concave chamber called the omphalos multiplies reflections. Cloud Gate was formally dedicated on May 15, 2006.

Country (pop., mid-2026 est.: more than 54.2 million), East Africa, area 580,367 sq km. The capital and largest city is [[Nairobi]]; the second-largest and oldest is [[Mombasa]], a port on Mombasa Island. Kenya is bordered by South Sudan, Ethiopia, Somalia, the Indian Ocean, Tanzania, and Uganda, and shares Lake Victoria with its western neighbors. Its landscape ranges from snow-capped peaks on [[Mount Kenya]], which at 5,199 m is Africa's second-highest, to arid and semi-arid regions and deserts such as the Chalbi.

Some of the earliest humans evolved in Kenya, and fossils have been found at Koobi Fora. In 1984, near [[Lake Turkana]], Richard Leakey and Kamoya Kimeu found the Turkana Boy, a 1.6-million-year-old [[Homo erectus]] fossil. Cushitic speakers settled the lowlands between 3,200 and 1,300 BC, Nilotic-speaking pastoralists began arriving around 500 BC, and Bantu people settled between 250 BC and AD 500. European contact began in 1500 with the Portuguese. A protectorate was established by the [[British Empire]] in 1895, and the Kenya Colony began in 1920. After the Mau Mau revolution, which began in 1952, independence was declared on December 12, 1963, and Kenya remained in the [[Commonwealth of Nations]].

Kenya is a presidential representative democratic republic; William Ruto took office as president in September 2022. Its economy, the largest in East and Central Africa, is lower-middle-income, with a per-capita gross national income of $2,110. Agriculture is the largest sector, with tea and coffee as traditional cash crops and fresh flowers a fast-growing export; tourism is also a major driver. Kenya belongs to the UN, the East African Community, and the [[International Monetary Fund]], among other bodies, and hosts the headquarters of the United Nations Environment Programme.

=== KEY: Organic_chemistry_f15c9d
FILE: data/ko/tr_out/t_250.json

ENGLISH:
Subdiscipline of chemistry concerned with the structure, properties, and reactions of organic compounds and organic materials, those containing [[Carbon|carbon]] atoms. It includes determining structural formulas, analyzing physical and chemical properties, evaluating reactivity, and the synthesis of natural products, drugs, and polymers.

The field covers [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbons]] (compounds of only carbon and hydrogen) and compounds that also contain oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and the halogens. Carbon's valence of four, with single, double, and triple bonds and delocalized electrons, makes organic compounds structurally diverse. They form the basis of all known life and underlie pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, agrichemicals, lubricants, solvents, plastics, fuels, and explosives. The field overlaps [[Organometallic chemistry|organometallic chemistry]], [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], medicinal chemistry, polymer chemistry, and materials science.

Before the 18th century, chemists generally believed that compounds from living organisms held a "vital force" (vitalism). Around 1816 Michel Chevreul showed that fats could be chemically changed into new compounds. In 1828 [[Friedrich Wöhler|Friedrich Wöhler]] produced urea from potassium cyanate and ammonium sulfate, a synthesis now generally accepted as disproving vitalism, though Wöhler himself was cautious. [[Justus von Liebig|Justus von Liebig]] is considered one of the field's principal founders. In 1856 William Henry Perkin accidentally produced the dye known as Perkin's mauve, and in 1858 Friedrich August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper independently developed the concept of chemical structure.

Bayer first manufactured [[Aspirin|aspirin]] in the last decade of the 19th century. By 1910 Paul Ehrlich's group was developing the arsenic-based Salvarsan against syphilis, beginning chemotherapy. Natural indigo production fell from 19,000 tons in 1897 to 1,000 tons by 1914 thanks to Adolf von Baeyer's synthetic methods. Total synthesis later reached molecules as complex as vitamin B12.

Solid terrestrial surface of Earth not submerged by the ocean or another body of water, also called dry land or ground. It makes up approximately 29% of Earth's surface and includes all continents and islands. It is almost entirely covered by regolith, a layer of rock, soil, and minerals forming the outer part of the crust. One-third of land is covered in trees, another third is used for [[Agriculture|agriculture]], and roughly one-tenth is covered in glacier ice; the remainder is desert, savanna, and prairie. About 30% has a dry climate. The word may also mean the natural resources land holds, including rivers, lakes, and the biosphere, and human modifications such as agriculture and architecture. The boundary between land and sea is the shoreline.

The word derives from Old English, from Proto-Germanic *landą*, "untilled land." Many place names end in *-land*, as in England, Greenland, and New Zealand; the Indo-Iranian equivalent *-stan* appears in Pakistan, Afghanistan, and elsewhere in Central Asia.

The earliest material found in the Solar System dates to 4.5672 ± 0.0006 billion years ago, and the primordial Earth had formed by 4.54 ± 0.04 billion years ago. Earth's atmosphere and oceans formed through volcanic outgassing, and the crust formed when the molten outer layer cooled. Continents are formed by [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], driven by the loss of heat from Earth's interior. Over hundreds of millions of years, supercontinents have formed and broken apart three times: Rodinia began to break apart roughly 750 million years ago, the continents recombined as Pannotia 600–540 million years ago, and then as [[Pangaea]], which broke apart 180 million years ago.

A continuous area of land surrounded by ocean is a landmass. There are four major landmasses: Africa–Eurasia, America, Antarctica, and Australia. They are subdivided into up to seven continents, which in order of land area are Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Antarctica, Europe, and Australia. In physical geology, land divides into mountain ranges and relatively flat interiors called cratons, both formed over millions of years. Streams shape the landscape, carve rocks, transport sediments, and replenish groundwater. At high elevations or latitudes, snow compacts into [[Glacier|glaciers]], which can be heavy enough to warp the crust. Terrain affects travel, mapmaking, ecosystems, and surface water flow, and it largely determines suitability for human settlement.

Earth's first cellular life likely originated on land, and biodiversity then evolved over hundreds of millions of years, interrupted by mass extinctions. Survival on land depends on fresh water, which constitutes only 3% of Earth's water. The study of land in general is geography; mineralogy studies minerals, petrology studies rocks, and soil science studies soils, including pedology (soil formation) and edaphology (the relationship between soil and life). Logarithmic scale for specifying the [[Acid|acidity]] or [[Base (chemistry)|basicity]] of an aqueous solution. pH is the negative base-10 logarithm of hydrogen ion activity. At 25 °C, solutions with a pH below 7 are acidic, those above 7 are basic, and pure water is neutral at 7. The neutral value falls below 7 as temperature rises above 25 °C. The scale is conventionally given as 0 to 14, but very concentrated strong acids can have values below 0, and strong bases can exceed 14. The pH of a solution can be measured with a glass electrode and pH meter or with a color-changing indicator. The concept was introduced in 1909 by Danish chemist Søren Peter Lauritz Sørensen at the Carlsberg Laboratory; the exact original meaning of the letter "p" remains disputed. Measurements are important in [[Chemistry]], [[Agronomy]], medicine and water treatment.

ENGLISH:
Late 19th-century art movement of French and Belgian origin, chiefly in poetry. It sought to represent absolute truths symbolically through language and metaphorical images, largely as a reaction against [[Realism (arts)|naturalism and realism]]. Its literary origins lie in [[Charles Baudelaire]]'s *Les Fleurs du mal* (1857). Baudelaire translated [[Edgar Allan Poe]] into French, and Poe's work was a major influence. [[Stéphane Mallarmé]] and Paul Verlaine developed the aesthetic in the 1860s and 1870s. Manifestos in the 1880s attracted a generation of writers, and the critic Jean Moréas coined the term "symbolist" to distinguish the movement from the related Decadents. His Symbolist Manifesto appeared in *Le Figaro* in 1886. The movement favored spirituality, imagination and dreams, and Symbolist painting spread across the Western world in many styles.
KOREAN:
프랑스와 벨기에에서 기원해 주로 시 분야에서 전개된 19세기 후반의 예술 운동. 언어와 은유적 이미지를 통해 절대적 진리를 상징적으로 나타내고자 했으며, 대체로 [[Realism (arts)|자연주의와 사실주의]]에 대한 반발이었다. 문학적 기원은 [[Charles Baudelaire|샤를 보들레르]]의 『악의 꽃』(1857)에 있다. 보들레르는 [[Edgar Allan Poe|에드거 앨런 포]]를 프랑스어로 번역했으며, 포의 작품은 큰 영향을 미쳤다. [[Stéphane Mallarmé|스테판 말라르메]]와 폴 베를렌이 1860~70년대에 이 미학을 발전시켰다. 1880년대의 선언들은 한 세대의 작가들을 끌어들였고, 비평가 장 모레아스는 이 운동을 관련 있는 데카당파와 구별하기 위해 “상징주의”라는 용어를 만들었다. 그의 상징주의 선언은 1886년 『르 피가로』에 실렸다. 이 운동은 영성, 상상력, 꿈을 중시했으며, 상징주의 회화는 다양한 양식으로 서양 전역에 퍼졌다.

=== KEY: Weight_8c489d
FILE: data/ko/tr_out/t_363.json
FLAGS: links differ, dropped ['Force'], added []
ENGLISH:
Quantity associated with the [[Gravity|gravitational]] [[Force|force]] acting on an object, measured in [[Newton (unit)|newtons]] in the SI system. Textbooks differ on whether weight is a vector or a scalar, or the reaction force that counteracts gravity and is measured by a spring scale; in free fall, by the last definition, weight is zero. An object with a [[Mass|mass]] of one kilogram weighs about 9.8 newtons on Earth and about one-sixth as much on the [[Moon]]. Although weight and mass are distinct, the terms are often confused in everyday use. The concept has been debated for centuries: [[Plato]] saw weight as the natural tendency of objects to seek their kin, and [[Aristotle]] tied it to falling motion. Under the [[Theory of relativity|theory of relativity]], gravity is a consequence of spacetime curvature, and several concepts of weight now coexist.
KOREAN:
물체에 작용하는 [[Gravity|중력]]과 관련된 양으로, SI 단위계에서 [[Newton (unit)|뉴턴]]으로 측정된다. 교과서마다 무게를 벡터로 볼지 스칼라로 볼지, 또는 중력에 맞서며 용수철저울로 측정되는 반작용력으로 볼지 의견이 다르며, 마지막 정의에 따르면 자유 낙하 중에는 무게가 0이다. [[Mass|질량]]이 1킬로그램인 물체는 지구에서 약 9.8뉴턴의 무게가 나가고 [[Moon|달]]에서는 약 6분의 1이 나간다. 무게와 질량은 서로 다르지만 일상에서는 이 용어들이 흔히 혼동된다. 이 개념은 수 세기 동안 논쟁되어 왔는데, [[Plato|플라톤]]은 무게를 물체가 자기와 같은 종류를 찾으려는 자연스러운 성향으로 보았고 [[Aristotle|아리스토텔레스]]는 이를 낙하 운동과 연결했다. [[Theory of relativity|상대성 이론]]에서는 중력이 시공간 곡률의 결과이며, 현재 무게에 대한 여러 개념이 공존한다.

=== KEY: Yin_and_yang_39b766
FILE: data/ko/tr_out/t_372.json
FLAGS: numbers missing ['10 thousand']
ENGLISH:
Pair of complementary cosmic principles in Chinese philosophy. Yin is retractive, passive and receptive, while yang is active, expansive and repelling, and the two interact so that the whole is greater than its parts. The concept originated in ancient China, and its dualities appear in natural processes from seasons to landscapes. Taiji, the Supreme Ultimate, is the undifferentiated state from which yin and yang arise. The organization of [[Qi|qi]], the material energy of the universe, forms the 10 thousand things between Heaven and Earth. The idea underlies [[Traditional Chinese medicine]], the [[I Ching]] and Chinese martial arts such as [[Baguazhang]], and it is central to [[Taoism]] and [[Confucianism]].
KOREAN:
중국 철학에서 서로 보완하는 한 쌍의 우주 원리. 음은 수축적이고 수동적이며 수용적인 반면 양은 능동적이고 팽창적이며 밀어내는 성질을 지니고, 둘이 상호작용하여 전체가 부분의 합보다 커진다. 이 개념은 고대 중국에서 비롯되었으며, 그 이원성은 계절에서 풍경에 이르기까지 자연 현상에 나타난다. 태극은 음과 양이 생겨나는 미분화 상태이다. 우주의 물질적 에너지인 [[Qi|기]]의 조직이 천지 사이의 만물을 이룬다. 이 사상은 [[Traditional Chinese medicine|중국 전통 의학]], [[I Ching|『주역』]], [[Baguazhang|팔괘장]] 같은 중국 무술의 바탕이 되며, [[Taoism|도교]]와 [[Confucianism|유교]]의 핵심이기도 하다.
