ENGLISH:
Philosophical problem articulated by [[David Hume]] concerning whether [[Morality|moral]] statements about what ought to be can be inferred from objective statements about what is. Hume distinguished descriptive from prescriptive statements and argued that the transition between them is not obvious. In *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), he insisted the step needs explanation. Hume's law, or Hume's guillotine, is the thesis that an ethical conclusion cannot be inferred from purely descriptive factual premises. G. E. Moore's open-question argument reaches a similar view and was aimed at ethical naturalists, who identify moral properties with natural ones. The problem is closely related to the fact–value distinction in [[Epistemology]], although that discussion may also encompass [[Aesthetics|aesthetics]]. Responses include non-cognitivist views such as emotivism, which treat "ought" statements as expressions of attitude, and John Searle's 1964 argument that institutional facts such as promises carry normative force. Consensus on a resolution remains elusive.

ENGLISH:
English polymath: mathematician, physicist, astronomer, alchemist, theologian, historian, and inventor, a key figure in the Scientific Revolution.

His book [[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica|*Principia*]] (1687) formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation and established [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]], the dominant scientific viewpoint for centuries until it was superseded by the theory of relativity. With it he derived Kepler's laws of planetary motion and accounted for tides, the trajectories of comets, and the precession of the equinoxes. His work on [[Optics|optics]], collected in *Opticks* (1704), rested on the observation that a prism separates white light into the colors of the visible spectrum; he also built the first reflecting telescope. He shares credit with the German mathematician [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] for formulating infinitesimal [[Calculus|calculus]], though he developed it years earlier. His priority disputes, notably with Robert Hooke over gravitation and with Leibniz over calculus, were bitter.

Newton was a fellow of Trinity College and the second Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at the [[University of Cambridge]], appointed at the age of 26. A devout but heterodox Christian, he privately rejected the doctrine of the Trinity and refused to take holy orders in the Church of England. He devoted much time to [[Alchemy|alchemy]] and biblical chronology, but most of that work remained unpublished until long after his death.

He served as Member of Parliament for the University of Cambridge in 1689–1690 and 1701–1702 and was knighted by Queen Anne in 1705. He was Warden (1696–1699) and Master (1699–1727) of the Royal Mint, where he increased the accuracy and security of British coinage, and president of the Royal Society (1703–27). He was the first scientist buried in [[Westminster Abbey]].

ENGLISH:
Syndrome of general decline in cognitive processes that impairs the ability to perform everyday activities, often associated with neurodegenerative diseases. It typically involves problems with memory, thinking, behavior, and motor control. Common symptoms also include emotional problems, difficulties with language, and decreased motivation. Dementia is incurable and progressive, ranging from mild to major severity, and is distinct from age-related decline in cognition and memory.

[[Alzheimer's disease]] is the most common cause, contributing to an estimated 60–70% of cases, followed by vascular dementia from impaired blood flow to the brain. Dementia can also be caused by brain injuries and [[Stroke|stroke]]. Subtypes are named for the underlying disorder, including [[Parkinson's disease]] dementia, [[Huntington's disease]] dementia, HIV dementia, frontotemporal dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and [[Prion|prion]] diseases.

Symptoms fall into cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and motor groups. Cognitive symptoms typically involve memory plus another region such as language, attention, problem solving, or perception. Neuropsychiatric symptoms affect more than 90% of cases at different stages and include agitation, disinhibition, depression, [[Hallucination|hallucinations]], delusions, apathy, and personality change.

Diagnosis is usually based on the history of the illness and cognitive testing with imaging. [[Blood test|Blood tests]] rule out reversible causes such as [[Hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]], and fluid biomarkers and PET scans can identify Alzheimer's disease. Aging is the greatest risk factor, but dementia is not a normal part of aging. Modifiable risk factors include smoking, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes.

In 2021 approximately 57 million people worldwide were affected, and 10 million new cases are reported every year as of 2025. There is no known cure. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil are used in some subtypes and may help in mild to moderate stages, though the overall benefit may be minor.

증상은 인지, 신경 정신, 운동 증상의 세 무리로 나뉜다. 인지 증상은 보통 기억에 더해 언어, 주의, 문제 해결, 지각 같은 다른 영역이 관련된다. 신경 정신 증상은 사례의 90% 넘게에서 여러 단계에 걸쳐 나타나며 초조, 탈억제, 우울, [[Hallucination|환각]], 망상, 무감동, 성격 변화가 포함된다.

ENGLISH:
Division of the year based on changes in weather, ecology, and hours of daylight in a given region. On Earth, seasons result from the [[Axial tilt|tilt]] of the planet's rotational axis, about 23.4° from its orbital plane, together with the axis's approximately constant direction toward Polaris through the year. From around March 20 to around September 22 the Northern Hemisphere tips toward the Sun, with the maximum on about June 21; the Southern Hemisphere's maximum is around December 21. The two instants when the Sun is directly overhead at the Equator are the [[Equinox|equinoxes]]. The low angle of the Sun in winter spreads radiation over a larger area, and with shorter days this accounts for most seasonal climate variation. Because of seasonal lag, June, July, and August are the warmest months in the [[Northern Hemisphere]].

Distance from the Sun is not the cause: Earth reaches perihelion in January and aphelion in July, and eccentricity produces a 7% variation in sunlight received.

Definitions vary by culture and region. In temperate and sub-polar regions four seasons based on the [[Gregorian calendar]], spring, summer, autumn, and winter, are generally recognized. Ecologists often use a six-season model not tied to fixed dates: prevernal, vernal, estival, serotinal, autumnal, and hibernal. Many tropical regions have two seasons, a rainy or [[Monsoon|monsoon]] season and a [[Dry season|dry season]]. The ancient Egyptians recognized flood, growth, and low-water seasons, based on the annual flooding of the [[Nile]]; in India, six seasons, or *Ritu*, have been recognized from ancient times for agriculture and trade.

Seasons hold special significance for agrarian societies, and their change is often attended by ritual.
KOREAN:

Contested genus of extinct hominins, sometimes treated as synonymous with [[Australopithecus]]. Three species are widely accepted: *P. aethiopicus*, *P. robustus* and *P. boisei*. They lived from about 2.9 to at least 1.2 million years ago, from the end of the [[Pliocene]] into the Middle Pleistocene. Their robust skulls had prominent sagittal crests, which suggest strong chewing muscles, and broad teeth for grinding. Their bodies were small: males of *P. robustus* were estimated at 40 kg and 132 cm, and males of *P. boisei* at 50 kg and 137 cm. *P. robustus* was probably an omnivore, while *P. boisei* was likely herbivorous. They are associated with bone tools and lived alongside early human species such as *Homo habilis* and *H. erectus*. Large carnivores of the time, including crocodiles, leopards and hyenas, preyed on them.

ENGLISH:
Aquatic invertebrate of the [[Phylum|phylum]] Porifera, most of them marine. Sponges are multicellular filter feeders that lack true tissues and organs, drawing water through pores and expelling it through an opening called the osculum. Flagellated choanocyte cells drive the water flow and capture most food. Sponges have no complex nervous, digestive or circulatory systems. Adult sponges are sessile, attached to a surface. Many have a mesh-like skeleton of spicules made of calcium carbonate or silicon dioxide, embedded in spongin, a modified collagen. Demosponges, the most numerous group, include all freshwater sponges. Fossil sponges such as Otavia date to as early as the Tonian period, around 800 Mya. Sponges may be the sister group to all other animals, though the evidence remains inconclusive.

Winners may be decided by physical events, such as scoring goals or crossing a line first, or by judges who score technical performance or artistic impression. [[Esports]] are not widely recognized by mainstream sports organizations.

Former U.S. Highway running from [[Chicago]] to Santa Monica, California. Established on November 11, 1926, it was one of the original U.S. Highways and covered 2,448 miles (3,940 km) through eight states. It became a major route for westward migration, especially during the [[Dust Bowl]] of the 1930s. Traffic declined as the [[Interstate Highway System]] bypassed towns, and the highway was removed from the U.S. Highway System in 1985. Nicknamed the "Mother Road" by [[John Steinbeck]] in *The Grapes of Wrath* (1939), it is preserved in part as Historic Route 66, a National Scenic Byway. It also featured in the 2006 animated film *Cars*.

Physical theory describing how the universe expanded from an early state of high density and temperature. Cosmological models based on it explain the abundance of light elements, the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, the [[Redshift|redshift]] of galaxies, and large-scale structure. The uniformity of the universe is explained by cosmic inflation, a phase of accelerated expansion in the earliest stages. Measurements of the expansion rate place the age of the universe at an estimated 13.787±0.02 billion years.

Extrapolating the expansion backward, the models describe an extraordinarily hot, dense primordial universe. The modern view incorporates inflation, followed by a reheating event that produced a hot, dense plasma. As the universe expanded and cooled, subatomic particles and then atoms formed. Primordial elements, mostly [[Hydrogen|hydrogen]] with some helium and lithium, coalesced under gravity, aided by [[Dark matter|dark matter]], into the first stars and galaxies. The theory describes the expansion of space itself, not an explosion in preexisting space.

Alexander Friedmann introduced the concept of an expanding universe in 1922 through the Friedmann equations. [[Edwin Hubble]] published the earliest empirical observation of expansion, now called Hubble's law, in 1929, showing that galaxies recede from Earth at rates proportional to their distance. In 1931 [[Georges Lemaître]] proposed that the universe emerged from a "primeval atom." The CMB was discovered accidentally in 1964, and its uniformity and spectrum fit the Big Bang models; by the late 1960s most cosmologists had rejected the competing steady-state model.

In the current universe, luminous matter makes up less than 5% of the density, dark matter 27%, and [[Dark energy|dark energy]], to which accelerating expansion is attributed, 68%. Unexplained aspects include the matter-antimatter imbalance and the nature of dark matter.

Landscape receiving little precipitation, and therefore supporting distinctive biomes and ecosystems. About one-third of Earth's land surface is arid or semi-arid. Deserts generally receive less than 250 mm (10 in) of precipitation a year. The category includes polar regions, sometimes called "cold deserts"; [[Antarctica]], about 98% thick continental ice sheet and 2% barren rock, is the world's largest.

[[Weathering]] forms deserts as large day–night temperature swings strain rocks until they break. Occasional downpours cause [[Flash flood|flash floods]], and wind carries sand and dust, sometimes forming sand or dust storms, and abrades surfaces. Sand ends up as level sheets or high [[Dune|dunes]]; other deserts are stony plains whose surfaces form a mosaic of smooth stones known as desert pavement. Temporary lakes and salt pans form where waters evaporate, and oases occur where springs or [[Aquifer|aquifers]] supply water. Deserts reflect more incoming light than forests or the sea, which moderates Earth's temperature.

Plants are tough and wiry, with small or no leaves, water-resistant cuticles, and often spines; some annuals germinate, bloom, and die within weeks of rain, while long-lived plants have deep roots. Many animals are nocturnal, shelter in shade or underground, conserve water, and may lie dormant for long periods before breeding rapidly after rain.

Nomads move flocks to available grazing, and oases support settled life. Cultivating semi-arid regions encourages erosion and desertification, but [[Irrigation|irrigation]] allows farming, as in California's Imperial Valley. Camel caravans carried salt, gold, ivory, and other goods along trade routes across the [[Sahara]], and large numbers of slaves were also taken north.

ENGLISH:
Means of protection from financial loss in which, for a fee, one party agrees to compensate another for a certain loss, damage, or injury.

A form of risk management, it protects against contingent or uncertain loss. The insurer issues a policy detailing when it will compensate the insured or a designated beneficiary. The policyholder pays a premium, accepting a small known loss for the promise of compensation, usually for something in which the insured has an insurable interest. After a loss, the insured submits a claim, processed by a claims adjuster. Insurers rely on the [[Law of large numbers|law of large numbers]] to forecast aggregate losses.

The Lex Rhodia, the general average principle of marine insurance, was established on [[Rhodes]] in approximately 1000 to 800 BC and preserved in the *Digesta* ordered by [[Justinian I]] in 533. Direct insurance of sea risks for a premium began in [[Belgium]] about 1300 AD, and separate insurance contracts were invented in [[Genoa]] in the 14th century; the first known dates from 1347. The earliest known life insurance policy was made at the Royal Exchange, [[London]], on June 18, 1583, for £383, 6s. 8d. on the life of William Gibbons.

Property insurance can be traced to the [[Great Fire of London]] in 1666, which destroyed more than 13,000 houses. In 1681 Nicholas Barbon and eleven associates established the first fire insurance company, the "Insurance Office for Houses". Insurance faces moral hazard, in which insured parties may take more risks, and insurer insolvency during correlated losses; regulation enforces solvency, transparency, and fair practices.

[[Barred spiral galaxy]] that includes the [[Solar System]]; also called the Milky Way Galaxy or simply the Galaxy. The name describes its appearance from Earth: a hazy band of light in the night sky formed by stars in other arms of the galaxy, too distant to be distinguished individually by the naked eye.

[[Galileo Galilei]] first resolved the band into individual stars with his telescope in 1610. Until the early 1920s most astronomers thought it contained all the stars in the universe. After the 1920 Great Debate between Harlow Shapley and Heber Doust Curtis, observations by [[Edwin Hubble]] in 1923 showed it to be only one of many galaxies.

The galaxy has a D25 isophotal diameter estimated at 26.8 ± 1.1 kiloparsecs (87,400 ± 3,600 light-years), and is surrounded by a dark matter area whose total diameter may approach 613 kpc (2 million light-years). The stars in the innermost 10,000 light-years form a bulge, from which the bar radiates. At the center is the intense radio source [[Sagittarius A*]], a supermassive black hole with a mass of 4.100 (±0.034) million solar masses. It is estimated to contain 100–400 billion stars. Its total mass, including [[Dark matter]], is estimated at 1.5 trillion solar masses.

The galaxy is estimated to have formed around 13.6 billion years ago and has grown through mergers with smaller galaxies. The Solar System lies on the inner edge of the Orion Arm, about 27,000 light-years (8.3 kpc) from the Galactic Center, and takes about 240 million years to orbit it. The Milky Way has several satellite galaxies and belongs to the [[Local Group]], part of the Virgo Supercluster, itself a component of the [[Laniakea Supercluster]].

=== KEY: Scientific_method_fb3b59

ENGLISH:
Empirical method of acquiring knowledge through careful observation, skepticism, hypothesis testing, and experimental validation. Developed from ancient and medieval practices, it acknowledges that cognitive assumptions can distort the interpretation of observations. It has characterized science since at least the 17th century.

Inquiry typically involves forming a testable [[Hypothesis|hypothesis]] through inductive reasoning, predicting its consequences, testing it through [[Experiment|experiments]] and statistical analysis, and adjusting or discarding it according to the results. A hypothesis must be falsifiable: some possible outcome must conflict with its predictions. Although often presented as a fixed sequence of steps, the method is better described as a set of general principles; not all steps occur in every inquiry or in the same order, and chance has played a role in some discoveries.

Early expressions of [[Empiricism|empiricism]] appear with the ancient Stoics, Aristotle, Epicurus, [[Ibn al-Haytham|Alhazen]], Avicenna, Al-Biruni, Roger Bacon, and William of Ockham. During the [[Scientific Revolution]], [[Francis Bacon]] and Robert Hooke furthered empiricism, [[René Descartes]] described a rationalist approach, and inductivism rose to prominence through Isaac Newton and his followers. The term "scientific method" emerged in the 19th century, as institutional development of science and words such as "scientist" and "pseudoscience" drew boundaries between science and non-science.

By the 1960s and 1970s, philosophers of science such as Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend had questioned its universality, favoring a view of science as a heterogeneous and local practice. In *Against Method* (1975), Feyerabend argued against any universal rules of science; Karl Popper and others disagreed. Lee Smolin's 2013 essay "There Is No Scientific Method" and Daniel Thurs's 2015 chapter, which called it a myth or at best an idealization, show that the debate continues.

American [[Aviation|aviation]] pioneers who built and flew the first successful powered [[Airplane|airplane]]. Their 1903 [[Wright Flyer]] made the first controlled, sustained flight of an engine-powered, heavier-than-air aircraft, near Kill Devil Hills, [[North Carolina]]. Their key invention was a three-axis control system that let the pilot steer and balance the aircraft, and it remains standard on airplanes. Unlike other experimenters who emphasized engine power, they focused on pilot control and used a small home-built wind tunnel to collect more accurate data. Their 1904 Flyer II made longer flights, including the first circle, and the 1905 Flyer III was the first truly practical [[Fixed-wing aircraft|fixed-wing aircraft]]. They learned mechanics in a [[Dayton, Ohio|Dayton, Ohio]] shop that worked on printing presses and bicycles, and they conducted [[Glider (aircraft)|glider]] tests from 1900 until 1903.

ENGLISH:
Chinese philosopher, historian, politician and poet of the Southern [[Song dynasty|Song dynasty]], and a leading figure in the development of Neo-Confucianism. His commentaries and editions of the Four Books, including the [[Analects|Analects]] and [[Mencius|Mencius]], became the core texts of the imperial civil service examinations from 1313 until their abolition in 1905. He emphasized the "investigation of things" and meditation as essential to moral and intellectual self-cultivation, and he criticized Buddhist approaches that claimed insight detached from ritual and learning. His thought became the official state ideology of China from the [[Yuan dynasty|Yuan dynasty]] onward and was later adopted in Japan, Korea and Vietnam. He wrote, compiled or edited almost a hundred books, and his followers recorded thousands of his conversations.
