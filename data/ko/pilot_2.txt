=== KEY: Natural_science_733d8d
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Natural science
ENTRY:
Branch of science concerned with the description, understanding and prediction of natural phenomena, based on empirical evidence from observation and experimentation. It is divided into two main branches: life science, also known as [[Biology|biology]], and physical science, which includes [[Physics|physics]], [[Astronomy|astronomy]], [[Earth science]] and [[Chemistry|chemistry]]. Natural science succeeded classical [[Natural philosophy|natural philosophy]]. It uses tools from mathematics and logic to convert information about nature into statements of natural laws. Systematic data collection succeeded natural history, which emerged in the 16th century by describing and classifying plants, animals and minerals. Mechanisms such as [[Peer review|peer review]] and reproducibility help ensure the validity of findings. [[Karl Popper]] proposed a controversial criterion of falsifiability to distinguish scientific endeavors from nonscientific ones.

=== KEY: Nuclear_chemistry_707a7c
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Nuclear chemistry
ENTRY:
Branch of chemistry concerned with radioactivity and nuclear processes. Its scope covers radioactive elements such as the [[Actinide|actinides]], [[Radium]] and radon, and equipment such as [[Nuclear reactor|nuclear reactors]], including corrosion and the behavior of materials in waste storage. It also studies how radiation alters biochemicals in living organisms, which underpins radiation biology and helps explain cancer radiotherapy. Radioactive sources are used in medicine, as industrial and environmental tracers, and to modify materials such as polymers. [[Henri Becquerel]] discovered radioactivity in uranium, and [[Marie Curie]] and Pierre Curie then isolated [[Polonium|polonium]] and radium from uranium ore. [[Ernest Rutherford]] showed that radioactive decay follows a simple equation, implying a characteristic [[Half-life|half-life]].

=== KEY: Nuclear_fission_54a6ea
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Nuclear fission
ENTRY:
Splitting of an atomic nucleus into two or more smaller nuclei, releasing large amounts of energy. The process often produces [[Neutron|neutrons]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]. The fission of one [[Uranium-235]] atom releases about 100 million times the energy of burning one [[Carbon|carbon]] atom in air to produce CO₂. [[Otto Hahn]] and Fritz Strassmann proved in December 1938 that fission had occurred, and [[Lise Meitner]] and Otto Frisch explained it theoretically in January 1939. Hahn and Strassmann predicted additional neutrons, opening the possibility of a chain reaction. Three isotopes, U-233, U-235 and Pu-239, can sustain such a chain reaction, which allows reactors to release energy in a controlled way and weapons to release it uncontrollably within about a microsecond. Most fissions are binary, producing two charged fragments, but three fragments form occasionally (2 to 4 times per 1000 events).

=== KEY: Nucleic_acid_4ea1f5
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Nucleic acid
ENTRY:
Large [[Biomolecule|biomolecule]] made of nucleotides, essential in all cells and viruses. Each nucleotide consists of a five-carbon sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base. The two main classes are [[DNA]], which contains deoxyribose, and [[RNA]], which contains ribose. The bases are adenine, cytosine, guanine, thymine and uracil; thymine occurs only in DNA and uracil only in RNA. In DNA, the bases pair in a double helix, with adenine paired to thymine and guanine to cytosine. Sequences of DNA store coded instructions as [[Gene|genes]], and RNA sequences help build the proteins that determine most chemical processes of life. Nucleic acids occur in all life forms, including bacteria, archaea, mitochondria, chloroplasts and viruses. Friedrich Miescher discovered the substance in 1869, and [[Francis Crick]] and [[James Watson]] proposed the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953.

=== KEY: Nutrition_cf6586
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Nutrition
ENTRY:
Biochemical and physiological process by which an organism uses food and water to support its life. The intake of these substances provides nutrients, divided into macro- and micronutrients, which are metabolized to create energy and chemical structures; too much or too little of an essential nutrient can cause [[Malnutrition|malnutrition]]. Nutritional science typically emphasizes human nutrition.

The type of organism determines which nutrients it needs and how it obtains them: by consuming organic matter, consuming inorganic matter, absorbing light, or some combination. All forms of life require carbon, energy, and water. Animals require complex nutrients such as [[Carbohydrate|carbohydrates]], [[Lipid|lipids]], and [[Protein|proteins]], obtained by consuming other organisms. Humans developed [[Agriculture|agriculture]] and cooking to replace foraging. Plants acquire nutrients through the soil and atmosphere, and fungi absorb them through the mycelium.

Approximately 30 elements are found in organic matter, with nitrogen, carbon, and phosphorus the most important. Micronutrients, needed in trace amounts, are classified as vitamins (organic) and minerals (inorganic). Essential nutrients are those the body cannot synthesize. Over-nutrition of macronutrients is a major cause of [[Obesity|obesity]] and raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, stroke, hypertension, coronary heart disease, osteoporosis, and some cancers.

Scientific analysis of food began during the chemical revolution of the late 18th century, and modern nutrition science began in the 1910s as micronutrients were identified. Thiamine was the first vitamin chemically identified, in 1926, and in 1932 [[Vitamin C]] was identified as a protection against [[Scurvy|scurvy]]. The first recommended dietary allowances were developed to address fears of deficiency diseases during the Great Depression and the Second World War.

ENTRY:
Hardwood tree or shrub of the genus *Quercus* in the [[Beech|beech]] family. Oaks are widely distributed across the Northern Hemisphere, with some 475 species, both deciduous and evergreen. Almost a third of oak species are threatened with extinction from [[Climate change|climate change]], pests and habitat loss. The oak is a symbol of strength and the national tree of many countries.

=== KEY: Pandemic_d5380d
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Pandemic
ENTRY:
Epidemic of an infectious disease that spreads across a large region, often worldwide. A pandemic involves a sudden increase in cases and affects a substantial portion of the human population. Widespread endemic diseases with stable case numbers, such as seasonal influenza, are generally excluded. The word comes from Greek *pan*, meaning all, and *demos*, meaning people. The [[Black Death]], caused by plague, killed up to half of Europe's population in the 14th century. The 1918 H1N1 influenza pandemic, known as the Spanish flu, is the deadliest in history. More recent pandemics include HIV/AIDS, the 2009 swine flu pandemic and the [[COVID-19 pandemic]]. In May 2025 the [[World Health Organization]] adopted a pandemic agreement, approved with 120 votes in favor and 70 abstentions, without the United States, which had withdrawn from the organization.

=== KEY: Pasteurization_cb5f38
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Pasteurization
ENTRY:
Process of preserving foods, such as [[Milk|milk]] and fruit juices, with mild heat. Packaged foods are heated to less than 100 °C (212 °F) to destroy or deactivate [[Microorganism|microorganisms]] and enzymes that cause spoilage or disease, including most vegetative bacteria, though most bacterial spores survive. The process is named after French microbiologist [[Louis Pasteur]], whose research in the 1860s showed that heat could deactivate unwanted microorganisms in wine. Milk is commonly heated to 72°C for 15 seconds, or to 63°C for 30 minutes. Non-thermal methods such as high-pressure processing and pulsed electric field treatment are also used.

=== KEY: Persepolis_141a17

ENTRY:
Ceremonial capital of the [[Achaemenid Empire|Achaemenid Empire]], in the plains of Marvdasht, Fars province, [[Iran|Iran]]. === KEY: Petrarch_eab87e

HEADWORD: Petrarch
ENTRY:
Italian scholar and poet, one of the earliest Renaissance [[Humanism|humanists]]. His rediscovery of [[Cicero]]'s letters is often credited with initiating the 14th-century Italian Renaissance and the founding of Renaissance humanism. === KEY: Poetic_Edda_4de5bb
LEVEL: 4  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Poetic Edda
ENTRY:
Collection of anonymous Old Norse narrative poems in alliterative verse, known as Eddic poems. The Poetic Edda is distinct from the *Prose Edda* of [[Snorri Sturluson]], and its basis is the medieval Icelandic manuscript *Codex Regius*, also called the Elder Edda. Most poems are in *fornyrðislag* ("old story meter"), with about a quarter in *ljóðaháttr* ("song form"). Like most early poetry, they were passed down orally for centuries. Dating is debated. The poem *Atlamál*, which claims a Greenland origin, must date from about 985 or later, when Scandinavians first reached Greenland. The poems include mythological narratives about gods such as Odin and Thor and heroic lays about legendary figures such as Sigurd. Iceland was settled only around 870, so earlier poems most likely originated in [[Scandinavia]].

=== KEY: Population_bottleneck_204520

HEADWORD: Population bottleneck
ENTRY:
Sharp reduction in the size of a [[Population (biology)|population]]. Famines, earthquakes, floods, disease and droughts can cause one, as can human actions such as [[Genocide|genocide]] and intentional culling. === KEY: Pre_Columbian_era_7933b3
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Pre-Columbian era
ENTRY:
Period of the history of the Americas from the initial peopling of the continents to the onset of European colonization.

The era, also called the pre-contact, pre-Hispanic, or (in Brazil) pre-Cabraline era, runs from the Upper Paleolithic to the voyage of [[Christopher Columbus|Columbus]] in 1492, though in some regions significant European influence came only decades or centuries later. Many Indigenous civilizations developed cities, agriculture, monumental architecture, and complex social hierarchies. Some had declined before the first permanent European colonies and are known mainly through [[Archaeology|archaeological]] research and oral histories; others, such as the [[Maya civilization|Maya]], who kept written records, were documented in European accounts.

Asian Paleo-Indians are thought to have entered the Americas by way of the Bering Land Bridge ([[Bering Strait]]) and possibly along the coast. The date is much debated. The [[Clovis culture]] has sites some 13,000 years old, but sites of 20,000 years have been claimed. A short chronology puts the first movement beyond Alaska no earlier than 14,000–17,000 years ago; a long chronology proposes 30,000–40,000 years ago or earlier.

By the time of contact, populations had diversified into thousands of cultures, estimated at 45–80 million people. Domesticated crops included [[Maize|maize]], potatoes, and quinoa. Major societies included the mound-building Mississippian culture, which built [[Cahokia]]; the Maya, with hieroglyphic writing and calendars; and the [[Inca Empire]] of the Andes, with stonework and roads spanning over 40,000 km. Many of these societies also practiced warfare, slavery, and human sacrifice. North American populations peaked around 1150 before regional declines.

=== KEY: Printing_b002a5
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Printing
ENTRY:
Process for mass reproducing text and images using a master form or template.

The earliest known form evolved from ink rubbings made on paper or cloth from texts on stone tablets in the sixth century, and printing by pressing an inked image onto paper (woodblock printing) appeared later that century. Mechanical woodblock printing on paper began in the 7th century in the [[Tang dynasty]] and spread through East Asia. The first completely surviving printed book is the *[[Diamond Sutra]]* of 868, found at Dunhuang and held in the [[British Library]].

From 900 to 1444 wooden and tin block printing (*ṭarsh*) was used across the Islamic world for amulets, tax receipts, and state decrees, and then vanished without explanation. In [[Europe]], block printing was common on cloth by 1300 and moved quickly to woodcut religious images and playing cards on paper once paper became easily available, around 1400. Block-books, cheaper than manuscripts, emerged in the mid-15th century.

Movable type allowed more flexible processes than hand copying or block printing. Bi Sheng created the first known system in China around 1040, using porcelain. Copper movable type originated in China at the beginning of the 12th century and was used to print Northern Song paper money, though woodblock remained the main method for Chinese, with its thousands of characters. Korea produced the *Jikji* (1377), the earliest extant book printed with metal movable type. [[Johannes Gutenberg]] invented the printing press in the 15th century in Europe, combining cast metal type with a screw press, and the [[Gutenberg Bible]] was printed around 1455.

Printing played a key role in the [[Renaissance]] and the [[Scientific Revolution]] and laid the material basis for the modern knowledge-based economy and the spread of learning to the masses. Methods today include offset lithography for commercial volumes and digital printing for on-demand production.

=== KEY: Privacy_c5f29b
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Privacy
ENTRY:
Condition or claim of an individual or group to be free from unwanted access, observation, or disclosure, and to determine how far they and information about them are exposed to others.

The concept has no single accepted definition. Lawyers, philosophers, and social scientists have understood it as a right to be let alone, as limited access to the self, as secrecy, or as control over personal information.

Distinctions between public and private life appear in many ancient societies, but privacy emerged as a distinct legal claim only in the 19th century, when printing and photographic technologies and the popular press prompted calls for protection. In "The Right to Privacy" (1890), the Boston lawyers Samuel D. Warren and Louis Brandeis argued that the [[Common law|common law]] should recognize a general "right to be let alone". In the second half of the 20th century the spread of computers shifted attention to the collection and processing of personal data, producing data protection law that began in Western Europe in the 1970s and has since spread worldwide.

Privacy is protected as a [[Human rights|human right]] by Article 12 of the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]], Article 17 of the [[International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights]], and regional instruments such as Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. European law tends to treat privacy as an aspect of personal dignity and regulates personal data comprehensively, whereas U.S. law has traditionally emphasized liberty against the state and regulates the private sector by sector.

Privacy overlaps with but differs from [[Security|security]]: information security protects data against unauthorized access, while privacy also concerns whether data should be collected and used at all. The [[Internet]], mobile phones, and large-scale data analysis have intensified debate over [[Surveillance|surveillance]] by governments and corporations. Responses include legislation, litigation, and privacy-enhancing technologies such as [[Encryption|encryption]].

Spiny, globular [[Echinoderm|echinoderm]] forming the class Echinoidea. Approximately 950 species live on the seabed in every ocean, from the intertidal zone to the deep sea. === KEY: Sun_ef6572
LEVEL: 2  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Sun
ENTRY:
Star at the center of the [[Solar System]], a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) and the main source of energy for life on Earth.

The Sun is a massive sphere of hot plasma, heated to incandescence by [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]] reactions in its core, radiating energy from its surface mainly as visible light and infrared radiation, with 10% at ultraviolet energies. It has been an object of veneration in many cultures and a central subject of astronomical research since antiquity. It orbits the [[Galactic Center]] at a distance of 24,000 to 28,000 light-years. Its mean distance from Earth is about 1.496×10⁸ km, or about 8 light-minutes; this distance was used to define the [[Astronomical unit|astronomical unit]] (au), now defined as 149.5978707×10⁶ km.

The Sun is the largest and most massive object in the Solar System. Its diameter is about 1,391,400 km (864,600 mi), around 109 times that of Earth, and its mass is around 330,000 times Earth's, about 99.86% of the system's total. It has an absolute magnitude of +4.83, estimated to be brighter than about 85% of the stars in the Milky Way, most of which are red dwarfs. It has no definite boundary; its radius is measured to the edge of the photosphere, the apparent visible surface. Its oblateness is 8.2×10⁻⁶, or 8 parts per million, making it the natural object closest to a perfect sphere ever observed. The photosphere is 74.9% hydrogen and 23.8% helium by mass; heavier elements account for less than 2%, chiefly oxygen (roughly 1%), carbon (0.3%), neon (0.2%), and iron (0.2%).

The Sun rotates faster at its equator than at its poles, with a period of approximately 25.6 days at the equator and 33.5 days at the poles relative to the stars, and about 28 days at the equator as seen from Earth. The early Sun rotated up to ten times faster; magnetic braking, as its magnetic field interacted with the outflowing [[Solar wind|solar wind]], slowed it.

The Sun formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of matter within a large [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]]; most of the matter gathered in the center, and the rest flattened into a disk that became the Solar System. Every second, its core fuses about 600 billion kg of hydrogen into helium and converts four billion kg of matter into energy. About four to seven billion years from now, as core hydrogen fusion diminishes, the Sun will expand into a [[Red giant|red giant]]. Models suggest it will then shed its outer layers and become a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]], which may glow for perhaps trillions of years before becoming an extremely dense black dwarf. The English word *sun* derives from Old English *sunne*; the adjective *solar* comes from Latin *sol*. The astronomical symbol is ☉.

=== KEY: Telecommunications_ba075f
LEVEL: 3  PERSON: no
HEADWORD: Telecommunications
ENTRY:
Transmission of information over a distance by electrical or electronic means, typically through cables, radio waves, or other technologies. Channels may be divided for multiplexing, letting one medium carry several concurrent sessions. Long-distance technologies of the 19th, 20th, and 21st centuries generally use electric power and include the electrical telegraph, [[Telephone|telephone]], [[Television|television]], and [[Radio|radio]]. The term was coined in 1904 by the French engineer and novelist Édouard Estaunié, who defined it as "remote transmission of thought through electricity". Slow methods such as postal mail are excluded from the definition.

Earlier methods included smoke signals, beacons, and homing pigeons; in 1849 Paul Julius Reuter ran a pigeon service carrying stock prices between Aachen and Brussels until the telegraph gap closed. In 1792 Claude Chappe built the first fixed visual telegraph line, between Lille and Paris; the last commercial line was abandoned in 1880. On July 25, 1837, William Fothergill Cooke and Charles Wheatstone demonstrated the first commercial electrical telegraph. [[Samuel Morse]] independently developed a version and a code that was an important advance. Many inventors contributed to the telephone, including [[Alexander Graham Bell]]. [[Guglielmo Marconi]], who won the 1909 Nobel Prize in Physics, led a revolution in wireless communication in the first decade of the 20th century.

Since the 1960s, digital technologies have supplemented voice with data, and the limits of metallic media prompted optical fiber. Mobile networks evolved from 1G analog to 5G. The [[Internet]] is independent of any given medium and has widened global access to services. In the United States, the AT&T monopoly was broken up in 1984; debates persist over natural monopoly in network deployment and the need for antitrust oversight.

=== KEY: Thomas_Young_scientist_8cd624

HEADWORD: Young, Thomas
ENTRY:
British polymath. Young made notable contributions to vision, light, solid mechanics, energy, physiology, language, musical harmony and Egyptology. He is credited with establishing [[Christiaan Huygens]]' wave theory of light, in contrast to the corpuscular theory of [[Isaac Newton]]. In 1801 he proposed a trichromatic theory of color vision and identified astigmatism, and in 1807 he formulated [[Young's modulus]], a measure of a material's stiffness. In 1814 he deciphered the demotic script of the [[Rosetta Stone]] and identified phonetic values in hieroglyphs. His work influenced [[Hermann von Helmholtz]], [[James Clerk Maxwell]] and [[Albert Einstein]]. Young also practiced as a physician and was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1794.
