ENTRY:
Gun that shoots projectiles using the explosive pressure generated by combustion (deflagration) of a chemical propellant, most often black powder in antique firearms and smokeless powder in modern ones. The term is defined variably, both technically and legally, in different countries. Small arms are the subset of light firearms designed to be readily carried and operated by an individual; they include handguns such as pistols, revolvers, and derringers, and long guns such as [[Rifle|rifles]], shotguns, submachine guns, and light machine guns.

The first firearms originated in 10th-century Song dynasty China, where bamboo tubes containing [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]] and pellet projectiles were mounted on spears to make the portable fire lance, which was used effectively as a shock weapon in the siege of De'an in 1132. In the 13th century, fire lance barrels were replaced with metal tubes and became the hand cannon, and the technology gradually spread throughout Eurasia during the 14th century. Beginning in the 1860s, black powder was gradually replaced by smokeless powder, which is mostly based on nitrocellulose. Most modern firearms, with the exception of smoothbore shotguns, have rifled barrels that impart a stabilizing spin to the bullet.

Modern firearms are described by caliber (bore diameter), by type of action (single-shot or repeating, such as bolt, lever, pump, revolver, semi-automatic, or fully automatic), by firing mechanism (such as matchlock, flintlock, or centerfire), and by intended use. The effective range of handguns and shotguns generally does not exceed 100 m, while most rifles are accurate to 500 m using iron sights, and sniper rifles to more than 2,000 m.

In the 2018 estimate of the Small Arms Survey, there were over one billion firearms worldwide, of which 857 million were in civilian hands; U.S. civilians accounted for 393 million. Firearm regulation remains the subject of ongoing public debate.

Profession of controlling and extinguishing [[Fire|fires]]; a person who does this is a firefighter. Firefighters undergo technical training in structural and [[Wildfire|wildland]] firefighting, and the work is dangerous because of smoke, oxygen deficiency, high temperatures, poisonous atmospheres and violent air flows. Firefighters carry self-contained breathing apparatus, and operations begin with reconnaissance to locate the fire and identify its risks. Fires are extinguished by removing water, fuel or oxidant, or by chemical flame inhibition, and the extinguisher must suit the fire type, such as grease, paper or electrical. Rome's Vigiles, a force of 7,000 people equipped with buckets and axes who also served as police, were the first organized firefighting force. In the 3rd century BC, the Alexandrian Greek Ctesibius built a double force pump.

Soft-bodied [[Beetle|beetle]] of the family *Lampyridae*, many of whose species produce light. Fireflies are also called lightning bugs or glowworms, and they glow mainly at twilight to attract mates. Light production is thought to have originated as a warning signal to predators, because the larvae were distasteful, and was later co-opted as a mating signal. Females of the genus *Photuris* mimic the flash pattern of *Photinus* males to lure and eat them. Most larvae are predators that feed on other larvae, [[Snail|snails]] and slugs, and most fireflies hibernate as larvae. Adults live a few weeks in summer. Populations are declining in many areas because of habitat loss, artificial light, [[Pesticide|pesticides]] and climate change.

Low-explosive pyrotechnic devices used for aesthetic and entertainment purposes. Fireworks produce four primary effects: noise, light, smoke and floating materials such as confetti. They are classed as ground or aerial. Aerial fireworks may be propelled by a rocket, as skyrockets are, or shot into the air as aerial shells from a [[Mortar (weapon)|mortar]]. Mismanagement can lead to accidents, and China remains the largest manufacturer and exporter of fireworks.

Law of thermodynamics applying [[Conservation of energy|conservation of energy]] to thermodynamic processes. Aquatic, anamniotic, gill-bearing vertebrates with a tough cranium protecting the brain. Many have fins used for directional stability and propulsion. Fish are grouped into the more basal jawless fish and the more common jawed fish, which include all living cartilaginous and bony fish and the extinct placoderms and acanthodians. Because tetrapods are descended from fish, modern phylogenetics treats fish as a paraphyletic group that includes all vertebrates except tetrapods, and the class Pisces of older reference works is no longer used. The plural is *fish* for individuals and *fishes* for species.

Most fish are cold-blooded, though large, active swimmers such as the white shark and tuna can maintain a higher core temperature. Many communicate acoustically. The study of fish is ichthyology. There are over 33,000 extant species, more than all other traditional vertebrate classes combined. Most belong to the class [[Actinopterygii]], the ray-finned fishes, and about 96% of living species are teleosts, a crown group that can protrude their jaws.

About 530 million years ago, during the [[Cambrian explosion]], fishlike animals with a notochord, such as *Haikouichthys*, appear in the fossil record. The ostracoderms, the earliest fish with dedicated respiratory gills and paired fins, had heavy bony plates as protection against invertebrate predators. The first jawed fish, the [[Placoderm|placoderms]], appeared in the Silurian and diversified greatly during the Devonian, the "Age of Fishes." Bony fish, distinguished by swim bladders and later ossified endoskeletons, became dominant after the end-Devonian extinction wiped out the placoderms. [[Tetrapod|Tetrapods]] evolved from lobe-finned fish during the Carboniferous, developing air-breathing lungs homologous to swim bladders.

Fish have been an important natural resource for humans since prehistoric times, especially as food. They are harvested from wild fisheries or farmed, caught for recreation, and kept in aquaria. In human culture they have served as deities, religious symbols, and subjects of art, books, and films.

Activity of trying to catch fish. Fish are often caught from freshwater or marine environments, but may also be caught from stocked bodies of water such as ponds, canals, and reservoirs. ENTRY:
Cell or organism with one or more whip-like appendages called [[Flagellum|flagella]]. The term describes a cell construction found in many prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and it does not imply a specific relationship among the organisms that bear flagella. Flagella are generally used for propulsion and sometimes to create currents that bring in food. In some flagellates they direct food into a cytostome, or mouth. Flagellates form a polyphyletic group of over 8,000 described species, many of them single-celled [[Protist|protists]]. Many parasites are flagellates in at least one life-cycle stage, including Naegleria, Trichomonas and Plasmodium. In aquatic ecosystems, flagellates are major consumers of bacteria and other protists.

ENTRY:
Hair-like cellular appendage that provides motility. Flagella protrude from certain plant and animal sperm cells, from [[Spore|fungal spores]] (zoospores) and from many [[Microorganism|microorganisms]]. The name is [[Latin]] for "whip" or "scourge," describing the lash-like swimming motion. Structure, protein composition and propulsion differ among the [[Bacteria]], [[Archaea]] and Eukaryota domains, but all provide motility. Bacterial flagella are helical filaments turned by rotary motors at their base; the archaeal version, the archaellum, is superficially similar but considered non-homologous. Eukaryotic flagella are membrane projections supported by the cytoskeleton that bend back and forth, with nine doublet microtubules surrounding two central singlets. A cell may have zero, one or many flagella. *Helicobacter pylori* uses its flagella to swim through the stomach to the mucous lining, and *Salmonella typhimurium* can sense wetness with them.
