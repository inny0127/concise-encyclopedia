Performer who uses mime, or pantomime, as a theatrical medium or performance art. Mime originated in [[Ancient Greece]], where the first recorded mime was Telestēs in the play *Seven Against Thebes* by [[Aeschylus]]. It was an aspect of Roman theater from its earliest times and became the predominant Roman drama under the [[Roman Empire]]; it was distinguished by its absence of masks and by female as well as male performers. Species are native to the [[Americas]], to eastern Africa, and to the Indian subcontinent and Indochina. The generic name derives from Greek *mimos*, meaning actor or mime, and refers to the "sensitive leaves" that seem to "mimic conscious life." *Mimosa pudica*, or touch-me-not, folds its leaves when touched or exposed to heat; it is widely cultivated as a houseplant and outdoors in the tropics, where it has become a weed in some areas, including [[Hawaii]]. *Mimosa tenuiflora* is used in shamanic [[Ayahuasca|ayahuasca]] brews for the psychedelic drug dimethyltryptamine in its root bark. Philosophical view that mental phenomena are non-physical, or that mind and body are distinct and separable. ENTRY:
Totality of psychological phenomena and capacities, encompassing both conscious and unconscious states. The mind is that which thinks, feels, perceives, imagines, remembers, and wills. Conscious processes make an individual aware of external and internal circumstances; unconscious processes can influence an individual without intention or awareness. Its exact nature is disputed: some characterizations stress internal aspects, saying that the mind transforms information and is not directly accessible to outside observers, while others understand mental phenomena as dispositions to engage in observable behavior. In a narrow sense the term covers only cognitive functions associated with perception, reasoning, awareness, and memory; in a broader sense it also includes feeling, motivation, and behavior.

The English word derives from [[Old English]] *gemynd*, meaning "memory", which gave rise to [[Middle English]] forms and slowly expanded to cover all mental capacities. Cognates include the ancient Greek μένος, the [[Latin]] *mens*, and the Sanskrit *manas*. Psyche and mentality are usually treated as synonyms, while soul, spirit, cognition, intellect, intelligence, and brain overlap with mind without meaning exactly the same. The brain is the physical organ responsible for most or all mental functions.

The mind encompasses [[Perception|perception]], [[Memory|memory]], thought, [[Imagination|imagination]], motivation, emotion, attention, learning, and consciousness. Perception interprets and organizes sensory information and is shaped by past experiences, cultural background, beliefs, knowledge, and expectations. Episodic memory concerns specific past events, semantic memory general knowledge, and procedural memory how to do things; short-term memory holds information briefly, while long-term memory can last a lifetime. Thinking is goal-oriented processing of information and includes logical reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making; some theorists hold that all thought happens through language. Imagination internally generates mental images and ideas without direct stimulation of the sense organs.

The mind–body problem is the challenge of explaining the relation between matter and mind. Traditionally the two were often thought of as distinct substances; René Descartes's 17th-century dualism conceived the mind as a non-spatial, spiritual substance interacting with the body through the pineal gland. The dominant philosophical position since the 20th century has been physicalism, which holds that everything is material, so that minds are aspects or features of certain material objects. The evolutionary history of the mind is tied to the development of nervous systems and brains, with particular brain areas dedicated to specific functions. Some people are affected by [[Mental disorder|mental disorders]], in which certain mental capacities do not function as they should. It is widely accepted that at least some non-human animals have some form of mind, but which animals is controversial, and theorists also discuss creating artificial minds with computers.

The main fields studying the mind are psychology, neuroscience, cognitive science, and [[Philosophy of mind|philosophy of mind]], which use methods ranging from empirical observation and neuroimaging to conceptual analysis and thought experiments.

ENTRY:
Naturally occurring solid with a fairly well-defined chemical composition and a specific crystal structure. The geological definition normally excludes compounds that occur only in living organisms. A mineral is distinct from a [[Rock (geology)|rock]], which may consist of one mineral or an aggregate of several.

The International Mineralogical Association (IMA), the standard body for naming mineral species, recognizes 6,228 species. It requires that a mineral be formed by natural geological processes, be solid in its natural occurrence, and have a well-defined crystallographic structure and fairly well-defined composition. Substances made only by human activity or in living beings, such as tungsten carbide and seashells, are excluded. Native [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] is a classified exception, because it was included before the current rules. Water [[Ice|ice]] is a mineral; carbon dioxide is not. Amorphous materials such as [[Opal|opal]] and obsidian are called mineraloids.

A compound with different crystal structures yields different species: [[Quartz|quartz]] and stishovite are both silicon dioxide. Composition may vary within a species, as in mackinawite, (Fe,Ni)₉S₈. Continuous series are sometimes split arbitrarily, as in the [[Olivine|olivine]] group, whose end-members forsterite and fayalite count as separate minerals. Varieties can have their own names; [[Amethyst|amethyst]] is a purple variety of quartz.

Descriptions include hardness, luster, color, streak, cleavage, magnetism, and reaction to acid. Minerals are classified by key chemical constituents, chiefly under the Dana and Strunz systems. Groups include native elements, sulfides (galena, PbS), oxides, halides (rock salt, NaCl), carbonates (calcite, CaCO₃), sulfates, phosphates, and silicates, which make up approximately 90% of [[Earth's crust|Earth's crust]].

Products include metals, [[Coal|coal]], gemstones, limestone, rock salt, potash, gravel, and clay; in a wider sense the term covers non-renewable resources such as petroleum and natural gas. An [[Ore|ore]] must contain a valuable constituent that can be extracted and sold for profit. Modern mining involves prospecting, assessing a proposed mine's profit potential, extraction, and reclamation of the land after closure.

The oldest known mine is the Ngwenya Mine in [[Eswatini]], about 41,000–43,000 years old according to radiocarbon dating, where Paleolithic people mined hematite to make red ochre. Flint mines such as Grimes Graves and Krzemionki are mostly Neolithic (c. 4000–3000 BC). The gold mines of Nubia were among the most extensive in ancient Egypt. The silver mines of Laurium, worked by over 20,000 slaves, helped support [[Athens]], and [[Philip II of Macedon]] captured the gold mines of Mount Pangeo in 357 BC to fund his campaigns. The Romans developed large-scale methods, using water brought by [[Aqueduct (water supply)|aqueducts]] for hydraulic mining.

Mining is associated with land degradation, water contamination, habitat loss, occupational health risks, and community displacement. Most nations regulate it, but governments frequently fail to enforce the rules fully, because mining often generates much of the business in remote or economically depressed communities. Unregulated or illegal mining, especially in developing economies, frequently contributes to human rights violations and environmental conflicts. Where enforced, modern practices have significantly improved safety.

River, central United States, flowing from northern Minnesota to the [[Gulf of Mexico]]. It is the primary river of the largest drainage basin in the U.S. and the second-longest in the country after the [[Missouri River|Missouri]]. From its traditional source at Lake Itasca it flows generally south for 2,340 miles (3,770 km) to the Mississippi River Delta. Its watershed drains all or parts of 32 U.S. states and two Canadian provinces; the total drainage basin is 1,151,000 square miles (2,980,000 sq km). It ranks as the world's tenth-largest river by discharge and the largest in North America. The name comes from *Misi-ziibi* ("Great River"), the Anishinaabe ([[Ojibwe]]) name rendered by the French as *Misi zipi*.

The river is divided into the Upper Mississippi, from the headwaters to the confluence with the Missouri at St. Louis; the Middle Mississippi, from there to the Ohio; and the Lower Mississippi, from the Ohio to the Gulf. Between Lake Itasca and St. Louis, 43 dams moderate the flow.

Native Americans have lived along the river for thousands of years; some, such as the Mound Builders, formed agricultural and urban civilizations. [[Hernando de Soto]] reached it in 1541. It formed borders for New Spain, New France, and the early United States. After the [[Louisiana Purchase]] in 1803 it and its tributaries became pathways for westward expansion, and it became the subject of American literature, notably the writings of [[Mark Twain]]. Its final capture by Union forces in the [[American Civil War]] marked a turning point toward Union victory.

In the 20th century, massive engineering works such as [[Levee|levees]], locks, and dams were built, partly to keep the lower river from shifting into the Atchafalaya River channel and bypassing New Orleans. Agricultural runoff is the primary contributor to the Gulf of Mexico dead zone.
