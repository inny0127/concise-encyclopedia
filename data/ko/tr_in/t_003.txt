Branch of mathematics that studies [[Algebraic structure|algebraic structures]], sets with specific operations acting on their elements. These include [[Group (mathematics)|groups]], [[Ring (mathematics)|rings]], [[Field (mathematics)|fields]], modules, vector spaces, lattices, and algebras over a field. The term was coined in the early 20th century to distinguish the subject from [[Elementary algebra]], which uses variables to represent numbers in computation and reasoning. The abstract viewpoint has become so basic to advanced mathematics that it is simply called "algebra," and "abstract algebra" is seldom used outside teaching. Algebraic structures and their homomorphisms form mathematical categories, and [[Category theory]] gives a unified framework for studying them.

About 1700 BC, Babylonians solved quadratic equations posed as word problems. Al-Khwarizmi originated the word "algebra" in AD 830, but fully symbolic algebra appeared only with François Viète's *New Algebra* (1591). In the late 18th century Leonhard Euler accepted negative and imaginary roots, though most European mathematicians resisted such numbers until the mid-19th century. George Peacock's *Treatise of Algebra* (1830) was the first attempt to put algebra on a strictly symbolic basis.

Group theory grew from several sources: Lagrange's 1770 study of the quintic, Gauss's 1801 work on modular arithmetic, Klein's 1872 Erlangen program, and Lie's 1874 theory of Lie groups. Galois in 1832 was first to use the term "group," for a collection of permutations closed under composition. [[Arthur Cayley]] defined a group in 1854 as a set with an associative operation and an identity, and Walther von Dyck in 1882 first required inverses. Disparate results unified into axiomatic definitions of groups, rings, and fields in the early decades of the 20th century, with major contributions from [[Emmy Noether]] on ring theory. Applications include the classification of symmetries in geometry and chemistry, error-correcting codes, and cryptography.

Art that uses shape, form, color, and line to create compositions that may exist with a degree of independence from visual references in the world. Abstraction lies on a continuum. Art that conspicuously alters color or form is partially abstract, as in fauvism, which deliberately alters color, and [[Cubism]], which alters the forms of real objects. Total abstraction bears no trace of anything recognizable, as in geometric and lyrical abstraction.

From the Renaissance to the mid-19th century, Western art rested on the logic of perspective and the illusion of visible reality. By the late 19th century many artists wanted an art that reflected changes in technology, science, and philosophy. [[Impressionism]] and [[Expressionism]], which stressed sensation and psychological states over subject matter, prepared the way. James McNeill Whistler's *Nocturne in Black and Gold: The Falling Rocket* (1872) put visual sensation ahead of the depiction of objects, and Georgiana Houghton exhibited abstract "spirit" drawings in 1871. [[Paul Cézanne]]'s aim of a logical construction of reality in flat areas of modulated color became the basis of Cubism. Mysticism and theosophy influenced pioneers such as Hilma af Klint and [[Wassily Kandinsky]], and the teachings of Georges Gurdjieff and P. D. Ouspensky influenced Mondrian.

Fauvism, led by Henri Matisse, and Cubism, notably through Pablo Picasso, opened the door to abstraction in the early 20th century. In 1912 František Kupka exhibited *Amorpha, Fugue in Two Colors*, and Guillaume Apollinaire named the work of Robert Delaunay and others Orphism. Kandinsky produced some of the earliest acknowledged abstract paintings around 1910–12; [[Kazimir Malevich]] introduced Suprematism with *Black Square* (1915); and [[Piet Mondrian]] developed geometric neoplasticism. Later movements include [[Abstract expressionism]].

=== KEY: Abstraction_ef974c

ENTRY:
Process of generalizing rules and concepts from specific examples, first principles or other methods. The result is a concept that acts as a common noun for subordinate concepts and groups related ideas into a category. For example, abstracting a leather soccer ball to the idea of a ball keeps the general attributes of balls and excludes the particulars of one ball. In a type–token distinction, a type such as 'ball' is more abstract than its tokens. Abstraction is central to Alfred Korzybski's general semantics and to mathematics and science, where it allows theorems and models to apply broadly. Philosophers from [[Aristotle]] to [[John Locke]] debated how general ideas arise from particulars.

ENTRY:
Two measures of observational error. Accuracy is how close a measurement or set of measurements is to the true value, and precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other. Precision describes random error, quantified by the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] of a set of results. In its more common sense, accuracy describes systematic error, or bias. The International Organization for Standardization defines *trueness* as the closeness of the mean of many results to the reference value. A measurement system may be accurate, precise, both or neither. Increasing sample size improves precision but does not correct a systematic error, while removing the error improves accuracy without changing precision. A measurement system is considered valid only if it is both accurate and precise.
