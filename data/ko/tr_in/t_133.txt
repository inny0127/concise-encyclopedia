[[Amorphous solid|Amorphous]] (non-crystalline) solid, often transparent and chemically inert, used for window panes, tableware, and optics. It is most often formed by rapid cooling (quenching) of the molten form. Some glasses, such as volcanic glass, occur naturally, and [[Obsidian|obsidian]] has been used to make arrowheads and knives since the Stone Age.

Archaeological evidence suggests glassmaking dates back to at least 3600 BC in [[Mesopotamia]], Egypt, or Syria. The earliest known glass objects were beads, perhaps created accidentally during metalworking or the production of faience. Glassblowing, invented around the first century BC in the [[Roman Empire]], allowed faster production of intricate shapes.

Soda–lime glass, made from silica (sand), soda ash ([[Sodium carbonate|sodium carbonate]]), and lime ([[Calcium oxide|calcium oxide]]), contains around 70% silica and accounts for around 90% of modern manufactured glass. Rather than melting at a distinct temperature like crystalline materials, glass undergoes a glass transition from a rigid solid to a viscous liquid on heating. Flat glass is commonly made by the float process, in which molten glass is poured onto a bed of molten tin. Glass has traditionally been used for vessels such as bowls, bottles, and jars, and can be colored by adding metal salts, which led to its use in stained glass windows. Boron gives borosilicate glass, used in laboratory equipment, and chemical tempering strengthens smartphone screens.

The refractive, reflective, and transmissive properties of glass suit it to optical [[Lens|lenses]], prisms, and optoelectronics. Extruded glass fibers serve as optical fibers in [[Telecommunications|telecommunications]] networks, as thermal insulation when matted as glass wool, and in glass-fiber reinforced plastic (fiberglass).

Process of increasing interdependence and integration among the economies, markets, societies, and cultures of different countries worldwide. It is attributed to the reduction of barriers to [[International trade|international trade]], the liberalization of capital movements, the development of transportation infrastructure, and advances in information and communication technologies. The term first appeared in the early 20th century but came into popular use in the 1990s to describe the growing connectivity of the post–[[Cold War]] world.

Large-scale globalization began in the 1820s, and its origins are traced to the 18th and 19th centuries, when transportation and communication advances following the [[Industrial Revolution]] increased global interactions and trade. After World War II, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade and its successor, the [[World Trade Organization]], cut average tariffs from over 40% in the 1940s to below 5% by the 2000s. Between 1990 and 2010 globalization progressed rapidly, driven by the information and communication technology revolution, trade liberalization, and the shift of manufacturing to emerging economies, particularly [[China]]. Academic literature commonly divides it into economic, cultural, and political globalization.

Proponents point to economic growth and broader societal development, crediting export expansion and foreign direct investment with lifting hundreds of millions of people, particularly in Asia, out of extreme poverty between 1990 and 2010. Opponents claim that globalization harms social well-being through ethnocentrism, environmental consequences, and other drawbacks. Critics also cite widening income inequality within advanced economies, where offshoring and import competition have depressed wages for low-skilled workers, and job displacement in manufacturing. Others hold that aggregate evidence shows net welfare gains from cheaper goods and the spread of innovation.

Large cold desert and grassland region in southern [[Mongolia]] and [[China|North China]]. It is the sixth-largest desert in the world, covering about 1,295,000 sq km (500,000 sq mi). The name comes from Mongolian *gov'*, meaning dryland, and much of the Gobi is bare rock rather than sand. Winter temperatures can fall to −40 °C and summer temperatures can reach 45 °C, and an average of about 194 mm of rain falls annually. The region is the source of important fossil finds, including the first dinosaur eggs, 26 of which were uncovered in 1923. Its animals include black-tailed gazelles, marbled polecats, wild [[Bactrian camel|Bactrian camels]] and the Mongolian wild ass.

Suborder of percomorph fishes, many of them called gobies. Gobioidei is by far the largest and most diverse group within Gobiiformes and one of the most diverse groups of ray-finned fishes, with about 2,211 species in seven families. Most are small, marine, bottom-dwelling fishes with large heads and tapered bodies, and roughly 10% live in fresh water. Lacking a gas bladder, most gobioids spend most of their time on or near the bottom. Traditionally classed as a suborder of [[Perciformes]], the group was elevated to an order in the 5th edition of *Fishes of the World* and is again placed as a suborder within an expanded Gobiiformes. The earliest known fossil member is *Carlomonnius*, from the Early Eocene of Italy.

ENTRY:
Supreme deity in theistic belief systems, believed to be the ultimate cause of all things in existence. God is seen as the creator, sustainer, and ruler of the universe and is often thought of as incorporeal and independent of the material creation. Some traditions conceive of God as the greatest entity in existence and as omnibenevolent, while deism holds that God is not involved with humanity apart from creation.

Conceptions vary considerably. In [[Judaism]], [[Christianity]], and [[Islam]], God is a singular, personal entity with attributes such as omnipotence, omniscience, eternity, incorporeality, and immutability, and is the moral lawgiver. Polytheistic and henotheistic systems feature multiple divine beings, sometimes elevating one as supreme. In Hinduism, [[Brahman]] is an impersonal, infinite reality from which deities such as Vishnu or Shiva arise as manifestations. [[Buddhism]] rejects a creator God. Pantheism identifies God with the universe, and panentheism holds that God encompasses and transcends it.

The existence and nature of God are debated in theology, philosophy of religion, and popular culture. [[Atheism]] rejects belief in God or any other deity; [[Agnosticism|agnosticism]] holds that God's existence is unknown or unknowable. Arguments for God's existence include ontological arguments, formulated by Anselm and René Descartes, and cosmological, teleological, aesthetic, and moral arguments, each of which has drawn counterarguments. Many traditions attach spiritual significance to a relationship with God through worship and prayer.

The English word derives from Proto-Germanic ǥuđan (reconstructed). Its earliest written Germanic form appears in the 6th-century Codex Argenteus. Germanic words for God were originally neuter and became masculine during the Christianization of the Germanic peoples.

River of south-central India, second only to the [[Ganges]] in length. It rises near Nashik in Maharashtra, about 80 km from the [[Arabian Sea]], and flows 1,465 km east across the [[Deccan Plateau]] before entering the [[Bay of Bengal]] through a broad delta. Its 312,812 sq km basin covers about 10% of India's area and is the third largest in India, after those of the Ganga and Indus. In length, catchment area and discharge it is the largest river of peninsular India, and it has been called the *Dakshina Ganga* ("Southern Ganges"). Barrages and dams now hold back much of its flow. The delta, with 729 persons per sq km, is nearly twice the Indian average density and faces a substantial flood risk.
