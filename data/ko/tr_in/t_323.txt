English and British imperial unit of mass equal to 14 avoirdupois [[Pound (mass)|pounds]] (about 6.35 kg). Stones were formerly standardized for trade in England and other Germanic-speaking countries of northern Europe, with values ranging from about 5 to 40 local pounds. With metrication, they were superseded or adapted to the [[Kilogram|kilogram]] from the mid-19th century. The Weights and Measures Act 1835 permitted a stone of 14 pounds for trade. Today the stone remains in customary use in the [[United Kingdom]] and Ireland, chiefly for body weight.

Prehistoric [[Megalith|megalithic]] monument on Salisbury Plain in Wiltshire, England. Its outer ring of sarsen standing stones, each about 13 feet (4.0 m) high and weighing around 25 tons, is topped by horizontal lintels joined with mortise and tenon joints. Inside are a ring of smaller bluestones and free-standing trilithons. Built in several phases from about 3100 BC to about 1600 BC, the monument is aligned to the sunrise on the [[Summer solstice]] and the sunset on the [[Winter solstice]]. It stands in one of the densest concentrations of [[Neolithic]] and [[Bronze Age]] monuments in England. Deposits of human bone date from as early as 3000 BC, suggesting a burial function from its beginnings. Added to the list of [[UNESCO]] World Heritage Sites in 1986, it is one of the most famous landmarks in the United Kingdom.

ENTRY:
Large, long-legged, long-necked wading bird with a long, stout bill, in the family Ciconiidae. Storks have 20 living species in six genera, and many live in drier habitats than related [[Heron|herons]] and [[Ibis|ibises]]. Most eat frogs, fish, insects, earthworms and small birds or mammals, and they communicate at the nest by bill-clattering. Many species migrate, and their soaring flight uses thermal air currents. The marabou stork, with a wingspan of 3.2 m, joins the [[Andean condor]] in having the widest wingspan of any living land bird. Storks are prominent in folklore, especially in Western tradition as deliverers of newborn humans. All 20 species have been assessed by the IUCN, though several assessments rest on incorrect assumptions.

Disturbed state of the natural environment or of the atmosphere of an astronomical body. A storm may be marked by strong wind, tornadoes, hail, thunder and lightning, heavy precipitation, or wind carrying material through the air, among other forms of severe weather. Its forms include the [[Thunderstorm]], the [[Blizzard|blizzard]], the ice storm, the [[Tropical cyclone|tropical cyclone]], and the [[Dust storm|dust storm]].

Storms form when a center of low pressure develops within a surrounding system of high pressure. This combination of opposing forces can create winds and storm clouds such as cumulonimbus. Small, localized [[Low-pressure area|low-pressure areas]] formed by hot air rising off hot ground produce smaller disturbances such as dust devils and whirlwinds.

Types are defined by their conditions. A blizzard combines gale-force winds, snow accumulating at a rate of at least 5 cm (2 in) per hour, and very cold conditions. Ice storms, among the most dangerous winter storms, need only 8 mm (0.31 in) of ice accumulation to start downing power lines and tree limbs. Thunderstorms occur throughout the world, most often in tropical rainforest regions, and individual storm clouds can measure 2–10 km across. A [[Tornado]] is a violent whirlwind storm occurring on land.

Storms can harm lives and property through storm surge, flooding, [[Lightning|lightning]], wildfires, and wind shear. Systems with significant rainfall and duration help relieve drought, and heavy snowfall enables recreation such as skiing. Storms also occur on other bodies; the [[Great Red Spot]] of Jupiter and the dust storms of Mars are examples. The English word comes from Proto-Germanic *sturmaz*, meaning "noise, tumult."

ENTRY:
Water body connecting two seas or water basins, usually a narrow channel between two landmasses. Water can flow through it in either direction at about the same elevation on both sides, though topography generally constricts the flow. Some straits have a dominant directional current, and their sediments form deltas or fans at the exits. Straits are the converse of [[Isthmus|isthmuses]], which connect two large landmasses between oceans. Some straits are not navigable because they are too narrow or shallow, or because of reefs.

ENTRY:
One of the four known fundamental interactions, binding quarks into hadrons and nucleons into atomic nuclei. Also called the strong force or strong nuclear force, it is studied in nuclear and particle physics. It confines [[Quark|quarks]] into protons, neutrons, and other hadrons; between nucleons it is called the nuclear force, or residual strong force.

Most of the mass of a proton or neutron results from strong-interaction energy; the individual quarks provide only about 1% of a proton's mass. At 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer), the strong force is approximately 100 times as strong as [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]], 10⁶ times as strong as the [[Weak interaction|weak interaction]], and 10³⁸ times as strong as gravitation.

In 1964, [[Murray Gell-Mann]], and separately George Zweig, proposed that baryons and mesons are composed of elementary particles, which Gell-Mann called quarks. The strong attraction between nucleons was then understood as a side effect of a more fundamental force binding quarks together. The theory of [[Quantum chromodynamics]] (QCD), part of the [[Standard Model]], describes this force. Quarks carry a "color charge," unrelated to visible color. The force carrier is the [[Gluon|gluon]], a massless gauge boson that itself carries color charge, unlike the electromagnetic photon.

The force acts at two ranges. Below about 0.8 fm, gluons hold quarks together in hadrons; this is color confinement. Up to about 3 fm, mesons carry the force and bind nucleons into nuclei. Nuclear fusion accounts for most energy production in the Sun and other stars, and the nuclear force's energy is partly released in nuclear power and nuclear weapons.

Chemical element with symbol Sr and atomic number 38, an [[Alkaline earth metal|alkaline earth metal]]. Strontium resembles [[Calcium|calcium]] and [[Barium|barium]] in its properties and is highly reactive, forming a dark oxide layer in air. It occurs mainly in the [[Mineral|minerals]] celestine and strontianite, the latter named for Strontian, Scotland, where it was found in 1790. [[Humphry Davy]] first isolated the metal in 1808 by [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]]. In the 19th century it was mostly used to refine sugar from [[Sugar beet|sugar beets]]. ENTRY:
Statistical test of whether the difference between two groups is significant. A test is a Student's t-test when its test statistic follows a [[Student's t-distribution|t-distribution]] under the null hypothesis, which holds under certain conditions when a scaling term such as the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] is estimated from data rather than known. Its most common use is to test whether two population means differ. The one-sample version compares a sample mean with a hypothesized value, with n − 1 degrees of freedom. For large datasets it gives results very similar to a Z-test. The test was introduced in 1908 by William Sealy Gosset, who published under the pseudonym "Student" in the journal *Biometrika* while working at the Guinness brewery in [[Dublin]], Ireland. [[Ronald Fisher]]'s work made it widely known.

ENTRY:
Method of filmmaking in which a small number of large studios dominated film production and distribution. The term most often refers to Hollywood from 1927, when sound films were introduced, to 1948, when the system began to end. Studios produced films on their own lots, used long-term contracts for actors and filmmaking personnel, and controlled exhibition through vertical integration, including ownership of theaters. The original Big Five were [[Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer]], [[Paramount Pictures]], Warner Bros., 20th Century-Fox, and RKO Radio Pictures, while [[Columbia Pictures]] and [[Universal Pictures]] were organized similarly on a smaller scale. A 1948 Supreme Court antitrust ruling sought to separate production from distribution and exhibition. By 1954, with [[Television|television]] competing for audiences, the historic era of the studio system was over.
