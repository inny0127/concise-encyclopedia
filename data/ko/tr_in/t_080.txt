Novel by Russian author [[Fyodor Dostoevsky]], serialized in twelve monthly installments in 1866. ENTRY:
Unlawful act punishable by a state or other authority. The term has no simple, universally accepted definition in modern criminal law. The most popular view is that crime is a category created by law: something is a crime if the applicable law declares it so. One proposed definition is that a crime is an act harmful not only to an individual but also to a community, society, or the state, a "public wrong."

Prohibitions on acts such as [[Murder|murder]], rape, and theft exist worldwide, but what counts as an offense is defined by the [[Criminal law|criminal law]] of each jurisdiction. In some [[Common law|common law]] nations no comprehensive criminal code exists, and courts have developed offenses themselves. Usually a crime requires both the act (*actus reus*) and criminal intent (*mens rea*), though strict liability crimes require no proof of intent. Not every violation of law is a crime: breaches of private law, such as [[Tort|torts]] and breaches of contract, are enforced through civil procedure.

Historically, criminal codes often divided offenders by class or caste, and in some tribal societies an entire clan was liable for a crime, leading to feuds lasting generations. Sentences range from community sentences to imprisonment, [[Life imprisonment|life imprisonment]], or in some jurisdictions death. Criminalization raises [[Human rights|human rights]] questions, since it can infringe on autonomy and subject people to unjust punishment.

International criminal law addresses serious offenses such as [[Genocide|genocide]], crimes against humanity, and [[War crime|war crimes]], defined through treaties and international custom. Intentional homicide rates range from under 1 per 100,000 population in many Asian and European countries to over 40 in certain Latin American and African nations. Some criminologists emphasize individual choices weighed against risks and rewards; others stress social and environmental influences.

Peninsula in Eastern Europe on the northern coast of the [[Black Sea]]. Body of law that defines [[Crime|crimes]] and prescribes their punishment. Most criminal law is established by statute and varies by jurisdiction. Unlike civil law, which emphasizes dispute resolution and victim compensation, criminal law includes the punishment and rehabilitation of offenders. Penalties range from fines, probation and house arrest to imprisonment and, in some jurisdictions, [[Capital punishment]] for the most serious crimes. The Code of Ur-Nammu, written around 2100–2050 BC by the king of [[Ur]], is among the earliest written codes, and the [[Code of Hammurabi]] formed the core of Babylonian law. The state's role in dispensing justice through courts became clear in 18th-century Europe, as countries began maintaining police services.

Interdisciplinary field that studies [[Crime|crime]] and [[Deviance (sociology)|deviant behavior]]. It draws on [[Sociology|sociology]], political science, economics, psychology, philosophy, biology and law, and examines the nature of crime and criminals, the origins of [[Criminal law]], the causes of crime, social reactions to it, and the work of law enforcement agencies and penal institutions. Roman government crisis of 235–284, also called the Military Anarchy. Repeated foreign invasions, civil wars and economic disintegration nearly destroyed the [[Roman Empire]]. Emperor Severus Alexander was assassinated by his own troops in 235, and at least 26 claimants to the title of emperor followed. By 268 the empire had split into three competing states: the Gallic Empire, the Palmyrene Empire under [[Zenobia]], and the Italian-centered Roman Empire proper. End point of a phase equilibrium curve in [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]]. Group of mainly aquatic [[Arthropod|arthropods]], traditionally ranked as the subphylum Crustacea. The name derives from the Latin *crustacea*, "those with shells." Crustaceans include decapods (shrimps, prawns, [[Crab|crabs]], [[Lobster|lobsters]], and crayfish), [[Krill|krill]], isopods, [[Barnacle|barnacles]], copepods, amphipods, and mantis shrimp.

The 67,000 described species range from *Stygotantulus stocki* at 0.1 mm (0.004 in) to the [[Japanese spider crab]], with a leg span of up to 3.8 m (12.5 ft) and a mass of 20 kg (44 lb). Crustaceans have an exoskeleton, which they molt to grow. They are distinguished from insects, myriapods, and chelicerates by biramous (two-parted) limbs and by larval forms such as the nauplius stage. The body has a head, thorax, and abdomen, and head and thorax may fuse into a cephalothorax under a carapace. Because hexapods (insects and entognathans) are now accepted as cladistically crustaceans, the two groups are combined in the clade Pancrustacea, which makes the traditional Crustacea paraphyletic.

Most crustaceans are free-living aquatic animals, but some are terrestrial (woodlice), some parasitic (fish lice), and some sessile (adult barnacles). The fossil record reaches back to the [[Cambrian]]. Krill and copepods may be the animals with the greatest biomass on the planet, and krill form the bottom layer of the Antarctic food chain. More than 7.9 million tons of crustaceans per year are harvested or farmed for human consumption, mostly shrimp and prawns. Some, such as the Chinese mitten crab, are significant invasive species. Their study is carcinology.

Constellation of the southern sky, the smallest of the 88 modern constellations. It is centered on four bright stars forming a cross-shaped asterism known as the Southern Cross, and its name is Latin for cross. Portions of Earth's surface where water is in solid form, including [[Sea ice|sea ice]], [[Snow|snow]], [[Glacier|glaciers]], [[Ice sheet|ice sheets]], ice on lakes and rivers, and frozen ground such as [[Permafrost|permafrost]]. ENTRY:
Practice and study of techniques for secure communication in the presence of adversaries; also called cryptology. Modern cryptography lies at the intersection of [[Mathematics|mathematics]], [[Computer science|computer science]], information security, electrical engineering, and physics. Its core concepts are data confidentiality, data integrity, authentication, and non-repudiation. Applications include electronic commerce, chip-based payment cards, digital currencies, passwords, secure messaging, and military communications.

Before the modern age, cryptography was effectively synonymous with [[Encryption|encryption]]: converting readable plaintext into unintelligible ciphertext that can be read only by reversing the process. A cipher is a pair of algorithms for encryption and decryption, controlled by a secret key. Early techniques included substitution ciphers such as the shift cipher attributed to [[Julius Caesar]]. Rotor cipher machines were developed in World War I, and computers in World War II made methods increasingly complex.

Symmetric systems, the only kind known until the 1970s, use one secret key to encrypt and decrypt. Asymmetric systems use a public key to encrypt and a related private key to decrypt, so parties can communicate without a shared secret. [[Public-key cryptography|Public-key cryptography]] is credited to Whitfield Diffie and Martin Hellman. In practice, asymmetric systems exchange a key, and a faster symmetric system such as AES then carries the communication. Modern algorithms rest on computational hardness assumptions.

Many governments classify cryptography as a weapon and limit its use or export. Critics have accused agencies such as the [[National Security Agency]] of pressing for deliberate weaknesses in standards, as in the unsuccessful Clipper chip initiative of the 1990s; the debate weighs privacy against national security.

Solid material with highly ordered microscopic structure. Its atoms, molecules or ions form a crystal lattice that extends in all directions, and macroscopic crystals are usually recognizable by flat faces at characteristic angles. The scientific study of crystals is [[Crystallography|crystallography]]. The word derives from the [[Ancient Greek|Ancient Greek]] *krustallos*, meaning both *ice* and *rock crystal*. Examples include [[Snow|snowflakes]], [[Diamond|diamonds]] and [[Salt|table salt]]. Most inorganic solids are polycrystals, made of many microscopic crystals fused together, including most metals, rocks, ceramics and ice. Solids without periodic order, such as [[Glass|glass]], wax and many plastics, are [[Amorphous solid|amorphous]]. Despite its name, lead crystal is a type of glass rather than a crystal.

Process producing a [[Crystal|crystal]], a solid with a uniform pattern of [[Atom|atoms]] or [[Molecule|molecules]]. Crystallization can occur by precipitation from [[Solution (chemistry)|solution]], freezing of a [[Liquid|liquid]] or deposition from a gas, and its results depend on temperature, air pressure, cooling rate and solute concentration. It has two stages: nucleation, in which clusters of molecules reach a critical size and become stable nuclei, and crystal growth, in which the nuclei enlarge. Many compounds have more than one crystal structure, a property called polymorphism; [[Titanium dioxide]] has the phases anatase and rutile. In [[Chemical engineering|chemical engineering]], crystallization separates a pure solid from a solution.
