ENTRY:
Large brown seaweeds in the order Laminariales. Kelp comprises about 30 genera and, despite using photosynthesis in chloroplasts, is not a plant but a stramenopile. It grows in dense, forest-like stands in shallow temperate and Arctic oceans, where the water is nutrient-rich and between 6 and 14 °C (43 and 57 °F). Macrocystis and Nereocystis can grow as fast as half a meter a day, reaching 30 to 80 meters. Kelp blades are held up by gas-filled bladders in some species, and the stipes are anchored to the seabed by a holdfast. Sea urchins can reduce kelp beds to barrens. Through the 19th century the word kelp referred to seaweeds burned to obtain soda ash, primarily [[Sodium carbonate]].

ENTRY:
Base unit of temperature in the [[International System of Units]] (SI), symbol K. The kelvin scale is an absolute temperature scale that starts at [[Absolute zero]], taken to be 0 K. A rise of one kelvin equals a rise of one degree [[Celsius]], and a Celsius temperature is converted to kelvins by adding 273.15. The scale was first proposed in 1848 by William Thomson, later [[Lord Kelvin]], whose paper gave a value of −273 °C for absolute zero. The kelvin was formally added to the SI in 1954, when 273.16 K was defined as the triple point of water. The 2019 revision of the SI instead fixes the Boltzmann constant at exactly 1.380649×10⁻²³ joules per kelvin.

Its landscape ranges from snow-capped peaks on [[Mount Kenya]], which at 5,199 m is Africa's second-highest, to arid and semi-arid regions and deserts such as the Chalbi.

Kenya belongs to the UN, the East African Community, and the [[International Monetary Fund]], among other bodies, and hosts the headquarters of the United Nations Environment Programme.
