ENTRY:
Star with a substantially larger radius and luminosity than a [[Main sequence|main-sequence]] star of the same surface temperature. Giant stars lie above the main sequence on the [[Hertzsprung–Russell diagram]], with radii up to a few hundred times that of the [[Sun]]. Ejnar Hertzsprung coined the terms giant and dwarf in 1905 or 1906 for stars of very different luminosity but similar temperature. A star becomes a giant after the hydrogen in its core is depleted, when hydrogen fusion continues in a surrounding shell and the outer layers expand and cool. Stars more luminous than giants are called [[Supergiant|supergiants]] and hypergiants. [[Arcturus]], an orange giant, has about 25 times the Sun's radius.

ENTRY:
Parasitic disease caused by the protist *Giardia duodenalis*, especially common in children and travelers. Infection causes steatorrhea, a [[Diarrhea|diarrhea]] of fatty, sticky stools, with abdominal pain, weight loss and weakness. Symptoms usually begin one to three weeks after exposure, and some people have mild or no symptoms. The disease spreads by the fecal-oral route, when cysts in contaminated food or water are swallowed. Diagnosis is by microscopic identification of cysts in feces. Treatment typically uses tinidazole or metronidazole. Infection may cause temporary [[Lactose|lactose]] intolerance. In 2013 about 280 million people worldwide had symptomatic cases, and the [[World Health Organization]] classifies it as a neglected disease.

Small, tailless [[Ape|ape]] of the family Hylobatidae, native to tropical and subtropical forests from eastern [[Bangladesh]] to [[Indonesia]]. ENTRY:
Thermodynamic potential, symbol *G*, measuring the maximum work, other than pressure–volume work, that a closed system can perform at constant temperature and pressure. It is defined as G = H − TS, where H is [[Enthalpy]] and S is [[Entropy]]. This maximum is attained only in a completely reversible process. A reduction in G is necessary for a reaction to be spontaneous under these conditions, and G is minimized when a system reaches [[Chemical equilibrium|chemical equilibrium]]. The concept, originally called "available energy," was developed in the 1870s by [[Josiah Willard Gibbs]].

Landform created by the action of [[Glacier|glaciers]]. Most of today's glacial landforms were created by the movement of large [[Ice sheet|ice sheets]] during the Quaternary glaciations, and some areas, such as Fennoscandia and the southern [[Andes]], have extensive examples. Erosional landforms include [[Cirque|cirques]], arêtes, glacial horns and U-shaped valleys, which become [[Fjord|fjords]] when filled with ocean water. Striations, grooves cut into rock by sediment carried beneath the ice, show the direction in which the glacier was moving. Depositional landforms, left when retreating glaciers drop their glacial drift, include moraines, eskers and kames, and many are reshaped by meltwater into fluvioglacial landforms. Rare, very old fossil glacial landforms occur in the [[Sahara]].

ENTRY:
Persistent body of natural ice, a form of rock, constantly moving under its own weight. A glacier forms where the accumulation of snow exceeds its ablation over many years, often centuries. It flows and deforms under the stresses of gravity and, as it moves, abrades the underlying rock to create landforms such as [[Cirque|cirques]], [[Moraine|moraines]] and [[Fjord|fjords]]. Distinct from the much thinner sea ice and lake ice, a glacier originates on land. About 99% of glacial ice lies in polar ice sheets, but glaciers occur in mountain ranges on every continent other than the Australian mainland. Glaciers cover about 10% of Earth's land surface, and [[Antarctica]]'s continental glaciers cover nearly 13 million km² (5 million sq mi). Glacial ice holds, together with ice sheets, about 69% of the world's freshwater, and glacial mass changes are considered among the most sensitive indicators of [[Climate change|climate change]].

ENTRY:
Armed combatant in violent entertainments in the [[Roman Republic]] and [[Roman Empire]], fighting other gladiators, wild animals and condemned criminals. Most gladiators were slaves, schooled under harsh conditions, socially marginalized and segregated even in death, although some were volunteers who risked their legal and social standing. Fighting or dying well could bring them admiration and popular acclaim. Evidence of gladiatorial combat appears in funeral rites during the [[Punic Wars]] of the 3rd century BC. The games lasted nearly a thousand years, peaking between the 1st century BC and the 2nd century AD.
