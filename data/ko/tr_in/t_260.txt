Ceremonial capital of the [[Achaemenid Empire|Achaemenid Empire]], in the plains of Marvdasht, Fars province, [[Iran|Iran]]. Encircled by the southern [[Zagros Mountains|Zagros Mountains]], it is a [[UNESCO|UNESCO]] World Heritage Site. Its earliest remains date to 515 BC. The complex, raised on a walled platform, included five halls and grand entrances, and was occupied seasonally rather than as a city. Its exact function remains unclear. In 330 BC the army of [[Alexander the Great|Alexander the Great]] took the complex, and its wooden parts were destroyed by fire. Exploration from the early 17th century led to the rediscovery of [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing.

Set of names by which an individual person or animal is known.

Also called a full name or prosoponym (from [[Ancient Greek]] *prósōpon*, "person," and *onoma*, "name"), it is in many cultures synonymous with the birth name or legal name.

In Western culture, nearly everyone has at least one given name (first name, forename, or Christian name) and a [[Surname|surname]] (last name or family name), which generally indicates membership in a family, tribe, or clan. In Arabic culture and in parts of Africa and Asia, a person's name is joined to a chain of names that begins with the father's name and usually ends with a family or tribe name. The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Rights of the Child declares that a child has the right to a name from birth.

ENTRY:
Behavioral, cognitive, and emotional patterns that make up an individual's unique, time-dependent adjustment to life.

Personality is relatively stable but can change over time through experience and developmental processes. There is no consensus on its definition; most theories focus on traits, [[Motivation|motivation]], skills, and [[Identity (social science)|identity]]. [[Personality psychology]] seeks to explain the characteristics that underlie differences in behavior, and personality is related to life outcomes such as work and relationship success, mental health, well-being, and longevity. Approaches to its study can be organized across dispositional, biological, intrapsychic (psychodynamic), cognitive-experiential, social and cultural, and adjustment domains. They reflect the influence of early theorists including [[Sigmund Freud]], Alfred Adler, [[Gordon Allport]], Hans Eysenck, [[Abraham Maslow]], and [[Carl Rogers]]. Psychodynamic theory stresses unconscious conflicts and early childhood experiences, humanistic theory emphasizes personal growth and self-actualization, and trait theory treats stable dispositional characteristics as the core of personality.

Personality is measured with objective tests and projective measures, among them the Big Five Inventory, the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI-2), and the Rorschach Inkblot test. The approach with the most support in the field is the Big Five: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism (or emotional stability). These components are generally stable over time, and about half of the variance appears attributable to a person's genetics rather than environment. Personality changes much more quickly during childhood, so personality constructs in children are called temperament, regarded as the precursor to personality.

The [[World Health Organization|World Health Organization]] ICD-11 classifies personality disorders dimensionally, by severity and traits, while the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-5-TR) uses categories. Categorical models have been criticized for weak evidence, heavy comorbidity and stigma. Among psychiatric patients, prevalence is estimated between 40 and 60%. Treatment is primarily [[Psychotherapy|psychotherapeutic]], including dialectical behavior therapy for [[Borderline personality disorder|borderline personality disorder]]. Some experts contest the validity of diagnoses, arguing that they reflect social and cultural judgments.
