ENTRY:
Loop of electric current induced within conductors by a changing magnetic field, also called Foucault's current. Eddy currents flow in closed loops perpendicular to the magnetic field and arise from time-varying fields, as in an AC electromagnet or [[Transformer|transformer]], or from relative motion between a magnet and a conductor. By Lenz's law, an eddy current creates a field that opposes the change that produced it, so a nearby conductive surface drags on a moving magnet, a principle used in eddy current brakes. Their resistance also dissipates energy as heat, causing losses in transformers, [[Electric motor|motors]] and generators that laminated or ferrite cores reduce. Eddy currents also heat metal in induction furnaces and reveal cracks in metal parts through eddy-current testing. [[Léon Foucault]] is credited with discovering them in 1855.

Transmission of knowledge and skills and the development of character traits. The term derives from the Latin *educare* ("to bring up") and *educere* ("to bring forth"), and it can refer to a process (schooling, teaching, and learning), to the mental states and qualities of educated people, or to the academic field that studies the methods, processes, and social institutions involved. Its exact definition is disputed, as are its aims.

Education is classified in several ways. Formal education happens in a complex institutional framework, such as public schools, with classes based on a student's age and progress. Non-formal education is also structured but occurs outside the formal schooling system, and informal education is unstructured learning through daily experiences. Formal and non-formal education are divided into levels: [[Early childhood education|early childhood]], [[Primary education|primary]], [[Secondary education|secondary]], and [[Tertiary education|tertiary]] education. Further classifications are based on teaching method (teacher-centered or student-centered), subject, medium, and funding.

Theorists have defined education in different ways. R. S. Peters outlined three essential features: that knowledge and understanding are imparted, that the process is beneficial, and that it is done in a morally appropriate manner. Critics note that such precise definitions exclude less common types of education, and alternatives rely on family resemblance among forms of education instead. Evaluative or thick conceptions hold that education by nature leads to some improvement, whereas thin conceptions are value-neutral. Many scholars stress [[Critical thinking|critical thinking]] as what distinguishes education from indoctrination, which instills beliefs regardless of whether they are rational. Others argue that the two cannot always be clearly separated, since young children must sometimes learn things, such as safety rules and hygiene practices, without understanding the reasons.

Education socializes children into society by teaching cultural values and norms and equips them with skills to become productive members of society. It stimulates [[Economic growth|economic growth]] and raises awareness of local and global problems; studies estimate that each additional year of schooling raises lifetime earnings by about 10%. Governments set education policy, determining when classes happen, what is taught, and who can or must attend, and international organizations such as [[UNESCO]] have promoted primary education for all children. Success depends on psychological factors (motivation, intelligence, and personality), social factors (socioeconomic status, ethnicity, and gender, which are often linked to discrimination), access to educational technology, teacher quality, and parent involvement. Some analyses find no systematic link between increased spending and student performance, attributing gaps to instructional quality, family background, and institutional incentives.

In prehistory, education was informal, taking place through oral communication and imitation. With the rise of ancient civilizations and the invention of writing, education shifted toward formal instruction, initially available mainly to elites and religious groups. The [[Printing press|printing press]] of the 15th century made books more widely available and increased literacy. From the 18th and 19th centuries public education became more important, leading to the worldwide effort to make primary education free and compulsory up to a certain age. Over 90% of primary-school-age children worldwide now attend primary school.

King of England (r. 1272–1307), also known as Edward Longshanks.
