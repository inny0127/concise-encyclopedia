ENTRY:
Solid that lacks the long-range order characteristic of a [[Crystal|crystal]]. Also called a non-crystalline solid, it includes [[Glass|glasses]], metallic glasses, and certain [[Plastic|plastics]] and [[Polymer|polymers]]. The terms 'glass' and 'glassy solid' are sometimes used as synonyms, but refer specifically to amorphous materials that undergo a glass transition. Amorphous materials show short-range order over typically 1–2 atomic spacings, but no repeating unit cell, so statistical measures such as the radial distribution function describe their structure. The freezing of a liquid into an amorphous solid is one of the important unsolved problems of physics. Below about 1–10 K, amorphous solids share anomalous properties, including nearly linear specific heat and nearly quadratic thermal conductivity, that current theories explain only partly.

ENTRY:
Class of ectothermic, anamniotic, four-limbed vertebrates.

In its broadest sense the group is paraphyletic, encompassing all [[Tetrapod|tetrapods]] except the amniotes (modern reptiles, birds, and mammals). All living amphibians belong to the subclass Lissamphibia, with three orders: Anura ([[Frog|frogs]] and toads), Urodela ([[Salamander|salamanders]]), and Gymnophiona ([[Caecilian|caecilians]]). About 8,000 species are known, nearly 90% of them frogs.

The typical life cycle begins with aquatic, gilled larvae (tadpoles) that undergo [[Metamorphosis|metamorphosis]], driven largely by thyroid hormones, into air-breathing, lunged adults. The skin serves as a secondary respiratory surface, and some small terrestrial salamanders and frogs lack lungs entirely. Though superficially similar to [[Lizard|lizards]], amphibians require access to water to breed, and adults generally cannot breathe underwater. Their permeable skins and complex reproductive needs make them ecological indicators of habitat conditions. The smallest amphibian, and smallest vertebrate, is the New Guinea frog *Paedophryne amauensis*, at 7.7 mm. The largest living amphibian is the South China giant salamander (*Andrias sligoi*), at 1.8 m; the prehistoric temnospondyl *Mastodonsaurus* could reach up to 6 m.

The earliest amphibians evolved in the [[Devonian]] from lobe-finned fishes that had developed primitive lungs. They diversified and became ecologically dominant in the Carboniferous and Permian periods, then were displaced on land by early [[Reptile|reptiles]] and basal synapsids. Modern lissamphibians first appeared in the Early Triassic, around 250 million years ago; their origin is contentious, the most popular hypothesis tracing them to temnospondyls and another to lepospondyls.

Amphibian populations have declined dramatically in recent decades. Of assessed species, 41% are threatened with extinction, owing to habitat destruction, pathogens such as *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis*, and climate-driven disruption. The study of amphibians is batrachology; that of reptiles and amphibians together is herpetology.

Branch of chemistry concerned with identifying the composition of materials and quantifying the components of mixtures.

Analytical chemists develop and apply methods to identify unknown compounds and to express a compound's presence as [[Amount of substance|amount of substance]], concentration, percentage by mass, number of moles, or, for gases, partial pressure. Qualitative analysis establishes which substances are present; quantitative analysis determines how much.

Classical techniques, such as titration and gravimetric analysis, remain the basis of undergraduate laboratory teaching, and qualitative tests include the acid test for gold. Modern work is dominated by instrumental methods: [[Spectroscopy|spectroscopy]], [[Chromatography|chromatography]], [[Mass spectrometry|mass spectrometry]], and electrochemical methods.

The field has been important since the early days of chemistry. [[Justus von Liebig]] developed systematic elemental analysis. The first instrumental analysis was flame emissive spectrometry, developed by Robert Bunsen and [[Gustav Kirchhoff]], who discovered rubidium and caesium in 1860. Most major developments came after 1900, as basic spectroscopic and spectrometric techniques were discovered in the early 20th century and refined in the late 20th century. From the 1970s, techniques were combined as hybrid methods to characterize samples completely, and the field grew to include biological questions (bioanalytical chemistry). Applications widened from academic questions to forensic, environmental, industrial, and medical ones, including pharmaceutical quality assurance and drug discovery.

In the 21st century the field has been shaped by digitalization. Large datasets from instruments such as Orbitrap mass spectrometers have made data analysis, including [[Machine learning|machine learning]], an essential skill. Real-time, point-of-care diagnostic sensors, automation, and miniaturization are strong trends, and Green Analytical Chemistry aims to minimize the environmental impact of analyses.

Political philosophy and movement seeking to abolish institutions that perpetuate authority, coercion, or hierarchy, chiefly the state and capitalism.

Anarchists advocate replacing hierarchical structures with stateless societies of horizontal networks of voluntary associations, based on free association and reciprocity. Anarchism is described as part of the libertarian wing of the socialist movement, known broadly as libertarian socialism.

The word comes from the Ancient Greek *anarkhia*, "without a ruler," and appears in English from 1642. [[Pierre-Joseph Proudhon]] was the first political philosopher to call himself an anarchist and give the word a positive meaning. Since the 1890s, beginning in France, [[Libertarianism|libertarianism]] has often been a synonym; in the United States some usages refer only to individualistic free-market philosophy. Many scholars reject anarcho-capitalism as a misunderstanding of anarchist principles.

Precursors are traced to Taoist philosophers Zhuang Zhou and [[Laozi]] and to the Stoics, to the Greek tragedians Aeschylus and Sophocles, and to [[Socrates]], who insisted on freedom of conscience. It flourished worldwide from the late 19th century into the early 20th, with a significant role in workers' struggles. Anarchists took part in several revolutions, most notably the [[Paris Commune]], the [[Russian Civil War]], and the [[Spanish Civil War]], whose conclusion marked the end of the classical era of anarchism. It has been resurgent since the late 20th century.

Approaches are often categorized as evolutionary, favoring gradual and often nonviolent change, or revolutionary, seeking to destroy oppressive states and institutions.

Branch of morphology concerned with the internal and external structure of organisms and their parts.

The word derives from the Greek *anatomē*, "dissection." Anatomy is a natural science with beginnings in prehistoric times, and it is distinct from physiology and biochemistry, which deal with function and chemical processes. An anatomist studies the shape, size, position, structure, blood supply, and innervation of an organ such as the [[Liver|liver]]; a physiologist studies, for example, the production of bile. The two are often studied together, and human anatomy is a basic science of medicine.

The discipline divides into macroscopic (gross) anatomy, the study of structures visible to the naked eye, which includes surface anatomy, and microscopic anatomy, which includes histology (the study of tissues), cytology (cells), and embryology. Regional anatomy treats all structures in one body region, such as the abdomen; systemic anatomy treats the structures of a body system, such as the digestive system. Methods include [[Dissection|dissection]], [[Endoscopy|endoscopy]], and angiography, which visualizes blood vessels. The term usually means human anatomy but also covers other animals, sometimes called zootomy; plant structures are studied in [[Plant anatomy]].

Study of anatomy dates to the Hippocratic school in the 5th century BC, which documented body structures through observation and animal dissections. In the Renaissance, [[Andreas Vesalius]] published *De Humani Corporis Fabrica* in 1543, correcting longstanding errors from [[Galen]] with illustrated dissections based on direct human observation. Methods advanced from dissection of animal carcasses and cadavers to 20th-century [[Medical imaging|medical imaging]], including X-ray, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging.
