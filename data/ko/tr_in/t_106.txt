ENTRY:
Latin-script alphabet of 26 letters, each with uppercase and lowercase forms, used to write [[Modern English|Modern English]]. It has five vowel letters, 19 consonant letters, and two letters, W and Y, that may function as either. Old English writing in the 5th century used the runic futhorc, and the Latin alphabet was adopted from the 7th century onward. By the 16th century the present set of 26 letters had largely stabilized. Diacritics are generally not used in native English words.

West Germanic language of the Indo-European family that emerged in early medieval England and has become a global [[Lingua franca|lingua franca]]. It is named after the Angles, one of the [[Germanic peoples]] who migrated to Britain after the end of Roman rule.

English is the most spoken language in the world, primarily because of the influence of the British Empire and the rise of the United States, and the most widely learned second language. It is only the third-most spoken native language, after Mandarin Chinese and Spanish. It is an official language of 57 sovereign states and 30 dependent territories, the most geographically widespread of any language, and a co-official language of the United Nations and the European Union. It is not explicitly defined by law in the United Kingdom, and in the United States it is the official language of the federal government but not of the country. Ethnologue estimated more than 1.4 billion speakers as of 2021.

[[Old English]], spoken from c. 450 to c. 1150, developed from West Germanic dialects brought to Britain from the 5th century by the [[Anglo-Saxons]]. It had four dialects: Mercian, Northumbrian, Kentish, and West Saxon. Through the influence of Wessex and King Alfred's educational reforms in the 9th century, West Saxon became the standard written variety; *Beowulf* is written in it. Late Old English borrowed grammar and core vocabulary from Old Norse.

[[Middle English]] emerged under Norman control and borrowed extensively from French, the source of approximately 28 percent of Modern English words, and from Latin, the source of another 28 percent. Through changes including the Great Vowel Shift it became [[Modern English]], which developed mainly from Mercian. Its grammar, phonology, and basic everyday vocabulary remain Germanic, and it is not mutually intelligible with any continental Germanic language.

Practice of incising a design into a hard, usually flat surface with a burin, a hardened steel tool. The result may be a decorated object, such as silver or gold, or an intaglio printing plate, usually of copper, for printing images on paper. Engraving is one of the oldest and most important techniques in [[Printmaking|printmaking]]. Photographic processes have replaced it in commercial applications, and etching has largely replaced it in printmaking. Form of activity that holds the attention and interest of an audience or gives pleasure and delight. It may be an idea or a task, but is more commonly one of the activities or events developed over thousands of years specifically to engage an [[Audience|audience]].

Storytelling, [[Music|music]], [[Drama|drama]], [[Dance|dance]], and other kinds of performance exist in all cultures, were supported in royal courts, and developed into sophisticated forms that eventually became available to the general public. The modern entertainment industry records and sells entertainment products. Entertainment can be adapted to any scale, from an individual choosing recorded material, to a banquet for two, to performances for thousands or global audiences. It is strongly associated with amusement, but many entertainments serve serious purposes, as in ceremony, religious festival, or [[Satire|satire]], and may offer insight or intellectual growth. The audience is a key element, transforming private recreation into entertainment. It may be passive, as in watching a play, opera, television show, or [[Film|film]], or active, as in games, where participant and audience roles can interchange. Entertainment may be scripted, as in theater and concerts, or spontaneous, as in children's games.

Most forms have persisted for centuries, evolving with cultural, technological, and fashion changes. Films and [[Video game|video games]] use newer media but continue to tell stories, present drama, and play music. Some entertainment, such as public executions, is now illegal in most countries. Fencing and archery, once used in hunting or war, have become spectator sports, and cooking has become a staged and broadcast performance among professionals. What entertains one group may be considered work or cruelty by another.

The English verb derives from Latin and French roots; the *Oxford English Dictionary* cites a 1490 usage by William Caxton and dates the sense "gratify, amuse" to the 1620s.

Entertainment is distinguished from education and marketing, though both borrow its appeal, producing hybrids called "edutainment" and "infotainment". Psychologists describe the function of media entertainment as "the attainment of gratification". Some people are entertained by others' misfortune, a response called schadenfreude. Entertainment can also take up philosophical questions. Shakespeare's *Hamlet* and the film *The Matrix* do so, as does *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, which began as a radio comedy and became a novel, film, television series, stage show, comic, audiobook, LP record, adventure game, and online game.

Storytelling is the oldest form and has been used to pass on cultural values, traditions, and history. Some entertainments are thought to have evolved from necessary activities such as running and jumping into competition, and then into entertainment. One account holds that pole vaulting began in the Netherlands as a way of crossing wide canals. One industry estimate puts global entertainment and media revenues at approximately $2.9 trillion in 2024.

A change in enthalpy is the preferred expression for measurements at constant pressure, which Earth's atmosphere conveniently provides. Study of inscriptions as writing, including the identification of graphemes, the clarification of their meanings, and the classification of their uses by date and cultural context. Group of neurological disorders characterized by recurrent seizures, which are abnormal, excessive and synchronized electrical discharges in the brain's [[Neuron|neurons]]. ENTRY:
Branch of [[Philosophy|philosophy]] that examines the nature, origin, and limits of knowledge. Also called the theory of knowledge, it explores propositional knowledge about facts, practical knowledge in the form of skills, and knowledge by acquaintance. The word, from Greek *episteme* (knowledge) and *logos* (study), was coined only in the 19th century.

Epistemologists study [[Belief|belief]], [[Truth|truth]], and justification, and investigate sources of justification such as perception, introspection, memory, reason, and testimony. Epistemology is normative, concerned with standards for evaluating beliefs, whereas psychology describes the beliefs people actually hold. Skepticism questions the human ability to attain knowledge, and fallibilism holds that knowledge is never certain. [[Empiricism|Empiricists]] hold that all knowledge comes from sense experience, while [[Rationalism|rationalists]] believe some knowledge does not depend on it. Coherentists argue that a belief is justified if consistent with other beliefs; foundationalists maintain that basic beliefs are justified independently of other beliefs. Internalists and externalists debate whether justification depends solely on mental states.

Separate branches address scientific, mathematical, moral, and religious knowledge; naturalized, formal, social, and historical epistemology take distinct approaches.

Reflection on knowledge appears in ancient Greek, Indian, and Chinese philosophy, and [[Plato]] explored it in the *Theaetetus*. Reason and faith were central in the medieval period, and the modern era was marked by empiricism and rationalism. A long-standing analysis defined knowledge as justified true belief. Edmund Gettier's 1963 paper presented counterexamples, now called Gettier cases, where a justified true belief fails to be knowledge because of luck.
