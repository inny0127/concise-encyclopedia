Waterfowl of several species in the family *Anatidae*, chiefly the [[Genus|genera]] *Anser* (gray geese and white geese) and *Branta* (black geese). Genus of large, primarily herbivorous great apes of equatorial Africa. The genus has two species, the western and eastern gorillas, with four or five subspecies. Gorillas are the largest living [[Primate|primates]], reaching heights of up to 1.8 m and weights of up to 227 kg. Their [[DNA]] is highly similar to human DNA, from 96 to 99%, and after the [[Bonobo|bonobos]] and [[Chimpanzee|chimpanzees]] they are the next closest living relatives to humans. Byzantine–Gothic war in Italy and nearby islands, 535–554. It was the last of the Gothic wars against the [[Byzantine Empire]], and it aimed to recover former Western Roman provinces lost to invading tribes during the [[Migration Period]], under [[Justinian I]]. Architectural style prevalent in Europe from the late 12th to the 16th century. The term "Gothic" was first applied contemptuously during the later Renaissance, and [[Giorgio Vasari]] attributed its features to the [[Goths]].

Medieval style of art, emerging in northern France in the early 12th century. Literary genre of fear and haunting, often called Gothic horror. Its name comes from the Renaissance-era pejorative use of gothic, meaning medieval and barbaric. System or group of people that leads a [[State (polity)|state]]. A government is responsible for directing the only entity whose use of force is socially acceptable, and it is the major organizing power in modern societies. Cambridge Dictionary defines it as an organization that officially manages and controls a country or region, creating laws, collecting taxes, and providing public services. The word is often used of the approximately 200 independent national governments on Earth and of their subsidiary state, provincial, and local governments. It derives from the Greek verb *kubernáō*, meaning to steer with a rudder, a metaphor attested in classical antiquity, including Plato's Ship of State.

Governments have been dominated by one person ([[Autocracy|autocracy]]), a small ruling class ([[Oligarchy|oligarchy]]), or the masses ([[Democracy|democracy]]). The main types of modern political system are democracies, totalitarian regimes, and authoritarian regimes, with hybrid regimes and exceptions such as monarchies, which preserve pre-modern forms of traditional legitimacy. ENTRY:
System of rules governing how a natural language is structured and used. In [[Linguistics|linguistics]], the term also covers the study of such rules, which includes [[Phonology|phonology]], [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphology]], and [[Syntax|syntax]], together with phonetics, [[Semantics|semantics]], and pragmatics. Speakers internalize most rules by observing others, largely in early childhood. A descriptive grammar records constructions in detail, in contrast to prescription, which codifies some constructions while marginalizing others. Outside linguistics, the word may also include spelling and punctuation, or mean prescriptive norms only.

The word derives from the Greek *grammatikḕ téchnē*, "art of letters." The first systematic grammar of [[Sanskrit]] originated in Iron Age India with Yaska (6th century BC), [[Pāṇini]] (6th–5th century BC), and later commentators including Patanjali (2nd century BC). The oldest known grammar handbook is the *Art of Grammar* by Dionysius Thrax (c. 170 – c. 90 BC), which remained the primary Greek grammar textbook until the twelfth century AD and whose format underlies grammar guides in many languages. Latin grammar followed Greek models from the 1st century BC. Irish grammar originated in the 7th century, and Arabic grammar emerged with Abu al-Aswad al-Du'ali in the same century.

In the Middle Ages grammar belonged to the trivium of the seven liberal arts. Antonio de Nebrija published the first Spanish grammar in 1492, and the first grammar of German appeared in 1578. From the latter part of the 18th century grammar became a subfield of modern linguistics. Franz Bopp's *Comparative Grammar* of 1833 was the starting point of modern [[Comparative linguistics|comparative linguistics]]. Theoretical approaches include generative grammar, which posits universal principles underlying diverse grammars, and functional approaches, which emphasize usage-based patterns; controversy persists over the relative contributions of innate capacities and emergent learning.
