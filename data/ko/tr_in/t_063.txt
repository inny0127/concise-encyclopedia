[[Dairy]] food made by curdling [[Milk|milk]], usually from cows, goats, or sheep and sometimes [[Water buffalo|water buffalo]]. Milk is usually acidified, and either the enzymes of rennet or bacterial enzymes with similar activity cause the protein casein to coagulate. The solid curds, consisting of proteins and milkfat, are separated from the liquid whey and pressed into finished cheese. Most cheeses are acidified by bacteria that turn milk sugars into [[Lactic acid|lactic acid]]; a few are curdled with acids such as vinegar or lemon juice. Some have aromatic molds on the rind or throughout.

Over a thousand types exist. Style, texture, and flavor depend on the origin of the milk (including the animal's diet), [[Pasteurization|pasteurization]], butterfat content, bacteria and mold, processing, and aging time. Cheese is valued for its portability, long shelf life, and high content of fat, protein, calcium, and phosphorus; hard cheeses such as Cheddar and Parmesan keep longer than soft ones such as Brie. High saturated fat and sodium content are associated with cardiovascular disease risk if intake is excessive, although some studies link moderate intake to lower stroke risk.

The word derives from [[Latin]] *caseus*, which also gave casein. Cheesemaking predates recorded history, and no conclusive evidence shows whether it began in Europe, Central Asia, or the Middle East. The earliest archaeological evidence, from 5500 BC, is strainers coated with milk-fat molecules found in Kuyavia, Poland. In Sumer, cheese figured in the sacred marriage rite of Dumuzi and [[Inanna]], in which the king of Uruk supplied the goddess with cheese and cream. The earliest preserved cheese, found on mummies at Xiaohe Cemetery in [[Xinjiang]], China, dates to as early as 1615 BC.

Association of [[Atom|atoms]] or [[Ion|ions]] to form [[Molecule|molecules]], crystals, and other structures. A bond may result from the electrostatic force between oppositely charged ions, as in ionic bonds; from the sharing of [[Electron|electrons]], as in [[Covalent bond|covalent bonds]]; or from some combination of these effects. Strong, or primary, bonds include covalent, ionic, and [[Metallic bonding|metallic bonds]]; weak, or secondary, bonds include dipole–dipole interactions, the London dispersion force, and [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonds]].

The attraction can be seen as the result of the behavior of the outermost, or valence, electrons of atoms. These behaviors merge into each other, so no clear line separates the types. In a covalent bond, one or more electrons, often a pair, are drawn into the space between two nuclei. Such bonds have a direction in space and often form small molecules, long chains as in polymers such as nylon, or networks as in diamond and quartz. In an ionic bond, an electron is transferred from one atom to another, leaving one positively and the other negatively charged; the bond results from electrostatic attraction between the ions. Ionic bonds are strong but brittle, generally have no particular orientation in space, and give rise to crystals such as table salt. In metallic bonding, each metal atom donates one or more electrons to a "sea" of electrons shared among many atoms.

All bonds can be described by quantum theory, but simplified rules such as the octet rule and VSEPR theory allow chemists to predict the strength, directionality, and polarity of bonds. More sophisticated treatments include [[Valence bond theory|valence bond theory]] and molecular orbital theory. Bonds determine the structure and properties of matter.

Substance composed of many identical molecules containing atoms from more than one [[Chemical element|chemical element]] held together by [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]]. A molecule consisting of atoms of only one element is therefore not a compound. A compound can be transformed into a different substance by a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]], in which bonds between atoms may be broken, formed, or both.

Four major types are distinguished by how the atoms are bonded: molecular compounds, held together by covalent bonds; ionic compounds, held together by ionic bonds; intermetallic compounds, held together by metallic bonds; and coordination complexes, held together by coordinate covalent bonds. Non-stoichiometric compounds, whose proportions may be reproducible but are not integral, as in palladium hydride, PdHₓ (0.02 < x < 0.58), form a disputed marginal case.

A [[Chemical formula|chemical formula]] specifies the number of atoms of each element in a molecule of a compound, using standard chemical symbols with numerical subscripts. Water, for example, consists of two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom, with the formula H₂O. Many compounds have a unique CAS number assigned by the Chemical Abstracts Service. Globally, more than 350,000 chemical compounds (including mixtures of chemicals) have been registered for production and use.

The term "compound", with a meaning similar to the modern one, has been used at least since 1661, when [[Robert Boyle|Robert Boyle]]'s *The Sceptical Chymist* was published. In his *Logick* (1724), the English minister and logician Isaac Watts contrasted element with compound in clear, modern terms.

Species of [[Atom|atom]] defined by its number of protons, called its [[Atomic number|atomic number]]. For example, oxygen has an atomic number of 8: each oxygen atom has 8 protons in its nucleus. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons, and are known as [[Isotope|isotopes]] of the element. Atoms of one element can be transformed into atoms of another in nuclear reactions, which change the atomic number. Almost all baryonic matter in the universe is composed of elements; neutron stars are among the rare exceptions.

The term has two closely related meanings: a kind of atom, and a pure chemical substance consisting of a single kind of atom. Water (H₂O), for instance, consists of the elements hydrogen and oxygen even though it contains neither H₂ nor O₂ molecules. Historically, an element meant a substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions, a definition that remains valid for most practical purposes. The properties of elements as atoms include atomic number, atomic weight, isotopes, ionization energy, electron affinity, oxidation states, and electronegativity; as substances, they include density, melting point, boiling point, and electrical and thermal conductivity.

Early human societies discovered native minerals such as carbon, sulfur, copper, and gold, though the modern concept of an element was not yet understood. Much of the modern understanding developed from the work of [[Dmitri Mendeleev|Dmitri Mendeleev]], a Russian chemist who published the first recognizable [[Periodic table|periodic table]] in 1869. The table orders elements by increasing atomic number into rows (periods) and columns (groups) that share recurring properties. Elements are commonly sorted into metals, nonmetals, and metalloids, the last forming a diagonal boundary with intermediate properties.

By November 2016 the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) recognized 118 elements. The first 94 occur naturally on Earth; the remaining 24 are [[Synthetic element|synthetic]], produced in nuclear reactions. Six of the natural ones, technetium, promethium, astatine, francium, neptunium, and plutonium, occur only in extreme trace amounts. Element 118 (oganesson) was reported in October 2006 and element 117 (tennessine) in April 2010.

Hydrogen and helium were created by Big Bang nucleosynthesis in the first 20 minutes of the universe, in a ratio of around 3:1 by mass, along with traces of lithium and beryllium. Almost all other natural elements were made by other forms of nucleosynthesis. Fewer than twenty elements, including gold, platinum, and the iron group metals, are sometimes found uncombined as native minerals; the rest occur as compounds or mixtures. Argon, a [[Noble gas|noble gas]], is chemically inert.

State of a [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] in which reactants and products have concentrations that no longer change with time. Process of chemical transformation of one set of substances into another, with a rearrangement of atoms and an accompanying energy change. Scientific study of the properties and behavior of matter. A physical science within the natural sciences, it studies the composition, structure, properties, and behavior of matter and the changes it undergoes during reactions with other substances, including the nature of [[Chemical bond|chemical bonds]] in compounds. Chemistry occupies an intermediate position between physics and biology and is sometimes called the central science because it provides a foundation for understanding both basic and applied scientific disciplines. It explains aspects of plant growth (botany), the formation of igneous rocks (geology), how atmospheric ozone forms and pollutants degrade (ecology), how medications work (pharmacology), and how DNA evidence is collected at a crime scene (forensics). Its applications are used widely in the chemical industry.

The word comes from a modification, during the Renaissance, of *alchemy*, which referred to earlier practices encompassing chemistry, metallurgy, philosophy, astrology, astronomy, mysticism, and medicine. [[Alchemy]] is often associated with the quest to turn lead or other base metals into gold. The word derives from the Arabic *al-kīmīā*, which may have Egyptian origins through Ancient Greek *χημία* and *Kemet*, the ancient name of Egypt; alternatively it may derive from *χημεία*, "cast together." Chemistry became a rigorous science in the 18th century, when [[Antoine Lavoisier|Antoine Lavoisier]] established principles such as the conservation of mass through precise experimentation.

The current model of atomic structure is the quantum mechanical model. The [[Atom|atom]] is the basic unit of chemistry: a dense nucleus of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons surrounded by a cloud of negatively charged electrons. A nucleon is approximately 1,836 times the mass of an electron, yet an atom's radius is about 10,000 times that of its nucleus. A pure substance composed of a single type of atom is a [[Chemical element|chemical element]], characterized by its atomic number; atoms of one element with different mass numbers are isotopes, so that carbon atoms may have mass numbers of 12 or 13. Elements are organized in the [[Periodic table|periodic table]] by atomic number into groups (columns) and periods (rows). A compound is a pure substance composed of more than one element, named under rules set by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) and indexed by CAS registry number. Molecules are typically sets of atoms bound by covalent bonds into electrically neutral units.

A [[Chemical reaction|chemical reaction]] is a transformation of substances into others through the rearrangement of electrons in bonds, and it can be depicted by a chemical equation with equal numbers of atoms on each side. Energy and entropy considerations matter in almost all studies. Substances are analyzed with tools such as spectroscopy and chromatography. Major branches include analytical, organic, inorganic, physical chemistry, and [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], which explores chemical processes within living organisms.
