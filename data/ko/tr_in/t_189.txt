Tool or weapon with a cutting edge or blade, usually attached to a handle or hilt. Knives are among the earliest tools used by humanity, appearing at least 2.5 million years ago, as evidenced by Oldowan tools. They were originally made of wood, bone, and stone such as flint and [[Obsidian|obsidian]]; with advances in metallurgy and manufacturing, blades came to be made of copper, [[Bronze|bronze]], iron, [[Steel|steel]], ceramic, and titanium.

A modern knife consists of a blade and a handle. The blade has a point, an edge (plain, serrated, or both), a grind (its cross-section shape), and a spine. The handle may include a tang extending into it, partial or full, as well as a bolster and a guard that protects the hand.

Blade materials have differing advantages. Carbon steel, an alloy of iron and carbon, takes a very sharp edge and is easy to sharpen but is vulnerable to rust and stains. [[Stainless steel]] contains chromium, possibly nickel and molybdenum, and little carbon; it takes a slightly less sharp edge but resists corrosion. [[Titanium]] has a higher strength-to-weight ratio and is more flexible than steel but is less hard. [[Ceramic|Ceramic]] blades are hard, brittle, lightweight, and noncorroding; they hold an edge for years but break if dropped or twisted. Steel blades are shaped by [[Forging|forging]], in which a heated piece is hammered or pressed, or by stock removal, in which metal is ground away; both methods are followed by heat treatment.

Knives serve many purposes, from hunting, combat, and camping to kitchen work (the chef's knife, paring knife, bread knife, and cleaver), table use, throwing, and religious ceremony or display, such as the *kirpan*. Weapons include the [[Dagger|dagger]] and the switchblade.

Branch of [[Topology|topology]] concerned with mathematical knots. A mathematical knot is a loop whose ends are joined so that it cannot be undone, formally an embedding of a circle in three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]]. Two knots are equivalent if one can be deformed into the other without cutting the string or passing it through itself. A fundamental problem is determining when two descriptions, such as knot diagrams, represent the same knot. More than six billion knots and links have been tabulated since the 19th century.

ENTRY:
Awareness of facts (descriptive knowledge), familiarity with individuals and situations (knowledge by acquaintance), or practical skill (procedural knowledge). Descriptive knowledge, also called propositional knowledge, is often characterized as true belief distinguished from opinion or guesswork by justification. Philosophers widely agree that propositional knowledge is a form of true belief, but controversies surround justification: how to understand it, whether it is needed at all, and whether something besides it is needed. It is closely related to intelligence, which concerns the ability to acquire, process, and apply information, whereas knowledge concerns information and skills a person already possesses.

The English word comes from the 12th-century Old English *cnawan*. Ancient Greek used four important terms: *epistēmē* (unchanging theoretical knowledge), *technē* (expert technical knowledge), *mētis* (strategic knowledge), and *gnōsis* (personal intellectual knowledge).

The main discipline investigating knowledge is [[Epistemology|epistemology]], which studies what people know, how they come to know it, and what it means to know something. It also considers the value of knowledge and philosophical skepticism, which questions whether knowledge is possible.

A long-discussed definition, which has roots in [[Plato]]'s exploration of the question, treats knowledge as justified true belief. Truth is a widely accepted feature, since one cannot know something false. Justification is included because some true beliefs, such as lucky guesses or beliefs based on superstition or erroneous reasoning, do not seem to be knowledge. Some philosophers hold that a belief is justified if it rests on evidence; others hold that it is justified if produced by a reliable process, such as sensory perception or logical reasoning.

In 1963, Edmund Gettier presented counterexamples of justified true beliefs that fail to constitute knowledge because of epistemic luck. In a well-known example, a driver in a country with many barn facades and one real barn stops by chance in front of the real barn and believes it is a barn; the belief is justified and true, yet the driver could not have told the difference. Such cases prompted alternative definitions, including reliabilism.

Knowledge can be produced in many ways. The main source of empirical knowledge is [[Perception|perception]], the use of the senses to learn about the external world. [[Introspection]] reveals one's own mental states and processes. Other sources include [[Memory|memory]], rational intuition, [[Inference|inference]], and testimony. According to foundationalism, some sources are basic and can justify beliefs without depending on other mental states. Coherentists hold that a sufficient degree of coherence among all of a believer's mental states is necessary, and infinitism holds that an infinite chain of beliefs is needed.

Knowledge is relevant to many fields. The sciences aim to acquire it through the [[Scientific method|scientific method]], based on repeatable experimentation, observation, and measurement. Various religions hold that humans should seek knowledge and that God or the divine is its source. The anthropology of knowledge studies how it is acquired, stored, retrieved, and communicated across cultures, and the [[Sociology|sociology]] of knowledge examines the circumstances in which it arises.

Armed conflict on the Korean Peninsula, fought from June 25, 1950, to July 27, 1953, between [[North Korea]] and [[South Korea]] and their allies. North Korea, backed by China and the [[Soviet Union]], invaded the South in June 1950. A [[United Nations]] force under a unified command, led by the [[United States]], supported South Korea, and the United States provided around 90% of its military personnel. After UN forces landed at Inchon on September 15, they recaptured Seoul and advanced into North Korea until Chinese forces entered the war that October. ENTRY:
Body of [[Myth|myths]] told by historical and modern Koreans, in two main forms. Literary mythology, recorded in traditional Hanja histories such as *Samguk sagi* and *Samguk yusa*, consists mostly of state-foundation myths about the founders of ancient kingdoms. The best known is that of Gojoseon and its legendary king Dangun, which became the founding myth of the whole Korean nation. Oral mythology is much larger and more varied. It consists of shamanic narratives sung by *mansin* (shamans or priestesses) in rituals that invoke the gods, and is revised with each performance. These narratives are often at odds with official ideologies and are frequently characterized as subversive of patriarchy. The mythology of southern [[Jeju Island]] is especially divergent. Two narratives, *Jeseok bon-puri* and *Princess Bari*, appear in nearly all regions. In the latter, a princess abandoned by her father revives her dead parents with the flower of life.
