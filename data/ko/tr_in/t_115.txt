Theorem in [[Number theory|number theory]] stating that no positive integers *a*, *b* and *c* satisfy aⁿ + bⁿ = cⁿ for any integer n greater than 2. [[Pierre de Fermat]] wrote around 1637, in the margin of his copy of [[Diophantus|Diophantus's]] *Arithmetica*, that he had a marvelous proof the margin was too narrow to contain. No proof was found after his death, and the claim long remained a conjecture. It also established much of the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture, later known as the modularity theorem, and the Abel Prize citation for Wiles in 2016 called the result a "stunning advance."

Apparent contradiction between the high likelihood of extraterrestrial civilizations and the lack of evidence for them. Given the vast number of stars and potentially habitable planets in the observable universe, the question is why no clear evidence of such civilizations has been found. The paradox is named after physicist [[Enrico Fermi]], who raised the question during lunch at Los Alamos in 1950. It first appeared in print in a 1963 paper by [[Carl Sagan]]. Subatomic particle with half-integer spin, following Fermi–Dirac statistics. Fermions obey the [[Pauli exclusion principle]], so no two identical fermions can occupy the same quantum state. They include all [[Quark|quarks]] and [[Lepton|leptons]] and composite particles made of an odd number of them, such as [[Baryon|baryons]] and many atoms and nuclei. English theoretical physicist [[Paul Dirac]] coined the names fermion, after [[Enrico Fermi]], and boson in 1945.

Creative work, chiefly narrative, portraying individuals, events, or places that are imaginary or in ways that are imaginary. Fictional portrayals are thus inconsistent with fact, history, or plausibility. In a traditional narrow sense, fiction refers to written narratives in prose, often specifically [[Novel|novels]], novellas, and short stories. More broadly, it encompasses imaginary narratives in any medium, including theatrical performances, films, television programs, comics, role-playing games, and video games.

The fictionality of a work is typically expressed publicly, so audiences expect it to deviate to some degree from the real world. Some works depict an independent [[Fictional universe|fictional universe]]; constructing one is called worldbuilding. The study of the nature, function, and meaning of fiction is literary theory, and the interpretation of specific texts is [[Literary criticism]]. The critic James Wood argues that "fiction is both artifice and verisimilitude," a requirement often linked to Samuel Taylor Coleridge's idea of the audience's willing suspension of disbelief.

Fiction contrasts with [[Non-fiction|non-fiction]], whose creators assume responsibility for presenting only historical and factual reality, although works such as autofiction and docudramas blur the boundary. Speculative fiction adheres less to realistic or plausible individuals, events, or places and includes fantasy, alternative history, and [[Science fiction]]. Realistic fiction has a real setting and believable events; its subgenres include [[Historical fiction]].

Early fiction was closely associated with history and myth. Greek poets such as [[Homer]], Hesiod, and Aesop developed fictional stories told first orally and then in writing, and prose fiction developed in [[Ancient Greece]]. Plato condemned fiction as thrice-removed imitation that deceives, while modern analyses frame it as non-deceptive pretense.

Species of [[Fig|fig]] tree also called the sycamore fig or fig-mulberry, because its leaves resemble those of the mulberry.

Native to [[Africa]] south of the Sahel and north of the Tropic of Capricorn, excluding central-west rainforest areas, *Ficus sycomorus* also grows in [[Lebanon]], the southern Arabian Peninsula, [[Cyprus]], very localized areas of [[Madagascar]], Palestine and [[Egypt]]. It is a protected tree in Israel, where it provides shade despite its roots cracking walls and pipes. The tree grows to 20 meters (66 ft) tall with a dense, round crown, and its large edible figs ripen from buff-green to yellow or red, borne in clusters on branchlets or the leaf axil.

Botanists Daniel Zohary and Maria Hopf note that the [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egyptians]] cultivated the species almost exclusively. Its remains appear in predynastic times and in quantity from the start of the third millennium BC, and it was the ancient Egyptian tree of life.
