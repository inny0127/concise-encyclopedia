Group of highly reactive metallic elements in Group 1 of the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. The alkali metals are [[Lithium|lithium]], sodium, potassium, rubidium, cesium and [[Francium|francium]]. They are soft, shiny metals that readily lose their outermost electron to form +1 ions, and their similar properties show clear group trends. They tarnish rapidly in air and must be stored under oil; cesium is the most reactive metal. All of them react with water, the heavier ones more vigorously. Sodium is the most abundant in nature, while francium is so radioactive that only minute traces occur. Rubidium and cesium are used in [[Atomic clock|atomic clocks]], and cesium clocks form the basis of the second. [[Sodium chloride|Table salt]] has been used since antiquity, and lithium is used in batteries. Sodium, potassium and possibly lithium are essential elements.

Alkanes, also called paraffins, have the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ and range from [[Methane|methane]] (CH₄) to very large molecules such as hexacontane (C₆₀H₁₂₂). Unsaturated [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbon]] with one or more carbon–carbon double bonds. Alkenes, also called olefins, form a series with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ, two hydrogens fewer than the corresponding [[Alkane|alkane]]. A double bond consists of a sigma bond and a pi bond. It is stronger than a single bond (611 kJ/mol against 347 kJ/mol) and shorter (1.33 Å against 1.53 Å). Rotation about the double bond is restricted, so cis and trans isomers can be handled separately. Alkenes are generally colorless, nonpolar and more reactive than alkanes. They are produced industrially by steam cracking and dehydrogenation of hydrocarbons from natural-gas condensate and [[Petroleum|petroleum]]. The simplest, [[Ethylene|ethylene]] (C₂H₄), is the organic compound produced on the largest scale, at approximately 225 million metric tons per year as of 2024. Ethylene and [[Propylene|propylene]] are the feedstocks for [[Polyethylene]] and [[Polypropylene]].

ENTRY:
Exaggerated immune response in which the body mistakenly identifies an ordinarily harmless substance, the allergen, as a threat. Allergic reactions give rise to hay fever, allergic conjunctivitis, allergic [[Asthma|asthma]], [[Atopic dermatitis|atopic dermatitis]], food allergies, and anaphylaxis. Symptoms include an itchy rash, sneezing, coughing, a runny nose, shortness of breath, or swelling. Common allergens include [[Pollen|pollen]], certain foods, metals, insect stings, medications, and latex.

Allergies develop through genetic and environmental factors. In the reaction, immunoglobulin E antibodies (IgE) bind to an allergen and then to a receptor on mast cells or basophils, which release inflammatory chemicals such as histamine. Insect stings, foods, and medicines such as [[Aspirin|aspirin]] and [[Penicillin|penicillin]] can produce anaphylaxis, a systemic reaction that may affect the skin, digestive, respiratory, and circulatory systems and can include low blood pressure, coma, and death.

Diagnosis is typically based on medical history. Skin or blood tests may help, but positive results do not necessarily indicate a significant allergy. Treatment includes avoiding known allergens and using medications such as steroids and antihistamines. Severe reactions call for injectable [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]] (epinephrine). Allergen immunotherapy, which gradually exposes people to larger amounts of allergen, is useful for some allergies, such as hay fever and reactions to insect bites; its use in food allergy is unclear.

In the developed world about 20% of people are affected by allergic rhinitis, food allergy affects 10% of adults and 8% of children, and about 20% have or have had atopic dermatitis. Depending on the country, about 1–18% of people have asthma, and anaphylaxis occurs in 0.05–2%. Rates of many allergic diseases appear to be increasing. Clemens von Pirquet first used the word "allergy" in 1906.

Mixture of chemical elements, in most cases at least one of them metallic, that retains the characteristics of a metal. Alloys often have properties that differ from those of their constituents. Most metals used commercially are alloyed to improve strength, hardness, or corrosion resistance, or to lower cost, as in alloys of gold and copper. Atoms in an alloy are joined by [[Metallic bonding|metallic bonding]] rather than the covalent bonds typical of chemical compounds.

Alloys are classified as substitutional or interstitial by atomic arrangement, and as homogeneous (a single phase), heterogeneous (two or more phases), or intermetallic. An alloy may be a solid solution in which all crystals have the same composition, or a mixture of phases forming a microstructure of different crystals. Each constituent has a saturation point: iron can hold at most 6.67% carbon, forming cementite.

Alloying changes mechanical behavior. Soft aluminum becomes much stronger when alloyed with copper. Adding a small amount of carbon to iron trades ductility for strength, producing [[Steel|steel]]; adding chromium raises corrosion resistance, producing [[Stainless steel|stainless steel]]. Other examples include red gold (gold and copper), white gold (gold and silver), sterling silver (silver and copper), solder, brass, and pewter.

Some alloys occur naturally, such as [[Electrum|electrum]], of silver and gold; meteorites are sometimes made of natural iron-nickel alloys. [[Bronze]], of tin and copper, was one of the first alloys made by humans and is much stronger and harder than either component. In ancient times steel could arise only as an accidental byproduct of smelting iron ore. Most alloys, including various aluminum, titanium, nickel, and magnesium alloys, were not created until the 1900s.

Ionizing particle of two protons and two neutrons, identical to the nucleus of a helium-4 atom. Alpha particles, also called alpha rays or alpha radiation, are named after the Greek letter α. They are generally produced in [[Radioactive decay|alpha decay]] but may also arise in other ways. The term was coined by [[Ernest Rutherford]] while he studied uranium radiation. Standard alpha decay produces alpha particles with a kinetic energy of about 5 MeV and a velocity of about 4% of the speed of light. They are highly [[Ionization|ionizing]] but penetrate little, stopped by a few centimeters of air or by the skin. Helium nuclei, which form 10–12% of cosmic rays, are usually of much higher energy and can penetrate far more deeply. Once an alpha particle gains electrons, it becomes a neutral helium atom.

ENTRY:
[[Writing system]] that uses a standard set of symbols, called graphemes or letters, to represent more or less the sounds of a spoken language. These graphemes largely correspond to [[Phoneme|phonemes]], the smallest sound segments that distinguish one word from another. Peter T. Daniels distinguishes true alphabets, which use letters for both consonants and vowels, from abjads, which generally lack vowel indicators, and abugidas, which mark vowels with diacritics. In this narrower sense the Greek alphabet was the first true alphabet; it derived from the Phoenician alphabet, an abjad.

The first letters were invented in Ancient Egypt to aid in writing [[Egyptian hieroglyphs]]. About 24 hieroglyphs, the uniliterals, gave single sounds; the system was used until the 5th century AD. A script appeared in Egyptian turquoise mines on the Sinai Peninsula c. 1840 BC, and an earlier version dated to c. 1800 BC was found at Wadi el-Hol in 1999. This Proto-Sinaitic script had no vowel characters. The best-attested Bronze Age alphabet is Ugaritic, an alphabetic cuneiform script of 30 signs invented in [[Ugarit]] before the 15th century BC.

Proto-Sinaitic developed into the [[Phoenician alphabet]] before c. 1050 BC. The oldest Phoenician text is an inscription on the sarcophagus of King Ahiram, c. 1000 BC. With only about two dozen letters, it was simple enough for trade, and it is the ultimate ancestor of many modern scripts, including Arabic, Cyrillic, Greek, Hebrew, and Latin. Aramaic gave rise to the [[Hebrew alphabet]].

The word comes from Late Latin *alphabetum* and Greek *alphabētos*, made from *alpha* and *beta*, which in turn came from Phoenician *aleph*, "ox," and *bet*, "house."
