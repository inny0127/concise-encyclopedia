[[Probability distribution]] of particle speeds in an idealized gas at thermal equilibrium. Named after [[James Clerk Maxwell]] and [[Ludwig Boltzmann]], it was first derived by Maxwell in 1860 on heuristic grounds, and Boltzmann later investigated its physical origins in the 1870s. Mathematically, it is a chi distribution with three degrees of freedom, with a scale parameter proportional to the square root of T/m, the ratio of temperature to particle mass. The distribution underlies the kinetic theory of gases, which explains properties such as [[Pressure|pressure]] and [[Diffusion|diffusion]]. It is an excellent approximation for rarefied gases at ordinary temperatures and for ideal plasmas.

Thought experiment of 1867 devised by [[James Clerk Maxwell]]. A demon controls a door between two gas-filled chambers, letting only fast-moving molecules pass one way and slow-moving ones the other. One chamber warms and the other cools, decreasing entropy without any work and seemingly violating the [[Second law of thermodynamics|second law of thermodynamics]]. Maxwell called the agent a "finite being"; [[Lord Kelvin]] later named it a "demon." The idea stimulated work on the relationship between thermodynamics and [[Information theory]]. Most scientists argue that no device can violate the second law in this way, and experimental versions differ from the original; none has been shown to violate it.

[[Mesoamerica|Mesoamerican]] civilization (c. 2000 BC–1697 AD), known for its temples, art, architecture, mathematics, calendar, and astronomical system. The Maya script is the most sophisticated and highly developed writing system in the pre-Columbian Americas. The civilization developed in an area comprising southeastern [[Mexico|Mexico]], all of [[Guatemala|Guatemala]] and [[Belize|Belize]], and the western portions of Honduras and El Salvador. Its descendants number well over 6 million and speak more than twenty-eight surviving Mayan languages. "Maya" is a modern collective term; Maya peoples had no common ethnic identity or political unity for most of their history.

The Preclassic period (c. 2000 BC to 250 AD) saw the first complex societies and the cultivation of staples such as maize, beans, squashes, and chili peppers. The first cities developed around 750 BC, and hieroglyphic writing was in use by the 3rd century BC. The Classic period, from around 250 AD, is largely defined by sculpted monuments bearing Long Count dates. Many city-states were linked by trade, and the rival cities of [[Tikal]] and Calakmul became powerful; the central Mexican city of [[Teotihuacan]] intervened in Maya dynastic politics. In the 9th century a widespread political collapse in the central region brought civil wars, abandoned cities, and a northward shift of population. The Postclassic period saw the rise of [[Chichen Itza]] in the north and of the Kʼicheʼ kingdom in the Guatemalan Highlands. In the 16th century the Spanish Empire colonized the region, and Nojpetén, the last Maya city, fell in 1697.

During the Classic period rule centered on the "divine king", thought to mediate between mortals and the supernatural realm. By the Late Classic the aristocracy had grown, reducing the king's exclusive power. The literate elite recorded history and ritual knowledge in screenfold books, of which only three uncontested examples remain, and on stelae and ceramics. They developed interlocking ritual calendars and mathematics that included one of the earliest known instances of the explicit zero. Their religion included human sacrifice.

Writing system of the [[Maya civilization]], the only Mesoamerican script that has been substantially deciphered. The earliest identifiable Maya inscriptions date to the 3rd century BCE in late Preclassic sites such as San Bartolo in Guatemala. Writing continued until the Spanish conquest of the 16th and 17th centuries, though knowledge of it was reserved to the elite. Maya script combined [[Logogram|logograms]] with syllabic glyphs, somewhat like modern Japanese writing, and recorded Classic Maya and, in northern Yucatán, Yucatec Maya. As of 2008, the sound of about 80% of the writing could be read and the meaning of about 60% understood with varying certainty.

Quantification of attributes of an object or event, determining how large or small a physical quantity is compared with a basic reference quantity of the same kind. Its scope depends on discipline. In natural science and engineering, measurement does not apply to nominal properties, but in statistics and the social and behavioral sciences it can occur at nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio levels. Measurement is a cornerstone of trade, science, technology, and quantitative research, and the science of measurement is metrology.

A measurement is characterized by its type, magnitude, unit, and uncertainty. The magnitude is the numerical value, usually obtained with a measuring instrument; the unit is a standard quantity assigned a weighting factor; the uncertainty represents the random and systematic errors of the procedure.

Historically, many measurement systems existed, often set by local agreement between trading partners. Since the 18th century, developments moved toward unified standards, resulting in the modern International System of Units (SI), which reduces all physical measurements to a combination of seven base units: kilogram, meter, candela, second, ampere, kelvin, and mole. These are defined without reference to a physical object, instead fixing values to physical constants or invariable natural phenomena. [[Charles Sanders Peirce]] first proposed tying a base unit to an experimental standard, defining the meter by the wavelength of a spectral line, which directly influenced the [[Michelson–Morley experiment]].

The General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), established in 1875 by the Metre Convention, oversees the SI. The meter was redefined in 1983 in terms of the speed of light, and the kilogram in 2019 in terms of the Planck constant. The British systems of English units and later imperial units, known in the United States as U.S. customary units, remain in use there and in a few Caribbean countries.

Animal tissue, mostly muscle, eaten as food. Humans have hunted and farmed animals for meat since prehistory, and the [[Neolithic Revolution|Neolithic Revolution]] allowed the domestication of chickens, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, and cattle starting around 11,000 years ago. Meat is important to economies, cultures, and faiths around the world. It is mainly composed of water, protein, and fat, and its quality is affected by the genetics, health, and nutrition of the animal. Without preservation, bacteria and fungi spoil unprocessed meat within hours or days. It is edible raw but mostly eaten cooked, as by stewing or roasting, or processed, as by smoking or salting.

The English word comes from Old English *mete*, meaning food in general. In modern usage it primarily means skeletal muscle with its associated fat and connective tissue. In the postwar period governments gave farmers guaranteed prices to raise output, and in 1966 the United States, United Kingdom, and other industrialized nations began factory farming of beef and dairy cattle and pigs. Intensive animal farming accounted for 30% of world meat production in 1990 and 40% by 2005.

Selective breeding, accelerated by techniques such as progeny testing, lets producers obtain desired qualities. After health concerns about saturated fats in the 1980s, the fat content of U.K. beef, pork, and lamb fell from 20–26 percent to 4–8 percent within a few decades, through breeding for leanness and changed butchery. Animals not previously exploited for meat, including antelope, zebra, water buffalo, camel, crocodile, emu, and ostrich, are now farmed..

Consumption of red and processed meat, as opposed to fish and poultry, increases the risk of certain negative health outcomes, including cancer, coronary heart disease, and diabetes. Meat production harms the environment by contributing to global warming, pollution, and biodiversity loss. Vegetarians and vegans choose not to eat meat for ethical, environmental, health, or religious reasons.

Engineering discipline concerned with the design, analysis, manufacture, and operation of mechanical systems. It combines engineering physics and mathematics with [[Materials science|materials science]] to develop and improve machines, structures, manufacturing processes, and fluid, thermal, and energy systems. Core areas include [[Mechanics|mechanics]], dynamics, [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]], design, and structural analysis. Engineers use computer-aided design, manufacturing, and engineering (CAD, CAM, CAE) and product lifecycle management for industrial equipment, heating and cooling systems, motor vehicles, aircraft, robotics, and medical devices.

The field emerged during the [[Industrial Revolution]] in Europe in the 18th century, but its development can be traced back several thousand years. The wedge and inclined plane were known since prehistoric times, the lever appeared around 5,000 years ago in the Near East, and the shadoof, the first crane, in Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC. Water wheels and watermills appeared in the Persian Empire by the early 4th century BC. [[Archimedes]] (287–212 BC) influenced mechanics in the Western tradition. [[Su Song]] (1020–1101 AD) incorporated an escapement into his astronomical clock tower two centuries before escapements appeared in European clocks. Al-Jazari wrote his book on ingenious mechanical devices in 1206.

In the 17th century, [[Christiaan Huygens]] invented the pendulum clock in 1657, the first reliable timekeeper for almost 300 years, and [[Isaac Newton]] formulated his laws of motion and developed calculus. In the early 19th century, machine tools developed in England, Germany, and Scotland allowed mechanical engineering to become a separate field. It overlaps with aerospace, civil, electrical, chemical, and industrial engineering, and today's engineers pursue composites, mechatronics, and nanotechnology.

Branch of [[Physics|physics]] concerned with the relationships between force, matter and motion among physical objects. Forces may cause displacements, which are changes in an object's position relative to its environment. Theoretical work on the subject began in ancient Greece, where philosophers were among the first to propose that abstract principles govern nature, and the main theory of mechanics in antiquity was Aristotelian mechanics. A parallel tradition used mathematics more extensively to analyze bodies statically or dynamically. Early modern scientists, including [[Galileo Galilei]], Johannes Kepler, Christiaan Huygens and [[Isaac Newton]], laid the foundation for [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]]. In the 20th century, new discoveries challenged these concepts and led to [[Relativistic mechanics|relativistic mechanics]] and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].
