Machine, especially one programmable by computer, capable of automatically carrying out a complex series of actions. A robot may be guided by an external or internal control device. Robots may be humanoid, but most are task-performing machines prioritizing function over aesthetics. They range from humanoids such as Honda's ASIMO to industrial robots, medical operating robots, swarm robots, UAV drones such as the General Atomics MQ-1 Predator, and microscopic nanorobots. [[Robotics]] is the branch of technology dealing with their design, construction, operation, and application. Software agents are usually called bots; an [[Android (robot)|android]] is a humanoid robot, and a [[Cyborg|cyborg]] is a human augmented with artificial machines.

The word comes from the Czech author Karel Capek's play *R.U.R.* (*Rossum's Universal Robots*), from Czech *robota*, "forced labor." Ancient cultures described artificial people: Greek myth included Talos, a bronze man guarding Crete, and the mechanical servants built by [[Hephaestus]]. Robots have replaced humans in repetitive and dangerous tasks and in extreme environments such as outer space and the sea bottom. They are blamed for rising technological unemployment, and their use in military combat raises ethical concerns, though studies cited in some accounts report productivity gains in robot-adopting industries.

Naturally occurring solid mass or aggregate of minerals or mineraloid matter, also called stone. Rocks are categorized by the [[Mineral|minerals]] included, their chemical composition, and how they formed. They make up the Earth's outer solid layer, the [[Earth's crust|crust]], and most of its interior, except for the liquid outer core and pockets of magma in the asthenosphere. [[Petrology]] studies the character and origin of rocks, and mineralogy their mineral components; the field may extend to planetary geology.

Silicate minerals account for about one-third of all known mineral species and about 95% of the earth's crust. Rocks are grouped into three classes, [[Igneous rock|igneous]], [[Sedimentary rock|sedimentary]], and [[Metamorphic rock|metamorphic]], and can be transformed from one type to another, as described by the rock cycle.

Igneous rocks form when magma cools in the crust or lava cools at the surface or seabed. Plutonic (intrusive) rocks cool slowly within the crust, as with [[Granite|granite]]; volcanic (extrusive) rocks reach the surface, forming pumice or basalt. Sedimentary rocks form by diagenesis and lithification of sediments produced by the weathering, transport, and deposition of existing rocks, as with limestone and sandstone. Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are transformed by high pressures and temperatures without significant melting, as with marble from limestone or slate from shale.

Humans have used rocks since the earliest times, from Stone Age tools to buildings and infrastructure, and mining extracts rocks and the metals within them. The study of rocks became a formal science in the 19th century; the discovery of radioactive decay in 1896 allowed radiometric dating, and understanding of [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]] developed in the second half of the 20th century.

Mountain range, western North America, the largest mountain system on the continent.

The Rockies stretch 3,000 mi (4,800 km) in a straight line from northern Western Canada to New Mexico in the southwestern United States, varying in width from 110 to 480 km (70 to 300 miles). They are the easternmost portion of the North American Cordillera and older than the Cascade Range and [[Sierra Nevada]], which lie farther west. Definitions of the northern end differ between Canada and the United States; it lies in northern [[British Columbia]] or farther north near the Alaska–Yukon border.

The system comprises dozens of subranges divided by high basins and wide tablelands. Most of the highest summits are in [[Colorado]]; Mount Elbert, at 4,401 m (14,440 ft), is the highest peak, and Mount Robson, at 3,954 m (12,972 ft), is highest in the Canadian Rockies. The Continental Divide of the Americas runs through the range, separating waters flowing to the Atlantic and Pacific. In Alberta, rivers feed the basin of the [[Mackenzie River]], which empties into the Beaufort Sea.

The Rockies formed 55 million to 80 million years ago during the Laramide orogeny, when plates began sliding at a shallow angle beneath the North American plate. Tectonic activity, erosion, and [[Glacier|glaciers]] have since sculpted the peaks and valleys. Humans began inhabiting the range at the end of the last ice age. The [[Lewis and Clark Expedition]] and Sir Alexander Mackenzie explored it, and minerals and the [[Fur trade|fur trade]] drove early economic exploitation, though the population has never been dense. Parks protect much of the range, a popular destination for hiking and skiing.

Western style of architecture, art and decoration that emerged in France in the 1730s as a reaction against the Louis XIV style. Characterized by extensive ornamentation, fluid curves, asymmetry and a smaller scale designed to foster intimacy, it often blends white and pastel colors with gilding and uses *rocaille* shell- and pebble-like motifs. Its visual art favors aristocratic leisure and the pastoral. It is regarded as the final expression of the [[Baroque]] movement, and it spread from French interiors to northern Italy, Austria, southern Germany, [[Central Europe]] and Russia, influencing [[Painting|painting]], [[Sculpture|sculpture]], furniture, music, theater and literature. It was later adapted to church architecture, especially in Central Europe, Portugal and [[South America]]. Critics first used the word in print in 1825 as a pejorative, and by the late 18th century the style had largely been replaced by [[Neoclassicism]].

ENTRY:
Mammals of the order Rodentia, characterized by a single pair of continuously growing incisors in each jaw.

Rodents make up about 40% of all mammal species and are native to every major landmass except Antarctica. The name comes from Latin *rodens*, "gnawing". Most are small, with robust bodies, short limbs, and long tails, and most eat plant material. Enamel covers only the front of each incisor, so the softer dentine behind wears away faster and leaves a chisel-like edge; the animals must gnaw constantly to keep the teeth from growing so long that they pierce the skull. Rodents have no canines.

Size ranges from most species weighing under 100 g (3.5 oz) to the [[Capybara|capybara]], the largest, at up to 66 kg (146 lb). Rodents live in nearly every terrestrial habitat and can be arboreal, burrowing, leaping, or semiaquatic. Many are social.

The fossil record begins in the Paleocene of Asia, and rodents diversified greatly in the [[Eocene]]. They reached South America and [[Madagascar]] from Africa and, until humans arrived, were the only terrestrial placental mammals to colonize Australia. Rodentia and [[Lagomorpha]] (rabbits, hares, and pikas) are sister groups forming the clade Glires; lagomorphs are distinguished by an extra pair of upper incisors.

Humans use rodents for food, clothing, pets, and laboratory research. The [[Brown rat]], the black rat, and the [[House mouse]] are serious pests that spoil stored food and spread disease, and introduced rodents are often invasive. Familiar rodents include mice, rats, squirrels, beavers, guinea pigs, and hamsters.
