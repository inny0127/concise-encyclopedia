ENTRY:
Indo-Aryan language native to the Hindi-Urdu Belt, spoken across northern, central, western, and eastern India and in Pakistan as a lingua franca. Also called Hindi–Urdu, it is pluricentric, with two standard registers: Hindi, a Sanskritized register written in the Devanagari script, and Urdu, a Persianized and Arabized register written in the Perso-Arabic Nastaliq script. Both are official languages of India, and Urdu is also the national language of Pakistan. Colloquial speech falls on a spectrum between the two, and conversion between them is generally done by transliteration; translation is usually needed only for religious and literary texts.

The core vocabulary derives from Prakrit and Sanskrit, with substantial loanwords from Persian and Arabic. Early forms developed from Apabhraṃśa vernaculars in the 7th–13th centuries, and the language emerged as a contact language around the Ganges-Yamuna Doab (Delhi, Meerut, and Saharanpur), based on the Khariboli dialect. Scholars trace its first written poetry, in Old Hindi, to the [[Delhi Sultanate]] era around the 12th and 13th centuries. [[Amir Khusrau]] used it in the 13th century and called it Hindavi. The campaigns of [[Alauddin Khalji]] carried it into the Deccan, where the dialect Deccani developed. It took shape as a Persianized vernacular under the Delhi Sultanate (1206–1526) and the Mughal Empire (1526–1858).

Hindi and Urdu diverged as standardized forms in the 19th century, largely along religious and cultural lines. [[Mahatma Gandhi]] endorsed Hindustani as a unifying language that could transcend communal divisions, and it became an expression of national unity during the [[Indian independence movement]]. It remains prominent in Bollywood films and songs.

According to Ethnologue, Hindi and Urdu together are the third-most-spoken language in the world after English and Mandarin, with 855 million native and second-language speakers, though this includes many who reported their language as Hindi on the Indian census but speak other Hindi languages.

ENTRY:
Greek astronomer, geographer and mathematician, born in Nicaea, Bithynia. He is considered the founder of [[Trigonometry]], built trigonometric tables and solved problems of spherical trigonometry. He is most famous for his incidental discovery of the precession of the [[Equinox|equinoxes]], and he compiled the first known comprehensive star catalog from the western world. He may have invented the [[Astrolabe]], and he built quantitative models of the motion of the [[Sun]] and [[Moon]] that survive. He contributed to [[Optics|optics]], developing an atomist theory of light. He is known to have worked as an astronomer between 162 and 127 BC, and he is often called the "father of astronomy," a title conferred by Jean Baptiste Joseph Delambre in 1817.

ENTRY:
Greek physician and philosopher of the classical period, born on the island of Kos and traditionally called the "Father of Medicine." He is credited with the use of prognosis and clinical observation, the systematic categorization of diseases, and the later discredited formulation of humoral theory ([[Humorism]]). His studies set out the basic ideas of specialties including [[Surgery|surgery]], urology, neurology, and acute medicine.

Little is known concretely about what Hippocrates himself thought, wrote, and did, because his achievements were conflated with those of later practitioners of Hippocratic medicine and the writers of the Hippocratic Corpus. The 2nd-century physician Soranus of Ephesus, his first biographer, wrote that his father, Heraclides, was a physician and that he learned medicine from his father and grandfather and studied other subjects with [[Democritus]] and Gorgias. Other biographical details are likely untrue. He taught and practiced medicine throughout his life, traveling at least as far as Thessaly, Thrace, and the [[Sea of Marmara]]. [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] mention him in passing.

Hippocrates is credited as the first to hold that diseases have natural causes rather than being punishments from the gods, attributing them to environmental factors, diet, and living habits. The Hippocratic or Koan school emphasized patient care and prognosis, whereas the Knidian school focused on diagnosis. Because Greek taboo forbade dissection of humans, medicine of the period knew almost nothing of anatomy and physiology, and many Hippocratic convictions rested on incorrect anatomy. A central concept was the crisis, the point in a disease at which the patient either succumbed or recovered.

The Hippocratic Corpus, a collection of about 60 treatises, summarized earlier medical knowledge and prescribed acceptable practices for physicians; most of it dates to the late 5th and early 4th centuries BC and reflects multiple authors. Hippocrates is credited with the Hippocratic Oath, still in use today, though its direct authorship is debated.

Large semiaquatic mammal, *Hippopotamus amphibius*, native to [[Sub-Saharan Africa]]. It is one of two living species in the family Hippopotamidae, the other being the pygmy hippopotamus, and after elephants and rhinoceroses it is the next largest land mammal. Its closest living relatives are [[Cetacean|cetaceans]], from which hippos diverged about 55 million years ago. Adult bulls average 1.5 t and cows 1.3 t. Hippos live in rivers, lakes and mangrove swamps, where territorial bulls each preside over a stretch of water with five to thirty cows and calves. Mating and birth occur in water, and the animals graze on grasses at dusk. Hippos are among the most dangerous animals in the world because of their aggressive and unpredictable nature. They are threatened by habitat loss and by poaching for meat and [[Ivory|ivory]] (canine teeth).

Systematic study of the past, focusing primarily on the human past. As an academic discipline, history analyzes and interprets evidence to construct narratives about what happened and to explain why, including the contexts, causal mechanisms, and human motives involved. In a more general sense, the term refers to the past itself, to periods in the past, or to individual texts about the past. A history in this last sense is a representation of the past that involves interpretation and reconstruction, and its narrative can change as historians discover new evidence or reinterpret known sources.

Historical research relies on primary and secondary sources to reconstruct past events and validate interpretations. Source criticism assesses the authenticity, content, and reliability of sources, and historians try to integrate the perspectives of several sources into a coherent narrative. Schools of thought, including [[Positivism|positivism]], the Annales school, [[Marxism]], and [[Postmodernism|postmodernism]], take distinct methodological approaches. Pseudohistory, by contrast, deviates from historiographical standards by relying on disputed evidence, selectively ignoring genuine evidence, or otherwise distorting the record, often for ideological purposes. Holocaust denial and Armenian genocide denial are prominent examples of historical denialism.

The discipline has many branches. Some focus on time periods, such as [[Ancient history|ancient history]]; others on regions, such as the [[History of Africa|history of Africa]]; others on themes, such as political, [[Military history|military]], social, and economic history. Branches defined by method or source include quantitative, comparative, and oral history. Historians have traditionally been concerned with written documents, leaving prehistory to fields such as [[Archaeology|archaeology]], but their scope broadened in the 20th century to include the human past before writing.

Historians debate whether history is a social science, part of the humanities, or a hybrid. Like social scientists, they formulate hypotheses and use objective evidence, but the reliance on interpretation, storytelling, and cultural heritage aligns the field with the humanities. Views on its purpose also differ. Some hold that its aim is the disinterested discovery of truth; others stress the lessons it offers for present decisions, an understanding of the human condition, or the social cohesion that comes from a shared past. For some scholars, including Whig historians and the Marxist E. H. Carr, history is a key to understanding the present and, for Carr, to shaping the future.

History emerged as a field of inquiry in antiquity to replace myth-infused narratives, with influential early traditions in Greece, China, and later the Islamic world. Historical writing became increasingly professional in the 19th century, when rigorous methodology and academic institutions were established. The English word comes from the Ancient Greek *historía*, meaning inquiry and testimony; it entered Middle English in the 14th century via Old French *histoire*, meaning "story, tale."

Collective past of the continent's East, South, Southeast, Central, North, and West Asian regions.

These arose in fertile river valleys suited to agriculture, likely developed writing, cities, states, and empires independently. [[Göbekli Tepe]] in Anatolia (c. 9500–8000 BC) has the world's oldest known megaliths, and Lahuradewa in India yielded rice-associated dates of 9000–8000 BC, which an excavation report called the earliest Neolithic site in South Asia. The [[Achaemenid Empire]], founded by Cyrus the Great, ruled from Greece to the Indus River from the 6th to 4th centuries BC.

Asia was the birthplace of the Abrahamic religions (Judaism, Christianity, and Islam), the Indian religions (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism), and the Iranian religions, including Zoroastrianism and the now extinct Manichaeism. Development of the planet from its formation to the present, reconstructed by nearly all branches of natural science.

Earth formed around 4.54 billion years ago by accretion from the solar nebula, a rotating cloud of dust and gas made of hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang and heavier elements ejected by supernovae. A giant impact with a planet-sized body named [[Theia (hypothetical planet)|Theia]] is thought to have formed the Moon. Earth then cooled, forming a solid crust and allowing liquid water on the surface.

Geologists divide this history into four eons, starting 4,540 Ma (million years ago). The [[Hadean]] eon, the time before a reliable fossil record, ended about 4.03 billion years ago. The Archean and Proterozoic eons saw the beginnings of life. The earliest undisputed evidence dates from at least 3.5 billion years ago, including stromatolites in 3.48 billion-year-old sandstone from Western Australia. Photosynthetic organisms appeared between 3.2 and 2.4 billion years ago and began enriching the atmosphere with oxygen, culminating in the [[Great Oxidation Event]] around 2.4 billion years ago. Complex multicellular life arose about 580 million years ago and diversified in the [[Cambrian explosion]] about 538.8 million years ago, which opened the [[Phanerozoic]] eon.

The Phanerozoic has three eras: the Paleozoic, an era of arthropods, fishes, and the first life on land; the Mesozoic, which spanned the rise and climactic extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs; and the Cenozoic, which saw the rise of mammals. Recognizable humans emerged at most 2 million years ago. An estimated 99 percent of all species that ever lived, over five billion, have gone extinct. [[Plate tectonics]] continues to reshape the continents and oceans.

Development of the region including [[China]], Japan, Korea, Mongolia and Taiwan, from prehistory to the present. Scholars regard East Asia as a distinct cultural region, one defined by adherence to Confucianism, the influence of Buddhism and the use of chopsticks. Recorded civilization began around 2000 BC in the [[Shang dynasty]] along the [[Yellow River]], and Gojoseon became the first organized state in Korea around 195 BC. Japan emerged as a unitary state with its first constitution in 604 AD. Under the Sui, Tang and Song, China was the most advanced civilization in East Asia. The Mongol Empire under [[Genghis Khan]], [[Subutai]] and [[Kublai Khan]] brought most of the region under one state, except Japan and Taiwan, and the Yuan dynasty's two invasions of Japan failed. The Ming and Joseon dynasties adopted Neo-Confucianism, while Japan fell into the Sengoku Jidai civil war, which lasted over a century and a half.

Development of one of the world's oldest civilizations, along the [[Nile]], from prehistory to the present. Egypt was unified around 3150 BC by King [[Narmer]], and dynastic rule lasted for about three millennia with a distinctive religion, arts, language and customs. The Old Kingdom built the pyramids, including those of Djoser and at Giza. Persian, Greek, Roman and Arab rule followed, and Egypt was joined to the [[Ottoman Empire]] in 1517. Britain controlled Egypt in the late 19th century. The 1952 Free Officers' revolution abolished the monarchy, and a republic was proclaimed in 1953 with [[Gamal Abdel Nasser]] rising to leadership. [[Anwar Sadat]] made a peace treaty with Israel in 1979. After the 2011 uprising ousted Hosni Mubarak, the [[Muslim Brotherhood]] governed briefly under Mohamed Morsi, who was removed in a 2013 counter-coup led by [[Abdel Fattah el-Sisi]], who now leads the country.

Development of one of Africa's oldest countries, from ancient kingdoms in the [[Horn of Africa]] to the present. In the 1st century AD the [[Kingdom of Aksum|Aksumite Kingdom]] grew into a major power on the [[Red Sea]], and under King Ezana in the early 4th century Christianity became the state religion. Aksum declined with the rise of Islam in Arabia, and the Zagwe dynasty built a new capital at Lalibela before the Solomonic dynasty took over in the 13th century. The decisive victory at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, under [[Menelik II]], preserved sovereignty during the [[Scramble for Africa]]. Fascist Italy occupied the country from 1936 to 1941. A 1974 coup brought the [[Derg]] military council to power, and its Marxist-Leninist regime was responsible for the Red Terror and the 1984–85 famine until it was overthrown in 1991.

Record of the continent's peoples and states from the first modern humans to the present.

The ancestors of contemporary Europeans appeared about 37,000 years ago during the Paleolithic. Farmers from Anatolia spread agriculture during the Neolithic (7000–2000 BC), and from about 4000 BC steppe pastoralists spread [[Indo-European languages]] westward. The city-states of ancient Greece mark the start of classical antiquity (c. 800 BC–AD 500). Christianity emerged under Roman rule in the 1st century AD.

The abdication of the last Western Roman Emperor in 476 traditionally begins the [[Middle Ages]] (c. 500–1500). The 8th-century Umayyad conquest of Hispania established Al-Andalus. In the early 9th century the Latin Church proclaimed the Frankish king [[Charlemagne]] "Emperor of the Romans." His divided empire evolved into the Kingdom of France and the Holy Roman Empire in the 10th century. The High Middle Ages (c. 1000–1300) brought the split between the Latin and Eastern Orthodox churches and the Crusades.

The 15th century brought the [[Renaissance]] and revolutions in printing and seafaring, and the Age of Discovery led to colonization, the Atlantic slave trade, and the Columbian exchange. The 16th-century Reformation challenged the Catholic Church. Modern science emerged in the 17th-century [[Scientific Revolution]]. The [[Industrial Revolution]] and liberal and nationalist upheavals shaped the 19th century. World War I (1914–1918) ended with multi-ethnic empires fragmented into nation states. World War II (1939–1945) began with Nazi Germany's invasion of Poland, and the regime perpetrated the Holocaust. Europe was divided during the Cold War (1947–1991) between a U.S.-led Western bloc and the Soviet-led Eastern bloc. The Soviet Union dissolved after the Revolutions of 1989, and the [[European Union]] was established in 1993.

Development of the western European territory once known as [[Gaul]], from the Iron Age to the present. Julius Caesar conquered Gaul in the Gallic Wars of 58–51 BC, and it became integrated into the Roman Empire. [[Clovis I]] united most of Gaul in the late 5th century, and Frankish power peaked under [[Charlemagne]]. The [[Hundred Years' War]] ended in a Valois victory in 1453 after [[Joan of Arc]] led French forces. Under [[Louis XIV]] France became a centralized absolute monarchy, and the [[French Revolution]] overthrew the monarchy in the late 18th century. After regime changes, the Third Republic was established in 1870. France played a decisive role in defeating the Central Powers in World War I, but was conquered by Nazi Germany in 1940, and the Fourth Republic was established after liberation in 1944. After the 1958 Algerian crisis, [[Charles de Gaulle]] established the Fifth Republic, and most of the colonial empire became independent.
