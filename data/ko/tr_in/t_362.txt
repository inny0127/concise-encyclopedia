Period of Chinese history, c. 475–221 BC. It comprises the final centuries of the [[Zhou dynasty]] (c. 1046–256 BC), when the Zhou king formally ruled but functioned as a figurehead. Unwanted or unusable material, any substance discarded after primary use or regarded as worthless or defective. A by-product, by contrast, is a joint product of relatively minor economic value. A waste product may become a by-product, joint product or resource through an [[Invention|invention]] that raises its value above zero. Examples include municipal solid waste (household garbage), hazardous waste, wastewater, radioactive waste and others.

The Basel Convention (1989) defines wastes as substances or objects disposed of, intended to be disposed of, or required to be disposed of by national law. The [[European Union]]'s Waste Framework Directive (2008/98/EC) defines waste as "an object the holder discards, intends to discard or is required to discard."

The [[OECD]] defines municipal solid waste as "waste collected and treated by or for municipalities," typically household, commercial, and demolition or construction waste. In 2018 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) concluded that 292.4 million short tons of municipal waste were generated, about 4.9 pounds per person per day; approximately 69 million tons were recycled and 25 million tons composted. U.S. hazardous waste, defined as waste capable of harming health or the environment, is regulated under the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act.

Globally, municipal solid waste reached 2.24 billion tonnes in 2020, or 0.79 kg per person per day, and is projected to rise to 3.8 billion tonnes by 2050. Only about 13.5% of waste is [[Recycling|recycled]] worldwide. Open dumping in developing areas contributes to methane emissions and [[Groundwater|groundwater]] contamination, and electronic waste exceeds 50 million tonnes yearly.

Processes that manage waste from its generation to final disposal, covering collection, transport, treatment, monitoring and regulation. Inorganic compound with the chemical formula H₂O, a transparent, tasteless, odorless and nearly colorless liquid at room temperature. Water is the main constituent of Earth's streams, lakes and oceans and the fluid of all known living organisms, in which it acts as a solvent. It provides neither food energy nor organic micronutrients, yet it is vital to all known life. Because it dissolves more substances than any other liquid, it is often called the "universal solvent," though it dissolves nonpolar substances poorly. As found in nature it almost always contains dissolved substances, and special steps are needed to obtain chemically pure water.

The word comes from Old English *wæter*, from Proto-Germanic *\*watar*, from Proto-Indo-European *\*wod-or*.

Water is a polar molecule, and strong intermolecular [[Hydrogen bond|hydrogen bonding]] largely determines its physical and chemical properties. Earth's surface temperature and pressure lie close to water's [[Triple point|triple point]], so water exists there as solid (ice), liquid and gas (water vapor or steam). Phase transitions are freezing, melting, vaporization, condensation, sublimation and deposition. At 1 atm, ice melts or water freezes at 0 °C and water boils at 100 °C; the boiling point falls by 1 °C every 274 m of altitude. Temperatures exceed 205 °C in the geyser Old Faithful and can exceed 400 °C in [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vents]], where water stays liquid under pressure.

Water is unusual in expanding as it cools within certain temperature ranges. At 1 atm its maximum [[Density|density]], 999.972 kg/m³, occurs at 3.98 °C. On freezing it expands by about 9%, reaching 917 kg/m³, which can burst pipes and crack rocks. Because ice floats and insulates the water below, most aquatic organisms survive winter in lakes. This anomalous behavior is also part of the [[Thermohaline circulation|thermohaline circulation]] that distributes heat around the oceans. Water is diamagnetic.

Water covers about 71% of Earth's surface. Seas and [[Ocean|oceans]] hold about 96.5% of the volume, groundwater 1.7%, glaciers and ice caps of Antarctica and Greenland 1.7%, and the air (vapor, clouds and precipitation) 0.001%. It moves continuously through the [[Water cycle|water cycle]] of evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation and runoff, usually reaching the sea.

Water is central to the world economy. Approximately 70% of the fresh water humans use goes to agriculture, and fishing provides 6.5% of global protein. Much long-distance trade moves by boat on seas, rivers, lakes and canals. Water, ice and snow are also used for cooling, heating, industry, cooking and washing, and in sports such as swimming, surfing, diving, ice skating and skiing.

Vertical wheels are further classed by where water strikes their paddles: undershot, overshot, breastshot and pitchback. Tide mills use the movement of the tide. The earliest evidence of a water-driven wheel appears in the treatises of Philo of Byzantium (ca. 280–220 BC), and the Roman engineer [[Vitruvius]] gave the first technical description of a watermill. Watermills alter [[River|river]] dynamics: channels tend to silt up behind them, and where mills have been removed, rivers cut deeper.

ENTRY:
Propagating dynamic disturbance, a change from equilibrium, of one or more quantities in a medium or field. Periodic waves oscillate repeatedly about an equilibrium value at some [[Frequency|frequency]]. Two identical periodic waves traveling in opposite directions superimpose to make a standing wave, which has nulls where the amplitude is small or zero. Viewed macroscopically, a wave is the dynamic response of a [[Field (physics)|field]] to effects that can only propagate at finite speed.

The two types most studied in classical physics are mechanical and electromagnetic waves. In a mechanical wave, stress and strain fields oscillate about a mechanical equilibrium, and the disturbance passes from particle to particle. Examples include [[Sound|sound]] waves, seismic waves generated by an [[Earthquake|earthquake]], surface waves and string vibrations. In an electromagnetic wave such as light, coupling between electric and magnetic fields sustains propagation according to [[Maxwell's equations]]. Electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum; by frequency they are called radio waves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. Other types include [[Gravitational wave|gravitational waves]], disturbances in spacetime, and plasma waves. Mechanical and electromagnetic waves transfer energy, momentum and information.

The simplest wave mathematically is a sinusoidal plane wave, in which the field undergoes [[Simple harmonic motion|simple harmonic motion]] at one frequency. A wave is transverse if the disturbance is perpendicular to the direction of propagation, and longitudinal if aligned with it. Electromagnetic plane waves are strictly transverse; sound waves in fluids are longitudinal. The orientation of the oscillation is the wave's [[Polarization (waves)|polarization]]. Wave speed relates to frequency and wavelength by v = fλ. In quantum mechanics, wave–particle duality means that entities such as electrons show both wave-like and particle-like behavior.
