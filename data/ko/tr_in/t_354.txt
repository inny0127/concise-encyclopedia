ENTRY:
Family of theories in normative ethics that prescribe actions maximizing happiness and well-being. It is a version of [[Consequentialism|consequentialism]], which holds that the consequences of an action alone determine whether it is right or wrong. [[Jeremy Bentham]] founded the modern tradition, describing utility as the capacity of actions to produce pleasure and happiness or to prevent pain. [[John Stuart Mill]] later distinguished higher intellectual pleasures from lower sensual ones. Proponents disagree on whether to judge individual acts by their likely results (act utilitarianism) or to follow rules that maximize utility (rule utilitarianism), and on whether total or average utility should be maximized. The theory has influenced cost-benefit analysis in economics and reforms in law and animal welfare. Critics argue that it can justify violating individual rights when the net gain in utility is large, and that utility is difficult to measure and compare across individuals.

Imagined community or society with highly desirable or near-perfect qualities for its residents. [[Thomas More]] coined the term in 1516 for his book [[Utopia (book)|Utopia]], which describes a fictional island society. The word comes from Greek for "no place," but More noted its similarity to eutopia, "good place," and the two are pronounced alike, which may have caused the shift in meaning. In standard usage it now describes a nonexistent society meant to be seen as far better than contemporary society. Lyman Tower Sargent argues that utopias are inherently contradictory, because societies contain conflicting desires. The opposite of a utopia is a dystopia, which surpassed utopia in popularity in fiction from the 1950s onward, chiefly because of George Orwell's [[Nineteen Eighty-Four]].

As president, he was instrumental in dismantling the Warsaw Pact and in the eastward enlargement of [[NATO]]. His opposition to Slovak independence and his general amnesty for those imprisoned under communism were controversial. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom, and the international airport in [[Prague]] was renamed for him in 2012.

Biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity to a particular infectious or malignant disease. A vaccine typically contains an agent resembling a disease-causing microorganism, often made from weakened or killed forms of the microbe, its toxins, or one of its surface proteins. The agent stimulates the [[Immune system|immune system]] to destroy it and to recognize and destroy associated microorganisms encountered later. Vaccines can be prophylactic, preventing future infection, or therapeutic, fighting an existing disease such as cancer. Giving a vaccine is called [[Vaccination|vaccination]]; the science of developing and producing them is vaccinology.

Inoculation against [[Smallpox|smallpox]] was first recorded in 16th-century China, with earlier hints in the 10th century, and was brought from Turkey to Britain in 1721. In 1796, [[Edward Jenner]] showed that exposure to cowpox protected against smallpox. The terms "vaccine" and "vaccination" come from *Variolae vaccinae* ("smallpox of the cow"), the phrase Jenner used in 1798. In 1881 [[Louis Pasteur]] proposed extending the terms to the new protective inoculations.

Vaccination is the most effective method of preventing infectious diseases and is largely responsible for the eradication of smallpox, declared in 1980, and the restriction of polio, [[Measles|measles]], and tetanus. The [[World Health Organization]] reports licensed vaccines for twenty-five preventable infections. Routine childhood immunizations are credited with preventing an estimated 154 million deaths since 1974. U.S. measles cases fell from 763,094 in 1958, with 552 deaths, to fewer than 150 per year after vaccines were introduced.

Host-related lack of response occurs in an estimated 2–10% of individuals, and adaptive immunity can take 1–2 weeks to develop. Common adverse events are mild, such as injection-site pain and fever; rare serious ones include anaphylaxis (about 1.3 per million doses). The scientific consensus is that vaccines are safe and effective, but debates over mandates, adjuvants, and long-term safety contribute to [[Vaccine hesitancy|vaccine hesitancy]].

ENTRY:
Membrane-bound organelle present in plant, fungal and some protist, animal and bacterial cells. Vacuoles are compartments filled with water containing inorganic and organic molecules, including [[Enzyme|enzymes]], and sometimes engulfed solids. They form by the fusion of membrane vesicles and have no fixed shape or size. [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] described the plant vacuole in 1676. In plant cells the vacuole stores water and maintains turgor pressure, which supports leaves and flowers. Vacuoles also contain waste, maintain an acidic internal pH and export unwanted substances. In seeds, protein-storage vacuoles hold proteins needed for germination. In protists they store absorbed food. In animal cells vacuoles are smaller and more numerous, and they mainly assist exocytosis and endocytosis.
