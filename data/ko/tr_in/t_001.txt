ENTRY:
Two categories of knowledge, justification or argument in [[Epistemology|epistemology]], distinguished by whether they depend on sensory experience. A proposition is known or justified a priori if it can be known independently of any experience beyond what is needed to understand it. It is known a posteriori if its justification depends on empirical evidence. That it is sunny in London today is known a posteriori; that it is either sunny or not sunny there can be known a priori. Mathematics and formal logic rely predominantly on a priori justification, while most sciences involve a posteriori justification. The distinction became prominent through *Critique of Pure Reason* by [[Immanuel Kant]], who related it to the analytic–synthetic distinction. W. V. O. Quine argued that the analytic–synthetic distinction is illegitimate, challenging the analytic explanation of a priori knowledge.
