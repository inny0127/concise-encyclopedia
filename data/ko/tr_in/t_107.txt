ENTRY:
Relation stating that two quantities or expressions have the same value, written A = B. Equality is often treated as a primitive notion, informally described as a relation each thing bears to itself and nothing else. Its reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity were understood in ancient times but were not stated symbolically until the late 19th century, by Giuseppe Peano. The equals sign was introduced by the Welsh mathematician Robert Recorde in *The Whetstone of Witte* (1557). In logic, equality is a primitive predicate with the law of identity and the substitution property. In [[Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory|Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory]], the most common foundation of mathematics, two sets are equal if they have the same members, a principle called the axiom of extensionality.

ENTRY:
Mathematical formula expressing the equality of two expressions by connecting them with the equals sign (=). In English, any well-formed formula of two expressions related by an equals sign counts as an equation.

Solving an equation means determining which values of the variables, called unknowns, make the equality true; those values are its solutions. An identity is true for all values of the [[Variable (mathematics)|variables]], and a conditional equation only for particular values. The most common type is the [[Polynomial|polynomial]] (algebraic) equation, as in Ax² + Bx + C − y = 0. Terms other than the unknowns are called constants, coefficients, or parameters. Unknowns are usually denoted by letters at the end of the alphabet (x, y, z) and parameters by letters at the beginning (a, b, c), as in the general [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equation]] ax² + bx + c = 0. The equation x² + y² = R² is, for R = 2, that of a [[Circle|circle]] of radius 2 about the origin.

An equation is analogous to a balanced weighing scale: whatever is done to one side must be done to the other. Two equations are equivalent if they have the same solutions. Adding or subtracting the same quantity on both sides, and multiplying or dividing both sides by a nonzero quantity, produce equivalent equations. Applying a function to both sides keeps the original solutions but may add extraneous ones: squaring x = 1 gives x² = 1, which introduces x = −1. Such transformations underlie most elementary solving methods and some less elementary ones, such as [[Gaussian elimination]].

The "=" symbol was invented in 1557 by Robert Recorde, who considered that nothing could be more equal than parallel straight lines of the same length.

Process of finding the values of unknown variables that make an equation true. A solution is an assignment of values to the unknowns that makes the equality hold, and the set of all solutions is the solution set. An equation may be solved numerically, admitting only numbers, or symbolically, using expressions. For example, x + y = 2x − 1 is solved for x by x = y + 1. Babylonians used geometric techniques for [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equations]] as early as 2000 BC. In 1824 Niels Henrik Abel proved that no general algebraic formula exists for quintic equations using radicals. Higher-degree equations are often approximated with methods such as [[Newton's method|Newton's method]].

Set of equations describing the motion of a physical system as a function of time. Its variables are usually spatial coordinates and time, and in classical mechanics the functions are defined in [[Euclidean space|Euclidean space]], while in relativity they are defined in curved spaces. If the dynamics of a system is known, its equations of motion are the solutions of the [[Differential equation|differential equations]] describing that dynamics. For constant acceleration, kinematic equations relate final velocity to initial velocity, acceleration and time: v = v₀ + at. Newton's second law expresses acceleration as net force divided by mass, and for variable forces it yields second-order differential equations that are solved numerically or analytically. Advanced formulations include Lagrange's equations and Hamilton's equations.

Circle of latitude at 0 degrees that divides Earth into the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. Halfway between the North and South Poles, it measures about 21,639 nautical miles (40,075 km; 24,902 miles) in circumference. Family of hoofed mammals in the order [[Perissodactyla]], including horses, asses and zebras.
The family evolved in the [[Eocene]] epoch, more than 50 million years ago, from small, multi-toed ancestors into larger, single-toed animals. The oldest known fossils, found in North America and about 54 million years old, belong to fox-sized browsers with three toes on the hind feet and four on the front. Humans domesticated the donkey about 5,000 years ago and the horse about 4,200 years ago.

Genus of vascular plants known as horsetails.
Equisetum is the only living genus in the family Equisetaceae, whose members reproduce by [[Spore|spores]] rather than seeds. The oldest remains of modern species appear in the Early Jurassic. The plants have hollow, jointed, ridged stems, and their leaves are reduced to sheaths at the nodes. Most temperate species die back in winter, while most tropical species are evergreen. Plants typically grow 20–150 cm tall, and some subtropical giant horsetails reportedly grow taller. Spores are borne in cone-like strobili and dispersed by four elaters that respond to moisture. Plants of the World Online accepts 18 species, though some researchers count 25. Silica in the stems historically made them useful for scouring and polishing.

Hypothesis that inertial and gravitational [[Mass|mass]] are equivalent.
The weak form, known for centuries, holds that masses of any composition in free fall follow the same trajectories and land at identical times. [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] found that gravitation is independent of the amount of mass accelerated. [[Isaac Newton|Newton]] compared the periods of pendulums of different materials, found them identical, and inferred that gravitational and inertial mass are the same. In 1907 [[Albert Einstein|Einstein]] introduced an extended form requiring that [[Special relativity|special relativity]] also hold in free fall and that the weak form be valid everywhere. The MICROSCOPE satellite mission (2016–2018) tested for composition-dependent violations of the weak form to 10⁻¹⁵ precision.

ENTRY:
Mathematical relation that is reflexive, symmetric and transitive.
An equivalence relation generalizes [[Equality (mathematics)|equality]]. It partitions a set into disjoint equivalence classes, and two elements are equivalent if and only if they belong to the same class. Reflexivity means every element is equivalent to itself. Symmetry means that if *a* is equivalent to *b*, then *b* is equivalent to *a*. Transitivity means that if *a* is equivalent to *b* and *b* to *c*, then *a* is equivalent to *c*. Examples include numerical equality, similarity and congruence of [[Triangle|triangles]], and congruence modulo *n* on the [[Integer|integers]]. The relation ≥ is reflexive and transitive but not symmetric. "Approximately equal to" is reflexive and symmetric but not transitive, so it is not an equivalence relation.
