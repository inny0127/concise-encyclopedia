Traffic is often classified as heavy motor vehicles, such as cars and trucks, other vehicles such as mopeds and bicycles, and pedestrians. Genre of drama based on human suffering, in which terrible or sorrowful events befall a main character or cast. Traditionally, tragedy aims to produce catharsis, a "pain [that] awakens pleasure," in its audience. The form originated in [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]] about 2,500 years ago, where [[Aeschylus]], [[Sophocles]], and [[Euripides]] wrote the best-known surviving works. Modern dramatists such as [[Samuel Beckett]] and [[Arthur Miller]] applied the form to despair and to the common man.

ENTRY:
Series of connected vehicles that run along a railway track and transport people or freight. The word comes from Old French *trahiner*, from Latin *trahere*, "to pull, to draw."

Trains are typically pulled or pushed by [[Locomotive|locomotives]], though some are self-propelled, such as multiple units or railcars. Most run on steel wheels on steel tracks, whose low friction makes them efficient. Conventional trains run on two rails of a [[Railway track|track]]; monorails use one.

Trains evolved from wagons running on stone wagonways, the earliest built by Babylon c. 2,200 BC. In 1804 British inventor Richard Trevithick built a [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]] that powered the first steam train. The opening of the Stockton and Darlington Railway in 1825 proved the value of steam outside coal mines: [[George Stephenson|George Stephenson]] ran *Locomotion No. 1* on the 40-km (25-mile) line, hauling over 400 passengers at up to 13 km/h (8 mph). Stephenson's *Rocket* followed in 1829, and a stock market bubble known as "Railway Mania" spread across the United Kingdom within a decade.

[[Rapid transit|Rapid transit]] and tram systems were first built in the late 1800s. From the 1920s, accelerating after World War II, diesel and electric locomotives replaced steam. Cars, trucks, highways, and faster airplanes then reduced the market share of trains, and many rail lines were abandoned.

Since the 1970s, governments and environmentalists have promoted rail for its fuel efficiency and lower [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gas]] emissions. [[High-speed rail|High-speed rail]], first built in the 1960s, competes with cars and planes over short to medium distances. [[Maglev|Maglevs]], which float above a guideway by magnetic levitation, have been under development since the 1970s.

ENTRY:
Intentional movement of humans, animals, and goods from one location to another. The term is British English; American English uses transportation.

Modes of transport include air, land (rail and road), water, cable, pipelines, and space. The field divides into infrastructure, vehicles, and operations, and transport enables the trade that is essential to the development of civilizations. Infrastructure consists of fixed installations such as roads, railways, waterways, [[Canal|canals]], and pipelines, together with terminals including [[Airport|airports]], stations, warehouses, and seaports. Means of transport are the facilities used to carry people or cargo: vehicles such as wagons, automobiles, bicycles, buses, trains, trucks, helicopters, watercraft, and aircraft, as well as riding and pack animals.

A journey may use one mode or several, a case called inter-modal or multi-modal transport. Each mode is chosen on the basis of cost, capability, and route. Governments regulate how vehicles are operated, and ownership of infrastructure and operations may be public, private, or a partnership, depending on the country and mode. Freight transport has become focused on containerization, though bulk transport is used for large volumes of durable items.

Human-powered transport, a form of sustainable transport, uses muscle power in walking, running, and swimming, extended by vehicles such as bicycles and wheelchairs. Animal-powered transport uses working animals as mounts or pack animals or to pull sleds and wheeled vehicles, and remains useful in rough terrain.

A [[Fixed-wing aircraft|fixed-wing aircraft]], commonly called an airplane, uses the movement of air over its wings to generate lift. Most need an airport, though some take off and land on ice, snow, or calm water. Remotely piloted [[Unmanned aerial vehicle|unmanned aerial vehicles]] are used for package delivery. Aircraft are the second fastest method of transport after the rocket: commercial jets reach up to 955 km/h (593 mph), piston-powered general aviation aircraft up to 555 km/h (345 mph) or more. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change estimates that a commercial jet flight has 2–4 times the climate effect of the same CO₂ emissions at ground level.

Transport plays an important part in [[Economic growth|economic growth]] and [[Globalization|globalization]], by connecting producers to markets and reducing costs through scale. Railroads during the Industrial Revolution accelerated industrialization, and 20th-century aviation shortened distances for trade and travel. Machine-propelled transport, however, causes [[Air pollution|air pollution]], uses large amounts of land, and relies on fossil fuels; about 28% of U.S. greenhouse gas emissions come from vehicle exhaust and related activities. Governments heavily subsidize transport, and good planning is regarded as essential to keep traffic flowing and restrain urban sprawl. Debate continues over decarbonization through electrification and alternative fuels and over the effectiveness of infrastructure investment.

NP-hard problem in combinatorial optimization. Given cities and the distances between each pair, the traveling salesman problem asks for the shortest route that visits each city exactly once and returns to the origin city. It is one of the most intensively studied problems in optimization and a benchmark for [[Heuristic|heuristics]] and exact algorithms, which can solve instances with tens of thousands of cities; instances with millions of cities can be approximated within a small fraction of 1%.
