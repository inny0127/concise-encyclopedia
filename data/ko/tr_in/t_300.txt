Institution designed to provide learning environments for the teaching of students, usually under the direction of teachers. Most countries have formal [[Education|education]] systems, sometimes compulsory, in which students progress through government or private schools. These generally include primary school for young children and secondary school for teenagers; higher education is commonly taught at a [[University|university]] or college. Private schools may be religious, such as the gurukula (Hindu), madrasa (Arabic), hawza (Shi'i Muslim), and yeshiva (Jewish). Homeschooling and distance education take place outside the school building.

The word derives from Greek *scholē*, originally "leisure." Formal schools have existed at least since ancient Greece, Rome, India, and China. In Western Europe cathedral schools were founded in the early Middle Ages to teach future clergy and administrators; the oldest still operating include The King's School, Canterbury (established AD 597). Denmark-Norway introduced obligatory attendance in 1739–41.

Proponents regard schools as effective means of education. Critics argue that they fail to prepare students for adult life, prescribe what students do and when, and, through grades and homework, inhibit natural curiosity and creativity. Compulsory attendance now lasts from 9 to 13 years in nearly all countries, yet approximately 250 million children remain out of school.

ENTRY:
Systematic discipline that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations about nature and society. It is driven by the [[Scientific method|scientific method]], an empirical cycle that typically involves making observations, producing hypotheses, testing them with evidence, and drawing conclusions. Science encompasses both this process and the body of knowledge it produces, which the scientific community continuously challenges, validates, and organizes.

Modern science is typically divided into two major branches: the [[Natural science|natural sciences]], which study the physical world, and the [[Social science|social sciences]], which study individuals and societies. Logic, mathematics, and theoretical computer science, sometimes called the formal sciences, are typically regarded as separate because they rely on deductive reasoning rather than the scientific method. Applied sciences, such as engineering and medicine, use scientific knowledge for practical purposes.

The word has been used in English since the 14th century in the sense of "the state of knowing," borrowed from Anglo-Norman and ultimately from Latin *scientia*, 'knowledge, awareness, understanding.' Scientists were once called "natural philosophers"; in 1834 William Whewell introduced the term *scientist*, crediting it to "some ingenious gentleman," possibly himself.

Science has no single origin; scientific thinking emerged gradually over tens of thousands of years. Direct evidence becomes clearer with the writing systems of the Bronze Age civilizations of Egypt and Mesopotamia (c. 3000–1200 BC), whose contributions to mathematics, astronomy, and medicine later entered Greek and medieval science. The Egyptians developed a non-positional decimal numbering system, used geometry, and devised a calendar. The Mesopotamians used knowledge of natural chemicals to manufacture pottery, glass, soap, and metals.

In classical antiquity there was no real analogue of the modern scientist; educated, usually upper-class men investigated nature as time allowed. The Milesian school, founded by [[Thales of Miletus]], first attempted to explain natural phenomena without relying on the supernatural. Leucippus and his student Democritus developed the theory of atoms, and Hippocrates established the tradition of systematic medical science. Greek [[Natural philosophy|natural philosophy]] was later advanced during the [[Islamic Golden Age]], which, with the Golden Age of India, contributed the Hindu–Arabic numeral system.

The recovery of Greek works and Islamic inquiries in Western Europe during the Renaissance revived natural philosophy, which the [[Scientific Revolution]], beginning in the 16th century, transformed. In the 19th century the institutional and professional features of science took shape, and "natural philosophy" gave way to "natural science." Contemporary research is often collaborative, carried out in universities, research institutions, government agencies, and companies, though major advances are also credited to individuals and recognized through awards such as the [[Nobel Prize]]. The replication crisis, in which results in fields such as psychology and biomedicine have repeatedly failed to reproduce, has prompted calls for preregistration and open data.

Emergence of modern science in Europe during the 16th and 17th centuries. It was an irreversible break with the natural philosophy that preceded it. The "New Science" departed from Greek conceptions and traditions, was more mechanistic in its worldview and more integrated with mathematics, and focused on acquiring and interpreting new evidence.

The period is frequently said to have begun in 1543 with the printing of *De humani corporis fabrica* by [[Andreas Vesalius]] and *De Revolutionibus* by [[Nicolaus Copernicus]]; other historians propose 1572, when [[Tycho Brahe]] observed a supernova. It is said to have culminated in 1687 with [[Isaac Newton]]'s *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*. Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion, Robert Hooke's *Micrographia* (1665), the founding of the Royal Society of London (1660) and the French Academy of Sciences (1666), and the launch of *Philosophical Transactions* (1665), the first scientific journal, belong to the same period.

Much of the change in attitude is credited to [[Galileo Galilei]], whose telescopic observations provided persuasive evidence for heliocentrism and who developed the science of motion, and to [[Francis Bacon]], whose announcement of scientific progress inspired societies such as the Royal Society.

The revolution built on earlier learning: translations from Greek and Arabic into Latin, begun in the 10th century and accelerating in the 12th and 13th, together with the medieval university, supplied its foundation. Copernicus, Galileo, Kepler, and Newton all studied at universities founded in the High Middle Ages.

The term was popularized by the historian Herbert Butterfield in *Origins of Modern Science*. David Wootton calls the period the most important transformation in human history since the Neolithic Revolution.

Empirical method of acquiring knowledge through careful observation, skepticism, hypothesis testing, and experimental validation. Developed from ancient and medieval practices, it acknowledges that cognitive assumptions can distort the interpretation of observations. It has characterized science since at least the 17th century.

Inquiry typically involves forming a testable [[Hypothesis|hypothesis]] through inductive reasoning, predicting its consequences, testing it through [[Experiment|experiments]] and statistical analysis, and adjusting or discarding it according to the results. A hypothesis must be falsifiable: some possible outcome must conflict with its predictions. Although often presented as a fixed sequence of steps, the method is better described as a set of general principles; not all steps occur in every inquiry or in the same order, and chance has played a role in some discoveries.

Early expressions of [[Empiricism|empiricism]] appear with the ancient Stoics, Aristotle, Epicurus, [[Ibn al-Haytham|Alhazen]], Avicenna, Al-Biruni, Roger Bacon, and William of Ockham. By the 1960s and 1970s, philosophers of science such as Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend had questioned its universality, favoring a view of science as a heterogeneous and local practice.
