ENTRY:
Large platform of glacial ice floating on the ocean, fed by one or more tributary glaciers. Ice shelves form along coastlines where the ice is too thin to displace the denser seawater, and they are found in [[Antarctica]] and the [[Arctic]], including along Greenland, northern Canada and the Russian Arctic. The largest are the Ross Ice Shelf and the Filchner-Ronne Ice Shelf in Antarctica, and the aggregate area of Antarctic ice shelves exceeds 1,550,000 sq km. Shelves range from about 100 to 1,000 m thick and act as brakes on the glaciers that feed them; without them, glaciers may speed up. Glaciologists have observed decreases in ice shelf extent through melt, calving and the complete disintegration of some shelves, such as the Thwaites and Larsen ice shelves.

ENTRY:
Set of ideals, values, or beliefs attributed to a person or group, especially those held for reasons not purely about belief in certain knowledge and in which "practical elements are as prominent as theoretical ones."

It is related to, but distinct from, a [[Worldview|worldview]]. The term was coined in 1796 by the French philosopher Antoine Destutt de Tracy, who proposed a "science of ideas" grounded in sensations and the ideas they produce. He formed the concept while imprisoned during the [[Reign of Terror]], envisioning a liberal philosophy defending individual liberty, property, free markets, and constitutional limits on state power. His *Elements of Ideology* appeared in 1804–15. The word acquired a negative sense when Napoleon described his opponents as "the ideologues," and [[Karl Marx]] adopted that sense, situating ideology within class struggle and domination. Others regarded it as a necessary part of institutional functioning and social integration. By 1907 the English term also meant a comprehensive framework of beliefs through which the world is interpreted, and it has since become largely neutral in the analysis of political opinions and social groups.

Ideologies may be political, social, epistemological, or ethical. Many analysts describe them as coherent systems of ideas resting on a few basic assumptions about reality that may or may not have any factual basis. Manfred Steger and Paul James define them as "patterned clusters of normatively imbued ideas and concepts, including particular representations of power relations." Willard A. The fish ascends rivers for 50–100 km (31–62 mi) to spawn during the southwest monsoons, and it is found in 11 countries, among them Iran, Iraq and Thailand. Bangladesh is the top hilsa-producing country in the world, followed by Myanmar and then India.

Product of a real number and the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]] *i*, which is defined by *i*² = −1. For example, 5*i* is imaginary and its square is −25; zero is considered both real and imaginary. [[René Descartes]] coined the term in the 17th century as a derogatory word for what he regarded as fictitious or useless. The concept gained wide acceptance through the work of [[Leonhard Euler]] in the 18th century and of [[Augustin-Louis Cauchy]] and [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] in the early 19th century. An imaginary number added to a real number forms a [[Complex number|complex number]], *a* + *bi*. Geometrically, imaginary numbers lie on the vertical axis of the complex plane, and multiplying by *i* rotates a point 90 degrees counterclockwise about the origin.
