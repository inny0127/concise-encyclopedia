Subregion of the European continent, a largely ambiguous term with geopolitical, cultural and socio-economic connotations. Its eastern boundary is conventionally drawn along the [[Ural Mountains]]; its western boundary varies, and narrow definitions include Belarus, Russia and Ukraine. Its cultural traditions have been shaped by the Slavs and by [[Eastern Christianity]]. During the Cold War the term denoted the communist states of the [[Eastern Bloc]], separated from the West by the Iron Curtain. The term is sometimes considered pejorative, and Central and Eastern Europe is sometimes used as a more neutral grouping.

Theater of World War II in Europe, fought between the Axis powers and the [[Soviet Union]] from June 22, 1941, when Germany invaded in Operation Barbarossa, until the capture of Berlin in May 1945. Of the estimated 70–85 million deaths attributed to the war, around 30 million occurred on this front. The Battle of Stalingrad, the bloodiest battle of the war, was a key turning point, and the Battle of Kursk crippled German offensive capabilities. Historian Geoffrey Roberts noted that more than 80 percent of all combat in the Second World War took place on the Eastern Front. Nazi military operations included mass deportations, starvation and massacres. The United States and United Kingdom provided substantial material aid to the Soviet Union through Lend-Lease, though they sent no ground troops.

Second-largest Christian body in the world after the [[Catholic Church|Roman Catholic Church]], officially the Orthodox Catholic Church and also known as the Greek Orthodox Church, with an estimated 260 to 300 million adherents.

It is a communion of autocephalous churches, each governed by its bishops through local synods, with no central authority analogous to the [[Pope|pope]]. The Ecumenical Patriarch of [[Constantinople]] is recognized as *primus inter pares* ("first among equals"), a title held by the patriarch of Rome before the [[East–West Schism]]. Its theology rests on the Scriptures and holy tradition, which includes the decrees of the seven ecumenical councils and the teaching of the Church Fathers. The church holds that it is the one, holy, catholic, and apostolic church established by Jesus Christ, with bishops who are successors of the apostles. It recognizes seven major sacraments, called holy mysteries, of which the [[Eucharist]] is the principal one. The Virgin Mary is venerated as the Theotokos ("God-bearer").

The churches of Constantinople, Alexandria, Jerusalem, and Antioch shared communion with the Church of Rome until the schism of 1054, the culmination of theological, political, and cultural disputes, particularly over the authority of the pope. Since 2018 an ongoing schism has divided Constantinople and [[Moscow]], which are not in full communion.

Eastern Orthodoxy is the primary confession in [[Russia]], Ukraine, Romania, Greece, Belarus, Serbia, Bulgaria, Georgia, Moldova, North Macedonia, Cyprus, and Montenegro. Roughly half of its adherents live in post-Eastern Bloc countries, mostly in Russia; communities in North America, Western Europe, and Australia arose through diaspora, conversion, and missionary activity.

Venomous pit viper of the eastern United States, *Agkistrodon contortrix*. Its range runs from southern [[New England|New England]] to the [[Florida|Florida]] Panhandle and west to eastern [[Texas|Texas]] and parts of [[Kansas|Kansas]]. Adults usually measure 50–95 cm (20–37 in) including the tail. Dark brown, hourglass-shaped crossbands on a light reddish-brown or grayish background provide camouflage in leaf litter, and young snakes have green or yellow tail tips that darken within a year. The species favors deciduous and mixed woodlands and hibernates in dens or limestone crevices, often with timber rattlesnakes. It ambushes prey including insects and small mammals. Because it often freezes rather than fleeing, people may step on it, and copperhead bites account for half of the treated snake bites in the United States.

ENTRY:
Collective term, also Asian or Oriental philosophy, for the philosophies that originated in East, Southeast, and South Asia. It includes [[Chinese philosophy]], Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese philosophy, which are dominant in East Asia, and [[Indian philosophy]], which is dominant in South Asia, Southeast Asia, Tibet, Japan, and Mongolia.

Indian philosophy comprises the ancient traditions (*darshana*, "world views") of the Indian subcontinent, including [[Hindu philosophy]], Jain philosophy, and [[Buddhist philosophy]]. Among the earliest surviving philosophical texts are the [[Upanishads]] of the later [[Vedas|Vedic]] period (1000–500 BC). Central concepts include dharma, karma, samsara, moksha, and ahimsa. Indian thinkers developed systems of epistemology and logic and wrote on political philosophy, as in the *Arthashastra* (c. 4th century BC).

Schools are classified as orthodox or heterodox depending on whether they accept the Vedas as a valid source of knowledge, the premises of Brahman and Atman, or an afterlife. The six major orthodox schools are Nyaya, Vaisheshika, Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa, and Vedanta; the five major heterodox schools are Jain, Buddhist, Ajivika, Ajnana, and Carvaka. Samkhya is a dualist tradition holding that the universe consists of *purusha* (consciousness) and *prakriti* (matter); the closely related Yoga school emphasized meditation and liberation and, unlike most Samkhya thinkers, allowed for the existence of God. Buddhism mostly disappeared from India after the Muslim conquest, surviving in the Himalayan regions and south India.

Chinese philosophy arose chiefly in the Zhou dynasty. Along with Indian thought, it flourished in the Axial Age of the 6th to 5th centuries BC, when figures such as [[The Buddha|the Buddha]] and [[Confucius]] articulated paths to self-realization and social harmony.

Umbrella term for cultures and nations, usually including Asia, the Arab world and, in historical contexts, the Mediterranean region. Originally a geographic term for the eastern part of the Old World, it contrasted the civilizations of Asia with those of [[Western Europe|Western Europe]]. The boundary between East and West is cultural and historical rather than geographical: [[Australia|Australia]] and New Zealand, founded as British settler colonies, are usually grouped with the Western world, while the Central Asian states of the former [[Soviet Union|Soviet Union]] are grouped with the East. Western scholarship in the tradition of Orientalism treated the East as one unified sphere covering all of Asia. Countries such as the [[Philippines|Philippines]] may be considered Western in some aspects of culture and politics because of influence from the [[United States|United States]] and Western Europe.

Natural science that studies the interactions between organisms and their environments. It considers organisms at the community, ecosystem, and biosphere levels and overlaps with biogeography, evolutionary biology, genetics, and ethology.

Ecology is a branch of [[Biology|biology]] concerned with the abundance, biomass, and distribution of organisms in their environmental context. It encompasses life processes, interactions, and adaptations; the movement of materials and energy through living communities; the successional development of ecosystems; cooperation, competition, and predation within and between species; and patterns of biodiversity and their effect on ecosystem processes. The term, from Ancient Greek *oîkos* ("house") and *-logía* ("study of"), was coined in 1866 by the German scientist [[Ernst Haeckel]]. The science as it is known today began with a group of American botanists in the 1890s, and concepts of adaptation and [[Natural selection|natural selection]] are cornerstones of modern ecological theory.

To make the field manageable, the biological world is organized into a hierarchy running from organisms to populations, guilds, communities, [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]], [[Biome|biomes]], and the [[Biosphere|biosphere]]. The hierarchy exhibits non-linear behavior: small changes to critical variables, such as the number of nitrogen fixers, can lead to disproportionate and perhaps irreversible changes in the system. The two main subdisciplines differ in paradigm. Population (or community) ecology focuses on the distribution and abundance of organisms, while ecosystem ecology focuses on fluxes of materials and energy. Ecosystems, in turn, vary from tiny to vast: several generations of an aphid population can live and die on a single leaf, and each aphid supports diverse bacterial communities.

Several concepts are central to the field. [[Biodiversity]] describes the diversity of life from genes to ecosystems and includes species, ecosystem, and genetic diversity. A species' [[Habitat|habitat]] is the environment over which it occurs and the type of community that forms there. The niche concept dates to 1917; in 1957 G. Evelyn Hutchinson defined it as the set of biotic and abiotic conditions in which a species can persist and maintain stable population sizes, and it is divided into fundamental and realized niches. Organisms also modify their habitats, from a beaver pond to decaying logs, a process called niche construction. Biomes categorize regions of the Earth's ecosystems mainly by the structure and composition of their vegetation and include tropical rainforest, temperate forests, taiga, tundra, hot desert, and polar desert.

Ecology has practical applications in conservation biology, wetland management, natural resource management, and human ecology. Studies of fishery collapses caused by overexploitation exceeding reproductive rates have informed resource management.

[[Social science]] that studies the production, distribution, and consumption of goods and services. It examines the behavior and interactions of economic agents and how economies work. [[Microeconomics]] analyzes individual agents, such as households, firms, buyers, and sellers, and markets; [[Macroeconomics|macroeconomics]] analyzes economies as systems in which production, distribution, consumption, savings, and investment interact, along with the factors of production (labor, capital, land, and enterprise), [[Inflation|inflation]], economic growth, and public policy. Other broad distinctions are between positive economics, which describes "what is," and normative economics, which advocates "what ought to be"; between economic theory and applied economics; between rational and behavioral economics; and between mainstream and heterodox economics. Economic analysis is applied to business, finance, health care, government, and such subjects as crime, education, the family, law, politics, religion, war, and the environment.

The earlier term was "political economy"; "economics" has been common since the late 19th century. It derives from Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, the "way (*nomos*) to run a household (*oikos*)." Some economic historians have called the Boeotian poet [[Hesiod]] the "first economist" for his writings on the distribution of resources.

Definitions have varied. [[Adam Smith]] (1776) described political economy as "an inquiry into the nature and causes of the wealth of nations," Jean-Baptiste Say (1803) as the science of the production, distribution, and consumption of wealth, and [[John Stuart Mill]] (1844) as the science tracing the laws of social phenomena arising from the combined operations of mankind for the production of wealth. Because it focuses on a form of behavior rather than a subject matter, it allowed economic analysis to extend to areas such as war; from the 1960s, rational-choice modeling, advanced by Gary Becker among others, broadened the field further. Buchanan and Ronald Coase, rejected Robbins's method-based definition in favor of subject-matter definitions like Say's, and Ha-Joon Chang has argued that no other science defines itself by a single method.

Schools of thought include the classical, neoclassical, Keynesian, and Austrian, which offer differing explanations of growth, business cycles, and the effects of policy. Economics informs policy on trade, money, and regulation, and economists continue to debate its predictive record, including its failure to anticipate the 2008 crisis, and the influence of ideology on the field.

Transactions occur when two parties agree on the price of a good or service, commonly expressed in a [[Currency|currency]], though monetary transactions account for only a small part of the economic domain.

The word derives from Middle French *yconomie*, Medieval Latin *oeconomia*, and Ancient Greek *oikonomia*, from *oikos* ("house") and *nemein* ("to manage").

[[Sumer]] developed a large-scale economy based on commodity money, and the [[Babylonia|Babylonians]] and neighboring city-states developed rules and laws on debt, contracts, and private property. In the Middle Ages most exchange occurred within social groups, but voyages by Marco Polo, Christopher Columbus, and Vasco da Gama led to a first global economy. The first [[Stock exchange|stock exchange]] was founded in Antwerp in 1513.

[[Adam Smith]] is regarded as the first economist in the modern sense. He held that goods are offered at a natural price generated by competition, or [[Supply and demand|supply and demand]], and by the division of labor, and that self-interest is the basic motive for free trade. The [[Industrial Revolution]] of the 18th and 19th centuries brought major changes in agriculture, manufacturing, mining, and transport.

Economies are classified as market systems, in which decentralized decisions coordinated by prices allocate resources; command systems, dominated by central planning; and mixed systems. Output is commonly measured by [[Gross domestic product|gross domestic product]] (GDP), though critics note that it overlooks unpaid household labor and environmental degradation.

Community of living organisms together with the nonliving components of their environment, linked through nutrient cycles and energy flows. Ecosystem processes are the transfers of energy and materials from one pool to another and take place across a wide range of scales.

The term was first used in 1935 by the British ecologist Arthur Tansley, who devised the concept to draw attention to transfers of materials between organisms and their environment; it was coined by Arthur Roy Clapham at Tansley's request.

Ecosystems are controlled by external and internal factors. External factors, including [[Climate|climate]], control an ecosystem's structure but are not influenced by it; climate determines the [[Biome|biome]] in which an ecosystem is embedded. Internal factors, including [[Decomposition|decomposition]], the species present, disturbance, and succession, both control and are controlled by ecosystem processes. An ecosystem's tendency to remain near equilibrium is termed resistance, and its capacity to absorb disturbance and reorganize while retaining the same function, structure, and identity is termed ecological resilience.

Plants bring energy into the system through [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]]; animals move matter and energy by feeding on plants and one another; and decomposers return carbon to the atmosphere and make nutrients available again to plants and microbes.

Ecosystems provide goods such as water, food, fuel, and medicinal plants, and services such as cleaning air and water, maintaining atmospheric oxygen, and crop [[Pollination|pollination]]. Many are degraded by human impacts including soil loss, [[Pollution|pollution]], habitat fragmentation, and [[Invasive species|invasive species]]; an ecosystem that has lost its defining features is considered "collapsed." Restoration can contribute to achieving the [[Sustainable development|Sustainable Development]] Goals.
