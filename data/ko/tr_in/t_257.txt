ENTRY:
Form of [[Intellectual property|intellectual property]] giving its owner the legal right to exclude others from making, using, or selling an invention for a limited period. In exchange, the owner publishes an enabling disclosure of the invention, so society receives the invention rather than secrecy. In most countries patent rights fall under private law, so the holder must sue infringers to enforce them. Under the [[World Trade Organization]]'s TRIPS Agreement, patents should be available for new inventions that involve an inventive step and are capable of industrial application, for a minimum term of twenty years. The word derives from Latin *patere*, "to lay open." The first statutory patent system is generally regarded as the [[Republic of Venice|Venetian]] Patent Statute of 1474. In the [[United States]], the first patent statute dates from 1790. Critics argue that overbroad grants can impede cumulative progress.

ENTRY:
Study of disease, especially its diagnosis through examination of [[Tissue (biology)|tissues]] and cells. In modern medicine the term usually refers to general pathology, a field of specialties that analyze samples for diagnosis and research. A physician who practices it is called a pathologist. General pathology has two major specialties, anatomical pathology and clinical pathology, which are further divided by sample type, organ, or purpose, as with forensic pathology. The field examines the cause of disease, its development (pathogenesis), structural changes in cells, and clinical manifestations. Study of disease dates to antiquity, and [[Hippocrates]] developed methods of diagnosis and prognosis in ancient Greece. In the 17th century, examination of tissue led [[Robert Hooke]] to coin the word "[[Cell (biology)|cell]]." Modern pathology emerged as a distinct field in the 19th century.

He was elected patriarch in 1652 and served until 1666. As patriarch he introduced liturgical reforms, including standardized service books and the three-finger sign of the cross, that were unpopular with conservatives. In December 1667 a church synod deprived him of his priestly functions and reduced him to the rank of simple monk.

Feeling of love, devotion and attachment to one's country or state. It may combine attachment to a homeland's language, ethnicity, culture, politics and history, and it is closely related to civic [[Nationalism|nationalism]]. In the 19th century patriotism and nationalism had similar meanings, but nationalism has since taken on negative connotations. [[George Orwell]] contrasted the two in *Notes on Nationalism*, describing patriotism as defensive and nationalism as inseparable from the desire for power. Among Enlightenment thinkers, [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] was an influential proponent of patriotism, while [[Samuel Johnson]] wrote in 1774 that patriotism is "the last refuge of the scoundrel." Johnson's remark is usually read as an attack on patriotism itself, though some argue he targeted false patriotism. Many patriotic people take pride in a distinct common culture and encourage [[Cultural assimilation|cultural assimilation]] by others, while civic forms of patriotism tend to de-emphasize ethnic culture.

ENTRY:
Quantum mechanics principle barring identical fermions from sharing a quantum state. Formulated by Austrian physicist [[Wolfgang Pauli]] in 1925 for electrons, and extended to all fermions through his spin–statistics theorem of 1940, it holds that identical particles with half-integer spin cannot occupy the same quantum state. For electrons in an atom, no two can have the same values of all four quantum numbers: principal, azimuthal, magnetic and spin. This restriction explains how electron shells fill and underlies the structure of the [[Periodic table]], which limits each orbital to two electrons with opposite spins. It also underpins the stability of everyday matter. Bosons, particles with integer spin such as [[Photon|photons]], are not subject to the principle.
