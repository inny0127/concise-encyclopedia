ENTRY:
Distance over which a wave's shape repeats. In physics and mathematics, wavelength is the distance between consecutive corresponding points of the same phase, such as adjacent crests, troughs or zero crossings. It is commonly designated by the Greek letter λ and applies to both traveling and standing waves. For a sinusoidal wave at fixed speed, wavelength is inversely proportional to [[Frequency|frequency]]. [[Electromagnetic radiation]] in free space travels at about 3×10⁸ m/s, so a 100 MHz radio wave has a wavelength of about 3 m. Visible light ranges from roughly 700 nm in deep red to 400 nm in [[Violet (color)|violet]]. The [[Speed of sound]] in air is 343 m/s, so audible sound, from 20 Hz to 20 kHz, has wavelengths between about 17 m and 17 mm. In a standing wave, the wavelength is twice the distance between nodes.

ENTRY:
One of the four known fundamental interactions, acting between subatomic particles. The others are electromagnetism, the strong interaction and gravitation. The weak interaction, also called the weak force or weak nuclear force, is responsible for the radioactive [[Beta decay|beta decay]] of atoms and participates in nuclear fission and [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]]. First suggested in 1933, it reached its current form in the 1950s and 1960s, and Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics for electroweak theory.

In the [[Standard Model]], the weak interaction has three carriers, the W⁺, W⁻ and Z bosons ([[W and Z bosons]]), which were not discovered until 1983 at [[CERN]]. Their large mass, approximately 90 GeV/c², makes them short-lived and gives the force a very short effective range of around 10⁻¹⁷ to 10⁻¹⁶ m, less than the diameter of a proton. The force is termed weak because its field strength over a set distance is typically several orders of magnitude less than that of electromagnetism; its coupling constant lies between 10⁻⁷ and 10⁻⁶, against about 10⁻² for electromagnetism. The carriers' masses are explained in the Standard Model by the [[Higgs boson|Higgs mechanism]].

The weak interaction is unique in several respects. It is the only interaction that changes the flavor of [[Quark|quarks]] and leptons and the only one that violates parity symmetry. In beta-minus decay, a down quark in a neutron changes into an up quark, converting the neutron to a proton and emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. This conversion makes the weak interaction important in the fusion of hydrogen into helium in stars. Neutrinos interact only through gravity and the weak interaction.

Any implement or tool used to deter, threaten, inflict physical damage on, harm or kill. Weapons serve hunting, crime, law enforcement, self-defense and warfare. Ordinary objects such as rocks and bottles can serve as weapons, but many objects are designed for the purpose, from clubs and swords to firearms, tanks, artillery, missiles, chemical, biological and [[Nuclear weapon|nuclear weapons]]. In a broader sense a weapon may be anything used to gain a tactical, strategic, material or mental advantage over an adversary.

The introduction of [[Gunpowder|gunpowder]] from Asia at the end of the period revolutionized warfare: musketeers protected by pikemen came to dominate open battles, and the cannon replaced the [[Trebuchet|trebuchet]] as the dominant siege weapon. Firearms differ from earlier weapons in releasing energy from combustible propellants rather than from a counterweight or spring.

ENTRY:
State of the Earth's atmosphere at a specific place and time, described in terms of temperature, humidity, cloud cover and stability. On Earth most weather occurs in the troposphere, the lowest layer of the atmosphere. Weather refers to day-to-day atmospheric conditions, whereas [[Climate|climate]] is their average over longer periods.

Weather is driven by differences in air pressure, temperature and moisture from place to place. These differences arise partly from the Sun's angle, which varies with latitude. The strong temperature contrast between polar and tropical air gives rise to the largest atmospheric circulations: the [[Hadley cell]], the Ferrel cell, the polar cell and the [[Jet stream|jet stream]]. Mid-latitude systems such as [[Extratropical cyclone|extratropical cyclones]] are caused by instabilities of the jet stream flow. Because Earth's axis is tilted relative to the ecliptic, sunlight arrives at different angles through the year, producing seasons, and annual surface temperatures usually range ±40 °C. Over thousands of years, changes in Earth's orbit affect the solar energy received and influence long-term climate ([[Milankovitch cycles]]). Surface temperature differences create pressure differences, and air moving from higher to lower pressure forms wind.

Weather forecasting applies science and technology to predict the state of the atmosphere at a future time and place. Numerical weather prediction, which began in the mid-20th century, has raised short-term reliability to approximately 90% accuracy for five-day outlooks, but the weather system is chaotic: small changes can grow into large effects, which limits predictability beyond about two weeks.

Jupiter's [[Great Red Spot]] is an anticyclonic storm known to have existed for at least 300 years.

Application of science and technology to predict atmospheric conditions for a given location and time. People attempted informal weather prediction for thousands of years, and formal prediction since the 19th century. The Babylonians predicted weather from cloud patterns and [[Astrology|astrology]] as early as 650 BC. Forecasts are made by collecting data on the current state of the atmosphere, land and ocean and applying [[Meteorology|meteorology]] to project changes. ENTRY:
Boundary separating [[Air mass|air masses]] that differ in density, wind, temperature and [[Humidity|humidity]]. Disturbed, unstable weather often develops along it. Cold fronts can bring bands of [[Thunderstorm|thunderstorms]] and cumulonimbus [[Precipitation|precipitation]] or be preceded by squall lines, while warm fronts are usually preceded by stratiform precipitation and [[Fog|fog]]. Some fronts produce no precipitation and little cloudiness, though there is invariably a wind shift. Cold fronts generally move from west to east and warm fronts poleward, although any direction is possible. Where the density contrast has diminished, for example after air flows over a uniformly warm ocean, a front can degenerate into a shear line, separating regions of differing wind velocity.

Deterioration of [[Soil|soils]], rocks and [[Mineral|minerals]] through contact with water, atmospheric gases, sunlight and living organisms. Weathering occurs in situ, with little or no movement, and is distinct from [[Erosion|erosion]], which transports material by water, ice, wind, waves and gravity. Physical weathering breaks rock apart mechanically through heat, water, ice and wind, while chemical weathering involves reactions with water, atmospheric gases and biologically produced chemicals; water is the principal agent in both. Method of [[Textile|textile]] production in which two sets of [[Yarn|yarns]] are interlaced at right angles to form cloth. Cloth is usually woven on a [[Loom|loom]], which holds the warp taut while the weft is passed through it. Weaving repeats three primary motions of the loom: shedding, which separates the warp threads to form a clear space called the shed; picking, which propels the weft across; and beating-up, which pushes the weft against the fell of the cloth with a reed. Secondary motions let off the warp and take up the fabric at a regulated rate, and stop motions halt the loom if a thread breaks.

Fabrication process that joins materials, usually [[Metal|metals]] or thermoplastics, by melting the parts so they fuse as they cool. Unlike [[Soldering|soldering]] and brazing, which do not melt the base metal, welding typically adds filler material to form a weld pool. Shielding protects the molten metal from contamination and oxidation. Heat sources include a gas flame, an electric arc, a [[Laser|laser]], an electron beam, friction and [[Ultrasound|ultrasound]]. ENTRY:
Government support intended to ensure that members of a society can meet basic human needs such as food and shelter. Social security may be synonymous with welfare, or refer specifically to social insurance programs, which support only prior contributors (for example pensions), as opposed to social assistance, given on the basis of need alone. More broadly, welfare can include subsidized healthcare, education, vocational training and public housing. In a [[Welfare state|welfare state]], the state assumes responsibility for such services. Articles 22 and 25 of the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]] assert a right to social security and an adequate standard of living.

Some historians view codified almsgiving, such as the zakat policy of the Rashidun caliph [[Umar]] (634), as an early example of universal government welfare. Welfare existed under Urukagina of [[Mesopotamia]]. In the Roman Empire, [[Augustus]] provided a monthly grain dole, the *Cura Annonae*, for citizens unable to afford food. The [[Song dynasty]] government supported state hospitals, orphanages and free pharmacies. One study called the 18th-century Qing relief system, based on granaries used to stabilize food prices, "the most elaborate relief system in world history."

From the 14th century Italian city governments partnered with the church to provide welfare and education. In 1523 Martin Luther devised a "Common Chest" plan for Leisnig, Saxony, and free imperial cities of the [[Holy Roman Empire]] managed welfare through city governments.

The first welfare state was Imperial Germany (1871–1918), where the Bismarck government introduced social security in 1889. The United Kingdom introduced it around 1913 and adopted the welfare state with the National Insurance Act 1946, under the Attlee government (1944–1951).

ENTRY:
Form of government in which the state, or a network of social institutions, protects and promotes the [[Well-being|well-being]] of its citizens. Welfare states rest on equal opportunity, equitable distribution of wealth and public responsibility for citizens unable to avail themselves of minimal provisions for a good life. Early features, such as public [[Pension|pensions]] and social insurance, developed from the 1880s in industrializing Western countries. Emperor [[Ashoka]] of India put forward an idea of a welfare state in the 3rd century BCE. The term was popularized during World War II by Anglican archbishop William Temple, and the fullest forms were developed after [[World War II]]. Comparisons of income before and after [[Tax|taxes]] and transfers show reductions in relative poverty of 20–28 percentage points in high-spending nations such as Finland and France. Critics argue that welfare systems can distort labor markets, foster dependency and impede economic growth.

Excavation or structure made by digging, driving or drilling to access liquid resources, usually water. The oldest and most common kind is the water well, which taps [[Groundwater|groundwater]] in underground [[Aquifer|aquifers]] and is drawn up by a pump or by containers raised by hand. Wells were first constructed at least eight thousand years ago, and linings of wood or wickerwork date back at least to the Iron Age. Traditional dug wells are still sunk by hand in rural areas of the developing world, often lined with brick or stone. Deeper wells are [[Drilling|drilled]] and usually cased with [[Steel|steel]] or [[Plastic|plastic]] pipe, reaching much greater depths than dug wells. Well water typically contains more dissolved minerals than surface water and may require treatment before it is potable. Soil salination can occur as the water table falls, and methane can seep into the water.

Measure of how well a person's life is going, also called welfare or quality of life. Well-being is what is intrinsically good for a person, and it contrasts with ill-being, which denotes negative conditions. Researchers distinguish subjective well-being, how a person feels about and evaluates their life, from objective well-being, which includes [[Health|health]], income and security. [[Hedonism]] holds that well-being depends on the balance of pleasure over pain, desire theories locate it in satisfied desires, and objective list theories combine diverse elements. Often discussed factors include [[Emotion|emotions]], life satisfaction, achievement, a sense of meaning, [[Interpersonal relationship|interpersonal relationships]] and health. It is studied in [[Philosophy|philosophy]], economics, psychology, sociology and medicine. Empirical research has intensified only since the second half of the 20th century.
