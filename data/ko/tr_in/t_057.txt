Class of yellow, orange or red organic pigments. Carotenoids are produced by plants, algae, several bacteria, archaea and fungi. More than 1,100 carotenoids have been identified, falling into two classes: xanthophylls, which contain oxygen, and carotenes, which are purely hydrocarbons. All are tetraterpenoids made from eight isoprene units, with 40 carbon atoms. They absorb light at wavelengths of 400 to 550 nm, which produces their color. Carotenoids are the dominant pigment in the autumn leaf coloration of about 15–30% of tree species.

Genus of ants (*Camponotus*) found nearly worldwide except in Antarctica and a few islands. It is the most species-rich ant genus, with over 1,500 described species. Although all are commonly called carpenter ants, only a few members nest in wood. They chew galleries out of dead, damp wood and discard a sawdust-like material rather than eating the wood. Their tunneling is a major cause of structural damage to wooden buildings, although it also aids forest [[Decomposition|decomposition]]. Colonies often include a central parent colony surrounded by smaller satellite colonies.

Coordinate system using perpendicular axes, named for René Descartes. Each point in a plane is specified by two real-number coordinates, the signed distances from two fixed perpendicular axes that meet at the origin, (0, 0). The common name derives from the 16th-century French or Portuguese *cassave*, from the [[Taíno]] *caçabi*.

Class of iron–carbon [[Alloy|alloys]] containing more than 2% carbon and about 1–3% silicon. Fixed social group into which an individual is born, within a system of [[Social stratification|social stratification]]. Members are expected to marry within the group (endogamy), often follow lifestyles linked to an occupation, and hold a ritual status within a hierarchy. ENTRY:
Fortified residence built during the [[Middle Ages]] mainly by [[Nobility|nobility]] or royalty. Scholars usually define a castle as the private fortified residence of a lord, distinct from a palace or villa built mainly for pleasure. European castles originated in the 9th and 10th centuries, after the [[Carolingian Empire]] fragmented among lords and princes who used them to control their surroundings and launch raids. Early castles relied on a central keep; in the late 12th and early 13th centuries, towers and [[Fortification|concentric defense]] emerged, partly from [[Crusades]] technology. Although [[Gunpowder]] reached Europe in the 14th century, castle building was affected only when [[Artillery|artillery]] could break stone walls in the 15th century, and true castles then declined. The word derives from Latin *castellum* and entered English shortly before the [[Norman Conquest]] of 1066.
