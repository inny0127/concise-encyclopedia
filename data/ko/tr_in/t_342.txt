Large cat of the genus *Panthera* native to Asia. The tiger (*Panthera tigris*) is the largest living member of the [[Felidae|cat family]], with a powerful, muscular body and orange fur marked with black, mostly vertical stripes. It inhabits mainly forests, from coniferous and temperate broadleaf forests in the Russian Far East and northeastern China to tropical forests in India and Southeast Asia. An [[Apex predator|apex predator]], it hunts mainly [[Ungulate|hooved mammals]] by ambush and lives a mostly solitary life. Females usually bear two or three cubs. Since the early 20th century tigers have lost at least 93% of their historic range, and the IUCN lists the species as Endangered. Major threats include [[Deforestation|deforestation]] and poaching for fur and for body parts used in traditional medicine. Tigers have featured prominently in the [[Folklore|folklore]] of cultures across their range.

Large ground shark, the only living member of the genus *Galeocerdo*. Females can exceed 5 m (16 ft 5 in), and the largest males reach about 4 m. Its dark stripes, which fade with age, resemble a tiger's pattern. The solitary, mostly nocturnal tiger shark has the widest food spectrum of any shark, eating [[Crustacean|crustaceans]], fish, [[Sea turtle|sea turtles]], [[Squid|squid]], [[Dolphin|dolphins]] and smaller sharks. It is known as a "garbage eater" because it swallows inedible objects, and its only recorded natural predator is the [[Orca|orca]]. King of Armenia, of the Artaxiad dynasty, reigning from 95 to 55 BC. Under Tigranes the [[Armenia|Armenian]] kingdom expanded beyond its traditional boundaries and reached its peak, and he took the title King of Kings. Roman forces under Lucullus defeated him at Tigranocerta in 69 BC and at Artaxata a year later. In 66 BC [[Pompey]] invaded; Tigranes surrendered, kept the heartland of his kingdom as a Roman buffer state, and lost his conquests.

Modern threats include geopolitical instability, dam projects, poor water management and climate change.

Ruined ancient [[Maya civilization|Maya]] city in the Petén Department of northern Guatemala. Tikal, likely called Yax Mutal in antiquity, was one of the largest Maya urban centers and the capital of one of its most powerful kingdoms. Monumental architecture dates to the 4th century BC, but the city peaked in the Classic Period (c. 200 to 900). It dominated much of the Maya region and interacted with [[Teotihuacan]] in the Valley of Mexico, and a ruling dynasty was founded by conquerors from Teotihuacan in the 4th century AD. Its long dynastic ruler list has helped archaeologists locate many royal tombs. The city was abandoned by the end of the 10th century after elite palaces were burned and the population declined. The mapped site covers more than 16 square km and includes about 3,000 structures on [[Limestone|limestone]] ridges. It has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1979.

They are important in artisanal fishing in Africa and in [[Aquaculture|aquaculture]]. Tilapia are mouth-brooders that carry their eggs and young in the mouth for several days. Agricultural preparation of [[Soil|soil]] by mechanical agitation, such as digging, stirring and overturning. Tillage can be done by hand with tools such as [[Hoe (tool)|hoes]] and shovels, or by draft animals and machines pulling [[Plough|plows]] and [[Harrow (tool)|harrows]]. Primary tillage, such as plowing, loosens the soil and mixes in fertilizer or plant material, leaving a rough surface. Secondary tillage produces finer soil, shapes the seedbed and controls weeds, which are uprooted or buried. Plows have been pulled by people, [[Ox|oxen]], mules and water buffalo, and the earliest evidence of plow use dates to around 4000 BCE in Mesopotamia. Mechanized [[Tractor|tractors]] enabled deeper soil inversion, but excessive plowing of native grasslands contributed to the [[Dust Bowl]] of the 1930s in the United States. Conservation tillage, which disturbs less of the soil surface and keeps crop residue, covered about 72% of U.S. cropland in 2017, helping to reduce [[Erosion|erosion]].

Continuous progression of existence in an apparently irreversible succession from the past, through the present, and into the future. Time sequences events, compares their durations or the intervals between them, and quantifies rates of change in material reality or conscious experience. It is often called the fourth dimension, in addition to the three spatial dimensions. Time has been a central subject of study in religion, philosophy, and science, and temporal measurement has been a prime motivation in navigation and astronomy.

Defining time without circularity has eluded scholars. Traditional definitions relied on periodic motion such as the sun's apparent path, the phases of the moon, and the swing of a pendulum. In physics, time is a fundamental concept used to define quantities such as velocity and is operationally defined as "what a clock reads," a count of repeating events such as the SI second. Physicists developed the concept of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], in which events have four coordinates, three for space and one for time. Under [[General relativity|general relativity]] time can be dilated by velocity and matter, though the effect is negligible outside extreme conditions such as relativistic speeds or the gravity of black holes. Measurable time is believed to have begun with the [[Big Bang]] 13.8 billion years ago. Reconciling general relativity with quantum mechanics, in which time is a universal and absolute parameter, is called the problem of time, and no generally accepted theory of quantum general relativity exists.

Measurement takes two forms: the [[Calendar|calendar]], used for periods longer than a day, and the clock, used for shorter periods. The [[Second|second]], the SI base unit, is defined by the electronic transition frequency of caesium atoms. Artifacts from the Paleolithic suggest the moon was used to reckon time at least 6,000 years ago. Lunar calendars had years of 12 or 13 lunar months (354 or 384 days), and lunisolar calendars add a thirteenth month to some years. The Maya developed interrelated calendars, including a 260-day sacred calendar, the Tzolk'in. The reforms of [[Julius Caesar]] in 45 BC put the Roman world on a solar calendar, which Pope Gregory XIII corrected in 1582; the [[Gregorian calendar]] is now the most commonly used worldwide. The French Republican Calendar, with ten hours of a hundred minutes of a hundred seconds in each day, was abolished in 1806. Egyptians are credited with dividing the day into smaller parts using the [[Sundial|sundial]]; obelisks used as gnomons were built as early as c. 3500 BC, and a device dating to c. 1500 BC also measured time by shadow.

Turco-Mongol conqueror, first ruler of the Timurid dynasty and founder of the [[Timurid Empire]], which ruled over modern-day Afghanistan, Iran, and Central Asia. Also known as Tamerlane, Timur was born into the Turkicized Mongol confederation of the Barlas in Transoxiana. By 1370 he controlled the western Chagatai Khanate, and from there led campaigns that defeated the Khans of the [[Golden Horde]], the [[Mamluk Sultanate]] in Egypt and Syria, the emerging [[Ottoman Empire]], and the [[Delhi Sultanate]] in the Indian subcontinent. He made [[Samarkand]] his capital.

His origins are debated. Historian Peter Jackson considers it generally accepted that Timur was of "unimpeachable Mongol ancestry," and Timurid histories trace his paternal descent to a male-line ancestor of [[Genghis Khan]]. No written evidence from Timur's lifetime or earlier, however, makes such a link to him directly. He sought to revive Genghis Khan's legacy and saw himself as restorer of the Mongol Empire, and by the end of his reign he controlled the remnants of the Chagatai Khanate, the Ilkhanate, and the Golden Horde and had even attempted to restore the Yuan dynasty in China.

Timur's armies were multi-ethnic and much feared, and his campaigns laid waste to large parts of Asia, Africa, and Europe. Scholars estimate that they caused the deaths of millions of people. He also patronized scholars and poets such as Ibn Khaldun and Hafez, and his reign led to the Timurid Renaissance. He died while on the way to invade Ming China, and his empire fragmented shortly afterward. He was the grandfather of [[Ulugh Beg]], an astronomer and mathematician who ruled Central Asia from 1411 to 1449, and the great-great-great-grandfather of Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire.

Soft, silvery-white [[Metal|metal]] with the symbol Sn and atomic number 50. Tin is a post-transition metal in group 14 of the [[Periodic table]], obtained chiefly from the [[Mineral|mineral]] cassiterite. It is malleable and bends by hand, producing a "tin cry" from the twinning of its crystals. Tin has the largest number of stable [[Isotope|isotopes]] in the periodic table, 10 in all. Perception of ringing or other sounds with no corresponding external source. The word comes from [[Latin]] *tinnire*, "to ring." It affects about 10–15% of people, but only 1–2% find it a severe problem. Tinnitus is a symptom rather than a disease, most often caused by hearing damage or age-related [[Hearing loss|hearing loss]], and it can also result from ear infections or [[Brain tumor|brain tumors]].
