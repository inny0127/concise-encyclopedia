Pseudoscientific system of [[Alternative medicine|alternative medicine]], conceived in 1796 by German physician Samuel Hahnemann. Its practitioners believe that a substance causing symptoms in healthy people can cure similar symptoms in the sick. Ancient Greek poet traditionally credited as the author of the [[Iliad|*Iliad*]] and the [[Odyssey|*Odyssey*]], two epic poems foundational to [[Ancient Greek literature]]. The *Iliad* centers on a quarrel between King Agamemnon and the warrior Achilles during the last year of the [[Trojan War]]. The *Odyssey* chronicles the ten-year journey of Odysseus, king of Ithaca, back home after the fall of [[Troy]]. Most researchers believe they were originally transmitted orally, and they are thought to have been composed around the late eighth or early seventh century BC.

Homer's identity and existence, the "Homeric Question," remain debated, and scholars generally regard the ancient conception of a single author as a fictional narrative. Many accounts of his life circulated in antiquity, the most widespread portraying him as a blind bard from Ionia, a region on the western coast of Anatolia, or from the islands of Chios and Samos. Other traditions named Smyrna, Argos, and Athens as his birthplace. Modern scholars consider these accounts apocryphal or "legendary." In antiquity many other works were attributed to him, including the Homeric Hymns, but these claims are not considered authentic today.

The poems shaped Greek culture and education, fostering ideals of heroism, glory, and honor. [[Plato]] described Homer as the one who "has educated Greece." In Dante's *Divine Comedy*, [[Virgil]] calls him "Poet sovereign," and [[Alexander Pope]] acknowledged in the preface to his translation of the *Iliad* that Homer has always been considered the "greatest of poets." The epics have inspired many works of literature, music, art, and film.

Tasks assigned to students by teachers to be completed at home. Common assignments include required reading, writing projects, math problems and review before tests. Its effects are debated. Homework has not been shown to improve academic performance among young children, though proponents claim it builds good study habits. Among teenagers, more homework generally goes with higher grades, but large amounts of homework worsen performance, even among older students. Act by which one person causes the death of another. Homicide requires only a volitional act, or an omission, that causes death, so it may result from accidental, reckless, or negligent acts even without intent to cause harm. Its overlapping legal categories include [[Murder|murder]], manslaughter, justifiable homicide, [[Assassination|assassination]], killing in [[War|war]] (lawful or as a war crime), [[Euthanasia|euthanasia]], and [[Capital punishment|capital punishment]].

Criminal homicide is divided into murder and manslaughter according to the perpetrator's state of mind and intent. Murder is the most serious charge. First-degree murder is the premeditated, unlawful, intentional killing of another person; second-degree murder is intentional and unlawful but without premeditation. In the United States, under the felony murder rule, a person committing a felony may be guilty of murder if someone dies as a result, regardless of intent. Manslaughter is voluntary, an intentional but unpremeditated killing in heat of passion, or involuntary, an unintentional killing through recklessness or negligence. The distinction between murder and manslaughter is sometimes said to have first been made by the Athenian lawmaker [[Draco (legislator)|Draco]] in the 7th century BC.

Not all homicides are prosecutable. Common defenses and excuses include self-defense, [[Mental disorder|mental incapacity]], the defense of infancy, and justifiable homicide, such as a lawful killing in battle.

A report by the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime in July 2019 documented nearly 464,000 homicide deaths worldwide in 2017, against 89,000 killed in armed conflicts. Another estimate gives approximately 458,000 in 2021, with the [[Americas]] showing the highest rates.

Algebraic structure associated with a [[Topological space|topological space]]. The term, originally introduced in [[Algebraic topology]], has three closely related usages. The first is the homology of a chain complex, a sequence of [[Abelian group|abelian groups]] whose homology groups are regarded as fundamental invariants of the complex. Homology theories associate a chain complex to another mathematical object and take its homology, examples being singular homology, Morse homology and Hochschild homology. Under suitable conditions, distinct homology theories applied to one topological space give the same groups, allowing a single homology of that space to be defined. This notion is closely related to ideas such as the holes of a surface or the cycles of a graph. The nth homology group is the quotient of cycles by boundaries, and [[Homological algebra]] relates the homology groups of different chain complexes.

ENTRY:
Quality of worthiness and respectability, expressed as a code of conduct. Honor is both a social teaching and a personal ethos, with elements such as valor, [[Chivalry|chivalry]], honesty and compassion. It affects the social standing and self-evaluation of an individual or institution, such as a family, school, regiment or nation. In his 1755 dictionary, [[Samuel Johnson]] defined honor first as nobility of soul, magnanimity and a scorn of meanness. Honor is distinguished from [[Dignity|dignity]], which is measured against conscience rather than community judgment. In the early medieval period, a lord's honor was the group of manors or lands he held. Honor has declined in importance in the modern West, where [[Conscience|conscience]] and the [[Rule of law|rule of law]] have largely replaced it. It remains important in the [[Military|military]] and in [[Scouting]] organizations, which hold courts of honor.

ENTRY:
Empirical law of physics relating the force needed to stretch or compress a [[Spring (device)|spring]] to its displacement, written F = kx, where k is the spring's [[Stiffness|stiffness]]. The law is named after 17th-century British physicist [[Robert Hooke]], who first stated it in 1676 as a Latin anagram and published its solution in 1678 as *ut tensio, sic vis* ("as the extension, so the force"). Hooke stated that he had known the law since 1660. In the modern theory of elasticity, strain is proportional to [[Stress (mechanics)|stress]], and the constant becomes a linear map, or [[Tensor|tensor]], represented by a [[Matrix (mathematics)|matrix]] of numbers. The law is a first-order approximation that holds only up to a limit, beyond which materials deform permanently. It is the principle behind the spring scale and the balance wheel of the mechanical [[Clock|clock]].

Colorful [[Bird|bird]] of the monotypic family Upupidae, found across Africa, Asia, and Europe and known for the crown of feathers it can raise or lower at will. Two living species are recognized, though some authorities treat them as one or recognize three, and the Saint Helena hoopoe became extinct in the 16th century, presumably owing to introduced species. The genus *Upupa* was introduced by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758. Both the Latin *Upupa* and the [[Ancient Greek]] *epops* imitate the bird's cry. Most European and north Asian hoopoes migrate to the tropics in winter, while African populations are sedentary. The common hoopoe is rated Least Concern on the IUCN Red List, but its numbers are declining in Western Europe. It needs bare or lightly vegetated ground for foraging and vertical cavities, such as in trees, cliffs, or walls, for nesting.
