ENTRY:
English king (r. 1199–1216). Youngest son of [[Henry II of England|Henry II]] and [[Eleanor of Aquitaine]], John was nicknamed Lackland for his expected lack of inheritance. He became king on the death of his brother Richard I in 1199, and [[Philip II of France|Philip II]] recognized his possession of the continental Angevin lands in 1200. Renewed war led to the loss of [[Normandy]] and most of his French lands by 1204, ending the Angevin Empire. A dispute with [[Pope Innocent III]] over the archbishop of Canterbury brought an interdict on England and John's excommunication. A baronial revolt forced him to seal [[Magna Carta]] in 1215, but neither side kept its terms, and civil war followed. John died of [[Dysentery|dysentery]] in late 1216. Chroniclers were largely critical of him, and historians have debated his record. His reign inspired Victorian fiction, and he remains a villain in [[Robin Hood]] legends.

Scottish Reformed minister and theologian. Born in Haddington and ordained a Catholic priest, Knox was influenced by George Wishart and was taken prisoner by French forces in 1547. His five-volume *The History of the Reformation in Scotland* was written between 1559 and 1566.

ENTRY:
English philosopher and physician, among the most influential thinkers of the Enlightenment and commonly called the "father of [[Liberalism|liberalism]]."

His major works, all of 1689–90, are *A Letter Concerning Toleration*, *Two Treatises of Government*, and *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. The *Letter* contends that religion is a matter for the individual and that churches are voluntary associations, ruling out religious coercion and uniformity. The *Two Treatises* argue for government based on the consent of the governed and for a right to revolt against tyranny. They are believed to have influenced the language [[Thomas Jefferson]] chose for the 1776 Declaration of Independence.

In the *Essay*, Locke argued that the mind at birth is a blank slate, or *tabula rasa*, and that knowledge comes only from experience through sense perception, a position known as [[Empiricism|empiricism]] and associated with the tradition of [[Francis Bacon]]. He is also credited with describing private property as a natural right arising when a person mixes labor with nature.

Born in Wrington, Somerset, to Puritan parents, he attended Westminster School and Christ Church, Oxford, where he spent 15 years as student and tutor and pursued medicine. From 1666 he was an associate of Lord Shaftesbury and held government posts through him. He lived in exile in the [[Netherlands]] in 1683–85, returned in 1689 after the [[Glorious Revolution]], and later served as a Commissioner of Trade and Plantations.

His ties to [[Slavery|slavery]] remain debated: he was a shareholder in the Royal Company of Adventures Trading to Africa, which traded in slaves, and was involved in the Carolina proprietorship, which permitted slavery. Scholars continue to discuss the contradiction with his theory of natural liberty.

Scottish engineer and television pioneer. In Hastings in the 1920s he built experimental apparatus from improvised parts, and in 1925 he transmitted the first grayscale television picture, of a ventriloquist's dummy.
