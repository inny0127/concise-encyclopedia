ENTRY:
Measure of the variation of a set of values about their arithmetic [[Average|average]], used in statistics. A low standard deviation means the values cluster near the average; a high one means they are spread over a wider range. It is most often represented by the Greek letter σ (sigma) and equals the [[Square root|square root]] of the [[Variance|variance]], so it is expressed in the same unit as the data. For a [[Normal distribution|normal distribution]], about 68.3% of values fall within one standard deviation of the average, 95.4% within two and 99.7% within three. It can also be used to calculate the [[Standard error|standard error]] for a finite sample and to determine statistical significance.

  standard deviation = 표준 편차
  standard error of the mean = 평균의 표준 오차
ENTRY:
Standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic, equal to the square root of the variance of an estimator. The standard error of the mean equals the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. Because the population standard deviation is seldom known, it is usually estimated by substituting the sample standard deviation. The standard error is often used in calculating [[Confidence interval|confidence intervals]] and also appears in [[Regression analysis|regression analysis]]. Small samples tend to underestimate the population standard deviation: with n = 2 the underestimate is about 25%, and with n = 6 only 5%. Reducing the error by a factor of two requires four times as many observations.

ENTRY:
Luminous spheroid of plasma held together by its own gravity. The nearest star to Earth is the [[Sun]]. Only about 4,000 stars are visible to the naked eye, all within the [[Milky Way]], and the observable universe is estimated to contain 10²² to 10²⁴ stars. Astronomers group prominent stars into constellations and asterisms and compile catalogs with standardized designations.

A star forms when a gaseous nebula of mostly hydrogen and helium collapses under gravity, and its total mass largely determines its evolution and fate. Protostar cores reach about 15 million kelvin, igniting hydrogen fusion and beginning the main sequence, the longest stage of a star's life. A star shines for most of its active life through [[Nuclear fusion|thermonuclear fusion]] of hydrogen into helium. Low- to intermediate-mass stars (up to about 8 solar masses) expand into red giants and leave a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]]. Massive stars end as [[Supernova|supernovae]], leaving a [[Neutron star|neutron star]] or a [[Black hole|black hole]]. Stellar nucleosynthesis creates almost all naturally occurring elements heavier than lithium, and mass loss and supernovae return them to the interstellar medium to be recycled into new stars. Stars are classified by spectral type (O, B, A, F, G, K, M), from surface temperatures over 30,000 K to about 3,000 K.

Stars have served civilizations for religious practice, divination, navigation, and calendars. The oldest accurately dated star chart is Egyptian, from 1534 BC, and Babylonian astronomers compiled the earliest known catalogs in the late 2nd millennium BC. The catalog of [[Hipparchus]] (2nd century BC) listed 1,020 stars. Chinese astronomers were first to record a supernova, in 185 AD, and SN 1006 was the brightest stellar event in recorded history.

Distinct form in which matter can exist, differing in how component particles are arranged and behave collectively. Four states are observable in everyday life: [[Solid|solid]], [[Liquid|liquid]], [[Gas|gas]], and [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]]. In a solid, particles are tightly packed and can only vibrate, giving a definite shape and volume. In a liquid, particles stay close but move past one another, so volume is fixed while shape follows the container. In a gas, particles move freely, filling the container. Plasma resembles a gas but contains ions and free electrons that move independently and respond to electric and magnetic fields; a gas becomes plasma through a large voltage difference or very high temperatures.

Changes between states include melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation (solid to gas), and deposition (gas to solid). A liquid's volume is usually greater than that of the corresponding solid, the best-known exception being water, H₂O. The term phase is sometimes used as a synonym, but one state can include several phases. Iron has a body-centered cubic structure below 912 °C (1,674 °F) and a face-centered cubic structure between 912 and 1,394 °C (2,541 °F). [[Ice]] has fifteen known solid phases.

Political entity that rules and governs a society and population within a definite territory. Governments act as the apparatus or instrument of states, and the term is often used as a metonym for them. A state is widely defined by its legitimate monopoly on the use of force. It may be a [[Unitary state|unitary state]] or a [[Federation|federal]] union; in the latter, the term "state" is sometimes applied to the federated polities, which may hold some attributes of a sovereign state but lack the same capacity to act internationally. A [[Sovereign state|sovereign state]] has sovereignty, though any group's claim to statehood is limited in practice by recognition from other states. Satellite states hold de facto sovereignty but are often indirectly controlled by another state.

For most of prehistory people lived in stateless societies, and the earliest states arose about 5,500 years ago. Societies became more stratified and developed institutions leading to centralized governments, gaining state capacity alongside the growth of cities, often spurred by insecurity and territorial competition. States have rested on varied justifications, such as divine right and the social contract. Today the modern nation state is the predominant form. Charles Tilly saw states in the archaeological record as of 6000 BC, appearing in Europe around 990 and becoming prominent after 1490.

The word derives from Latin *status*, "condition, circumstances", and entered Middle English c. 1200. Machiavelli's early 16th-century works helped popularize its near-modern sense. Contractarian views hold that states form because cooperation benefits everyone; predatory views stress the mismatch between the interests of rulers and people, and Tilly likened states to organized extortion that sells protection from itself.

A state is distinct from a [[Nation|nation]], defined by identity and cultural or historical factors; from a [[Government|government]], which is authorized to act on the state's behalf; and from society. Controversies concern the extent of state intervention in individual liberties.

ENTRY:
Method of statistical inference for deciding whether data provide sufficient evidence to reject a particular hypothesis. The test typically computes a test statistic, then decides by comparing it with a critical value or by evaluating the [[P-value|p-value]] computed from it. The null hypothesis (H₀), usually a statement of "no effect" or "no difference," is tested against the alternative hypothesis (H₁). If the p-value is less than or equal to the significance level (α), the null hypothesis is rejected, but the test does not conclude that it is false. Conclusions are never certain. A type I error rejects a true null hypothesis, with probability α, while a type II error accepts it when the alternative is true, with probability β. The power of a test, 1−β, measures its ability to detect a true alternative. Roughly 100 specialized statistical tests are in use.

ENTRY:
Discipline concerned with the collection, organization, analysis, interpretation, and presentation of data. A study usually begins with a [[Statistical population|statistical population]] or a statistical model, such as all people living in a country or every atom in a crystal. Statistics covers every aspect of data, including the design of surveys and experiments, and is closely related to mathematics, physics, chemistry, and geography.

When [[Census|census]] data covering every member of a population cannot be collected, statisticians use experiment designs and survey samples. Random [[Sampling (statistics)|sampling]] allows conclusions to be extended from the sample to the whole population. An experimental study measures a system, manipulates it, and measures again, whereas an [[Observational study|observational study]] involves no manipulation.

Two main methods are used. Descriptive statistics summarize a sample with indexes such as the mean or [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]], describing central tendency and dispersion. Inferential statistics draw conclusions from data subject to random variation, using the framework of [[Probability theory|probability theory]]. Probability deduces results for samples from a given theory, while statistics induces statements about a population from a data set. A standard procedure proposes a hypothesis against a null hypothesis of no relationship and applies tests to the data. Two kinds of error are recognized: Type I (rejecting a true null hypothesis, a false positive) and Type II (failing to reject a false one, a false negative). Measurement is also prone to random error (noise), systematic error (bias), and blunders, and missing data or censoring can bias estimates. In the Mathematics Subject Classification, statistics is class 62-XX.

The English word comes from German *Statistik*, originally "description of a state, a country", ultimately from Latin *status*. Gottfried Achenwall coined the German term; it entered English in 1770 for the study of political arrangements and gained its modern meaning in the 1790s in the works of John Sinclair. Statistics emerged in the 17th century as "political arithmetic", with John Graunt analyzing demographic data in London. [[Karl Pearson]] advanced the study of correlation and [[Ronald Fisher]] experimental design and significance testing in the 20th century. Applications now span public health, business, government policy, and the analysis of large datasets, where concerns include bias in algorithms.
