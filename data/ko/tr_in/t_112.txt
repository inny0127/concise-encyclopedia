ENTRY:
Sensory organ that detects light and converts it into electrochemical impulses in [[Neuron|neurons]], forming part of an organism's visual system. In higher organisms it is a complex optical system that regulates the intensity of light, focuses it through adjustable lenses to form an image, and sends electrical signals along the optic nerve to the visual cortex and other brain areas.

Eyes with resolving power have come in ten fundamentally different forms, classified as compound or non-compound. Non-compound, or simple, eyes have a single lens that focuses light onto the retina and are common in mammals, including humans. In most vertebrates and some molluscs, cone cells (for color) and rod cells (for low-light contrasts) in the retina convert light into neural signals. The eyes of most [[Cephalopod|cephalopods]], fish, amphibians, and snakes focus by telescoping a fixed-shape lens, as in a [[Camera|camera]]. The simplest eyes, such as the ocelli of some snails, distinguish only light from dark.

[[Compound eye|Compound eyes]], common in insects and crustaceans, consist of many small visual units, each with its own lens and photosensitive cells. Some have up to 28,000 sensors arranged hexagonally, giving a full 360° field of vision; they are very sensitive to motion, but their physics limits resolution to no better than 1°. The extinct [[Trilobite|trilobites]] had clear calcite lenses, from one to thousands per eye. The [[Mantis shrimp|mantis shrimp]] has the most complex color vision system known.

The first proto-eyes evolved about 600 million years ago, about the time of the [[Cambrian explosion]].

ENTRY:
Polygenic phenotypic trait determined by the pigmentation of the iris and the scattering of light in the iris stroma. In humans, iris pigmentation ranges from light brown to black, depending on the concentration of [[Melanin|melanin]]. Blue, green and hazel eyes result from light scattering in the stroma, a process similar to [[Rayleigh scattering]] that makes the sky blue; neither blue nor green pigments are present in the human iris. As of 2010, as many as 16 [[Gene|genes]] had been associated with human eye color, the main two being OCA2 and HERC2.

Series of tests performed to assess vision, focusing ability and other functions of the eyes, usually by an optometrist, ophthalmologist or orthoptist. A full examination begins with a medical history and includes eight steps: visual acuity, pupil function, extraocular muscle motility, intraocular pressure, visual fields, external examination, slit-lamp examination and dilated fundoscopic examination. Corticosteroids can raise intraocular pressure, which can lead to [[Glaucoma|glaucoma]].

ENTRY:
Bird of prey in the genus *Falco*, which includes about 40 species. Falcons are found on every continent except [[Antarctica]]. Adults have thin, tapered wings that allow high speed and rapid changes of direction. Falcons kill prey with a tomial tooth on the beak, while hawks, [[Eagle|eagles]] and other birds of prey of the family Accipitridae use talons. The largest species is the gyrfalcon, up to 65 cm (26 in) long; the smallest is the Seychelles kestrel, at 15–24 cm. As with hawks and owls, females are typically larger than males. The [[Peregrine falcon]] has been recorded diving at 320 km/h (200 mph). The genus was introduced by [[Carl Linnaeus]] in 1758, and falcons are not closely related to other birds of prey; their nearest relatives are [[Parrot|parrots]] and [[Songbird|songbirds]].

ENTRY:
[[Archipelago]] (pop., 2021: 3,662) in the South Atlantic Ocean, a British Overseas Territory. The main islands lie about 500 km east of [[Patagonia]] and include East Falkland, where the capital, Stanley, is located, and West Falkland, with 776 smaller islands. The territory has internal self-government, while the United Kingdom is responsible for defense and foreign affairs. The economy rests on fishing, tourism and sheep farming, with wool exports. Argentina maintains a claim to sovereignty; British rule was reasserted in 1833, and Argentina's 1982 invasion prompted the [[Falklands War]]. In a 2013 referendum almost all votes cast favored remaining a UK overseas territory. Oil exploration remains controversial because of maritime disputes with Argentina.

ENTRY:
Undeclared 1982 war between [[Argentina]] and the [[United Kingdom]] over the [[Falkland Islands]] and South Georgia. Argentina invaded the Falklands on 2 April 1982, and a British naval task force retook them. Argentina surrendered on 14 June after 74 days of conflict, which killed 649 Argentine military personnel, 255 British personnel and three Falkland Islanders. The defeat hastened the fall of Argentina's military government. In Britain, the government of [[Margaret Thatcher]] was re-elected with a larger majority the following year. Diplomatic relations were restored in 1989, but the sovereignty dispute remains unresolved.

ENTRY:
Capture of [[Constantinople]], capital of the [[Byzantine Empire]], by the [[Ottoman Empire]] on 29 May 1453. The Ottoman army, which far outnumbered the defenders, was led by Sultan [[Mehmed II]]; the Byzantines were commanded by Emperor Constantine XI Palaiologos. The 53-day [[Siege|siege]] began on 6 April. Ottoman cannons fired stone balls weighing 500 kilograms (1,100 lb) over the Theodosian walls, which had protected the city for about 800 years and created breaches for the assault. Mehmed II made Constantinople the Ottoman capital, replacing Adrianople, and the [[Hagia Sophia]] was converted into a mosque. The fall marked the effective end of the [[Roman Empire]], which had begun around 27 BC, and for many historians it marks the end of the [[Middle Ages]].
