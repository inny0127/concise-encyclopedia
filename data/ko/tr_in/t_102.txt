ENTRY:
Source of electric power consisting of one or more electrochemical cells with external connections for powering electrical devices. When a battery supplies power, its positive terminal is the cathode and its negative terminal the anode; electrons flow from the negative terminal through the external circuit to the positive terminal, driving a redox reaction in which higher-energy reactants become lower-energy products. "Battery" once meant multiple cells, but the term now includes single cells.

Primary batteries are used once and discarded, because their electrode materials change irreversibly during discharge; the alkaline battery is a common example. Secondary batteries can be recharged by an applied current; examples are the lead–acid automotive battery and the lithium-ion batteries in portable electronics and electric cars. Sizes range from cells for hearing aids and wristwatches to room-sized banks providing standby power for telephone exchanges and [[Data center|computer data centers]]. Batteries have much lower specific energy than fuels such as gasoline.

[[Benjamin Franklin]] first used the term "battery" in 1749 for linked Leyden jar capacitors. [[Alessandro Volta]] built the first electrochemical battery, the voltaic pile, in 1800: a stack of copper and zinc plates separated by brine-soaked paper disks. Volta did not realize that the voltage came from chemical reactions; [[Michael Faraday]] showed in 1834 that the associated corrosion was unavoidable. The Daniell cell, invented in 1836 by John Frederic Daniell, was the first practical source of electricity and powered telegraph networks. Dry cells, which replaced liquid electrolyte with a paste, made portable devices practical near the end of the 19th century.

Between 2010 and 2018, battery demand grew by 30% annually, reaching 180 GWh in 2018. Production involves mining of lithium, cobalt, and nickel, which can contaminate water sources and ecosystems.

Physical property of [[Matter|matter]] that causes it to experience a force in an electromagnetic field. Charge is positive or negative: like charges repel and unlike charges attract. In ordinary matter, negative charge is carried by [[Electron|electrons]] and positive charge by the [[Proton|protons]] in atomic nuclei. Charge is quantized in integer multiples of the elementary charge, about 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, and in an isolated system the total charge does not change. Machine that converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. Most electric motors work through the interaction between the motor's magnetic field and current in a wire winding, which generates [[Lorentz force]] as torque on the motor's shaft. An electric generator is mechanically identical but operates inversely.

Motors may be powered by direct current sources such as batteries or by alternating current sources such as a power grid, and may be brushed or brushless, single-phase or three-phase, air-cooled or liquid-cooled. They produce linear or rotary force and so are a type of actuator. The largest, used for marine propulsion, pipeline compression, and pumped storage, exceed 100 megawatts. Other uses include fans, pumps, machine tools, household appliances, power tools, vehicles, and disk drives. In regenerative braking, traction motors run in reverse as generators to recover energy otherwise lost as heat and friction.

A motor has two mechanical parts: the rotor, which moves, and the stator, which does not. Electrically it consists of field magnets, electromagnets or permanent magnets, and the armature, wire windings on a ferromagnetic core; together they form a magnetic circuit. The stator core is made of thin insulated laminations, which reduce losses from circulating eddy currents. In commutated motors, a commutator, a rotary switch contacted by carbon brushes, reverses the current in the rotor windings every half turn (180°) so the torque keeps one direction; such motors have been mostly replaced by brushless designs.

British scientist [[Michael Faraday]] constructed the first primitive electric motor in 1821 using a mercury bath and wire. Moritz Hermann von Jacobi created the first practical rotating motor in 1834, and Thomas Davenport patented a practical direct-current motor in 1837. [[Nikola Tesla]] patented the induction motor in 1888.

Engineering discipline concerned with the study, design, and application of equipment, devices, and systems that use electricity, electronics, and electromagnetism. It emerged as an identifiable occupation in the latter half of the 19th century, after the commercialization of the electric telegraph, the telephone, and electrical power generation, distribution, and use. Engineers typically hold a degree in the subject and may belong to professional bodies such as the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE).

Set of physical phenomena associated with the presence and motion of matter possessing an electric charge. Electricity is related to magnetism, both being parts of [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]], as described by [[Maxwell's equations]]. Common phenomena include lightning, static electricity, electric heating, and electric discharges.

A positive or negative [[Electric charge|electric charge]] produces an electric field. The motion of charge carriers is an [[Electric current|electric current]] and produces a magnetic field. In most applications, Coulomb's law determines the force on a charged particle. Electric potential is the work done to move a charge within an electric field, typically measured in volts. Electricity serves in electric power and in electronics, which deals with circuits involving active components such as vacuum tubes, transistors, diodes, and integrated circuits. It is integral to transport, heating and cooling, lighting, communications, and computation.

People were aware of shocks from electric fish long before any knowledge of electricity. Ancient Egyptian texts dating from 2750 BC described them as the "protectors" of all other fish. Thales of Miletus made observations on static electricity around 600 BC, noting that rubbed amber attracts light objects. In 1600 the English scientist William Gilbert wrote *De Magnete*, distinguishing the lodestone effect from static electricity produced by rubbing amber, and coined the Neo-Latin word *electricus*, from the Greek *elektron*, "amber." The late 19th century brought the greatest progress in electrical engineering, through figures including Alexander Graham Bell, Thomas Edison, [[Nikola Tesla]], and George Westinghouse. Self-propagating wave of the electromagnetic field that carries momentum and radiant energy through space. It is classified by frequency, which is inversely proportional to wavelength, in a spectrum running from radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays, to gamma rays. All forms travel at the speed of light in a vacuum and exhibit [[Wave–particle duality|wave–particle duality]], behaving both as waves and as discrete particles called photons.

The radiation is produced by accelerating charged particles and may be emitted naturally, as by the Sun, or generated artificially. Its energy needs no propagating medium. Its interaction with matter depends on wavelength, which shapes its uses. Radio waves enable broadcasting and wireless communication, infrared is used in thermal imaging, and visible light is essential for vision. X-rays and gamma rays are applied in medical imaging, cancer treatment, and industrial inspection, and radiation in the ultraviolet and above has been associated with negative health effects.

Electric and magnetic fields obey superposition, so light waves can interfere constructively or destructively. In homogeneous, isotropic media the radiation is a transverse wave: the electric and magnetic fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, as [[Maxwell's equations]] require. The waves can be polarized, reflected, refracted, or diffracted. Composite light disperses into a visible spectrum when passing through a prism, because the refractive index depends on wavelength.

In quantum mechanics, the radiation consists of photons, uncharged elementary particles with zero rest mass that are the quanta of the electromagnetic field. Wave characteristics are more apparent over large timescales and distances, particle characteristics over small ones; the self-interference of a single photon displays both. Quantum electrodynamics is the theory of how the radiation interacts with matter on an atomic level; further sources include electron transitions to lower energy levels and black-body radiation.

Interaction between particles with [[Electric charge|electric charge]], mediated by electromagnetic fields. The electromagnetic force is one of the four fundamental forces of nature and the dominant force in the interactions of atoms and molecules. It relates [[Electricity|electricity]], magnetism, and optics, studied in the subdisciplines of electrostatics, magnetostatics, and electrodynamics. It is the second strongest of the four known fundamental forces and has unlimited range; at high energy it unifies with the weak force as the electroweak interaction.

The electrostatic attraction between atomic nuclei and their electrons holds atoms together, and electric forces allow atoms to combine into molecules. The force underlies electrical energy production and distribution, fiber optic and wireless communication, sensors, computation, electrolysis, electroplating, and mechanical motors and actuators.

It has been studied since ancient times to explain lightning, static electricity, and the attraction between magnetized pieces of iron ore and compasses. In the 18th and 19th centuries scientists developed mathematical relationships between these phenomena and electric charges and currents, and showed that they could create self-sustaining electromagnetic waves, which form visible light and other [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]].

A disturbance in the electric field can create one in the magnetic field, which in turn affects the electric field, producing an oscillation that propagates through space as an electromagnetic wave. The requirement that observations stay consistent across moving frames of reference, and the fixing of the speed of light by the permeability and permittivity of the medium, helped inspire Einstein's theory of special relativity in 1905. Quantum electrodynamics modifies Maxwell's equations to be consistent with the quantized nature of matter, expressing changes in the field as discrete excitations called photons.

[[Subatomic particle]] whose electric charge is negative one elementary charge. It is an elementary particle contained in the matter that makes up the universe. Its charge is −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs and its rest mass approximately 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg, about 1/1836 that of a proton. In atoms, an electron's matter wave occupies orbitals around the positively charged nucleus, and the configuration of an atom's electrons determines its chemical properties. The outermost, or valence, electrons are the least tightly bound and form chemical bonds between atoms.

Electrons belong to the first generation of the lepton family, elementary particles that do not interact with the strong nuclear force, and have no known substructure. Their intrinsic angular momentum (spin) is ħ/2. As fermions, no two electrons can occupy the same quantum state, according to the [[Pauli exclusion principle]]. They show properties of both particles and waves, and can be diffracted like light. Their antiparticle, the [[Positron|positron]], is identical except for opposite charge; when the two collide they can annihilate, producing gamma ray photons.

In metals the outermost electrons are delocalized and move freely, accounting for high electrical and thermal conductivity. In semiconductors the number of mobile charge carriers, electrons and holes, can be tuned by doping, temperature, voltage, and radiation, the basis of modern electronics. Free electron beams in a vacuum are used in cathode ray tubes, electron microscopes, electron beam welding, lithography, and particle accelerators.

The ancient Greeks noticed that amber attracted small objects when rubbed with fur. In 1600 William Gilbert coined the Neo-Latin term *electrica* for such substances, from the Greek *ēlektron*, "amber." The British physicist J. J. Thomson discovered the electron in 1897 through experiments with cathode rays, showing that they consist of negatively charged particles much smaller than atoms. [[Robert Millikan]] measured its charge in 1909.
