The [[World Health Organization]] notes that non-English languages and cultures use different words for sexual activity with slightly different meanings. Researchers commonly use the term for penile–vaginal intercourse and specific terms for other acts.

Sexual activity carries a risk of [[Sexually transmitted infection|sexually transmitted infections]], and health professionals recommend safer sex practices. Jurisdictions restrict certain acts, such as adultery, incest, sexual activity with minors, prostitution, rape, zoophilia, sodomy, premarital sex, and extramarital sex. [[Religion|Religious]] views vary widely between and within religions, though a common theme is the prohibition of adultery.

Between non-human animals the term copulation is more usual, and sperm may be introduced in non-vaginal ways, such as by cloacal copulation. Most non-human mammals mate at estrus, the most fertile period of the female cycle. [[Bonobo|Bonobos]], dolphins, and [[Chimpanzee|chimpanzees]] have sex regardless of estrus and with same-sex partners, behavior presumed to be for pleasure and to strengthen social bonds.

ENTRY:
Fine-grained, clastic sedimentary rock formed from mud of clay minerals and silt-sized particles. Shale splits into thin layers, a property called fissility, and it is the most common sedimentary rock. Its clay minerals are largely [[Kaolinite|kaolinite]], montmorillonite and illite, mixed with [[Quartz|quartz]] grains. A typical shale is about 58% clay minerals and 28% quartz. Red and brown colors reflect iron oxides, while black shale contains more than one percent carbonaceous material. Shales and other mudrocks contain roughly 95 percent of the organic matter in all sedimentary rocks, though in an average shale this is less than one percent by mass.

ENTRY:
Spiritual practice in which a practitioner, the shaman, interacts with the spirit world through altered states of consciousness such as trance. The usual goal is to direct spirits or spiritual energies into the physical world for healing, divination, or other help to people.

The word derives from Russian *shaman*, which comes from a Tungusic term, possibly from the Evenki or Manchu language. It is sometimes connected to a Tungus root meaning "to know", a link the linguist Juha Janhunen questions. Mircea Eliade suggested the [[Sanskrit]] *śramaṇa*, a wandering monastic figure whose word spread across Central Asia with [[Buddhism]], as a possible ultimate origin. Russians adopted the term from the Indigenous peoples of [[Siberia]]. Nicolaes Witsen carried it to Western Europe in his book *Noord en Oost Tataryen* (1692), and a 1698 account of a Russian embassy to China by Adam Brand introduced it to English speakers.

Anthropologists have no single agreed definition. Manvir Singh proposes three basic features: entering non-ordinary states, engaging with unseen realities, and providing services such as healing and [[Divination|divination]]. Ronald Hutton identified four definitions in use, ranging from anyone who contacts a spirit world in an altered state to the Indigenous religions of Siberia and neighboring parts of Asia.

Western anthropologists later applied the term broadly to unrelated practices in Asia, Africa, Australasia, and the Americas. Critics say this misapplies the words to the varied spiritual practices of [[Indigenous peoples]], each of which has its own terms. Studies report trance-related brain changes similar to those in meditation or hypnosis and psychological benefits such as reduced anxiety, but claims of literal contact with spirits lack verifiable evidence beyond subjective experience.

Unpleasant self-conscious emotion arising from negative self-evaluation against social norms and personal moral standards. It can generate feelings of pain, exposure, powerlessness and worthlessness, which may give rise to [[Anger|anger]], denial, avoidance and a tendency to blame others. Its focus is on the self or identity, in relation to a perceived audience. [[Charles Darwin|Darwin]] described its physical signs, including blushing, downcast eyes and a lowered head, in *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*. In individualistic Western contexts shame is often seen as an internal failure of the self, whereas collectivist cultures may treat it as a relational emotion tied to social harmony. Shame is relevant to [[Depression (mood)|depression]], social phobia and some [[Eating disorder|eating disorders]].
