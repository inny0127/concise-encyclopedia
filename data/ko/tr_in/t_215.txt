Branch of mathematics that studies functions, spaces, and operators through methods of approximation and convergence. It grew out of [[Calculus|calculus]], especially the use of derivatives and integrals to study variable quantities, and in the 19th century its foundations were reformulated with greater rigor. Its basic objects include the real numbers, functions, sequences, series, and limits. Analysis connects closely with the sciences, providing methods for formulating mathematical models, constructing approximations, and estimating their errors.

Typical questions concern how functions, sequences, and operators behave under limiting processes: convergence and continuity, regularity and stability, quantitative bounds, and the accuracy of approximations. Modern analysis studies these in many settings, including Euclidean, metric, topological, and measure spaces and function spaces. Major areas include [[Complex analysis|complex analysis]], [[Functional analysis|functional analysis]], measure theory, [[Harmonic analysis|harmonic analysis]], and the theory of ordinary and partial differential equations.

Analysis formally developed in the 17th century during the [[Scientific Revolution]], but many of its ideas are older. An infinite geometric sum is implicit in Zeno's paradox of the dichotomy. Method of proof showing that a statement holds for every natural number. The proof has two parts. The base case establishes the statement for the first case, usually n = 0 or n = 1, and the induction step shows that if it holds for n = k, it also holds for n = k + 1. The idea is often illustrated by falling dominoes or climbing a ladder. Mathematical induction is an inference rule in formal proofs and the foundation of most correctness proofs for computer programs. It extends to well-founded structures such as trees, a generalization known as structural induction that is closely related to [[Recursion|recursion]]. Despite its name, it differs from [[Inductive reasoning|inductive reasoning]] in philosophy, because it gives a rigorous proof by a finite chain of deductive reasoning rather than a probable conclusion. Al-Karaji used an implicit form of the argument around 1000, and [[Pierre de Fermat|Pierre de Fermat]] employed it in number theory in the 17th century.

Deductive argument for a mathematical statement, showing that the stated assumptions logically guarantee the conclusion. A proof may use previously established theorems, but every proof can in principle be built from basic assumptions called [[Axiom|axioms]] and accepted rules of inference. Proofs establish logical certainty, unlike empirical arguments or non-exhaustive [[Inductive reasoning|inductive reasoning]]; presenting many cases in which a statement holds is not enough, since a proof must show it true in all possible cases. A proposition believed true but not yet proved is a [[Conjecture|conjecture]].

Proofs combine mathematical symbols with natural language, and most mathematical literature uses rigorous informal logic. Purely formal proofs, written entirely in a [[Formal language|formal language]], are the subject of proof theory: a formal proof is a sequence of formulas, each a logical consequence of the preceding ones. The standard of rigor is not absolute and has varied through history, and a proof must meet communal standards to be accepted.

The word derives from the Latin *probare*, "to test". Plausibility arguments using pictures and analogies preceded strict proof, which is primarily the product of ancient Greek mathematics. Thales and Hippocrates of Chios gave some of the first known proofs of geometric theorems. [[Euclid]] revolutionized the field by introducing the axiomatic method still in use, which starts from undefined terms and axioms and proves theorems by deductive logic. His *Elements* includes the [[Pythagorean theorem|Pythagorean theorem]], and a proof that the square root of two is irrational. In medieval Islamic mathematics, Al-Hashimi proved algebraic propositions about numbers, including the existence of irrational numbers, and Al-Karaji used an inductive proof in the *Al-Fakhri* (1000) to prove the [[Binomial theorem|binomial theorem]] and properties of Pascal's triangle. Modern proof theory treats proofs as inductively defined data structures and does not require axioms to be true in any sense, allowing parallel theories such as axiomatic set theory and [[Non-Euclidean geometry|non-Euclidean geometry]].

Field of knowledge concerned with abstract concepts such as numbers, geometric shapes, sets, functions, and probabilities. It uses logical reasoning and proof to establish their properties, often expressed as theorems, formulas, and equations. Mathematics is widely used to model and solve problems in science, [[Engineering|engineering]], technology, [[Economics|economics]], and everyday life. Its objects are either abstractions from nature or purely abstract entities stipulated to have certain properties, called [[Axiom|axioms]]. Although it is used to model empirical phenomena, its results are established by deductive proof rather than by experiment, and the relationship between mathematical truth, logic, and reality is a subject of philosophical debate.

Major areas include [[Number theory|number theory]] (the study of integers and their properties), algebra (the study of operations and the structures they form), geometry (the study of shapes and the spaces that contain them), analysis (the quantitative study of approximation and convergence), and set theory, presently used as a foundation for all mathematics. Some areas, such as game theory, develop in close correlation with applications and are grouped under applied mathematics; others develop independently but often find practical applications later. The 2020 Mathematics Subject Classification lists 63 first-level areas.

Mathematical written records first appeared in ancient Egypt and Mesopotamia, but the concept of proof and its associated rigor began in ancient Greek mathematics, exemplified in [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]]. Until the 16th and 17th centuries mathematics was primarily divided into geometry and arithmetic; during the Renaissance, modern algebra and calculus became new areas, and combinatorics became a separate branch in the 17th century. Before then, pseudosciences such as numerology and astrology were not clearly distinguished from it. At the end of the 19th century the foundational crisis of mathematics led to the systematic use of the axiomatic method, which greatly increased the number of areas and their applications.

Number theory evolved from the manipulation of natural numbers, later extended to integers and rational numbers. Two prominent early number theorists were Euclid and Diophantus of Alexandria, and the modern abstract study is largely attributed to [[Pierre de Fermat]] and [[Leonhard Euler]]. Fermat's Last Theorem, stated in 1637, was proved only in 1994 by Andrew Wiles, using tools from algebraic geometry, category theory, and homological algebra. Goldbach's conjecture, stated in 1742, asserts that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two prime numbers and remains unproven.

Geometry is one of the oldest branches, beginning with empirical recipes for surveying and architecture. The ancient Greeks introduced the requirement that every assertion be proved, systematized by Euclid around 300 BC. Euclidean geometry developed without change of methods until the 17th century, when [[René Descartes]] introduced Cartesian coordinates, allowing algebra and later calculus to be used to solve geometric problems.

Rectangular array of numbers or other mathematical objects arranged in rows and columns. A matrix with m rows and n columns is an m × n matrix, and a matrix with the same number of rows and columns is called square. Matrices are used in [[Linear algebra|linear algebra]] as linear maps, in geometry for transformations such as rotations, and in numerical analysis, where many computational problems are reduced to matrix computations. A square matrix is invertible if and only if its [[Determinant|determinant]] is nonzero. Matrix theory began as a sub-branch of linear algebra but grew to include [[Graph theory|graph theory]], algebra, combinatorics and statistics. In physical science, anything that has mass and occupies space by having volume. All everyday objects that can be touched are ultimately composed of [[Atom|atoms]], which are made up of interacting subatomic particles. Matter generally includes atoms and anything made up of them, and any particles that act as if they have both rest mass and volume. It does not include massless particles such as photons, or other energy phenomena or waves such as light or heat.

Matter exists in various states, or phases. These include the everyday solid, liquid, and gas, as with water as ice, liquid, and steam, and also plasma, Bose–Einstein condensates, fermionic condensates, and quark–gluon plasma. An atom can be imagined as a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by a cloud of orbiting electrons, but this is only somewhat correct, because subatomic particles are governed by quantum mechanics: they can act like waves as well as particles and lack well-defined sizes or positions. In the Standard Model of particle physics, matter is not a fundamental concept, because the elementary constituents of atoms have no inherent size or volume in the everyday sense. Because of the exclusion principle and other fundamental interactions, fermions (quarks and leptons) and many composites and atoms are effectively forced to keep a distance from other particles, which produces the property that matter takes up space.

The idea that matter is built of discrete building blocks, the particulate theory of matter, appeared in both ancient Greece and ancient India. Early proponents include the Indian philosopher [[Kaṇāda]] (c. 6th century BCE) and the Greek philosophers Leucippus (c. 490 BCE) and [[Democritus]] (c. 470–380 BCE).

Definitions vary by scale. One definition holds that matter is made of atoms, which also includes charged atoms and molecules and so covers plasmas and electrolytes. A finer definition counts anything made of protons, neutrons, and electrons, which includes electron beams in an old cathode ray tube television and white dwarf matter. At the level of elementary particles, ordinary matter is everything composed of quarks and leptons, excluding antiquarks and antileptons, which are antimatter. Ordinary matter is composed entirely of first-generation particles, the up and down quarks plus the electron and its neutrino. The W and Z bosons, which mediate the weak force, are not ordinary matter even though they have mass.

Matter is distinct from mass. Mass is a well-defined, extensive property of matter and other substances or systems, not a substance itself. Most of the mass of everyday objects comes from the interaction energy of its elementary components: the three quarks in a nucleon sum to approximately 12.5 MeV/c², low compared to the nucleon's approximately 938 MeV/c², with most of the rest coming from the binding energy of quarks within protons and neutrons.
