Branch of mathematics concerned with the properties of a geometric object that are preserved under continuous deformations such as stretching, twisting, crumpling, and bending, but not closing or opening holes, tearing, or gluing.

A [[Topological space]] is a set with a structure, called a topology, that allows continuous deformation to be defined. Examples include Euclidean spaces and, more generally, [[Metric space|metric spaces]], since any metric defines a topology. The deformations considered are homeomorphisms and homotopies, and a property invariant under them is a topological property. Basic examples are dimension, which distinguishes a line from a surface; compactness, which distinguishes a line from a circle; and connectedness, which distinguishes a circle from two non-intersecting circles. Intuitively, two spaces are homeomorphic if one can be deformed into the other without cutting or gluing, so a topologist cannot distinguish a coffee mug from a doughnut.

The underlying ideas go back to [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]], who in the 17th century envisioned *geometria situs* and *analysis situs*. [[Leonhard Euler]]'s 1736 paper on the [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg]] showed that no route through the town (now Kaliningrad) could cross each bridge exactly once, a result depending only on connectivity, and the problem led to [[Graph theory|graph theory]]. His polyhedron formula, V − E + F = 2, is regarded by some authorities as the first theorem of topology. Johann Benedict Listing introduced the term *Topologie* in 1847, and the English form appeared in 1883 in his obituary in *Nature*. [[Henri Poincaré]] consolidated and extended their work, and his 1895 paper *Analysis Situs* introduced the concepts now known as [[Homotopy|homotopy]] and homology. The idea of a topological space was developed only in the first decades of the 20th century.

Self-propelled underwater weapon launched from above or below the water surface, with an explosive warhead for contact or proximity detonation. The word first named [[Electric ray|electric rays]], and the term originally covered many devices now called [[Naval mine|mines]]. In 1866 Robert Whitehead developed the first practical self-propelled torpedo, at Fiume in [[Austria-Hungary]]. In modern warfare, a submarine-launched torpedo is almost certain to hit its target, and the best defense is a counterattack with another torpedo.

Rotational equivalent of linear [[Force|force]], a twist applied to an object about an axis, also called the moment of force. Torque is the product of the perpendicular component of a force and the distance from the axis to the line of action, known as the [[Lever|lever]] arm. In vector form it is the [[Cross product|cross product]] of the position and force vectors, with direction given by the [[Right-hand rule|right-hand rule]]. Its SI unit is the newton-meter (N·m). Torque causes angular acceleration in rigid bodies, appearing in applications from levers and pulleys to [[Engine|engines]]. The term comes from the Latin *torquēre*, "to twist," and was suggested by James Thomson, appearing in print in 1884. In mechanical engineering in the UK and the U.S., it is usually called the moment of force, a usage traced to [[Siméon Denis Poisson]] in 1811.

Land-dwelling [[Turtle|turtle]] of the family Testudinidae, a [[Reptile|reptile]] with a hard shell that protects it from [[Predation|predation]] and retracts its head and neck into the shell. Tortoise species vary widely in size. The [[Galápagos tortoise]] exceeds 1.2 m (3.9 ft) in length, while the speckled Cape tortoise's shell measures only 6.8 cm (2.7 in). Deliberate infliction of severe pain or suffering on a person to punish, extract a confession, gather information or intimidate third parties. Some definitions restrict torture to acts by the [[State (polity)|state]], while others include non-state actors. Most victims are poor and marginalized people suspected of crimes. Torturers more often act out of fear or limited resources than sadism, and beating is the most common physical method. Since the twentieth century, many torturers have preferred non-scarring or psychological methods. Torture can cause chronic pain and mental harm, and survivors have some of the highest rates of [[Post-traumatic stress disorder|post-traumatic stress disorder]]. Many Western countries abolished official judicial torture in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. It is prohibited under [[International law]] for all states in all circumstances, and opposition to it helped stimulate the [[Human rights|human rights]] movement after [[World War II]]. Despite its decline, torture is still practiced in or by most countries.

Doughnut-shaped [[Geometry|geometric]] surface generated by rotating a [[Circle|circle]] one full revolution about an axis coplanar with it. If the axis does not touch the circle, the result is a ring torus, sometimes called a doughnut.
