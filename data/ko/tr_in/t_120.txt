ENTRY:
Military construction designed for the defense of territory in warfare, also used to establish rule in a region during peacetime. The word derives from [[Latin]] *fortis* ("strong") and *facere* ("to make"). Fortification is usually divided into permanent and field fortification, with an intermediate semipermanent branch. Castles differ from the generic fort in being the residence of a monarch or noble and commanding a specific defensive territory.

Walls have been necessary for cities from early history. The walled town of Sesklo in Greece dates from 6800 BC, and Solnitsata in Bulgaria, from 4700 BC, held 350 people in two-story houses behind a stone wall. [[Uruk]] in Sumer is one of the world's oldest known walled cities, and the Egyptians built frontier fortresses and mud brick city walls. A prehistoric stone wall of 3200 BC, 4 m thick and 4 m tall, surrounded the Ness of Brodgar temple in Scotland. In [[Mycenaean Greece]], large "cyclopean" stone walls were fitted without mortar, and Athens built the Long Walls to its seaport at Piraeus. The [[Celts]] of Central Europe built large fortified settlements called oppida.

The [[Castle|castle]] emerged in the 9th century in the [[Carolingian Empire]], with Roman forts and hill forts as its main antecedents. [[Cannon|Cannons]] of the 14th century made medieval-style fortifications largely obsolete. Fortifications in the age of black powder became much lower, with ditches and earth ramparts to absorb the energy of cannon fire. Explosive shells of the 19th century disrupted the intricate arrangements of star forts, and steel-and-concrete fortifications were common in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The advances in modern warfare since World War I have made large-scale fortifications obsolete in most situations.

Flammable carbon compound or [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbon]] material formed naturally in the Earth's crust from the buried remains of prehistoric organisms. They originate in the anaerobic decomposition of buried dead organisms over millions of years and so are considered nonrenewable. Terrestrial plants tend to form coal and methane, and many coal fields date to the Carboniferous period.

The theory that fossil fuels formed from dead plants was first introduced by Andreas Libavius in 1597 and later by [[Mikhail Lomonosov]]. Born in Żelazowa Wola and raised in [[Warsaw]], he left Poland shortly before the November 1830 Uprising and settled in [[Paris]]. Geometric shape or set with detailed structure at arbitrarily small scales. Fractals are typically self-similar, with parts resembling the whole, and their fractal dimension strictly exceeds their topological dimension. ENTRY:
Number representing a part of a whole or, more generally, any number of equal parts. The word comes from [[Latin]] *fractus*, "broken." A simple fraction, such as 1/2 or 17/3, consists of an [[Integer|integer]] numerator written above a line (or before a slash) and a non-zero integer denominator written below (or after) it. For positive integers, the numerator counts equal parts and the denominator states how many such parts make up a whole. In 3/4, the numerator 3 means three equal parts, and the denominator 4 means that four parts make up a whole.

Fractions represent ratios and division: 3/4 can stand for the ratio 3:4 or for the division 3 ÷ 4. In mathematics, a [[Rational number|rational number]] is a number representable as a/b, where a and b are integers and b is not zero. The set of rational numbers is commonly written ℚ or Q, for "quotient."

English denominators are generally expressed as ordinal numbers, pluralized when the numerator is not 1, as in one-fifth and two-fifths; exceptions include half for denominator 2, quarter for 4, and percent for 100. Spelled-out fractions are hyphenated, so two-fifths is a single value, whereas two fifths means two separate instances of 1/5.

Babylonian mathematicians used a base-60 system around 1800 BC to express fractional parts. The Egyptians, in the Rhind Papyrus from approximately 1650 BC, represented fractions mainly as sums of distinct unit fractions, such as 2/3 = 1/2 + 1/6. Arab mathematicians introduced the horizontal fraction bar. Fractions are classed as proper (numerator less than denominator), improper (numerator greater than or equal to denominator), and mixed numbers.

Abstract [[Coordinate system|coordinate system]], with an origin, orientation and scale specified in physical space, used to describe position and motion.
