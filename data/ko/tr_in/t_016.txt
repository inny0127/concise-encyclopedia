Polytheistic belief system of the Celtic peoples of Europe, also called Celtic polytheism or Celtic paganism. The Celts left no native written records of their beliefs, so evidence comes from archaeology, Greco-Roman accounts (some hostile and probably not well-informed) and early Christian-period literature. Local deities varied, but gods such as Lugus, Toutatis, Taranis, Cernunnos, Epona and Belenos were widely worshipped, and many deities were seen as threefold, as in the Three Mothers. Sacred springs were often linked to healing deities. The [[Druid|druids]] were the priests, though little is definitively known about them. Ceremonies took place in sacred groves and other natural shrines called *nemetons*, and votive offerings were deposited in water, wetlands, shafts and wells. Sacrificed animals were almost always livestock or working animals. Some evidence suggests human sacrifice, and [[Julius Caesar|Caesar]] claimed the Gauls burned criminals in a wicker man. In the 20th century the religion became the basis for Celtic neopaganism, whose groups range from Reconstructionists to New Age and Neo-Druidry.

Bronze Age civilization along the lower [[Nile]] in northeastern Africa, and a cradle of civilization.

It emerged around 3150 BC (by conventional chronology), when Upper and Lower Egypt were united by Menes, whom most Egyptologists believe to be the same person as [[Narmer]]. Its history comprised stable kingdoms, the Old, Middle, and New, separated by Intermediate Periods of instability. The New Kingdom was the pinnacle of Egyptian power, extending its rule over much of [[Nubia]] and a considerable portion of the [[Levant]]. Egypt then slowly declined and was invaded or conquered by the Hyksos, Kushites, Assyrians, Persians, Greeks, and Romans. The end of ancient Egypt is variously placed at 332 BC, during the wars of [[Alexander the Great]], or at 30 BC, when the Greek-ruled [[Ptolemaic Kingdom]] fell to Rome.

Success rested on the Nile's predictable flooding and controlled irrigation, which produced surplus crops and supported dense population. A bureaucracy of scribes, religious leaders, and administrators served the [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]], who was believed to embody the god Horus on earth. Predynastic cultures with firm control of agriculture and animal husbandry had developed by about 5500 BC; the Naqada culture grew over about 1,000 years from small farming communities into a powerful civilization.

Achievements include quarrying, surveying, and construction techniques behind monumental pyramids, temples, and obelisks; mathematics, as in the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus; medicine; irrigation; the first known planked boats; faience and glass technology; and the earliest known peace treaty, with the Hittite Empire. Religion centered on preparation for the afterlife through mummification. Egyptian art and architecture were widely copied, and its ruins inspired travelers and writers for millennia.

Set of changing styles of building in ancient Egypt, most of whose surviving examples are religious and funerary structures. Because wood was scarce, the main materials were sun-dried mud brick and stone, chiefly limestone, with sandstone and granite used in considerable quantities. From the Old Kingdom onward stone was generally reserved for tombs and temples, while brick was used for palaces, fortresses and town walls. Monumental buildings used post-and-lintel construction, with thick sloping walls and closely spaced [[Column|columns]], and many were aligned astronomically. Although the [[Arch]] was developed in the fourth dynasty, monumental buildings did not adopt it. Column capitals were carved to resemble plants such as the [[Papyrus|papyrus]], and walls bore painted carvings. The best-known examples are the [[Pyramid|pyramids]] and the [[Sphinx]]. The Great Hypostyle Hall at Karnak, built by [[Ramesses II]], has columns up to 20 meters high. Egyptian motifs later influenced architecture elsewhere, first in the Greek Orientalizing period and again in 19th-century Egyptomania.

Body of writing in the Egyptian language from the pharaonic period to the end of Roman domination. Writing appeared in the late 4th millennium BC, and the literature is the oldest corpus of Egyptian writing; with Sumerian literature, it is considered the world’s earliest. Old Kingdom works included funerary texts, letters, hymns, poems and autobiographies of administrative officials. A narrative literature emerged only in the early Middle Kingdom, reflecting a rising class of scribes and unprecedented literacy. Writing was an elite exercise, monopolized by scribes attached to government offices and the royal court. Popular tales included *The Story of Sinuhe* and *The Eloquent Peasant*, and teaching texts such as *The Instructions of Amenemhat* were often pseudonymous, attributed to famous figures. Texts survive on [[Papyrus|papyrus]], ostraca, wooden boards, monuments and coffins. The moist Nile floodplain destroyed most papyri, so much of the literature is lost.

Polytheistic belief system of ancient Egypt, centered on interactions with about 1,500 known deities. Rituals such as prayer and offerings sought the gods’ favor. Formal religion centered on the [[Pharaoh|pharaoh]], regarded as divine, who maintained *Ma’at*, the cosmic order, and repelled *Isfet*, chaos, through rituals and offerings. The state devoted enormous resources to temples. Individuals sought help through prayer or magic, and this popular tradition grew more prominent as the pharaoh’s status declined. Civilization of culturally and linguistically related but independent [[City-state|city-states]] in the northeastern Mediterranean, from the Greek Dark Ages (12th–9th centuries BC) to the end of classical antiquity (c. 600 AD).

Before Roman rule the area was unified only once, from 338 to 323 BC under Macedon. Ancient Greece begins with [[Mycenaean Greece]], which declined in the Bronze Age collapse c. 1100 BC. Urban city-states (poleis) formed in the 8th century BC, ushering in the Archaic period (c. 800–500 BC) and Greek colonization of the Mediterranean; Near Eastern contact inspired coinage and the Greek alphabet.

The Classical period runs from the Persian invasion of 480 BC to the death of Alexander the Great in 323 BC. It included the Golden Age of Athens, the [[Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BC) between Athens and Sparta, and the creation of the Hellenic league under [[Philip II of Macedon]], whose son [[Alexander the Great]] conquered Persia's Achaemenid Empire. Athenian direct democracy was restricted to free adult males. Alexander's conquests spread Greek culture into North Africa and Western Asia in the Hellenistic period (323–146 BC), which ended c. 30 BC when Egypt's Ptolemaic Kingdom was annexed by Rome. Late antiquity closed with Justinian I's shutting of the Academy of Athens in 529.

Ancient Greece is the first period attested in comprehensive narrative history. [[Herodotus]], called the "father of history," wrote the *Histories* between the 450s and 420s BC, and his accuracy is debated. Successors such as Thucydides were mostly Athenian or pro-Athenian, so more is known of Athens than of other cities. Classical Greek culture, as exemplified by the [[Parthenon]], especially its philosophy, strongly influenced [[Ancient Rome]], and Classical Greece is generally considered the cradle of Western civilization.

Period from the beginning of writing and recorded human history through late antiquity, covering all inhabited continents c. 3000 BC–AD 500.

Recorded history spans roughly 5,000 years, beginning with the development of Sumerian [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] script. The three-age system divides the period into the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age, with recorded history usually counted from the Bronze Age; the start and end of each age vary by region. The world population grew exponentially as the [[Neolithic Revolution]] progressed: an estimated 2 million in 10,000 BC, 45 million by 3000 BC, 72 million by 1000 BC, and 209 million by AD 500, a hundredfold increase in 10,500 years.

Knowledge of the preceding prehistory comes largely from archaeology. *Homo erectus* spread across Eurasia 1.8 million years ago. Dates for the emergence of *Homo sapiens* range from 250,000 to 160,000 years ago; some 50,000 years ago they migrated out of Africa, reaching Australia about 45,000 years ago and the Americas about 15,000 years ago. Evidence for agriculture appears about 9000 BC in eastern Turkey and spread through the [[Fertile Crescent]]; settlement at [[Göbekli Tepe]] began around 9500 BC and may have the world's oldest temple. Farming arose independently in the Nile Valley, West Africa, China, New Guinea, and Mesoamerica. Dogs were domesticated at least 15,000 years ago, sheep and goats around 9000 BC, cattle and water buffalo around 7000 BC, and horses, donkeys, and camels by about 4000 BC. Copper smelting began around 6000 BC in western Asia, and bronze alloys about 3500 BC in Mesopotamia and independently in China by 2000 BC. The potter's wheel was invented between 5000 and 4000 BC and adapted into wheeled vehicles by 3000 BC.

Writing developed separately in Mesopotamia, Egypt, India, China, and Mesoamerica. Proto-literate cuneiform spread in the Middle East by 3400 BC, Egyptian hieroglyphs appeared about 3200 BC, and the undeciphered Indus script had developed by 2800 BC in the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]]. Chinese characters arose in oracle bone script dated 1600 to 1100 BC, and writing in Mesoamerica dates to 600 BC.

The ancient Near East is considered the cradle of civilization: it produced the first centralized governments, law codes, and empires, along with social stratification, slavery, and organized warfare, and began astronomy and mathematics. In [[Mesopotamia]], agricultural communities emerged with the Halaf culture around 8000 BC, and cities began in the Uruk period (4000–3100 BC). [[Hammurabi]] created a Babylonian empire from the former kingdoms of Sumer and Akkad. Akkad reached its height between about 2330 and 2150 BC under [[Sargon of Akkad]], and by 1450 BC its language had become the main language of diplomacy in the Near East. Assyria, which at its height controlled the Fertile Crescent, Egypt, and much of Anatolia, ruled in three periods: Old, Middle, and Neo-Assyrian.

Complex indigenous societies of South America, stretching 4,000 km along the [[Andes]] from southern Colombia through Ecuador and Peru to northern Chile and northwest Argentina.

The Caral or Norte Chico civilization of coastal Peru, dating back to 3500 BC, is the oldest known civilization in the Americas, and scholars deem Andean civilization "pristine," meaning it developed independently of other civilizations. Earliest settlement of the region dates to about 15,000 BC. Later complex societies included [[Chavín culture]] (900–200 BC), Paracas (800–200 BC), its successor Nazca (200 BC–800 AD), Moche (100–700), Wari and [[Tiwanaku]] (both 600–1000), and [[Chimor]] (900–1470). In 1438 the Incas, from [[Cusco]], founded Tahuantinsuyu, the [[Inca Empire]], the largest empire the Americas had seen; it rose from the Kingdom of Cuzco, founded around 1230. In the 16th century Spanish colonizers subjugated the indigenous kingdoms and brought the region into the Spanish Empire.

Despite high mountains and hyper-arid desert, Andean peoples domesticated crops of worldwide importance, including [[Potato|potatoes]], peppers, peanuts, manioc, and coca. Notable achievements were monumental architecture, an extensive road system, and textile weaving.

Andean civilizations lacked a written language, using instead the quipu, a system of knotted and colored strings that survives in few examples and has not been fully deciphered; scholars differ on whether it recorded only numerical data or also narrative. They also lacked wheeled vehicles and draft animals: goods moved by human or by [[Llama|llama]], which could carry loads up to one-fourth of its weight, a maximum of 45 kg. Most economies were organized on reciprocity and redistribution rather than money.

Longest continental mountain range in the world, forming a continuous highland along the western edge of [[South America]].

The range is 200 to 700 km (120 to 430 mi) wide, widest between 18°S and 20°S latitude, and has an average height of about 4,000 m (13,000 ft). It runs south to north through seven countries: Argentina, Chile, Bolivia, Peru, Ecuador, Colombia, and Venezuela.

The Andes are the highest mountain range outside Asia. The highest peak, [[Aconcagua]] in Argentina, rises to about 6,961 m (22,838 ft). Chimborazo in Ecuador is farther from the Earth's center than any other point on the Earth's surface, because of the equatorial bulge. ENTRY:
Landlocked microstate in the eastern Pyrenees, between [[France]] and [[Spain]] (pop., July 2026: 90,021). Believed to have been created by [[Charlemagne]], it was ruled by the count of Urgell until 988, when it passed to the Diocese of Urgell. It was formed as a polity by a charter in 1278 and was a lordship within the Principality of [[Catalonia]] until 1715. It is headed by two co-princes, the Bishop of Urgell and the president of France. Its capital, Andorra la Vella, is the highest capital city in Europe, at 1,023 m. The sole official language is Catalan. Tourism brings about 8 million visitors a year. Andorra is not a member of the [[European Union]], but it has belonged to the [[United Nations]] and the [[Council of Europe]] since 1993, and it adopted the [[Euro|euro]] in 2002.

ENTRY:
French physicist and mathematician, one of the founders of classical electromagnetism. He referred to the field as electrodynamics and made contributions to chemistry and philosophy. Largely self-taught, he became a professor at the École polytechnique and the Collège de France and was a member of the French Academy of Sciences. He coined the term *solenoid* and invented the electrical telegraph. His work on the forces between current-carrying wires followed Hans Christian Ørsted’s discovery of electromagnetism in 1820. His name is one of 72 inscribed on the [[Eiffel Tower]]. The SI unit of [[Electric current|electric current]], the [[Ampere|ampere]], is named after him.

ENTRY:
Italian Renaissance architect active in the Venetian Republic. Influenced by Roman and Greek architecture, above all the writings of [[Vitruvius]], he designed churches and palaces but was best known for country houses and villas. His treatise *The Four Books of Architecture* (Venice, 1570) gained him wide recognition and influenced later architects, including [[Thomas Jefferson]]. Trained as a stonemason in Vicenza, he was employed in 1538–1539 by the humanist Gian Giorgio Trissino, who introduced him to the monuments of ancient Rome and gave him the name Palladio. The city of Vicenza, with 23 buildings designed by Palladio, and his 24 villas in the [[Veneto]] are listed by [[UNESCO]] as a [[World Heritage Site]].
