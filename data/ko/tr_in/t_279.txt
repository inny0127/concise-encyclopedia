Categorization of humans into groups viewed as distinct within a society, based on shared physical or social qualities. The term came into common use in 16th-century [[Europe]] and the Western world for geographic and [[Kinship|kinship]] groups, often arranged in a hierarchy. Modern scholarship views racial categories as socially constructed: race is assigned by rules made by society and has no inherent physical or biological meaning, although it is partly based on physical similarities within groups. Researchers continue to disagree. Some argue that genomic data show continental-scale clusters, while others argue that race has no taxonomic significance because all living humans belong to the same subspecies, *Homo sapiens sapiens*. Since the second half of the 20th century, race has been associated with discredited theories of [[Racism|scientific racism]], and in 2023 the U.S. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine renounced its use in genetics.

Belief, practice, or system of prejudice, discrimination, or antagonism directed at people because of their race or ethnicity. In its core sense it is the belief that groups of humans possess different behavioral traits corresponding to inherited attributes and can be ranked by the superiority of one race or ethnicity over another. Modern variants are often based in social perceptions of biological differences between peoples. Racism can take the form of social actions, practices, or beliefs, or of political systems such as [[Apartheid|apartheid]], and may involve nativism, xenophobia, segregation, and supremacism.

Attempts to legitimize racist beliefs through "scientific racism" have been overwhelmingly shown to be unfounded. Most biologists, anthropologists, and sociologists reject a taxonomy of races, and [[Human genome]] research indicates that race is not a meaningful genetic classification. The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination declares superiority based on racial differentiation scientifically false, morally condemnable, socially unjust, and dangerous. Contemporary social science treats race and [[Ethnicity|ethnicity]] as separate concepts, though popular usage has long equated them.

The word came into widespread use in the Western world in the 1930s. Racism is frequently described as a modern phenomenon that evolved during the age of European [[Imperialism|imperialism]] and was shaped by the [[Atlantic slave trade]]. It underlay racial segregation in the United States in the 19th and early 20th centuries and played a role in genocides including [[The Holocaust|the Holocaust]] and the Rwandan genocide. [[Indigenous peoples]] have often been subject to racist attitudes.

Interpretation of present-day disparities is contested. Some scholars attribute them to "systemic racism," while critics argue that this overemphasizes fixed structures over factors such as family, education, and culture.

ENTRY:
System that uses radio waves to determine the distance, direction, and radial velocity of objects relative to the site. It is used to detect and track aircraft, ships, spacecraft, guided missiles, motor vehicles, weather formations, and terrain. The term RADAR was coined in 1940 by the U.S. Navy as an acronym for "radio detection and ranging" and has since become a common noun.

A radar system consists of a transmitter producing electromagnetic waves in the radio or microwave domain, a transmitting antenna, a receiving antenna (often the same one), and a receiver and processor. Waves reflect off objects and return to the receiver, giving their locations and speeds; range comes from time of flight and velocity from the [[Doppler effect]]. The device was developed secretly for military use by several countries before and during [[World War II]].

In 1886 [[Heinrich Hertz]] showed that radio waves could be reflected from solid objects. In 1897, while testing ship-to-ship communication in the [[Baltic Sea]], Alexander Popov noted interference caused by a third vessel but did not pursue it. In 1904 the German inventor Christian Hülsmeyer demonstrated detecting a ship in dense fog and obtained a British patent for a full system, the *telemobiloscope*; it was tested in [[Cologne]] and Rotterdam harbor but rejected. U.S. Navy researchers observed ships and then aircraft disturbing radio signals in 1922 and 1930, and British work led by Robert Watson-Watt produced a national network by 1937. The [[Cavity magnetron]], developed in the United Kingdom, allowed small systems with sub-meter resolution.

Modern uses include [[Air traffic control]], radar astronomy, air defense, marine navigation, precipitation monitoring, and self-driving cars. The related [[Lidar]] uses predominantly infrared light from lasers instead of radio waves.

Dimensionless [[International System of Units|SI]] unit of angle, the standard unit of angular measure in many areas of [[Mathematics|mathematics]]. One radian is the angle subtended at the center of a [[Circle|circle]] by an arc equal in length to the radius, so an angle in radians equals the arc length divided by the radius. A complete revolution is 2π radians, equal to 360 [[Degree (angle)|degrees]], and one radian is approximately 57.2958 degrees. The symbol is rad, although it is often omitted in mathematical writing, and angles without a stated unit are generally assumed to be in radians. The radian was formally recognized as an SI coherent derived unit in 1995. It is preferred in [[Calculus]] because the derivatives of [[Sine and cosine|sine and cosine]] hold without additional conversion factors only when angles are in radians.

Radiation includes [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]], such as radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, [[X-ray|X-rays]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]; particle radiation, such as alpha, beta, proton and neutron radiation; acoustic radiation, such as sound and ultrasound; and gravitational waves. [[James Clerk Maxwell]] predicted electromagnetic waves in his 1873 theory of electromagnetism. On November 11, 1886, [[Heinrich Hertz]] first observed radio waves that he had generated with a spark-gap transmitter. In 1895 [[Guglielmo Marconi]] sent a wireless Morse code message to a recipient over a kilometer away, and on December 12, 1901, the first transatlantic signal. Reginald Fessenden made an amplitude-modulation voice broadcast in 1906, the year of [[Lee de Forest]]'s Audion vacuum tube. The first commercial broadcast was transmitted on November 2, 1920, when Westinghouse in Pittsburgh, under the call sign KDKA, broadcast the live returns of the U.S. presidential election.

The word derives from the Latin *radius*, meaning "spoke of a wheel, beam of light, ray." It was first applied to communications in 1881, when [[Alexander Graham Bell]], at the suggestion of Ernest Mercadier, adopted *radiophone* as an alternate name for his photophone. Early radio was called wireless telegraphy. The 1906 Berlin Radiotelegraphic Convention adopted "radio" internationally, and the public came to prefer it in the 1920s with the introduction of broadcasting. The emission of radio waves is regulated by law, coordinated by the International Telecommunication Union, which allocates frequency bands.

Process by which an unstable [[Atomic nucleus|atomic nucleus]] loses energy by radiation. The three most common types are alpha, beta, and gamma decay. In alpha decay the nucleus ejects a helium-4 nucleus (two protons and two neutrons), lowering the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. In beta decay a neutron converts to a proton or vice versa, emitting an electron or positron and changing the atomic number by 1.

Decay is a random process at the level of single atoms: according to quantum theory, it is impossible to predict when a particular atom will decay. For many identical atoms, however, the overall rate can be expressed as a decay constant or a [[Half-life|half-life]]. Half-lives range from nearly instantaneous to far longer than the age of the universe, from 164 microseconds for polonium-214 to 5,730 years for carbon-14. Except in gamma decay, the parent nuclide is transmuted into a daughter with a different number of protons or neutrons; when the number of protons changes, a different chemical element results.

Twenty-eight naturally occurring elements on Earth are radioactive, consisting of 35 primordial radionuclides that predate the formation of the Solar System. [[Uranium]] and thorium are examples.

Radioactivity was discovered in 1896 by [[Henri Becquerel]], who found that uranium salts blackened a photographic plate wrapped in black paper. In 1897 [[Marie Curie]] began studying the radiation and showed it to be a property of atoms; she and Pierre Curie isolated two new elements, [[Polonium|polonium]] and [[Radium|radium]], and coined the term "radioactivity." With Frederick Soddy, Ernest Rutherford realized that decay can transmute one element into another. The dangers of ionizing radiation, including that of X-rays discovered by [[Wilhelm Röntgen]] in 1895, were not immediately recognized.

ENTRY:
Form of atmospheric [[Precipitation|precipitation]] composed of water droplets that have condensed from atmospheric water vapor and fall by gravity. By intensity it is described as drizzle (very light, sporadic), a shower (brief, moderate), a cloudburst (brief, heavy), or a downpour (prolonged, heavy). Rain is a major component of the [[Water cycle]] and deposits most of the fresh water on Earth, supplying hydroelectric plants, crop irrigation, and ecosystems.

Rain forms when air is cooled to its [[Dew point|dew point]], the temperature at which it becomes saturated. Four mechanisms cool air to that point: adiabatic cooling as air rises and expands, conductive cooling on contact with a colder surface, radiational cooling by infrared emission, and evaporative cooling. Water vapor then [[Condensation|condenses]] on nuclei such as dust, ice, and salt to form a [[Cloud|cloud]]. Droplets grow by coalescence, fusing when turbulence makes them collide, until they are heavy enough to fall.

The major cause of rain is moisture moving along [[Weather front|weather fronts]]. Mountains force moist air upward so that rain falls on windward slopes, while desert climates can occur on the leeward side. The movement of the monsoon trough, or [[Intertropical Convergence Zone]], brings rainy seasons to savanna climates. The urban heat island effect increases rainfall downwind of cities.

Globally averaged annual precipitation is 715 mm over land and 990 mm over the whole Earth. Rainfall is measured with rain gauges and estimated by weather radar. Intense rain can cause flooding and erosion, while prolonged deficits contribute to [[Drought|droughts]].
