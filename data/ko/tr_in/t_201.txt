Chemical element with symbol Li and atomic number 3, a soft, silvery-white [[Alkali metal|alkali metal]]. It is the least dense metal and solid element under standard conditions and is highly reactive, so it is stored in inert liquids such as purified [[Kerosene|kerosene]] or mineral oil. Lithium occurs mainly in pegmatitic minerals and in brines. Its name derives from the Ancient Greek word for stone. In 1932 the transmutation of lithium atoms to [[Helium]] was the first fully human-made nuclear reaction, and lithium deuteride serves as a fusion fuel in thermonuclear weapons. Batteries consume more than three-quarters of lithium production. Lithium-based drugs are used as a mood stabilizer and antidepressant in treating [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]].

Rigid outermost rocky shell of a [[Terrestrial planet|terrestrial planet]] or [[Natural satellite|natural satellite]]. On Earth it consists of the crust and the lithospheric mantle, the uppermost part of the mantle, which behaves elastically over time scales of up to thousands of years or more. It lies above the weaker, hotter asthenosphere, and their boundary is set by the temperature at which [[Olivine|olivine]] becomes ductile, about 1,000 °C (1,830 °F). Earth's lithosphere is divided into [[Plate tectonics|tectonic plates]]. The term was introduced by the American geologist Joseph Barrell. Oceanic lithosphere thickens as it ages and moves away from [[Mid-ocean ridge|mid-ocean ridges]], and the oldest is typically about 140 kilometers thick.

The liter is not an [[International System of Units|SI]] unit, which uses the cubic meter; one liter equals 0.001 cubic meter. Vertebrate organ, the largest internal organ and gland, central to metabolism, digestion, and detoxification. It is found only in vertebrates and has a similar structure in all of them. In humans it lies in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, below the diaphragm and mostly shielded by the lower right rib cage. Estimates of its functions vary but are generally cited as around 500, so it is sometimes called the body's chemical factory.

The liver detoxifies the body, synthesizes proteins and other biochemicals needed for digestion and growth, and carries out carbohydrate metabolism. It produces hormones, converts and stores nutrients such as [[Glucose|glucose]] and [[Glycogen|glycogen]], and breaks down red blood cells. As an accessory digestive organ it produces bile, which emulsifies dietary fat; the gallbladder stores and concentrates the bile. The Greek root *hepat-* gives terms such as hepatology and [[Hepatitis|hepatitis]].

A human liver is a dark reddish-brown, wedge-shaped organ that normally weighs about 1.5 kg and is about 15 cm wide. It has two lobes when viewed from above and four (left, right, caudate, and quadrate) from below, and is functionally divided into eight segments. Its dual blood supply comes from the hepatic artery, carrying oxygen-rich blood, and the portal vein, carrying blood rich in digested nutrients from the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, and pancreas. These vessels lead through sinusoids to hepatic lobules, the functional units, each made up of millions of hepatocytes, the basic metabolic cells.

How to compensate for the absence of liver function in the long term is not known. Liver dialysis can be used in the short term, no artificial liver has been developed for long-term replacement, and as of 2018 liver transplantation is the only option for complete liver failure.

Fastening device released by a physical key, keycard, or [[Fingerprint|fingerprint]], or by secret information such as a [[Password|password]], and the key that operates it. A typical key is a small piece of metal with a bit that slides into the keyway and a bow that allows torque to be applied. Common metals include [[Brass|brass]], nickel silver, and [[Steel|steel]]. Locks have been in use for over 6,000 years, and an early example was found in the ruins of [[Nineveh]], the capital of ancient [[Assyria]]. In 1778 Robert Barron invented the lever tumbler lock, which Jeremiah Chubb greatly improved in 1818. Opening a lock without a key is called lock picking.

ENTRY:
Chamber for raising and lowering boats between stretches of water at different levels on rivers and [[Canal|canals]]. Locks make a river more navigable or allow a canal to cross uneven land. The most common type is the pound lock, with a watertight chamber, a gate at each end, and gear to fill and empty the chamber. Pound locks first appeared in medieval China during the [[Song dynasty]] (960–1279). In 984, the tax administrator Qiao Weiyue installed a pair of sluice gates 250 feet apart, effectively creating a pound lock. The first true European pound lock was built in 1396 at Damme near [[Bruges]], Belgium. Passing through a lock usually takes 10 to 20 minutes, depending on the size of the lock and the water level on arrival.

Self-propelled railway vehicle that provides the motive power for a train, also called the engine of a train. Locomotives traditionally pulled trains from the front, but push–pull operation is now common, and longer freight trains use distributed power, with locomotives at the front, rear and intermediate points under the control of the leading unit. Locomotives draw power from fuel such as wood, coal, petroleum or natural gas, or from an outside source of electricity. The first full-scale working [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]] was built by Richard Trevithick in 1802. In 1812 Matthew Murray's Salamanca first ran and is generally regarded as the first commercially successful locomotive. [[George Stephenson]] built Locomotion No. 1 for the Stockton & Darlington Railway, the first public steam railway. Steam locomotives remained the most common type until after [[World War II]], when they were less efficient than diesel and electric types and needed larger crews.
