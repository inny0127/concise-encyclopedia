Order of mammals comprising lemurs, lorises, galagos, tarsiers, monkeys, and apes.

Primates are divided into the strepsirrhines, which include lemurs, galagos, and lorisids, and the haplorhines, which include tarsiers and simians (monkeys and apes). They arose 74–63 million years ago from small terrestrial [[Mammal|mammals]] adapted to life in tropical forests. Many characteristics reflect adaptation to the treetops: large brains relative to body size, binocular and [[Color vision|color vision]], shoulder girdles permitting wide movement of the upper limbs, and, in most species, opposable thumbs. Reliance on visual acuity is greater, and on the [[Sense of smell|sense of smell]], dominant in most mammals, lesser. Madame Berthe's mouse lemur weighs 30 g, and the eastern gorilla over 200 kg. There are 376–524 living species, depending on the classification used; over 25 were described in the 2000s, 36 in the 2010s, and six in the 2020s.

All species have adaptations for climbing trees, though some, including [[Gorilla|gorillas]], humans, and [[Baboon|baboons]], are mainly ground-dwelling. Primates are among the most social animals, forming pairs, family groups, one-male harems, and multi-male, multi-female groups. They develop more slowly than similarly sized mammals, mature later, and live longer. Non-human primates have been recorded using tools, and communicate by facial and hand gestures, smells, and vocalizations.

The name derives from Latin *primas*, "one of the first", and was given by [[Carl Linnaeus]], who considered the order the "highest" of animals.

Close contact between humans and non-human primates can transmit zoonotic diseases, including herpes, measles, ebola, rabies, and hepatitis. About 60% of species are threatened with extinction, chiefly by [[Deforestation|deforestation]], hunting, and clearing of tropical forest for agriculture.

ENTRY:
Natural number greater than 1 that is not a product of two smaller natural numbers.

A natural number greater than 1 that is not prime is called composite. The number 5 is prime because its only factorizations, 1 × 5 and 5 × 1, involve 5 itself, while 4 is composite because 4 = 2 × 2. Equivalently, a prime has exactly two positive divisors, 1 and itself, so 1 is not prime. Primes are central to [[Number theory|number theory]] because of the [[Fundamental theorem of arithmetic]]: every natural number greater than 1 is either prime or a product of primes that is unique up to order.

The earliest surviving records of the study of primes come from ancient Greek mathematicians. [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]] (c. 300 BC) proves that there are infinitely many primes. The [[Sieve of Eratosthenes]] is still used to list primes. [[Fibonacci]] brought Islamic innovations to Europe, and his *Liber Abaci* (1202) was the first work to describe trial division for testing primality. In 1640 [[Pierre de Fermat]] stated Fermat's little theorem without proof.

No known simple formula separates primes from composites, but their distribution can be modeled statistically. The [[Prime number theorem]], proven at the end of the 19th century, says roughly that the probability of a randomly chosen large number being prime is inversely proportional to its number of digits.

Trial division is simple but slow. The Miller–Rabin test is fast but has a small chance of error. Unsolved questions include Goldbach's conjecture, that every even integer greater than 2 is the sum of two primes, and the twin prime conjecture. Primes underlie [[Public-key cryptography|public-key cryptography]], which relies on the difficulty of factoring large numbers into prime factors.

Theorem describing the asymptotic distribution of [[Prime number|prime numbers]] among the positive integers. It states that the prime-counting function π(x), the number of primes less than or equal to x, is asymptotically equivalent to x / log x, in the sense that their ratio approaches 1 as x grows without bound. Consequently the average gap between consecutive primes among the first N integers is roughly log N. Among positive integers of at most 1000 digits, about one in 2300 is prime.

Flowers are bisexual and radially symmetrical, with a stamen opposite each petal, and the ovules are attached to a central axis without partitions. [[Leaf|Leaves]] are simple and usually lack stipules, often forming a rosette at the base of the stem. Seeds develop in capsules that split apart to release them, and the family includes garden favorites and wildflowers.

Transparent optical element with flat, polished surfaces that [[Refraction|refract]] light. At least one surface must be angled, since elements with only two parallel surfaces are windows. The most familiar type is the triangular prism. Prisms are made from transparent materials such as glass, acrylic and [[Fluorite|fluorite]]. A dispersive prism splits white light into its spectral colors, the colors of the [[Rainbow|rainbow]], a phenomenon demonstrated by Isaac Newton. ENTRY:
Condition or claim of an individual or group to be free from unwanted access, observation, or disclosure, and to determine how far they and information about them are exposed to others.

The concept has no single accepted definition. Lawyers, philosophers, and social scientists have understood it as a right to be let alone, as limited access to the self, as secrecy, or as control over personal information.

Distinctions between public and private life appear in many ancient societies, but privacy emerged as a distinct legal claim only in the 19th century, when printing and photographic technologies and the popular press prompted calls for protection. In "The Right to Privacy" (1890), the Boston lawyers Samuel D. Warren and Louis Brandeis argued that the [[Common law|common law]] should recognize a general "right to be let alone". In the second half of the 20th century the spread of computers shifted attention to the collection and processing of personal data, producing data protection law that began in Western Europe in the 1970s and has since spread worldwide.

Privacy is protected as a [[Human rights|human right]] by Article 12 of the [[Universal Declaration of Human Rights]], Article 17 of the [[International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights]], and regional instruments such as Article 8 of the European Convention on Human Rights. European law tends to treat privacy as an aspect of personal dignity and regulates personal data comprehensively, whereas U.S. law has traditionally emphasized liberty against the state and regulates the private sector by sector.

Privacy overlaps with but differs from [[Security|security]]: information security protects data against unauthorized access, while privacy also concerns whether data should be collected and used at all. The [[Internet]], mobile phones, and large-scale data analysis have intensified debate over [[Surveillance|surveillance]] by governments and corporations. Responses include legislation, litigation, and privacy-enhancing technologies such as [[Encryption|encryption]].

Computer network that uses [[IP address|private IP addresses]], commonly in homes, offices and enterprises. Both [[IPv4]] and [[IPv6]] define private address ranges. Because most Internet service providers allocate only one public IPv4 address per residence, a network address translator usually connects several devices to the Internet. Private addresses are not allocated to any organization, anyone may use them, and they cannot be routed across the public Internet.
