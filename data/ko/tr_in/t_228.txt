ENTRY:
Earth's only [[Natural satellite|natural satellite]], a differentiated, terrestrial body with a diameter of 3,474 km.

The Moon orbits Earth in an elliptical path, completing an orbit relative to the Earth and Sun every 29.5 days (a lunar month) and relative to the stars every 27.3 days. Gravitational attraction between the two bodies produces tides on Earth and has pulled the Moon into [[Tidal locking|tidal locking]], so that it always shows the same near side to Earth. Libration makes about 59% of the surface visible from Earth.

Its mass is 1.2% that of Earth, and its diameter is roughly one-quarter of Earth's. It is larger and more massive than any known dwarf planet and is the fifth-largest moon in the Solar System, the largest relative to its parent planet. Its surface gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's, second-highest among moons after Io. It has only a minuscule hydrosphere, atmosphere, and magnetic field. The surface is covered with regolith dust and marked by impact craters, some with bright rays. The Moon was volcanically active until 1.2 billion years ago; lava filling ancient craters, mostly on the thinner near side, formed the dark basaltic plains called maria ('seas'). The reflectance of its surface is comparable to that of asphalt, yet it is the brightest object in Earth's night sky.

Isotope dating of lunar samples suggests the Moon formed in the first 200 million years after the Solar System's origin. The prevailing theory holds that a giant impact of a Mars-sized body named [[Theia (hypothetical planet)|Theia]] with the proto-Earth ejected material that accreted into the Moon. Its apparent size matches the Sun's, so it covers the Sun completely during a total solar eclipse, and when it passes through Earth's shadow a [[Lunar eclipse|lunar eclipse]] occurs. Its changing illuminated portion produces the lunar phases.

The first spaceflights to another body were to the Moon: Luna 1 flew past in 1959 and Luna 2 struck it, both launched by the [[Soviet Union]]; Luna 9 made the first soft landing in 1966, and Luna 10 the first orbital insertion. Humans first orbited it on Apollo 8 on December 24, 1968, and landed on [[Apollo 11]] on July 20, 1969. By 1972 six Apollo missions had landed twelve people, making the Moon the only celestial body beyond Earth that humans have visited. Plans to return, including the Artemis program and its Artemis II crewed flyby of 2026, are driven partly by robotic confirmation of water on the Moon.

Opiate analgesic found naturally in [[Opium]], a resin from the latex of opium poppies (*[[Papaver somniferum]]*). It is used mainly for severe acute and chronic [[Pain]], including pain from myocardial infarction, kidney stones and labor. It acts directly on the central nervous system, and dependence, tolerance and withdrawal may develop with repeated use. Common side effects include drowsiness, nausea and constipation; serious ones include decreased respiratory effort and low blood pressure. German pharmacist Friedrich Sertürner first isolated it in 1804, and Merck began marketing it in 1827. The name honors Morpheus, the god of dreams. Approximately 523 tons were produced in 2013, and about 70% was used to make other [[Opioid|opioids]] such as [[Heroin]]. It is on the [[World Health Organization]]'s List of Essential Medicines.

ENTRY:
Branch of [[Linguistics]] that studies how words are formed and how they relate to one another. Morphology analyzes words in terms of [[Morpheme|morphemes]], the smallest units with independent meaning or grammatical function. A root such as *catch* can be a word by itself, while an affix such as *-ing* appears only in larger words. The field also examines how words behave as parts of speech and how they are inflected for number, tense and aspect. Morphology operates at a larger scale than [[Phonology]] and a smaller scale than [[Syntax]]. Ancient Indian analysis dates to [[Pāṇini]], who formulated 3,959 rules of [[Sanskrit]] morphology. The term was introduced into linguistics by August Schleicher in 1859.

ENTRY:
Telecommunications method that encodes text as standardized sequences of two signal durations, called dots and dashes or dits and dahs. A dah lasts three times as long as a dit, and the letters of a word are separated by a space of three dits. The ITU International Morse code covers the 26 [[Latin alphabet|Latin letters]], the numerals 0 to 9, some punctuation and procedural signals, with no distinction between upper and lower case. [[Samuel Morse]] devised a preliminary code, but the alphabet was developed by Alfred Vail, whose version was used for commercial [[Telegraphy]] in North America, while Friedrich Gerke's revision became the European standard. Morse code is usually sent by on-off keying of an electric current, [[Radio]] waves, light or sound. It remains in use for distress signals such as SOS and in [[Amateur radio]].

Short-barreled [[Artillery]] piece that fires explosive shells in high arcing trajectories. A modern mortar is usually a lightweight, man-portable, muzzle-loaded tube fixed to a base plate and bipod, used for indirect close fire support. Historically, mortars were heavy siege artillery. The earliest reported use was in a 1413 naval battle in Korea, where gunsmiths used gourd-shaped wan'gu mortars. In 1701 Baron Menno van Coehoorn invented a transportable mortar that fired a shell fused by hot gases on firing, which led to the bomb vessel. Mortars were heavily used in the [[American Civil War]], and at the 1863 Siege of Vicksburg [[Ulysses S. Grant|Ulysses S. Grant]] reported making them from logs. Short-range trench mortars were fired from frontline trenches in [[World War I]].

Extinct genus of large marine [[Lizard|lizards]] from the Late Cretaceous. Its first fossils were skulls found in a [[Chalk]] quarry near Maastricht and were initially thought to be crocodiles or whales. In 1808 [[Georges Cuvier]] concluded that it was a giant marine lizard, an idea that helped support the developing concept of [[Extinction]]. The largest species, *M. hoffmanni*, may reach 12 meters. It had paddle-like limbs, a powerful tail fluke and dozens of large teeth. Its diet probably included fish, sharks, [[Cephalopod|cephalopods]], birds and other marine reptiles. Its exact relationship to modern lizards and snakes remains debated.
