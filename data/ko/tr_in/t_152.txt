Development of the central European region now forming the Federal Republic of Germany, from the Paleolithic to the present. Germanic tribes resisted Roman annexation, and in 962 Otto I became the first emperor of the [[Holy Roman Empire]]. The Reformation, begun by [[Martin Luther]] after 1517, divided the empire along religious lines, and the [[Thirty Years' War]] (1618–48) brought great destruction. The Holy Roman Empire dissolved in 1806 during the Napoleonic Wars. [[Otto von Bismarck]] unified Germany as the German Empire in 1871. After defeat in World War I, the Weimar Republic was established. In 1933 [[Adolf Hitler]] established a totalitarian regime, and the Nazis committed the Holocaust, which killed 11 million people, including 6 million Jews. After 1945 Germany was divided into the NATO-aligned West Germany and the Warsaw Pact-aligned East Germany. The [[Berlin Wall]] was opened in 1989, and the two states were reunited.

Study of how building traditions, regions, and styles have changed over time.

Architecture is thought to begin in the basic human need for shelter and protection. The term generally refers to buildings but also covers urbanism, civil engineering, and naval, military, and landscape architecture. Great apes build sleeping nests, and the earliest hominins may have built nests or temporary enclosures. The earliest confirmed purpose-built structures are at Terra Amata in France, with the earliest evidence of artificial fire, c. 400,000 years ago. Where bone was available, structures survive better, as in the mammoth-bone dwellings at Mezhirich dated to 15,000 years ago. The Neolithic period (10,000–2000 BC) brought larger, permanent communities and new styles of building and settlement.

Mesopotamia produced ziggurats, step-pyramid temples such as the [[Ziggurat of Ur]], and Egypt the [[Great Pyramid of Giza]], a pharaonic tomb. Greek and Roman builders developed the Doric, Ionic, and Corinthian orders, seen in the [[Parthenon]]. The Byzantine [[Hagia Sophia]] combined a vast dome with mosaics, while Islamic architecture developed mosques with minarets and geometric patterns, and Gothic cathedrals used pointed arches, ribbed vaults, and flying buttresses. The Renaissance revived classical forms. Brunelleschi's dome crowned the [[Florence Cathedral]], and Michelangelo worked on St. Peter's Basilica. The Baroque style used dramatic ornament and curved facades, as at the [[Palace of Versailles]]. Neoclassicism later returned to simplified Greek and Roman motifs. Steel, cast iron, tile, reinforced concrete, and glass helped [[Art Nouveau]] appear and made Beaux Arts more grandiose. Modernists such as Louis Sullivan, who coined "form follows function," and the [[Bauhaus]] movement rejected ornament, as in the Bauhaus building at Dessau (1926).

Study of objects made by humans, valued chiefly for their aesthetic visual form.

Such objects serve spiritual, narrative, philosophical, symbolic, decorative, and functional purposes. They can be classified as fine or applied arts, or by medium, such as architecture, sculpture, painting, film, photography, and graphic arts. The field is often told as a chronology of masterpieces, but it can also include folk art and craft.

Among the first art objects are decorative artifacts from Middle Stone Age Africa, including containers that may have held paints as far back as 100,000 years ago. Small prehistoric statuettes known as Venus figurines include the Venus of Hohle Fels and the [[Venus of Willendorf]]. Carved figurines and the oldest musical instruments found so far, dated between 43,000 and 35,000 BC, come from caves in the Swabian Jura. The best-known prehistoric works are the Paleolithic [[Cave painting|cave paintings]] of animals at Lascaux in France. Several hundred decorated caves are known, dating c. 38,000–12,000 BC, mostly in France and Spain.

In [[Mesopotamia]], the first cities and the earliest writing appeared in the 4th millennium BC. [[Sumer]] produced ziggurats, cylinder seals, and [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing, and Assyria is best known for detailed stone reliefs of court life, hunting, and battle. In Babylon, the Ishtar Gate was covered with blue glazed bricks and reliefs of dragons, bulls, and lions, and the [[Code of Hammurabi]] was inscribed on a stele. After conquering Babylon in the mid-6th century BC, the Achaemenid Empire made [[Persepolis]] its capital, with art that drew on elements from across the empire.

Later periods include the [[Renaissance]] (c. 1400–1600), which revived classical antiquity through humanism, perspective, and naturalism, followed by the Baroque and Rococo.
