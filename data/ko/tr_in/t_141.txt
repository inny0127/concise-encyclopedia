German physicist and mathematician. In 1845, while still a student, he formulated two laws of electrical circuits, now known as [[Kirchhoff's circuit laws]]. He coined the term [[Black-body radiation|black body]] in 1860 and proposed a law of thermal radiation in 1859. With chemist Robert Bunsen, he improved Fraunhofer's spectroscope, using it to show in 1859 that the [[Sun]] contains [[Sodium]]. The pair discovered [[Caesium]] and [[Rubidium]] in 1861. Kirchhoff formalized three laws describing the light emitted by incandescent objects, work that helped establish [[Spectroscopy]]. In 1857 he calculated that an electric signal in a resistanceless wire travels at the speed of light. He spent his later career at the University of [[Berlin]] from 1875 until his death.

Ionized region of interstellar hydrogen, typically within a [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]] in which stars have formed. The short-lived blue stars it contains emit copious [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light that ionizes the surrounding gas. H II regions range from one to hundreds of light-years across, with densities from a few to about a million particles per cubic centimeter. They may give birth to thousands of stars over several million years, until [[Supernova|supernova]] explosions and stellar winds disperse the remaining gas. The [[Orion Nebula]], the first such object discovered, was observed by Nicolas-Claude Fabri de Peiresc in 1610. They are concentrated in the spiral arms of galaxies such as the [[Milky Way]], and extragalactic H II regions, such as NGC 604 in the [[Triangulum Galaxy]], help determine the distances and chemical composition of galaxies.

Main industrial method of making [[Ammonia|ammonia]] from nitrogen and hydrogen. Also called the Haber–Bosch process, it passes the gases over a solid iron catalyst at high pressure and temperature. Most of the ammonia is made into nitrogen fertilizer, directly or as [[Ammonium nitrate|ammonium nitrate]] and [[Urea|urea]]. The German chemists [[Fritz Haber]] and Carl Bosch developed it in the first decade of the 20th century; Haber showed it working in the laboratory in 1909, and Bosch scaled it up for BASF. Most of the hydrogen comes from [[Natural gas|natural gas]]. In 2018 the process produced about 230 million tonnes of ammonia, but it uses 1–2% of the world's energy and causes about 3% of global [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] emissions.
