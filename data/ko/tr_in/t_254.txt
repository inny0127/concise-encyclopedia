ENTRY:
Rare, lustrous silvery-white metal and [[Chemical element|chemical element]] with atomic number 46 and symbol Pd. English chemist William Hyde Wollaston discovered it and named it after the asteroid 2 Pallas, itself named for an epithet of the Greek goddess [[Athena]]. Palladium, [[Platinum]], [[Rhodium]], ruthenium, iridium and osmium form the platinum group metals, which have similar chemistry. Palladium has the lowest melting point and is the least dense of them. It is soft and ductile, does not tarnish in air, and dissolves readily in [[Aqua regia|aqua regia]]. More than half of supply goes into [[Catalytic converter|catalytic converters]], which convert as much as 90% of harmful exhaust gases into nontoxic substances. It is also used in electronics, dentistry, medicine, hydrogen purification and jewelry. Ore deposits are rare; the most extensive lie in South Africa, Montana, Ontario and Russia. Recycling, mostly of scrapped catalytic converters, is also a major source.

Small wild cat of Central Asian mountains, also called the manul. Pallas's cat (*Otocolobus manul*) has long, dense, light gray fur and low-set rounded ears. Its head-and-body length is 46–65 cm (18–26 in), with a bushy tail of 21–31 cm (8.3–12.2 in). It inhabits rocky montane grasslands and shrublands from the Caucasus and Iran to Mongolia and Tibet, where it shelters in rock crevices and burrows and preys mainly on lagomorphs and rodents. ENTRY:
Interdisciplinary medical approach to relieving and preventing suffering in people with serious, complex and often terminal illnesses. The term comes from Latin *palliare*, to cloak. Palliative care aims to optimize quality of life and reduce suffering, and it can be given alongside curative treatment. Since the 2000s the [[World Health Organization]] (WHO) has advocated applying its principles early in any chronic, ultimately fatal illness, rather than only at the end of life. Care is ideally delivered by teams that may include physicians, nurses, therapists, psychologists, social workers, chaplains and dietitians, in hospitals, outpatient clinics or at home. Historically focused on incurable [[Cancer|cancer]], it is now also applied to severe [[Heart failure|heart failure]], [[Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease|chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]] and [[Multiple sclerosis|multiple sclerosis]]. An estimated 40 million people a year need palliative care, about 78% of them in low- and middle-income countries, but only 14% of that population can receive it.

Ancient oasis city in central Syria and a caravan center on the [[Silk Road]]. Archaeological finds date to the Neolithic period, and documents first mention the city in the early 2nd millennium BC. Its merchants, the Palmyrenes, grew wealthy from trade caravans and founded colonies across the [[Roman Empire]]; their wealth paid for the Great Colonnade, the Temple of Bel and distinctive tower tombs. Palmyra became a subject of Rome in the 1st century AD. It reached its height in the 260s under King Odaenathus, who defeated the Sasanian emperor Shapur I. His widow [[Zenobia]] rebelled against Rome and established the Palmyrene Empire. In 273 the Roman emperor Aurelian leveled the city, which [[Diocletian]] later restored at a reduced size. In 2015 the [[Islamic State]] captured Palmyra and destroyed large parts of the ancient city; the Syrian Army recaptured it on 2 March 2017.

Artificial waterway 82 km (51 mi) long across the Isthmus of Panama, linking the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. Locks lift ships to Gatun Lake, an artificial freshwater lake 26 m (85 ft) above sea level created by damming the Chagres River, and lower them on the far side. The original locks allow passage of Panamax ships. A third, wider set of locks was built between September 2007 and May 2016, and the expanded canal began commercial operation on 26 June 2016, accommodating larger Neopanamax ships. The canal avoids the long, hazardous route around South America via the [[Drake Passage]]. French work began in 1881 but stopped in 1889. The United States took over in 1904 and opened the canal on 15 August 1914, and Panama took full control in 1999. A single ship passage uses an average of 200,000,000 liters of fresh water, so droughts threaten the canal.

Thin sheet of matted [[Cellulose|cellulose]] fibers, largely derived from lignocellulose, made from a pulp dissolved into a slurry that is drained and dried into sheets. The word derives from Latin *papyrus*, but [[Papyrus|papyrus]], a lamination of natural plant fiber, is a separate technology from paper, which is made from fibers changed by maceration.

Papermaking developed in [[East Asia]] at least as early as AD 105 by the Han court eunuch [[Cai Lun]], although paper-like material from the 2nd century BC has been found in [[China]]. Papyrus was replaced because it was limited geographically to Egypt, more expensive and laborious to produce, and more fragile. Popular history credits the Battle of Talas in 751 with spreading papermaking to the Islamic world, but an archive of 76 paper writings found in Panjikent predates the city's capture by Arabs in 722, and paper was being made in [[Baghdad]] by the late 8th century. Knowledge of paper spread to Europe in the 13th century, where the first water-powered paper mills were built.

Before industrialization the most common paper was rag paper. In the 19th century, industrialization greatly reduced costs; Charles Fenerty of Canada and Friedrich Gottlob Keller of Germany independently developed processes for pulping wood fibers, and with the [[Second Industrial Revolution]] pulpwood paper became dominant. Chemical pulping dissolves lignin; the kraft process, invented in the 1870s, is now the most common and recovers and reuses all inorganic reagents. Mechanical pulping yields more than 95%, but the remaining lignin turns paper yellow and brittle over time. Packaging accounts for about 65% of output, and manufacture contributes to [[Deforestation|deforestation]] if forests are unmanaged.
