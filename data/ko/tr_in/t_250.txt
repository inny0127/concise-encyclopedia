Subdiscipline of chemistry concerned with the structure, properties, and reactions of organic compounds and organic materials, those containing [[Carbon|carbon]] atoms. It includes determining structural formulas, analyzing physical and chemical properties, evaluating reactivity, and the synthesis of natural products, drugs, and polymers.

The field covers [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbons]] (compounds of only carbon and hydrogen) and compounds that also contain oxygen, nitrogen, sulfur, phosphorus, and the halogens. Carbon's valence of four, with single, double, and triple bonds and delocalized electrons, makes organic compounds structurally diverse. They form the basis of all known life and underlie pharmaceuticals, petrochemicals, agrichemicals, lubricants, solvents, plastics, fuels, and explosives. The field overlaps [[Organometallic chemistry|organometallic chemistry]], [[Biochemistry|biochemistry]], medicinal chemistry, polymer chemistry, and materials science.

Before the 18th century, chemists generally believed that compounds from living organisms held a "vital force" (vitalism). Around 1816 Michel Chevreul showed that fats could be chemically changed into new compounds. In 1828 [[Friedrich Wöhler|Friedrich Wöhler]] produced urea from potassium cyanate and ammonium sulfate, a synthesis now generally accepted as disproving vitalism, though Wöhler himself was cautious. [[Justus von Liebig|Justus von Liebig]] is considered one of the field's principal founders. In 1856 William Henry Perkin accidentally produced the dye known as Perkin's mauve, and in 1858 Friedrich August Kekulé and Archibald Scott Couper independently developed the concept of chemical structure.

Bayer first manufactured [[Aspirin|aspirin]] in the last decade of the 19th century. By 1910 Paul Ehrlich's group was developing the arsenic-based Salvarsan against syphilis, beginning chemotherapy. Natural indigo production fell from 19,000 tons in 1897 to 1,000 tons by 1914 thanks to Adolf von Baeyer's synthetic methods. Total synthesis later reached molecules as complex as vitamin B12.

Subclass of [[Chemical compound|chemical compounds]] containing carbon. Little consensus exists on the exact definition, and the only universally accepted one is that organic compounds are the subject matter of [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]]. Generally, any large compound containing a carbon-hydrogen or carbon-carbon bond counts as organic, so [[Alkane|alkanes]] such as [[Ethane|ethane]] and their derivatives are included. Small carbon-containing molecules such as carbon dioxide, [[Cyanide|cyanide]] and carbonates are generally excluded for historical and disciplinary reasons. Carbon's ability to form chains accounts for the millions of known organic compounds. Although they make up a small share of Earth's crust, all known life is based on them. Synthetic organic compounds are ultimately derived from [[Petrochemical|petrochemicals]], mainly hydrocarbons.

Agricultural system that emphasizes naturally occurring, non-synthetic inputs. Also called organic agriculture, ecological farming or biological farming, it uses compost, manure and green manure, and emphasizes crop rotation, companion planting and biological pest control. Organic standards allow some naturally occurring substances, such as pyrethrin, but prohibit most synthetic [[Pesticide|pesticides]] and [[Fertilizer|fertilizers]], [[Genetically modified organism|genetically modified organisms]], and growth hormones. The system originated early in the 20th century. Its standards are set largely by the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM), founded in 1972, and are legally enforced by the [[European Union]] and individual nations. About 96,000,000 hectares were farmed organically worldwide as of 2022, approximately 2 percent of world farmland. Organic farming can benefit local [[Biodiversity|biodiversity]], but its lower yields can increase pressure to convert other land, with negative effects on biodiversity and [[Climate change|climate]].

ENTRY:
Living thing that functions as an individual, a definition that raises more problems than it solves because the concept of an individual is itself difficult. Several criteria have been proposed, few widely accepted. The most common is autonomous reproduction, growth, and [[Metabolism|metabolism]], which would exclude [[Virus|viruses]]: they cannot reproduce, grow, or metabolize on their own, and cannot synthesize the organic compounds of which they are formed.

The English term appeared in the 1660s, from the Ancient Greek *organismos*, related to *organon* ("instrument, implement, tool"). In his 1790 *Critique of Judgment*, [[Immanuel Kant]] defined an organism as "both an organized and a self-organizing being". Other proposed criteria include noncompartmentability, stated by [[Richard Dawkins]] as "the quality of being sufficiently heterogeneous in form to be rendered non-functional if cut in half" (though many organisms can be cut into pieces that regrow); individuality; an immune response; "anti-entropy", the ability to maintain order, first proposed by [[Erwin Schrödinger]]; the capacity to maintain information content, as in [[Claude Shannon]]'s information theory; and having cells. Some scientists consider the concept inadequate for biology.

Colonial organisms are problematic cases. A colony of eusocial insects shows adaptive organization and germ-soma specialization, and a siphonophore is composed of organism-like zooids. David Queller and Joan Strassmann propose cooperation as the defining trait, which would count a [[Lichen|lichen]], a partnership of fungi with algae or cyanobacteria, and the permanent sexual partnership of an [[Anglerfish|anglerfish]] as organisms. Others argue that autonomy, genetic homogeneity, and genetic uniqueness should be examined separately.

Unicellular organisms (protists, bacteria, archaea) consist of a single cell; multicellular organisms (animals, plants, fungi, algae) of many. Organisms are commonly classified into three domains: Bacteria, Archaea, and Eukarya.

Entity comprising one or more people with a particular purpose. Organizations include [[Company|companies]], [[Corporation|corporations]], [[Government|governments]], [[Institution|institutions]], associations and [[Non-governmental organization|non-governmental organizations]]. Some operate secretly, as with secret societies, or illegally, as with criminal organizations. Governments recognize an organization through incorporation, through societal pressure such as advocacy groups, or through its role as a negotiating spokesperson. Jack Knight writes that organizations are a narrow version of institutions, since they contain internal institutions that govern relations among members. Management science holds that most organizations fall into four types: [[Jury|juries]] or committees, ecologies, matrix organizations, and pyramids or hierarchies. Condorcet's jury theorem holds that adding members improves majority decisions only if the average member votes better than chance, so staffing is crucial.

Security-oriented intergovernmental organization with 57 participating states in Europe, North America and Asia. Study of compounds with at least one carbon-metal bond. The field combines aspects of [[Inorganic chemistry|inorganic]] and [[Organic chemistry|organic chemistry]]. Organometallic compounds are used in research and industrial reactions, and as catalysts that increase reaction rates, in producing [[Polymer|polymers]] and pharmaceuticals.

Standard collection of six works by [[Aristotle]] on logic. The works are *Categories*, *On Interpretation*, *Prior Analytics*, *Posterior Analytics*, *Topics* and *On Sophistical Refutations*. The name is Greek for instrument or tool, reflecting the works' role as tools of philosophical inquiry. Their order is not chronological but was chosen to form a well-structured system. *Categories* classifies existence into ten types, from substance and quantity to action and passion. *On Interpretation* introduces the [[Proposition|proposition]], and *Prior Analytics* introduces Aristotle's syllogistic method. Only *Categories* and *On Interpretation* were widely available in the early Middle Ages, translated into Latin by Boethius. The rest reached Western Christendom in the 12th century. [[Averroes|Averroes]] and [[Maimonides|Maimonides]] studied the works in Arabic translations, and [[Thomas Aquinas|Aquinas]] and other scholastic philosophers wrote commentaries on the [[Dialectic|dialectic]] and [[Logic|logic]] they contain.
