Personal [[Armour|armor]] of small metal rings linked together in a pattern to form a mesh. Mail was widely used in European militaries from the 3rd century BC to the 16th century AD, and in Asia, Africa and the Middle East until the 18th century. A coat of mail is often called a hauberk. The earliest surviving examples, dated to the 3rd century BC, were found in the Carpathian Basin, and its invention is commonly credited to the [[Celts]]. Its use declined in the 16th and 17th centuries as [[Plate armour|plate armor]] and more advanced [[Firearm|firearms]] were developed. Mail is still used in industries such as butchery and as protection against the bites of [[Shark|sharks]].

ENTRY:
Formula in [[Calculus|calculus]] for differentiating the composition of two differentiable functions. If z depends on y, and y depends on x, the derivative of z with respect to x is the product of the derivatives dz/dy and dy/dx, so that dz/dx = dz/dy · dy/dx. Archaeological period, also called the Copper Age or Eneolithic, that followed the [[Neolithic]] and preceded the [[Bronze Age]]. Personal quality of magnetic charm, persuasion or appeal. The English word derives from the [[Ancient Greek]] *charisma*, meaning a favor freely given, from *charis*, grace. In Christian theology, charisma is a spiritual gift, an extraordinary power given by the [[Holy Spirit]]; [[Paul the Apostle]] introduced this meaning in 1 Corinthians. In sociology and political science, [[Max Weber]] described charismatic authority, which derives legitimacy from a leader's demonstrated heroic or supernatural capabilities during crises, in contrast to traditional and rational-legal authority. Charisma is studied in [[Psychology]] as a multifaceted trait, and in [[Management]] as a style of leadership. Empirical studies suggest that moderate levels may enhance innovation and unity, while extremes risk overconfidence and manipulation.

King of the Franks (r. 768–814), King of the Lombards from 774, and first emperor of the Carolingian Empire from 800. He united most of Western and Central Europe and was the first recognized emperor to rule from the west since the fall of the Western Roman Empire about three centuries earlier.

The eldest son of Pepin the Short and Bertrada of Laon, he became king of the [[Franks]] in 768 with his brother Carloman I and sole ruler three years later. Continuing his father's policy, he became the chief defender of the papacy and ended Lombard rule in northern Italy in 774. His campaigns brought Bavaria, Saxony, and northern Spain under his rule. He spread Christianity into conquered lands, sometimes by force: the Saxon Wars involved mass executions, including the Massacre of Verden, and forced baptisms.

On Christmas Day 800 he was crowned emperor in Rome by Pope Leo III. Historians debate the coronation's significance, but it brought him into conflict with the Eastern Roman Empire in [[Constantinople]], and he is considered the forerunner of the Holy Roman Emperors. He carried out reforms in administration, law, education, military organization, and religion. His reign began a period of cultural activity known as the Carolingian Renaissance.

He died in 814 and was buried in the Palatine Chapel, now part of Aachen Cathedral, in [[Aachen]], his imperial capital. His empire fragmented after his death among his heirs. Many historians call him the 'Father of Europe,' and several European states and royal houses claim him as a founding figure.

English mathematician, philosopher, inventor and mechanical engineer. Babbage is credited with inventing the first mechanical computer, the difference engine, and is often called the "father of the computer". His analytical engine, programmed with [[Punched card|punched cards]] using a principle borrowed from the Jacquard loom, contained the essential ideas of the modern computer, though he died before many of his designs were completed.
