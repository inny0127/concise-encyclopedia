Pelagic lampriform fish of the family Regalecidae, comprising three species. The giant oarfish (*Regalecus glesne*) is the longest bony fish alive, reaching about 8 m (26 ft). ENTRY:
Medical condition, considered a disease by multiple organizations, in which excess body fat has accumulated to such an extent that it can have negative effects on health. People are classified as obese when their [[Body mass index|body mass index]] (BMI), a person's weight divided by the square of height, is over 30 kg/m²; the range 25–30 kg/m² is defined as overweight. Obesity is correlated with [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular diseases]], [[Diabetes|type 2 diabetes]], obstructive sleep apnea, certain types of [[Cancer|cancer]], and [[Osteoarthritis|osteoarthritis]].

Causes include diet, low physical activity, automation, [[Urbanization|urbanization]], genetic susceptibility, medications, mental disorders, economic policies, and endocrine disorders. BMI is criticized because it ignores lean body mass: more than half of NFL players are classified as obese by BMI, yet their mean body fat percentage is 14%, and 40% of college-aged [[Sumo|sumo]] wrestlers in one study were no longer classified as obese when body fat percentage was used.

Diet and exercise are the main recommended treatments, and medications, a gastric balloon, or surgery may be used when they fail. The [[World Health Organization]] stresses that healthy dietary choices should be the most available, affordable, and accessible options. Many people lose weight, but maintaining the loss long-term is rare.

In 2022, over 1 billion people lived with obesity (879 million adults and 159 million children), more than double the adult cases and four times the child cases registered in 1990. The share of adults who were obese rose from 12.1% in 2012 to 16.2% in 2024, with 18.6% projected by 2030. Obesity is stigmatized in many parts of the world, while some cultures have viewed it as a symbol of wealth and fertility.

Double-reed woodwind instrument. Oboes are usually made of wood, though plastic, resin and hybrid composites are also used. The most common type, the soprano oboe pitched in C, is about 65 cm (25½ in) long, with metal keys, a conical bore and a flared bell. Orchestras tune to a concert A played by the first oboe, because the oboe is the one orchestral instrument that cannot change pitch by moving part of its body. Body of salt water covering approximately 70.8% of Earth. The ocean is conventionally divided into five large bodies of water, also called oceans; in descending order by area they are the [[Pacific Ocean]], the [[Atlantic Ocean]], the [[Indian Ocean]], the Antarctic or [[Southern Ocean]], and the [[Arctic Ocean]]. These are mostly divided into seas, gulfs, and smaller bodies of water. The ocean's volume exceeds 1.3 billion cubic km, and its average salinity is roughly 35 grams of dissolved salts per liter. It contains 97% of Earth's water and is the primary component of the [[Hydrosphere|hydrosphere]], acting as a huge reservoir of heat for Earth's energy budget, carbon cycle, and water cycle, and forming the basis for climate and weather patterns worldwide. It harbors most of Earth's animals and protist life, originated [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]], and still supplies half of Earth's atmospheric oxygen.

In general use "the ocean" and "the sea" are interchangeable. Strictly, a sea is a body of water, generally a division of the world ocean, partly or fully enclosed by land, such as the North Sea or the Red Sea. The term World Ocean was coined in the early 20th century by the Russian oceanographer Yuly Shokalsky. The word ocean comes from Oceanus, the elder of the Titans in classical Greek mythology, whom the ancient Greeks and Romans believed to be the divine personification of an enormous river encircling the world.

Scientists divide the ocean into horizontal and vertical zones. Horizontally, it covers the oceanic crust and, at its margins, the shallow continental shelves. Vertically, the pelagic zone is the open ocean's water column. The photic zone, defined as the depth at which light intensity is only 1% of the surface value (approximately 200 m in the open ocean), is where photosynthesis occurs and is the most biodiverse zone; the deeper ocean is cold and dark.

Surface temperatures can exceed 30 °C in the tropics and fall to about −2 °C near the poles where sea ice forms; deep ocean temperatures range between −2 °C and 5 °C. Temperature and salinity differences, wind, tides, and the Coriolis effect drive currents such as the [[Gulf Stream]], Kuroshio Current, Agulhas Current, and Antarctic Circumpolar Current, which transport water, gases, pollutants, and heat around the world. As the ocean absorbs carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, it undergoes acidification, a drop in pH.

Geological evidence from the Isua Greenstone Belt shows that water existed on Earth 3.8 billion years ago, though the origin of Earth's oceans is unknown. The ocean is the habitat of over 230,000 known species, perhaps over two million in all. It provides ecosystem services, seafood, and transport, but faces marine pollution, overfishing, warming, acidification, and sea level rise.

The first settlers of Australia, New Guinea, and the large islands just to the east arrived more than 60,000 years ago. The rock art of [[Aboriginal Australians]] is the longest continuously practiced artistic tradition in the world. Europeans first explored the region from the 16th century onward: between 1512 and 1526 Portuguese explorers reached the Tanimbar Islands, some of the Caroline Islands, and west New Guinea, followed by Spanish, Dutch, British, and French explorers. On his first voyage, [[James Cook]] went to Tahiti and followed the east coast of Australia for the first time; he later reached the Hawaiian Islands. Britain established a penal colony in Australia in 1788. European settlement significantly altered the social and political landscape, and the Pacific theater saw major action in both world wars.

The name derives from the French *Océanie*, from the Greek word for ocean, and was coined by French geographers in the early 19th century; it reflects the ocean's role in linking the region's parts. Definitions vary. The broadest encompasses the islands between mainland Asia and the Americas, with Zealandia usually considered a microcontinent. The United Nations has used a definition since 1947 based on four subregions, Australasia, Melanesia, Micronesia, and Polynesia, which excludes Indonesia, Japan, the Philippines, Singapore, and Taiwan. In 1973 anthropologists Roger Green and Andrew Pawley proposed the terms Near Oceania and Remote Oceania. The islands generally considered the geographic extremes are the Bonin Islands (Japan), Hawaii (United States), Clipperton Island (France), Easter Island (Chile), and Macquarie Island (Australia).

Long, narrow depression of the sea floor, typically 50 to 100 km (30 to 60 mi) wide. Trenches lie 3 to 4 km (1.9 to 2.5 mi) below the surrounding floor and can extend thousands of kilometers. About 50,000 km (31,000 mi) of them exist, mostly around the [[Pacific Ocean]]. They mark convergent [[Plate tectonics|plate boundaries]], where oceanic lithosphere sinks beneath another plate. The deepest point, the [[Challenger Deep]] in the [[Mariana Trench]], lies 10,994 m (36,070 ft) below sea level. Trenches generally lie about 200 km (120 mi) from a volcanic arc. Fluid trapped in subducting sediments returns to the surface at trenches, supporting unique [[Biome|biomes]] based on chemotrophic microorganisms. Unlike troughs, which have flat bottoms, trenches have a V-shaped profile.

Earth science devoted to the scientific study of the ocean. Also called oceanology, sea science or marine science, it draws on physics, chemistry, biology and geology. Topics include [[Ocean current|ocean currents]], waves, geophysical fluid dynamics, chemical fluxes across the ocean's boundaries, ecosystem dynamics, and [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]] and seabed geology. Oceanographers also draw on astronomy, geography, hydrology and meteorology. Early exploration was mainly for [[Cartography|cartography]] and limited to surfaces, but the Portuguese campaign of Atlantic navigation became an early systematic project, sustained over many decades, that studied the currents and winds of the Atlantic. The HMS Challenger expedition of 1872–1876 measured depths, temperatures and salinities along global routes.

Medium-sized spotted wild cat, *Leopardus pardalis*, native to the southwestern United States, Mexico, Central and South America and the [[Caribbean]] islands of Trinidad and Margarita. Adults reach 40–50 cm (16–20 in) at the shoulders and weigh between 7 and 15.5 kg (15 and 34 lb) on average. Efficient climbers, leapers and swimmers, ocelots prey on small mammals such as [[Armadillo|armadillos]] and [[Opossum|opossums]], mostly at twilight and night. They are solitary and territorial, and females give birth to one to three kittens. Rated Least Concern by the IUCN, the species is threatened by habitat destruction, hunting and traffic accidents.

Family of natural clay earth pigments made of ferric oxide mixed with clay and sand. Colors range from yellow to deep orange or brown. Red ocher takes its tint from [[Hematite|hematite]], an iron oxide. Sienna and umber contain manganese, which makes them darker. Ocher is one of the earliest human pigments: its processing in Africa dates to around 300,000 years ago, broadly coinciding with the emergence of *Homo sapiens*, and its use in Australia dates to around 50,000 years ago. Clays colored with iron oxide from [[Tin|tin]] and [[Copper|copper]] extraction may also be called ocher.

Standard measure of a fuel's resistance to knocking in an [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engine]]. A higher octane number means the fuel can withstand more compression before auto-igniting. It does not indicate energy content or power output, which depend on engine design. Higher-octane fuel suits high-compression [[Gasoline|gasoline]] engines, while [[Diesel engine|diesel engines]] use fuels with lower octane and higher cetane numbers. The octane rating of aviation fuel was crucial to aircraft engines in [[World War II]]. Tetraethyl [[Lead|lead]] additives raised ratings from the 1920s, but were phased out in the [[United States]] from 1973 to 1996.
