Stringed instrument of the [[Lute|lute]] family, classed as a yoke lute. Lyres were used in several ancient cultures around the [[Mediterranean Sea]], and the earliest known examples date to c. 2700 BC in Mesopotamia. The eastern, or flat-based, lyres of the [[Fertile Crescent]] were found in Egypt, Syria, Anatolia and the Levant. The round lyre, which also originated in Syria and Anatolia, died out in the east c. 1750 BC but reappeared in [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]] c. 1700–1400 BC and later spread throughout the [[Roman Empire]]. The round lyre gave rise to the Germanic lyre or rotte, widely used in north-western Europe from pre-Christian to medieval times. In ancient Greece, the instrument accompanied vocal performances and lyric poetry.

Either of two species of ground-dwelling Australian birds in the genus *Menura*. Lyrebirds are named for their outer tail feathers, which are broad and curved in an S-shape that resembles a [[Lyre|lyre]]. They are large [[Passerine|passerine]] birds able to mimic a wide variety of natural and artificial sounds. During courtship the male fans its huge tail. Lyrebirds are ancient Australian animals, and the Australian Museum holds fossils about 15 million years old. Lyrebirds form the family Menuridae, which contains only this genus. The birds have unique plumes of neutral-colored tailfeathers and are among Australia's best-known native birds. Their closest relatives are generally thought to be the scrub-birds, though a link to bowerbirds remains controversial.

ENTRY:
Membrane-bound organelle found in animal cells, though not in [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]], and rarely in [[Plant cell|plant cells]]. They serve as the cell's degradation center, breaking down proteins, polysaccharides and lipids into amino acids, monosaccharides and free fatty acids. Their acidic interior, isolated by a single lipid membrane, contains over 60 different hydrolases, enzymes that work best at low pH. They measure 0.1–1.2 μm and take in extracellular particles by endocytosis and intracellular components by autophagy. They also contribute to plasma membrane repair, energy metabolism, cell signaling and the immune response. Belgian scientist Christian de Duve is credited with discovering lysosomes in the 1950s and was awarded the 1974 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Field-effect transistor with an insulated gate, most commonly made by controlled oxidation of [[Silicon|silicon]]. Complementary pairs of MOS transistors make switching circuits with very low power consumption, as CMOS logic.

Some species have adapted to human settlements, and in urban areas of [[Southeast Asia]] they have become problematic because they can carry transmittable diseases. Their similarity to humans in emotional and cognitive development means they are often used in medical experiments. Most macaque species are listed as vulnerable to critically endangered on the IUCN Red List.

Special administrative region of [[China]] on the western side of the Pearl River estuary, consisting of the Macau Peninsula and the islands of Taipa and Coloane. Tragedy by [[William Shakespeare]], first performed c. 1606. It is Shakespeare's shortest tragedy, first published in the Folio of 1623, and it dramatizes the physically violent and psychological effects of political ambition. A Scottish general receives a prophecy from three witches that he will become king. Spurred by his wife, Lady Macbeth, he murders King Duncan and takes the throne. Racked with guilt and paranoia, he commits further murders and becomes a tyrannical ruler, and the couple's violence leads to their insanity and deaths. Shakespeare drew on the account in *Holinshed's Chronicles* (1587), though the events differ extensively from the history of the real Macbeth, a king of Scotland. Of Shakespeare's plays written during the reign of James I, it contains the most allusions to James, patron of Shakespeare's acting company. Ancient kingdom in the northeastern part of the Greek peninsula, later the dominant state of Hellenistic [[Greece]]. Centered at Aegae and later Pella, it was ruled by the Argead dynasty. Under Philip II (359–336 BC), Macedonia subdued mainland Greece and the Thracian [[Odrysian kingdom]], and it defeated Athens and Thebes at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BC. His son, [[Alexander the Great]], overthrew the [[Achaemenid Empire]] and conquered territory as far as the [[Indus River]]. After Alexander's death in 323 BC, Macedonia remained a Greek cultural and political center. [[Aristotle]], Alexander's tutor, wrote works that became a keystone of Western philosophy. Macedonia's decline began with the rise of [[Rome]]. The monarchy was abolished in 168 BC, and after a short-lived revival in 150–148 BC, the Roman province of Macedonia was established.

ENTRY:
Dimensionless quantity in [[Fluid dynamics|fluid dynamics]], the ratio of flow velocity past a boundary to the local speed of sound. It is named after Austrian physicist [[Ernst Mach]]. At Mach 1 the flow velocity equals the speed of sound; flow below Mach 1 is subsonic, and flow above it is supersonic. Compressibility effects are small when M is below 0.2–0.3 and the flow is quasi-steady and isothermal, allowing simplified incompressible flow equations. In dry air at mean [[Sea level|sea level]] and a standard temperature of 15 °C, the speed of sound is 340.3 meters per second.
