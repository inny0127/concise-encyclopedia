Position fixing by sighting stars, the [[Sun|Sun]] and other bodies. The practice relies on timed angular measurements, or sights, between a body and the visible [[Horizon|horizon]], and it does not depend on dead reckoning or electronic positioning. Two sights give two lines of position that intersect at the observer's location. The simplest method, noon sight navigation, uses one observation of the Sun's altitude at local noon, combined with the time at the [[Prime meridian|prime meridian]], to give a [[Latitude|latitude]] and [[Longitude|longitude]] fix. Despite the dominance of [[Satellite navigation|satellite navigation]], it remains a backup for maritime and aviation safety.

ENTRY:
Conceptual tool in astronomy and navigation. It is an abstract sphere of arbitrarily large radius, concentric with [[Earth|Earth]], onto which objects in the sky are conceived as projected regardless of their distance. The [[Equator|celestial equator]] divides it into northern and southern hemispheres. Because it is so large, parallel lines appear to meet at a single point on it, much as in graphical [[Perspective (graphical)|perspective]]. Nearby objects such as the [[Moon]] show [[Parallax|parallax]], a small offset from a mean position that observers can calculate. Astronomers use the sphere for coordinates analogous to [[Latitude|latitude]] and [[Longitude|longitude]], so that a position such as the Moon's can be given in equatorial coordinates.

Basic structural and functional unit of all forms of life. The term comes from the Latin *cellula*, 'small room'. A biological cell consists of a semipermeable [[Cell membrane|cell membrane]] enclosing [[Cytoplasm|cytoplasm]] that contains genetic material. Most cells are visible only under a [[Microscope|microscope]]. Except for highly differentiated types such as [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]] and gametes, most cells can replicate and synthesize protein, and some are motile. Cells emerged on Earth about four billion years ago.

Cells were discovered by [[Robert Hooke]] in 1665, who named them for their resemblance to the cells of a monastery. Cell theory, developed through the work of Matthias Jakob Schleiden in 1838 and Theodor Schwann in 1839, established the cell as the basic structural unit of plants and animals. It states that all organisms are composed of one or more cells, that cells are the fundamental unit of structure and function, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells.

All organisms are grouped into prokaryotes and eukaryotes. Prokaryotes, which include [[Archaea|archaea]] and [[Bacteria|bacteria]], are single-celled and lack a membrane-bound nucleus, having a nucleoid region instead. Most are the smallest of all organisms, 0.1 to 5.0 μm in diameter; the largest known bacterium, *Thiomargarita magnifica*, averages 1 cm in length and can reach 2 cm. A bacterial cell is enclosed in an envelope, typically a plasma membrane covered by a cell wall of peptidoglycan. Its DNA usually forms a single circular chromosome, and extra genes, such as those for antibiotic resistance, may sit on circular plasmids. Archaea have unique ether-linked membrane lipids, a form of metabolism called methanogenesis, and many extremophile species that thrive in extreme heat, cold, acidity, alkalinity, or salt. No archaean pathogens are known.

Eukaryotes can be single-celled, as in diatoms, yeasts, and some algae, or multicellular, as in animals, plants, most fungi, and seaweed. Their cells can be 2 to 100 times larger in diameter than a typical prokaryotic cell and have a membrane-bound nucleus, the largest organelle. Other organelles present in all eukaryotic cells include the endoplasmic reticulum, ribosomes, the Golgi apparatus, mitochondria, lysosomes, peroxisomes, endosomes, and vesicles. Plant cells also contain chloroplasts, which make sugars by photosynthesis. The diploid cells that form the body of an animal or plant are called somatic cells, which excludes the haploid gametes.
