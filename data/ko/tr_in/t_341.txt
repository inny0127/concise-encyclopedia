Imaginary scenario meant to elucidate or test an argument or theory. A thought experiment is often an experiment that would be hard, impossible or unethical to perform, and it can also be an abstract hypothetical that tests intuitions about morality or other philosophical questions. Geometric model in which three values, called coordinates, are required to determine the position of a point. Most commonly the term means three-dimensional [[Euclidean space]], which models physical space; more general three-dimensional spaces are called 3-manifolds. In classical physics it serves as a model of the universe in which all known matter exists, and in [[General relativity|relativity theory]] it is a local subspace of space-time. Any three directions not lying in one plane may serve as coordinate directions; when pairwise perpendicular, the values are often labeled width, height or depth, and length.

In the [[Cartesian coordinate system]], three axes, usually labeled x, y, and z, are perpendicular to one another at the origin, and each point is given by an ordered triple of real numbers measuring its distance from the planes determined by the other two axes. Cylindrical and spherical coordinates are other common methods. The set of such triples is denoted ℝ³.

[[Aristotle]] held that three dimensions are all there are, since what is divisible in three directions is divisible in all. Books XI to XIII of [[Euclid's Elements|Euclid's *Elements*]] treat solid geometry, including perpendicularity, parallelism, and the five regular Platonic solids. In the 17th century [[René Descartes]] described space with coordinates in *La Géométrie*, and Pierre de Fermat independently developed similar ideas. In the 18th century [[Leonhard Euler]] derived the first analytical geodesic equation and, with Gaspard Monge, laid foundations for differential geometry. In the 19th century [[William Rowan Hamilton]] developed quaternions and coined the terms scalar and vector. Josiah Willard Gibbs later identified the dot and cross products in their own right, and Arthur Cayley applied matrix mathematics to n-dimensional geometry.

Type of reverse [[Fault (geology)|fault]] with a dip of 45 degrees or less. A thrust fault is a break in the [[Earth's crust]] across which older rocks are pushed above younger rocks. If the fault plane lies at a shallow angle, often less than 15 degrees from horizontal, and the overlying block is displaced far, the fault is called an overthrust. Human rights organizations report restrictions on religious practice, expression and assembly, although the extent of these restrictions is debated.

Parasitic [[Arachnid|arachnid]] of the order Ixodida, part of the mite superorder Parasitiformes. Adult ticks are about 3 to 5 mm long but grow larger when engorged with blood, and have eight legs. Ticks feed on the blood of mammals, birds, and sometimes reptiles and amphibians, and are widely distributed, especially in warm, humid climates. ENTRY:
Gravitational state in which a body's rotation period matches its orbital period around a larger partner. Tidal locking occurs when gravitational interaction slows a body's [[Rotation|rotation]] until its rotation rate no longer changes over a complete orbit. As a result, the [[Moon]] always shows the same face to [[Earth]]. Usually only the satellite is locked to the larger body, but where both masses and distances are relatively small, each can be locked to the other, as with [[Pluto]] and [[Charon (moon)|Charon]]. Friction from tidal bulges dissipates rotational energy as heat, and the process that ends in locking is called tidal despinning. Some sources use the term only for 1:1 synchronous rotation, while others include resonances such as that of [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]], which completes three rotations for every two orbits of the [[Sun]].

Periodic rise and fall of sea level, caused mainly by the gravity of the [[Moon]] and the [[Sun]]. Tides result from differential gravitational forces combined with the orbital motion of the Earth–Moon system and the [[Earth's rotation|Earth's rotation]]. [[Isaac Newton]] described the gravitational cause mathematically in 1687. Most coasts have ocean tides that rise and fall about twice a day, though the shape of ocean basins, [[Coast|coastlines]] and friction modify their height and timing. Tidal range varies over a two-week cycle: near [[Full moon|full]] and new moons, when the Sun and Moon align, spring tides produce higher high waters and lower low waters, while at quarter moons neap tides are milder. Storm surges caused by [[Wind|wind]] and barometric pressure can also change sea level, especially in shallow seas and near coasts.

Archipelago off the southern tip of South America, across the [[Strait of Magellan]]. Its main island, Isla Grande de Tierra del Fuego, covers 73,746 sq km (28,473 sq mi). The western part, about two-thirds of the archipelago, belongs to [[Chile]], and the eastern part to Argentina. Its southernmost point, [[Cape Horn]], lies just north of 56° south.
