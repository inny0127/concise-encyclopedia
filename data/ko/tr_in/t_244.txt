Region of the [[Nile]] valley in northern Sudan and southern Egypt, between the confluence of the [[Blue Nile]] and [[White Nile]] near [[Khartoum]] and the First Cataract south of [[Aswan]]. It was the seat of the Kerma culture, one of ancient Africa's earliest civilizations, which lasted from around 2500 BC until the [[New Kingdom of Egypt]] conquered it under Thutmose I around 1500 BC. Reunited under the Khedivate of Egypt in the 19th century, it is now split between Egypt and Sudan. The study of Nubia, combining archaeology, history and philology, is called Nubiology.

ENTRY:
Splitting of an atomic nucleus into two or more smaller nuclei, releasing large amounts of energy. The process often produces [[Neutron|neutrons]] and [[Gamma ray|gamma rays]]. The fission of one [[Uranium-235]] atom releases about 100 million times the energy of burning one [[Carbon|carbon]] atom in air to produce CO₂. [[Otto Hahn]] and Fritz Strassmann proved in December 1938 that fission had occurred, and [[Lise Meitner]] and Otto Frisch explained it theoretically in January 1939. Hahn and Strassmann predicted additional neutrons, opening the possibility of a chain reaction. Three isotopes, U-233, U-235 and Pu-239, can sustain such a chain reaction, which allows reactors to release energy in a controlled way and weapons to release it uncontrollably within about a microsecond. Most fissions are binary, producing two charged fragments, but three fragments form occasionally (2 to 4 times per 1000 events).

Combination of atomic nuclei into a larger nucleus, with release or absorption of energy. Use of nuclear reactions to produce electricity. It can be obtained from [[Nuclear fission|nuclear fission]], nuclear decay, and [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]], but the vast majority comes from fission of [[Uranium|uranium]] and [[Plutonium|plutonium]] in nuclear power plants. Decay powers niche applications such as space probes like [[Voyager 2]]. Reactors producing controlled fusion have operated since 1958 but have yet to generate net power.

Fission was discovered in 1938, and a self-sustaining chain reaction was confirmed experimentally in 1939. The first human-made reactor, Chicago Pile-1, achieved criticality on December 2, 1942, at the [[University of Chicago]] as part of the [[Manhattan Project]]. A reactor first generated electricity on December 20, 1951, at EBR-I near Arco, Idaho.  The pressurized water reactor, developed by the U.S. Navy for submarines, became the standard for power generation. The Obninsk plant in the USSR became the first to supply a power grid on June 27, 1954, and Calder Hall in England became the first commercial station in 1956.

Global installed capacity reached 100 GW in the late 1970s and 300 GW by 1990. The 1979 Three Mile Island accident and the 1986 [[Chernobyl disaster]] brought increased regulation and public opposition. Nuclear plants supplied 2,602 terawatt hours of electricity in 2023, about 9% of global generation and the second largest low-carbon source after hydroelectricity. As of November 2025, 416 civilian fission reactors operated worldwide with a capacity of 376 GW. The United States has the largest fleet.

Supporters point to one of the lowest fatality rates per unit of energy and to the absence of direct greenhouse gas emissions. The anti-nuclear movement cites radiological hazards and accidents such as Fukushima in 2011, and contends that nuclear power is too expensive compared with alternatives.

Mathematical object used to count, measure, order, and label. The most basic examples are the [[Natural number|natural numbers]]: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and so forth. Numbers are represented in language by number words and by symbols called numerals; "eleven" is a number word and "11" the corresponding numeral. Because only a limited list of symbols can be memorized, a [[Numeral system|numeral system]] represents any number in an organized way. The most common is the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], a decimal system that displays any non-negative integer using ten digits. Numerals serve for counting, labeling (telephone numbers), ordering (serial numbers), and codes (as with [[ISBN|ISBNs]]).

Over the centuries the notion of number was extended to include zero, [[Negative number|negative numbers]], [[Rational number|rational numbers]] such as one half, [[Real number|real numbers]] such as the [[Square root|square root]] of 2 and pi (π), and complex numbers, which extend the reals with a square root of −1 (i). Calculations use the operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and exponentiation; their study is called arithmetic, a term that may also refer to number theory, the study of the properties of numbers.

Bones with cut marks, possibly tallies, include the Lebombo bone (about 43,000 years old) and the Ishango bone (about 22,000 to 30,000 years old), though their interpretation as arithmetic artifacts is disputed. Its key was the symbol for zero, developed by ancient Indian mathematicians around 500 AD.

The first known recorded use of zero as an integer dates to AD 628, in the *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* of Brahmagupta, who treated 0 as a number and gave rules for its use with positive and negative numbers. By 130 AD Ptolemy used a symbol for zero within a sexagesimal system. The Maya developed zero as a cardinal number, and the concept reached Europe through Islamic sources around the year 1000. The abstract concept of negative numbers was recognized as early as 100–50 BC in China, in *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*.

Branch of pure mathematics devoted primarily to the study of the [[Integer|integers]] and arithmetic functions. Number theorists study [[Prime number|prime numbers]], together with objects constructed from integers, such as rational numbers, or defined as generalizations of them, such as algebraic integers. Integers can be considered in themselves or as solutions to equations (Diophantine geometry). Analytic number theory studies objects such as the [[Riemann zeta function]] that encode properties of integers and primes, while Diophantine approximation studies how irrational numbers can be approximated by fractions. Elementary number theory uses elementary methods, and algebraic number theory employs structures such as fields and rings.

The subject is one of the oldest branches of mathematics, alongside geometry. Plimpton 322, a broken clay tablet dated c. 1800 BC, lists Pythagorean triples. Euclid devoted part of his *Elements* to primes and divisibility, giving the [[Euclidean algorithm]] and a proof implying the infinitude of primes. [[Diophantus]] of Alexandria, who probably lived in the 3rd century AD, wrote the *Arithmetica*, a collection of problems seeking rational solutions to polynomial equations.

Number theory deals with statements that are simple to understand but very difficult to solve. [[Fermat's Last Theorem]] was proved 358 years after its original formulation, and Goldbach's conjecture has remained unsolved since the 18th century. [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] remarked, "Mathematics is the queen of the sciences—and number theory is the queen of mathematics." The field was regarded as pure mathematics with no outside applications until the 1970s, when prime numbers became the basis of public-key cryptography algorithms such as the [[RSA cryptosystem]].

Biochemical and physiological process by which an organism uses food and water to support its life.
