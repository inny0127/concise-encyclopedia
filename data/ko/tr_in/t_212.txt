Polish-born French physicist and chemist who pioneered research on radioactivity, a term she coined. Born Maria Salomea Skłodowska in [[Warsaw]], then in the Russian Empire, she studied at the clandestine Flying University, which admitted women, and in 1891 followed her elder sister Bronisława to Paris, where she earned her higher degrees. In 1895 she married [[Pierre Curie]], with whom she conducted her early research. She shared the 1903 [[Nobel Prize]] in Physics with Pierre and [[Henri Becquerel]] for their joint researches on the radioactivity phenomena Becquerel had discovered. Pierre died in a Paris street accident in 1906, and she became the first woman to hold a professorship at the University of Paris that year.

In 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of the elements [[Radium|radium]] and [[Polonium|polonium]], by the isolation of radium and the study of its nature and compounds. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the first person to win twice, and the only person to win in two different scientific fields; she and Pierre were the first married couple to win. She named polonium after her native Poland and never lost her sense of Polish identity, teaching her daughters Polish and taking them on visits there.

During [[World War I]] she developed mobile radiography units, known as *petites Curies*, to provide [[X-ray]] services to field hospitals.

She died in 1934 of aplastic anemia, likely caused by radiation exposure in her research and radiological work. In 1995 she became the first woman entombed in the Paris Panthéon on her own merits. The synthetic element curium is named in her honor.

Inefficient allocation of goods and services by a free market. In neoclassical economics, market failure is a situation in which the allocation is not Pareto efficient, often leading to a net loss of economic value. The term was first used by economists in 1958, although the concept has been traced to the Victorian writers [[John Stuart Mill]] and Henry Sidgwick. Market failures are associated with public goods, externalities, information asymmetries, failures of competition, principal–agent problems and unequal bargaining power. A [[Monopoly|monopoly]] can block mutually beneficial trade, and a natural monopoly arises where per-unit cost falls as output rises. Mainstream economists generally accept that governments or other organizations can sometimes improve market outcomes, as with building codes and [[Endangered species|endangered species]] laws. Critics argue that government interventions such as taxes, subsidies and regulations can also produce inefficiency, sometimes called government failure.

ENTRY:
Process of acquiring, satisfying, and retaining customers, and a primary component of business management and [[Commerce|commerce]]. Marketing is usually conducted by the seller, typically a [[Retail|retailer]] or manufacturer, and products may be marketed to other businesses (B2B) or directly to consumers (B2C). Tasks are sometimes contracted to marketing, market research, or [[Advertising|advertising]] agencies.

The American Marketing Association (AMA) currently defines marketing as "the activity, set of institutions, and processes for creating, communicating, delivering, and exchanging offerings that have value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large." The interests of society at large were added in 2008. Philip Kotler defined marketing in 1980 as "satisfying needs and wants through an exchange process".

The "marketing concept" holds that an organization should anticipate the needs and wants of potential consumers and satisfy them more effectively than its competitors. It originated in the work of [[Adam Smith]] but did not become widely used until nearly 200 years later. Market segmentation divides markets into groups of buyers with distinct needs, characteristics, or behaviors. The marketing mix is commonly framed as the four Ps: product, price, place, and promotion.

Historically, marketing shifted from a production focus during the Industrial Revolution to a consumer orientation after World War II. Because it draws on social science, [[Psychology|psychology]], sociology, mathematics, economics, anthropology, and [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], it is now widely recognized as a science, though it was once seen mainly as a creative industry. One estimate credits marketing investment with 0.18 percentage points of annual U.S. output growth from 1987 to 2020. Critics point to potentially manipulative persuasion, while defenders argue that it facilitates voluntary exchanges that enhance welfare.
