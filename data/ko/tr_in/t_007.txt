Cultivation of plants and raising of animals to produce food, fibers, fuels, and raw materials. Broader definitions include forestry and aquaculture.

Agriculture was a key factor in the rise of sedentary human [[Civilization|civilization]], since farming of domesticated plants and animals created food surpluses that allowed people to live in cities. Humans gathered wild grains from at least 105,000 years ago, but deliberate planting began only about 11,500 years ago. Sheep, goats, pigs, and cattle were domesticated about 10,000 years ago. Crops were independently developed in at least 11 regions of the world. The word derives from Latin *agricultura*, from *ager* ("field") and *cultura* ("cultivation").

Regional beginnings differ. Rice was domesticated in southern China, cattle from the wild [[Aurochs|aurochs]] in what is now Turkey and Pakistan, and pigs from wild boar in Eurasia. In the Andes, the potato, beans, coca, llamas, alpacas, and guinea pigs were domesticated; in [[Mesoamerica]], the wild grass teosinte was bred into [[Maize|maize]] between 10,000 and 6,000 years ago; sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea and sorghum in the Sahel. The horse was domesticated on the Eurasian Steppe around 3500 BC. The Sumerians, relying on the Tigris and Euphrates and a canal system for [[Irrigation|irrigation]], had plows by about 3000 BC, and Egyptian farming depended on the Nile's seasonal flooding. China had a nationwide granary system from the 5th century BC and iron-share heavy plows by the late 2nd century. The Aztecs built terraces and artificial islands called chinampas.

Major products are foods (cereals, vegetables, fruits, cooking oils, meat, milk, eggs), fibers, fuels, and raw materials such as rubber and timber. Annual output is about 11 billion tonnes of food, 32 million tonnes of natural fiber, and 4 billion m³ of wood; roughly 14% of food is lost before reaching retail. As of 2021, small farms of one hectare or less produced about a third of the world's food, while the 1% of farms larger than 50 hectares held more than 70% of farmland.

In the 20th century, industrial agriculture based on large monocultures came to dominate output. Plant breeding, fertilizers, pesticides, and mechanization sharply raised yields, and advocates credit these advances, often grouped as the [[Green Revolution]], with averting famine and supporting population growth. Critics and researchers also link intensive agriculture to [[Deforestation|deforestation]], depletion of aquifers, antibiotic resistance, pollution, biodiversity loss, and contributions to climate change, while agriculture is itself sensitive to soil degradation, desertification, and a changing climate. Genetically modified organisms are widely used, although some countries ban them, and the use of pesticides and animal-husbandry methods remains debated on environmental and animal-welfare grounds.

ENTRY:
Vehicle or machine that flies by gaining support from the air. An aircraft counters gravity with static lift or the dynamic lift of an airfoil, or, in a few cases, with direct downward thrust from its engines. Examples include airplanes, drones, rotorcraft such as [[Helicopter|helicopters]], airships, gliders, and hot-air balloons. Aircraft are classified by lift type, propulsion, and use; a crewed craft has an onboard pilot, while an [[Unmanned aerial vehicle|unmanned aerial vehicle]] is remotely controlled or autonomous.

Lighter-than-air craft, or aerostats, float by buoyancy in cells filled with helium, hydrogen, or hot air. Heavier-than-air craft, or aerodynes, are denser than air and must generate lift, either aerodynamically, with air flowing past a wing, or by powered lift from downward engine thrust.

Kites flown in China several hundred years BC are considered the earliest man-made flight. In the 15th century [[Leonardo da Vinci]] designed flying machines that proved unworkable. The [[Montgolfier brothers]] invented the hot-air balloon in the late 18th century, and the discovery of hydrogen led to the hydrogen balloon; France established military balloon companies during the [[French Revolution]]. Advances in fluid dynamics and Newton's laws of motion produced modern aerodynamics, notably through George Cayley.

The [[Wright brothers]] built and flew the first airplane in 1903, after research on wing design and aircraft control. Their flight of December 17 at Kill Devil Hills, North Carolina, covered 120 feet in 12 seconds. Rigid airships pioneered by Ferdinand von Zeppelin dominated long-distance flight until the 1930s, when large flying boats took over trans-oceanic routes. After [[World War II]], land-based airplanes replaced flying boats, made more capable first by improved propeller engines and then by jet engines, which transformed civilian air travel and military aviation. Digital electronics later brought fly-by-wire systems, and pilotless drones now serve military, commercial, and recreational purposes.

Warship designed to carry, launch, recover and support aircraft at sea. Its full-length flight deck and hangars make it a mobile airbase, and it is usually the capital ship of a carrier battle group.
