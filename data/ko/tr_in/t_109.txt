Landlocked country in the [[Horn of Africa]], East Africa (pop., 2026: around 138 million). Officially the Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia, it covers a land area of 1,104,300 km² and is the 2nd most populous country in Africa. The capital and largest city, [[Addis Ababa]], lies several kilometers west of the East African Rift, which splits the country into the African and Somali tectonic plates.

The [[Kingdom of Aksum]] maintained a unified civilization for 900 years, and Christianity arrived there in 330 AD. Yekuno Amlak overthrew the Zagwe dynasty in 1270, inaugurating the [[Ethiopian Empire]] and the Solomonic dynasty. Under [[Menelik II]], the empire expanded, remained independent during the Scramble for Africa, and defeated an Italian invasion at Adwa in 1896. Italy invaded again in the [[Second Italo-Ethiopian War]], and Ethiopia lost its sovereignty in 1936; it was liberated in 1941, and full sovereignty was restored in 1944. The [[Derg]], a Soviet-backed military junta, took power in 1974 after deposing Emperor Haile Selassie and ruled for nearly 17 years. After 1991 the Ethiopian People's Revolutionary Democratic Front dominated the country under a constitution establishing ethnic-based federalism. Since 2018, regional and ethnically based factions have carried out armed attacks in multiple ongoing wars.

Ethiopia has more than 80 ethnic groups. Christianity is the most widely professed faith, the largest denomination being the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, followed by Islam. The country hosts the headquarters of the [[African Union]] Commission and became a full member of BRICS in 2024. Agriculture accounted for over 37% of GDP as of 2022. Despite consistent growth, Ethiopia remains among the poorest countries in Africa in per capita income.

Civil war in Ethiopia and present-day [[Eritrea]], 1974–1991. It was fought between the [[Derg]], a military junta, and Ethiopian and Eritrean rebel groups. The Derg overthrew Emperor [[Haile Selassie]] in a coup on 12 September 1974 and established a communist state. Rebel fronts, including the Eritrean People's Liberation Front and the Tigrayan People's Liberation Front, had already been fighting the imperial regime. The Derg used counterinsurgency campaigns and the Red Terror to repress them. In 1977 Somalia invaded to back rebels in the Ogaden, prompting a large Soviet and Cuban intervention. Famine in 1983–1985 and economic decline increased support for the rebels. The Derg dissolved its military junta in 1987 and became civilianized, but it was overwhelmed by rebel groups. The war ended on 28 May 1991, when rebels entered [[Addis Ababa]] and President Mengistu Haile Mariam fled to [[Zimbabwe]]. At least 1.4 million people died, 1 million of them in famine-related deaths.

Country in the Horn of Africa (1270–1974), historically known as Abyssinia. Founded by Yekuno Amlak, who claimed descent from King Solomon and the Queen of Sheba, the Solomonic dynasty replaced the Agaw kingdom of the Zagwe. Amda Seyon I and Dawit I expanded it into the dominant force in the Horn of Africa, and it reached its peak under [[Zara Yaqob|Zara Yaqob]] (r. 1434–1468). Emperor Fasilides founded [[Gondar]] in the 1630s, beginning a golden age. After a period of decentralization known as the Zemene Mesafint, Emperor Tewodros II reunified the empire. Italy occupied the empire during World War II until it was driven out with British help. Emperor [[Haile Selassie]] returned from exile, and the empire became a founding member of the [[United Nations]]. The 1973 Wollo famine and domestic discontent led to the 1974 coup by the Derg.

Rugged mountain mass in northeastern Africa, extending from [[Ethiopia|Ethiopia]] into [[Eritrea|Eritrea]]. The Great East African Rift System bisects it diagonally. The highlands began rising 75 million years ago, and a flood basalt plateau formed around 30 million years ago. The former Kingdom of Kaffa, in the southern highlands, was the point from which the [[Coffee|coffee]] plant was exported to the [[Arabian Peninsula|Arabian Peninsula]].

ENTRY:
Conflict between two or more ethnic groups in which the individuals involved expressly fight for their group's position within society. Political, social, economic or religious sources may underlie it. Academic explanations fall into three schools: primordialist, instrumentalist and constructivist. Primordialists argue that ethnic groups rest on kinship and primordial ties such as territorial location, a view associated with [[Clifford Geertz|Clifford Geertz]]. Other political scientists argue that institutional, political and economic factors cause conflicts, and that the concept of ethnic war is misleading. Primordial accounts struggle to explain why ethnic violence occurs in sporadic outbursts. Scholars debate whether ethnic conflict has become more prevalent since the end of the [[Cold War|Cold War]]. Management approaches include consociationalism and [[Federalism|federalism]]. Although [[Yugoslav Wars|Yugoslavia]] broke up in ethnic violence in the 1990s, it had enjoyed decades of peace before the Soviet Union collapsed.

ENTRY:
Group of people who identify with one another on the basis of perceived shared attributes that distinguish them from other groups. The attributes may include language, culture, common ancestry, traditions, society, religion, history, region, or social treatment. Ethnic identities may rest on a narrow or broad spectrum of genetic ancestry, and some groups have mixed ancestry. Ethnicity is sometimes used interchangeably with [[Nation|nation]], and also with [[Race (human categorization)|race]], although not all ethnicities identify as racial groups.

Individuals or groups may shift from one ethnic group to another over time through assimilation, acculturation, amalgamation, language shift, intermarriage, adoption, and religious conversion. Ethnic groups may divide into subgroups or tribes, which can become separate ethnic groups in turn through endogamy or physical isolation. Formerly separate ethnicities may also merge into a panethnicity. The formation of a separate ethnic identity, whether through division or amalgamation, is called ethnogenesis.

The word *ethnic* derives from the Greek *ethnos*, through the adjective *ethnikos*, borrowed into Latin as *ethnicus*. In Early Modern English and until the mid-19th century it meant "heathen" or "pagan," because the [[Septuagint]] used *ta ethne*, "the nations," to translate the Hebrew *goyim*. The sense of "different cultural groups" arose in the 1930s and 1940s, replacing *race*, which was becoming deprecated because of its association with ideological racism. *Ethnic group* was first recorded in 1935 and entered the [[Oxford English Dictionary]] in 1972. *Nationality* may mean either ethnicity or [[Citizenship|citizenship]], depending on context.

Groups can be classified by the source of identity emphasized: ethno-linguistic (French Canadians), ethno-national (Austrians), ethno-racial (African Americans), ethno-regional (South Islanders of New Zealand), ethno-religious (Sikhs, [[Jews]]), ethno-civic (ancient Romans), and ethno-cultural (Travellers). The categories often overlap, and membership is frequently determined by more than one aspect. Armenian ethnicity, for example, can be defined by citizenship, heritage, native use of the Armenian language, or membership of the Armenian Apostolic Church.

[[Herodotus]] gave a famous account of Greek identity as shared descent, shared language, shared sanctuaries and sacrifices, and shared customs. The orator Isocrates, by contrast, wrote that the name Hellenes applied to those who shared Athenian culture rather than a common blood.

Two main theories exist in understanding ethnicities, primordialism and constructivism. Early 20th-century primordialists viewed ethnic groups as real phenomena whose distinct characteristics have endured since the distant past. Perspectives that developed after the 1960s increasingly viewed them as social constructs, with identity assigned by societal rules. Anthropologists such as Fredrik Barth and Eric Wolf do not consider ethnic identity universal, regarding it as a product of specific kinds of inter-group interactions. Other accounts emphasize that ethnic affiliations often correspond to patterns of genetic similarity formed through endogamy and relative isolation, and they dispute the view that ethnicity is purely a social invention.

Application of one's own [[Culture|culture]] as the standard for judging other cultures, practices, beliefs and peoples, usually negatively. The term was first applied in the [[Social science|social sciences]] by American sociologist William G. Sumner, who in his 1906 book *Folkways* called it "the technical name for the view of things in which one's own group is the center of everything, and all others are scaled and rated with reference to it." Branch of [[Anthropology|anthropology]] devoted to the systematic study of individual cultures from the viewpoint of their members. Ethnographers rely heavily on participant observation, in which the researcher takes part in a setting, often in a marginal role, to document patterns of social interaction and participants' perspectives. Ethnographers mainly use qualitative methods, though they may include quantitative data, and a typical study is holistic, including a brief history and an analysis of the terrain, climate and habitat. The method originated in social and cultural anthropology in the early 20th century and spread notably to [[Sociology|sociology]]. Studied groups include traditional communities, youth gangs, religious cults and organizations. Some studies rely on interviews or documents, as in research on the [[NASA]] Challenger disaster, and online "virtual" settings are studied through netnography. *Histories* by [[Herodotus]] is the usual starting point for ancient ethnographic writing.

Academic discipline that compares and analyzes the characteristics of different peoples and the relationships between them. Unlike [[Ethnography|ethnography]], which studies single groups through direct contact with their culture, ethnology compares and contrasts the research ethnographers have compiled. The term was used and defined by Adam Franz Kollár in a work published in Vienna in 1783. It has been considered an academic field since the late 18th century. Among its goals have been reconstructing human history, identifying cultural invariants such as the incest taboo, and formulating generalizations about [[Human nature|human nature]], a concept criticized since the 19th century. Cultural anthropology became dominant in the United States and [[Social anthropology|social anthropology]] in Great Britain, and the distinction between the three terms is increasingly blurred. The progress of the field, for example through [[Claude Lévi-Strauss]]'s structural anthropology, led to criticism of linear progress models.

Branch of [[Zoology|zoology]] that studies the behavior of non-human animals. Its roots lie in the work of [[Charles Darwin]] and of American and German ornithologists of the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Ancient Greek mathematician (fl. 300 BC), active as a geometer and logician in [[Alexandria]]. Considered the "father of geometry," he is chiefly known for the [[Euclid's Elements|*Elements*]], a 13-book treatise that established the foundations of geometry that largely dominated the field until the early 19th century. It deduces theorems from a small set of axioms, and its system, now called [[Euclidean geometry]], synthesized theories of earlier Greek mathematicians, including Eudoxus of Cnidus, Hippocrates of Chios, Thales, and Theaetetus. With [[Archimedes]] and [[Apollonius of Perga]], Euclid is generally considered among the greatest mathematicians of antiquity.

Very little is known of his life, and most information comes from the scholars Proclus and Pappus of Alexandria, writing many centuries later. Efficient [[Algorithm|algorithm]] for computing the greatest common divisor of two [[Integer|integers]]. Named after the ancient Greek mathematician [[Euclid]], who described it in his *Elements* (c. 300 BC), it is one of the oldest algorithms in common use. It rests on the principle that the greatest common divisor does not change if the larger number is replaced by its remainder when divided by the smaller. The algorithm reduces fractions, performs division in [[Modular arithmetic|modular arithmetic]], helps solve [[Diophantine equation|Diophantine equations]] and forms part of the cryptographic protocols that secure the [[Internet]].

Set out in Euclid's *Elements*, it begins with plane geometry, still taught in secondary school as the first axiomatic system, and goes on to solid geometry of three dimensions. Euclid's five postulates, including the parallel postulate, seemed so intuitively obvious that their theorems were considered absolutely true for more than two thousand years. Two self-consistent [[Non-Euclidean geometry|non-Euclidean geometries]], hyperbolic and elliptic, are now known. An implication of [[Albert Einstein]]'s general relativity is that physical space itself is not Euclidean, although Euclidean space approximates it over short distances and weak gravity. Euclidean geometry is synthetic, proceeding logically from axioms, in contrast to [[Analytic geometry|analytic geometry]], introduced almost 2,000 years later by [[René Descartes]], which uses coordinates.

Organism of the domain Eukaryota (or Eukarya), whose cells have a distinct [[Cell nucleus|nucleus]] enclosed by a nuclear membrane, membrane-bound organelles, and a [[Cytoskeleton|cytoskeleton]]. Extant eukaryotes are traditionally grouped into four kingdoms: animals, plants, fungi, and [[Protist|protists]]. Protists are a basal paraphyletic group that includes all algae, amoebae, flagellates, and ciliates. In the prevailing three-domain system, eukaryotes stand alongside the two domains of [[Prokaryote|prokaryotes]], Bacteria and Archaea, though cladistically they emerged within the archaeal phylum Promethearchaeota.

Eukaryotes first emerged during the Paleoproterozoic. The leading theory holds that they resulted from a [[Symbiogenesis|symbiogenesis]] between an anaerobic archaeon and an aerobic alphaproteobacterium that became the proto-[[Mitochondria|mitochondrion]]. They range from picozoans under 3 micrometers across to the blue whale, weighing up to 190 tonnes, and the coast redwood, up to 120 m tall. Multicellularity has evolved independently at least 25 times within the group. Eukaryotes are a small minority of organisms by number, but their collective biomass of 468 gigatons far exceeds that of prokaryotes at 77 gigatons.

Mild or indirect word or expression substituted for one considered offensive or unpleasant. Euphemisms soften references to taboo or sensitive topics such as [[Death|death]], [[Disability|disability]], bodily functions, [[Pain|pain]] and violence, and are often used to soften [[Profanity|profanity]]. The term dates from the mid-17th century and derives from the Greek *euphēmismos*, meaning "use of words of good omen." Critics argue that in political and military contexts, euphemisms such as "enhanced interrogation" for [[Torture|torture]] or "collateral damage" for civilian casualties obscure causal realities and moral implications.
