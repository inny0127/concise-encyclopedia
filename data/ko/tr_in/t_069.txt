Third sector of society, distinct from [[Government|government]] and business and including the family and the private sphere. Some definitions describe it as the aggregate of [[Non-governmental organization|nongovernmental organizations]] and institutions that advance citizens' interests, or as individuals and organizations independent of government. Others use the term for elements of a [[Democracy|democratic]] society, such as free speech and an independent judiciary. Scholars do not agree on a single understanding. The concept's literature has origins in Scottish Enlightenment philosophy and in the work of Hegel, [[Alexis de Tocqueville|Tocqueville]], and [[Karl Marx|Marx]]. In the late 20th century, [[Václav Havel|Václav Havel]] and other Eastern European dissidents used the term for civic associations threatened by communist states. Researchers Gabriel Almond and Sidney Verba argued that civil society helps citizens become better informed, make better voting choices, and hold government accountable.

ENTRY:
War between organized groups within the same state or country. Its aim may be to take control of the country or a region, achieve independence for a region, or change government policies. Most modern civil wars involve intervention by outside powers. According to Patrick M. Regan, about two thirds of the 138 intrastate conflicts between the end of [[World War II]] and 2000 involved international intervention. Civil wars often involve regular armed forces and large numbers of casualties. Since 1945 they have caused over 25 million deaths and forced the displacement of millions more. Civil wars averaged just over four years after World War II, compared with one-and-a-half years in 1900–1944. Nations including [[Somalia]], Burma (Myanmar), [[Uganda]], and [[Angola]] were considered to have promising futures before civil wars engulfed them.

ENTRY:
Complex society characterized by the development of the state, urbanization, and standardized symbolic communication in the form of [[Writing system|writing systems]]. Civilizations are organized around densely populated settlements and divided into more or less rigid hierarchical social classes, often with a ruling elite and subordinate urban and rural populations. They engage in intensive agriculture, mining, small-scale manufacture, and trade, and are marked by elaborate architecture, infrastructure, technological advancement, currency, taxation, regulation, and specialization of labor. Civilization concentrates power and extends human control over nature, including over other human beings. The term is often applied to a larger and supposedly more advanced culture, in implied contrast to non-centralized tribal societies, nomadic pastoralists, Neolithic societies, and hunter-gatherers.

The word relates to the Latin *civitas*, "city," and the National Geographic Society has described its most basic definition as "a society made up of cities." The earliest civilizations are generally connected with the final stages of the [[Neolithic Revolution]] in West Asia, culminating in rapid urban revolution and state formation associated with a governing elite. Among the earliest were those of [[Mesopotamia]] (c. 3500 BC), [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]] (c. 3100 BC), Caral–Supe in Peru (c. 3000 BC), the Indus Valley (c. 2600 BC), and the Yellow River in China (from c. 2000 BC).

The abstract noun came from French in the 1760s; its first known French use is from 1757, and its first English use is attributed to Adam Ferguson in 1767. In the [[Age of Enlightenment|Enlightenment]] it was opposed to barbarism and meant the progress of humanity as a whole, and it was used only in the singular. Use of "civilizations" as a countable noun became much more common in the later 20th century. In 1996 Samuel P. Huntington defined a civilization as simply a "culture writ large." Already in the 18th century the idea was not always seen as an improvement: Rousseau held that civilization is not fully in accord with human nature, and later German thinkers, beginning with Herder, regarded cultures as natural organisms and civilization as unnatural and productive of vices such as guile, hypocrisy, envy, and avarice. Leo Strauss argued during World War II that this view lay behind Nazism and German militarism and nihilism.

Social scientists such as V. Gordon Childe have listed traits that distinguish civilizations, including subsistence, settlement patterns, forms of government, social stratification, economic systems, and literacy. All civilizations have depended on agriculture, with the possible exception of some early Peruvian ones that may have relied on maritime resources, and most depended on cereal agriculture. The traditional surplus model holds that stored grain surpluses allowed soldiers, artisans, priests, and other specialists to emerge. One study in the *Journal of Political Economy* contradicts this, arguing that only cereal farming produced civilization because its yearly harvest could be taxed. Some [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherers]], such as indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest, have also had food surpluses. Andrew Nikiforuk argues that civilizations relied on the energy of enslaved people and considers [[Slavery|slavery]] a common feature of pre-modern civilizations.

Group of organisms comprising a common ancestor and all of its descendants. Also called a monophyletic group, a clade is the fundamental unit of cladistics, a modern approach to [[Taxonomy|taxonomy]] adopted by most biological fields. Group of people united by actual or perceived [[Kinship|kinship]] and descent. Members may claim descent from a founding or apical ancestor who symbolizes the group's unity. Traditional anthropology classified such ancestors, frequently animals, as [[Totem|totems]]. In many societies, exogamy rules apply by clan, so members are excluded from marriage with their own clan, or with the clans of their parents or grandparents, as [[Incest|incest]]. Often a clan is a smaller, integral part of a larger society such as a [[Tribe|tribe]], chiefdom, or state. The word derives from the Gaelic *clann*, meaning children or descendants, and entered English around 1406 as a label for society in the Scottish Highlands. Clans also exist in Irish, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese society, and in post-Soviet countries the term describes informal networks.

German instrument maker Johann Christoph Denner is generally credited with inventing the clarinet around 1700 by adding a register key to the chalumeau, an earlier single-reed instrument. Branch of [[Algebraic number theory|algebraic number theory]] concerned with abelian extensions. It describes all abelian Galois extensions of local and global fields using objects associated with the ground field. A key result is Artin reciprocity, which gives a canonical isomorphism between the Galois group of a finite abelian extension and a quotient of the idele class group. The theory takes its name from class fields, the abelian extensions that correspond to open subgroups of finite index. The theory has three main generalizations: higher class field theory, the Langlands program, and anabelian geometry.

Style of written Chinese in which the classics of Chinese literature were composed, from about the 5th century BC. For millennia afterward, scholars imitated its [[Syntax|syntax]] in a form called Literary Chinese, used for almost all formal writing in China until the early 20th century. Each character usually corresponds to a single independent word, making the language terse and difficult for readers without literary training. From the 2nd century AD, Literary Chinese spread to [[Vietnam|Vietnam]], [[Korea|Korea]], [[Japan|Japan]], and the Ryukyu Islands, where it served as the common written language and as an international auxiliary language for administration and scholarship. Because its conservative tradition left many later spoken changes unrecorded, it is only partially intelligible when read aloud by those familiar only with modern forms.

Period of cultural European history between the 8th century BC and the 5th century AD, comprising the interwoven civilizations of ancient Greece and Rome, known together as the Greco-Roman world. It followed the Greek Dark Ages and was succeeded by [[Late antiquity]]. Its influence reached much of Europe, North Africa, and West Asia.

The period is conventionally considered to begin with the earliest recorded Greek epic poetry of [[Homer]] (8th–7th centuries BC) and to end with the [[Fall of the Western Roman Empire|fall of the Western Roman Empire]] in 476 AD.

The Archaic period saw advances in political theory and the beginnings of democracy, philosophy, theater, and poetry. The traditional date for the founding of the [[Ancient Olympic Games]] is 776 BC. [[Carthage]] was founded in 814 BC. According to legend, Rome was founded on 21 April 753 BC by Romulus and Remus. The monarchy was expelled in 510 BC and Rome became a republic in 509 BC. Classical Greece corresponds to most of the 5th and 4th centuries BC, from the end of the Athenian tyranny in 510 BC to the death of [[Alexander the Great]] in 323 BC. Rome codified legal principles in the Twelve Tables, later compiled in the *Corpus Juris Civilis* under [[Justinian I]] in the 6th century.

Greco-Roman culture has been immensely influential on the language, politics, law, education, philosophy, science, literature, ethics, art, and architecture of the Western world and, through it, the modern world. Surviving fragments of classical culture helped produce a revival beginning in the 14th century that became known as the [[Renaissance]], and neoclassical revivals followed in the 18th and 19th centuries.

ENTRY:
Branch of physics describing the effect of forces on the motion of macroscopic objects and bulk matter, without considering quantum effects and often without relativistic effects.

It is used to describe projectiles, machinery, spacecraft, planets, stars, galaxies, deformable solids, and fluids. The earliest formulation, often called Newtonian mechanics, rests on the 17th-century work of [[Isaac Newton]], whose three laws of motion and law of universal gravitation appeared in 1687, and on mathematical methods developed by Newton, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, [[Leonhard Euler|Euler]], and others. Energy-based methods developed in the 18th and 19th centuries by Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange, William Rowan Hamilton, and others produced analytical mechanics, which includes [[Lagrangian mechanics]] and [[Hamiltonian mechanics]]. The two are equivalent by a Legendre transformation. All equations of motion can be derived from the principle of least action, one consequence being [[Noether's theorem]], which connects conservation laws to symmetries.

Traditionally the subject is divided into statics (force and torque on systems in equilibrium), kinematics (the "geometry of motion"), and dynamics, which also considers the forces involved. It may alternatively be divided by region of application: celestial, continuum, relativistic, and statistical mechanics.

If the present state of an object obeying its laws is known, its future and past motion can be determined, although [[Chaos theory]] shows that long-term predictions are unreliable. The theory is accurate for objects that are not extremely massive and whose speeds are far below that of light. Atom-sized objects require [[Quantum mechanics]], speeds approaching that of light require [[Special relativity]], and extremely massive objects require general relativity.

Tradition of art music of the Western world, distinct from Western folk and popular music.

It is characterized by formality and complexity of musical form and harmonic organization, notably the use of polyphony. Since at least the ninth century it has been chiefly a written tradition, with a sophisticated notational system and an accompanying critical and scholarly literature. The English *classical* derives from the Latin *classicus*, originally referring to the highest class of Roman citizens.

Surviving early medieval music is chiefly religious, monophonic, and vocal. The earliest extant manuscripts date from the [[Carolingian Empire]] (800–887), when Western plainchant gradually unified into [[Gregorian chant]]. Staff notation developed in the 11th century. By the mid-12th century France was the major musical center, where the Notre-Dame school first fully explored organized rhythms and polyphony. In the early 15th century the Franco-Flemish School brought choral music, particularly the mass and motet, to new standards.

The [[Baroque]] period (1580–1750) saw standardization of common-practice tonality and growth of instrumental ensembles. Italy was the birthplace of opera, the concerto, sonata form, the oratorio, and the cantata, and the fugue technique championed by [[Johann Sebastian Bach]] exemplified the period's complexity. In the Classical period (1750–1820), [[Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart]], Joseph Haydn, and [[Ludwig van Beethoven]] produced symphonies, string quartets, and concertos. Romantic music (1800–1910) emphasized programmatic works, virtuosity, and nationalism, converging in the operas of [[Richard Wagner]].

In the 20th century stylistic unification dissolved and popular music grew in prominence. Many composers adopted modernism, some abandoning tonality for serialism. After World War II audiences for the first time valued older music over contemporary works. Practitioners from the Americas, Africa, and Asia now hold crucial roles.

Long-term weather pattern of a region, typically averaged over 30 years.

More rigorously, climate is the mean and variability of meteorological variables over a span from months to millions of years. The variables most commonly measured are temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, wind, and precipitation. In a broader sense, climate is the state of the whole climate system: the [[Atmosphere|atmosphere]], hydrosphere, cryosphere, lithosphere, and biosphere and the interactions among them. The word derives from Ancient Greek *klima*, 'inclination'. The standard averaging period of 30 years was defined by the World Meteorological Organization (WMO), whose technical commission designated 1901–30 as the reference period for standard climate normals in 1934. The 1961–90 normals serve as the baseline reference period. The popular phrase "Climate is what you expect, weather is what you get" summarizes the difference between climate and weather.

A location's climate is affected by its latitude, longitude, terrain, altitude, land use, and nearby bodies of water and their currents. Over historical time spans these determinants are nearly constant and change only over millions of years through processes such as [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Others are more dynamic: [[Thermohaline circulation|thermohaline circulation]] of the ocean warms the northern Atlantic Ocean by 5 °C (9 °F) compared with other ocean basins, other ocean currents redistribute heat regionally, and vegetation affects heat absorption, water retention, and rainfall. Changes in atmospheric greenhouse gases, particularly [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]] and [[Methane|methane]], determine how much solar energy the planet retains.

Climates are classified by average and typical variables, most commonly temperature and precipitation. The most widely used scheme is the [[Köppen climate classification]], first developed in 1899, which was designed to identify climates associated with certain biomes. The Thornthwaite system, in use since 1948, adds evapotranspiration, and the Bergeron and Spatial Synoptic systems focus on the origin of air masses. Originally, climes were defined in Ancient Greece by a location's latitude. A common shortcoming of all such schemes is that they draw distinct boundaries, while real climates change gradually.

Paleoclimatology studies past climates. Because few direct observations exist before the 19th century, it relies on proxy evidence: sediments, ice cores, pollen, and rocks, as well as tree rings and coral. Climate models are mathematical models of past, present, and future climates.

Present-day climate change includes global warming, the ongoing increase in global average temperature, and its effects on the climate system. The modern rise is driven by human activities, especially burning of [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]] since the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Fossil fuel use, deforestation, and some agricultural and industrial practices release [[Greenhouse gas|greenhouse gases]], which absorb heat radiated by the Earth. The atmosphere now holds roughly 50% more carbon dioxide than at the end of the pre-industrial era, at levels not seen for millions of years.

The year 2024 was the warmest on record, at +1.60 °C (2.88 °F) since regular tracking began in 1850. Heat waves and wildfires are becoming more common. Amplified warming in the [[Arctic]] has contributed to thawing permafrost, retreat of glaciers, and sea ice decline. Higher temperatures also cause more intense storms and droughts, and force many species to relocate or become extinct. The World Health Organization calls climate change one of the biggest threats to global health in the 21st century.

The scientific literature shows broad agreement that human activities are the main driver of recent warming. Under the 2015 Paris Agreement, nations agreed to keep warming "well under 2 °C", but with current pledges warming would still reach about 2.8 °C (5.0 °F) by the end of the century. Proposed responses include ending fossil fuel subsidies, conserving energy, switching to wind, solar, hydro, and nuclear power.

Variations in climate that persist longer than individual weather events, from seasons to millennia. [[Climate change|Climate change]] refers to variations persisting for decades or more and is now often used for contemporary global warming. The climate system receives nearly all its energy from the [[Sun|sun]]; when incoming energy exceeds outgoing energy, the climate warms. Internal variability arises from natural processes, such as the Pacific decadal oscillation and Atlantic multidecadal oscillation, while external forcing includes changes in solar output and volcanism. Since the [[Industrial Revolution]], human activities have increasingly affected the climate, and atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm to over 420 ppm. Some analyses emphasize human dominance in recent warming, while others highlight natural variability.
