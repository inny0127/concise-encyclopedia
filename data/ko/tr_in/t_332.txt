ENTRY:
Practice by which a [[Teacher|teacher]] transmits skills, including knowledge, know-how and interpersonal skills, to a learner or other audience. Teaching is closely related to [[Learning|learning]], the student's activity of absorbing that knowledge, and it forms part of the broader concept of [[Education|education]]. In psychology, teaching is often defined by the teacher's intent to help others learn. Children show teaching abilities early: as young as twelve months, they help adults find an object when the adult shows ignorance of its location, and by age four they prefer to transmit true information. Caro and Hauser proposed a functional definition for animals: a behavior counts as teaching if it occurs only in the presence of a naive individual, costs the teacher or gives no direct benefit, and produces a learning gain in the learner.

ENTRY:
Application of conceptual knowledge to achieve practical goals, especially in a reproducible way. The word also denotes the products of such efforts, both tangible, such as tools and machines, and intangible, such as software. Technology plays a critical role in science, engineering, and everyday life.

The term dates to the early 17th century, when it meant "systematic treatment"; it derives from Greek *tekhnē* ('craft, art') and *-logia* ('study, knowledge'). From the 19th century, continental Europeans used *Technik* (German) or *technique* (French) for a "way of doing" and *Technologie* for the academic study of the "methods of arts and crafts"; English translated both as technology. In the 20th century, with scientific progress and the Second Industrial Revolution, it came to mean the systematic use of knowledge to practical ends.

The earliest known technology is the stone tool. Hominids learned to make the first stone tools about 2 mya (million years ago) by hammering flakes off a pebble, and the practice was refined 75 kya (thousand years ago) into pressure flaking. Evidence points to continuous human use of fire at least 1.5 mya; Charles Darwin described its discovery as "possibly the greatest ever made by man". Fire allowed cooking, which improved nutrient value. The cooking hypothesis holds that cooking promoted growth in hominid brain size, though some researchers find the evidence inconclusive. Hearths dated to 790 kya may have intensified socialization and contributed to the emergence of language. Evidence of clothing dates to 90–120 kya and of shelter to 450 kya.

The [[Neolithic Revolution]] accelerated innovation and social complexity. Polished stone axes allowed large-scale forest clearance and farming, and agriculture fed larger populations. Increased labor specialization, trade, war, and the need for collective action such as irrigation are thought to have contributed to the first cities, such as Uruk, and the first civilizations, such as Sumer. Writing became the basis for history, libraries, schools, and scientific research. Furnaces and bellows enabled smelting of metals; native copper was probably used from about 10 kya, alloys such as bronze and brass appeared about 4,000 BC, and iron alloys such as steel date to around 1,800 BC.

The earliest record of a ship under sail is a Nile boat dating to around 7,000 BC. The Sumerians in Mesopotamia diverted the Tigris and Euphrates by canals and levees for irrigation. The wheel was invented independently in Mesopotamia, the Northern Caucasus, and Central Europe; estimates range from 5,500 to 3,000 BC, with most experts favoring closer to 4,000 BC. Drawings of wheeled carts date from about 3,500 BC.

Later inventions, including the [[Printing press|printing press]], telephone, and the [[Internet]], lowered barriers to communication and ushered in the knowledge economy. Technology contributes to economic development and prosperity but can also cause pollution, resource depletion, and technological unemployment resulting from [[Automation|automation]]. Philosophical and political debates continue over its role, its ethics, and ways to mitigate its downsides.

Physical quantity expressing relative hotness or coldness, reflecting the average [[Kinetic energy|kinetic energy]] of the particles in a system in classical thermodynamics and kinetic theory. Temperature is important across natural science, including physics, chemistry, Earth science, astronomy, medicine, biology, and metallurgy. It affects the phase and density of materials, the rate of chemical reactions, thermal radiation, and the speed of sound, which in a gas is proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature.

Temperature is measured with a [[Thermometer|thermometer]], calibrated in scales that historically relied on various reference points. The [[Celsius]] scale (°C), used in most of the world, sets 0 °C at the freezing point of water and 100 °C at its boiling point at sea-level atmospheric pressure. The [[Kelvin]] scale (K) is an absolute scale used predominantly for science; an increment of one degree Celsius equals one kelvin, and the scales differ by an exact offset of 273.15. The kelvin is one of the seven base units of the [[International System of Units]], defined since May 2019 through particle kinetic theory and statistical mechanics.

[[Absolute zero]], 0 K, equals −273.15 °C or −459.67 °F. Under the third law of thermodynamics it can be approached but never reached; the lowest temperature attained experimentally is 38 pK, or 38 trillionths of a kelvin. Matter there still has quantum-mechanical zero-point energy. Negative absolute temperatures exist and are hotter than any positive temperature.
