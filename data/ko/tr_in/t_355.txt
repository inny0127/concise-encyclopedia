ENTRY:
Space devoid of matter; in practice, a region with gaseous pressure much less than [[Atmospheric pressure|atmospheric pressure]]. Physicists often discuss ideal results in a perfect vacuum and use the term partial vacuum for an actual imperfect one, as in a laboratory or in space. The quality of a partial vacuum is how closely it approaches a perfect one: lower gas pressure means higher quality. A typical [[Vacuum cleaner|vacuum cleaner]] reduces air pressure by around 20%, whereas ultra-high vacuum chambers, common in chemistry, physics, and engineering, operate below one trillionth (10⁻¹²) of atmospheric pressure (100 nPa) and can reach around 100 particles/cm³. [[Outer space]] is an even higher-quality vacuum, with just a few hydrogen atoms per cubic meter on average in intergalactic space.

Ancient Greek philosophers debated the void in the context of atomism. [[Lucretius]] argued for its existence in the first century BC, and Hero of Alexandria tried unsuccessfully to create an artificial vacuum in the first century AD. Following [[Plato]], however, the idea of a featureless void met considerable skepticism, and [[Aristotle]] believed that no void could occur naturally. Empirical study began in the 17th century, when Evangelista Torricelli produced the first laboratory vacuum in 1643 by filling a tall glass container closed at one end with mercury and inverting it in a bowl. In [[Quantum field theory|quantum field theory]], the vacuum is not simply empty but the lowest-energy ground state of the quantum fields, in which virtual particles briefly emerge and annihilate, contributing to phenomena such as the Casimir effect and zero-point energy.

Physical constant, the absolute dielectric permittivity of classical vacuum, denoted ε₀. Also called the permittivity of free space or the electric constant, it measures how dense an [[Electric field|electric field]] is permitted to form in response to [[Electric charge|electric charges]]. Device that controls electric current in a high vacuum between electrodes, also called an electron tube or thermionic valve. The first and simplest type, the diode or Fleming valve, was invented in 1904 by John Ambrose Fleming and allowed current to flow in only one direction. Adding control grids produced triodes and tetrodes that could amplify as well as rectify signals. Vacuum tubes were central to [[Radio|radio]], [[Television|television]], [[Radar|radar]] and early computers in the first half of the 20th century. From the 1940s, the invention of [[Semiconductor device|semiconductor devices]] made solid-state electronics possible, and from the mid-1960s tubes began to be replaced by the [[Transistor|transistor]]. Cathode-ray tubes remained in displays until the early 21st century. Tubes persist in some applications, such as the magnetron in [[Microwave oven|microwave ovens]], and many audio enthusiasts prefer tube amplifiers, including [[Electric guitar|electric guitar]] players.

Chemical element with symbol V and atomic number 23, a hard, silvery-gray, malleable [[Transition metal|transition metal]]. The pure metal is rarely found in nature, and an oxide layer somewhat stabilizes it against further oxidation. Spanish scientist Andrés Manuel del Río first identified its compounds in 1801, but his claim was retracted after the element was mistaken for impure chromium. In 1831 Swedish chemist Nils Gabriel Sefström rediscovered it and named it after the Norse goddess Vanadís, for the many colorful compounds it forms. Henry Enfield Roscoe isolated the pure metal later in the 19th century. Vanadium occurs in about 65 minerals and fossil fuel deposits. Approximately 93% of it is used in steel alloys, such as high-speed tool steels. Vanadium pentoxide is an important catalyst in the production of [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]], and some ocean organisms use vanadium as an active center of enzymes.

Major seaport city in the Lower Mainland of [[British Columbia]], Canada. The city had 662,248 residents in the 2021 census, making it the most populous in the province. Greater Vancouver had 2.6 million people that year, the third-largest metropolitan area in Canada after [[Toronto]] and [[Montreal]]. Vancouver has the highest population density in Canada and is one of its most ethnically and linguistically diverse cities. It is consistently ranked among the most livable cities, though it is also one of the most expensive. The modern city was renamed Vancouver in 1886 through a deal with the Canadian Pacific Railway, and it is nicknamed Hollywood North for its film production.

East Germanic tribe, first mentioned by Roman writers in the 1st century. Under King Genseric they crossed from the [[Iberian Peninsula|Iberia]] into North Africa in 429, and by 439 they had formed a kingdom covering the Roman province of Africa and several Mediterranean islands. The Vandals fended off several Roman attempts to recapture Africa and sacked the city of Rome in 455. Their kingdom collapsed in the Vandalic War of 533–534, when forces of Emperor [[Justinian I|Justinian I]] reconquered North Africa for the [[Roman Empire|Eastern Roman Empire]]. [[Renaissance|Renaissance]] writers cast the Vandals as barbarians, giving English the word vandalism, though some modern historians stress their continuation of Roman culture.

American family that gained prominence during the [[Gilded Age]] through the shipping and railroad empire of [[Cornelius Vanderbilt]]. The family was once the wealthiest in the United States, and Cornelius's son William Henry Vanderbilt acquired the fortune after his father's death in 1877.
