Strait separating the [[North Island|North Island]] and [[South Island|South Island]] of New Zealand. ENTRY:
Art, science, and craft of using heat to make food more palatable, digestible, nutritious, or safe. Also called cookery, it ranges from grilling over an open [[Fire|fire]] to ovens and boiling, reflecting local conditions and traditions. It is an aspect of all human societies and a cultural universal. Preparing food with heat is an activity unique to humans.

Archaeological evidence of cooking fires dates to at least 300,000 years ago, though some estimate that humans began cooking as early as 2 million years ago. Controlled fire at Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa dates to 1 million years ago, and the oldest evidence of cooking, heated fish teeth from a deep cave, dates to about 780,000 years ago. Richard Wrangham suggested that early [[Homo habilis]] cooked regularly, but unequivocal evidence for controlled fire begins at 400,000 BCE. Widespread cooking fires are thought to have begun about 250,000 years ago, when hearths first appeared.

Techniques include dry-heat methods such as grilling and frying, moist-heat methods such as steaming and simmering, and combination methods such as braising. Cooking manipulates the chemistry of the proteins, carbohydrates, and fats in ingredients; heating sugars with proteins causes the [[Maillard reaction]], a basic flavor-enhancing process.

The invention of [[Pottery|pottery]] for boiling water expanded techniques. The [[Columbian exchange]] transformed cuisines: [[Potato|potatoes]], tomatoes, maize, chocolate, and vanilla reached the Old World, while cattle, wheat, rice, and apples reached the New. In the 19th century, cuisine became a symbol of national identity. The [[Industrial Revolution]] brought mass production, canning, and standardization of food, and fast food restaurants emerged in the 1920s.

Soft, malleable, ductile metallic element (symbol Cu, from [[Latin]] *cuprum*; atomic number 29). Pure copper is orange-red, one of few metals with a natural color other than gray or silver. Its electrical conductivity is very high (59.6×10⁶ S/m), and its thermal conductivity is second only to silver among pure metals. It is used as a conductor, as a building material, and in alloys such as sterling silver, cupronickel (marine hardware and coins), and constantan (thermocouples). Electrical applications account for over 60% of consumption.

Copper is one of the few native metals, occurring naturally in usable form, which led to human use from c. 8000 BC. It was the first metal to be [[Smelting|smelted]] from sulfide ores (c. 5000 BC), the first cast in a mold (c. 4000 BC), and the first purposely alloyed with tin to make [[Bronze|bronze]] (c. 3500 BC). The name derives from Latin *aes cyprium*, "metal of [[Cyprus]]."

Copper does not react with water but slowly forms a brown-black oxide layer that, unlike rust on iron, protects the metal from further corrosion. Green verdigris forms on old copper roofs and on the [[Statue of Liberty]]. Copper(II) salts give blue or green colors to minerals such as azurite, [[Malachite|malachite]], and turquoise and have been used as pigments. Copper compounds also serve as fungicides and wood preservatives.

There are 29 isotopes; ⁶³Cu and ⁶⁵Cu are stable. Copper makes up about 50 ppm of Earth's crust, the 26th most abundant element. The largest mass of elemental copper found weighed 420 tonnes, discovered in 1857 on the Keweenaw Peninsula in [[Michigan]]. Copper is essential to all aerobic organisms; adult humans contain 1.4 to 2.1 mg per kilogram of body weight.

Heart disease involving reduced blood flow to the cardiac muscle, caused by plaques in the coronary arteries. It is the most common of the [[Cardiovascular disease|cardiovascular diseases]] and the most common cause of death globally: in 2015 it affected 110 million people and caused 8.9 million deaths, or 15.6% of all deaths. Subfamily of enveloped RNA [[Virus|viruses]] in the family Coronaviridae. Coronaviruses infect [[Mammal|mammals]] and birds. In humans, mild varieties cause some cases of the [[Common cold]], while more lethal ones cause SARS, MERS and [[COVID-19]]. In cows and pigs they cause [[Diarrhea]], and in mice they cause [[Hepatitis]] and encephalomyelitis. Their genomes, of 26 to 32 kilobases, are among the largest of any RNA virus. The name derives from Latin *corona*, meaning "crown" or "wreath", for the club-shaped spikes that project from the virus surface and give it a halo-like appearance under electron microscopy. As of 2020, 45 species are officially recognized.

ENTRY:
Legal entity authorized by the state to act as a single entity and recognized in law as a legal person. Early corporations were established by charter, an act of a monarch or legislature; most jurisdictions now allow incorporation through registration. Corporations are usually divided by whether they can issue stock and whether they are formed to make a profit.

Shareholders own a registered corporation, and their liability is generally limited to their investment. Limited liability separates control from ownership: a passive shareholder is not personally liable for the corporation's contractual obligations or torts. Supporters credit it with encouraging investment and risk-taking, while critics argue that it can encourage moral hazard.

The word derives from Latin *corpus*, "body." Roman law recognized corporate entities as *universitas*, *corpus*, or *collegium*. After the Lex Julia under [[Julius Caesar]], *collegia* required the approval of the Senate or emperor; they could own property, make contracts, and sue and be sued. The concept was revived in the 11th–14th centuries with the recovery of Justinian's [[Corpus Juris Civilis]]. Early entities carrying on business included the *sreni* of the [[Maurya Empire]] in India. The alleged oldest commercial corporation, the Stora Kopparberg mining community in Falun, [[Sweden]], received a charter in 1347.

In the 17th century, chartered companies such as the [[Dutch East India Company]] and the [[Hudson's Bay Company]] led European colonial ventures. The Dutch company defeated Portuguese forces in the Moluccas to profit from European demand for [[Spice|spices]]; its investors held paper share certificates, traded them on the Amsterdam [[Stock|stock]] exchange, and were granted limited liability. Speculation in joint-stock companies also contributed to the 1720 South Sea Bubble.
