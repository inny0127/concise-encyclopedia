Any of various short-horned grasshopper species, in the family Acrididae, that have a swarming phase. Usually solitary and harmless, locusts change dramatically after drought is followed by rapid vegetation growth, when [[Serotonin|serotonin]] in their brains triggers breeding and gregarious behavior. ENTRY:
Mathematical function giving the exponent to which a fixed base must be raised to produce a given number. For example, the logarithm of 1000 to base 10 is 3, because 1000 = 10³; this is written log₁₀ 1000 = 3. More generally, if x = bʸ, then y = log_b x, so the logarithm to base b is the inverse of exponentiation with base b. Other examples are log₂ 16 = 4, since 2⁴ = 16, and log₁₀ 150 ≈ 2.176.

The base-10 logarithm is the decimal or common logarithm, widely used in science and engineering. The natural logarithm has the number e ≈ 2.718 as its base and is widespread in mathematics and physics because of its simple derivative. The binary logarithm, base 2, is used in computer science, information theory, music theory, and photography. The logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms of the factors: log_b(xy) = log_b x + log_b y.

John Napier introduced logarithms in 1614 to simplify calculation. Henry Briggs introduced base-10 logarithms and published extensive tables in *Arithmetica Logarithmica* (1624), and Adrian Vlacq extended them to numbers up to 100,000 by 1628. The slide rule, first described by William Oughtred in 1622, is also based on logarithms. The present-day notion comes from [[Leonhard Euler]], who connected logarithms to the exponential function in the 18th century and introduced the letter e for the base of natural logarithms.

Logarithmic scales compress wide-ranging quantities: the decibel expresses ratios of signal power and amplitude, pH measures acidity, and the Richter scale measures earthquake intensity. In general settings the logarithm is multi-valued; the discrete logarithm, which inverts the exponential function in finite groups, is used in public-key cryptography.

ENTRY:
Study of correct reasoning. Logic includes formal and informal branches. Formal logic studies deductively valid inferences and logical truths, examining how conclusions follow from premises by the structure of [[Argument|arguments]] alone, independent of topic and content. Informal logic is associated with informal fallacies, critical thinking, and argumentation theory, and examines arguments expressed in natural language. Used as a countable noun, "a logic" means a specific formal system. Logic plays a central role in philosophy, mathematics, computer science, and linguistics.

Logic studies arguments, each consisting of premises that lead to a conclusion. An example runs from the premises "it's Sunday" and "if it's Sunday then I don't have to work" to the conclusion "I don't have to work." Premises and conclusions express propositions that can be true or false. An argument is correct if its premises support its conclusion. Deductive arguments give the strongest support: if their premises are true, the conclusion must be true. Ampliative arguments reach new information not found in the premises and divide into inductive arguments, which are statistical generalizations, and abductive arguments, which are inferences to the best explanation. Arguments that fall short of correct reasoning often embody fallacies. A rule of inference, such as modus ponens ("p; if p then q; therefore q"), is a pattern that guarantees validity regardless of what the terms stand for. Formal logic uses formal languages with a limited vocabulary and exact syntactic rules, so natural-language arguments must be translated before their validity can be assessed.

The word comes from Greek *logos*, variously translated as "reason," "discourse," or "language." Logic has been studied since antiquity; early approaches include Aristotelian logic, Stoic logic, Nyaya, and Mohism. [[Aristotle]], in the 4th century BC, developed syllogistic reasoning in works later collected as the *Organon*, and his system was the main logic of the Western world for over two millennia. Modern formal logic has roots in the work of 19th-century mathematicians. [[George Boole]] introduced algebraic approaches in *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847). [[Gottlob Frege]]'s *Begriffsschrift* (1879) established modern quantificational logic. [[Bertrand Russell]] and [[Alfred North Whitehead]] wrote *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), which sought to ground mathematics in logic, and Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) revealed limits of formal systems.

Today the most commonly used system is classical logic, which consists of [[Propositional logic|propositional logic]], concerned with relations between whole propositions, and [[First-order logic|first-order logic]], which also treats predicates and quantifiers. Extended logics apply classical intuitions to questions of necessity, what is or should be, and what is believed or known. Deviant logics, such as intuitionistic logic, which rejects the law of excluded middle, reject certain classical principles. Logic underpins set theory and proof theory, and in computer science it supports programming languages, artificial intelligence, and circuit design.

ENTRY:
Written character that represents a word or morpheme, a semantic component of a language. The term derives from Ancient Greek *logos*, "word," and *gramma*, "that which is drawn or written." [[Chinese characters]], Egyptian hieroglyphs, and characters of [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] script are logograms, and a writing system that primarily uses them is called a logography. Non-logographic systems, such as alphabets and syllabaries, are phonemic: their symbols represent sounds directly and lack inherent meaning.

Some of the earliest recorded writing systems are logographic; the first historical civilizations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica all used some form of logographic writing. All known logographies have a phonetic component, generally based on the rebus principle, in which a symbol stands for a sound-alike word. Adding a phonetic component to pure ideographs is considered a key innovation in enabling a script to encode human language adequately. In cuneiform as used for Akkadian, most glyphs carry sound values rather than word meanings.

Logoconsonantal scripts extend graphemes according to the consonants of the words, ignoring vowels; the main examples are Egyptian hieroglyphic, hieratic, and demotic. Logosyllabic scripts have graphemes for morphemes that, when extended phonetically, represent single syllables; they include cuneiform, Linear B, Chinese characters, Maya script, and Aztec script. Egyptian and Chinese add determinatives or radicals that narrow a character's meaning. Most Chinese characters combine a radical indicating a nominal category with a phonetic element.

Some logograms belong to no single language. The ampersand, a combination of the letters e and t, is a logogram in the Latin script representing "and" in many languages. Unicode, the generally accepted standard for computer character encoding, aims to include every character from every language.

Because of [[Earth's rotation]], a difference of 15° of longitude corresponds to a one-hour difference in local time, so longitude can be found by comparing local time with an absolute time.
