ENTRY:
Device created or adapted to make musical sounds. In principle any sound-producing object can be a musical instrument; purpose makes it one. Early instruments may have served rituals, such as a horn signaling a hunt, before cultures developed melodies for entertainment.

The date and origin of the first instrument are widely disputed. The oldest object identified by scholars as an instrument is a simple [[Flute|flute]] dated 50,000–60,000 years ago. Many historians consider dating the first impossible, as most early instruments were made of perishable materials. The Divje Babe flute a perforated bone found in 1995 in northwest [[Slovenia]] by Ivan Turk, is estimated at 43,400 to 67,000 years old; many argue that carnivores chewing the bone produced it, while Turk and others argue it is a [[Neanderthal]]-made flute. Mammoth bone and swan bone flutes of 30,000 to 37,000 years ago from the Swabian Alps of Germany are more commonly accepted as the oldest known instruments.

Instruments developed independently in many regions, but contact among civilizations spread and adapted most of them. By the post-classical era, instruments from [[Mesopotamia]] were in maritime [[Southeast Asia]], and Europeans played instruments originating from North Africa. Many new instruments appeared between roughly 1750 and 1900, and electricity led to new electric and electronic instruments such as the [[Electric guitar|electric guitar]], the [[Synthesizer|synthesizer]], and the theremin.

The most common academic classification, the Hornbostel–Sachs system, groups instruments by how they produce sound. Developed in 1914, it divides them into idiophones, membranophones, chordophones, and aerophones; electrophones were added later. The academic study of instruments is called organology.

System of symbols for representing music visually, recording the elements considered important for performance in a given tradition. Ancient cultures used symbols for melodies and rhythms, but none was particularly comprehensive. The earliest known notation is a [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] tablet from Nippur in [[Babylonia]], dating to about 1400 BCE. The direct ancestor of the modern Western system emerged in medieval Europe, where it was developed to standardize the performance of plainsong melodies. Notation developed further during the Renaissance and [[Baroque]] periods and continued to evolve with instrumental technology in the Classical and Romantic eras. Since the 20th century some composers have used graphical notation, and computer-based scorewriter programs have been used since the 1980s. Classical performers mostly use staves, time signatures, key signatures and noteheads, the Nashville Number System is the main method in professional country music, and [[Guitar|guitar]] players often use tablature. Notation has been written on stone, clay tablets, [[Papyrus|papyrus]] and [[Parchment|parchment]], and later printed.

Academic, research-based study of music, as distinct from composition or performance. Musicology draws on fields including [[Psychology|psychology]], [[Sociology|sociology]], [[Acoustics|acoustics]], neurology, the natural sciences and computer science. It is traditionally divided into three branches. Historical musicology studies the history of musical traditions, the origins of works and the lives of composers. Ethnomusicology draws on anthropology, particularly [[Field research|field research]], to understand how and why people make music. Formal musicology education was re-established in German and Austrian universities in the 19th century.

Smoothbore, muzzle-loaded long gun that used black powder. It appeared in the early 16th century as a heavier variant of the [[Arquebus|arquebus]], capable of penetrating [[Plate armour|plate armor]]. The first recorded use of the term came in 1499. As heavy armor declined, this form gradually disappeared by the mid-16th century, but musketeers remained the generic term for smoothbore long guns until the mid-19th century. Medium-sized semiaquatic [[Rodent|rodent]] native to North America and the only living species in the genus *Ondatra*. Adults weigh 0.6–2 kg (1¼–4½ lb) and measure 20–35 cm (8–14 in) in body length, excluding the tail. Their short, thick fur is medium to dark brown, and their scaly tails are laterally compressed and mainly help with directional stability in the water. Webbed hind feet provide most of their propulsion, and they can swim underwater for 12 to 17 minutes. Muskrats live in families of a male and female pair and their young. They build nests in banks with underwater entrances, and in marshes they construct mounds called push-ups, up to 90 cm (3 ft) high, from vegetation and mud. They feed mostly on cattails and other aquatic vegetation and occasionally on small animals. The muskrat is the largest species in the subfamily Arvicolinae, which includes mostly [[Vole|voles]] and [[Lemming|lemmings]]. It has been introduced to parts of Eurasia and South America, and it is a source of food and fur for people.

Death (infarction) of part of the heart muscle caused by decreased or stopped blood flow in a coronary artery; commonly called a heart attack. The most common symptom is retrosternal chest pain or discomfort that classically radiates to the left shoulder, arm, or jaw. Other symptoms include shortness of breath, nausea, and a cold sweat. Women more often present without chest pain. It may cause [[Heart failure|heart failure]], an irregular heartbeat, cardiogenic shock, or [[Cardiac arrest|cardiac arrest]].

Most cases result from [[Coronary artery disease|coronary artery disease]]; the usual mechanism is complete blockage of a coronary [[Artery|artery]] by the rupture of an [[Atherosclerosis|atherosclerotic]] plaque. Risk factors include high blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, obesity, high cholesterol, poor diet, and excessive alcohol intake.
