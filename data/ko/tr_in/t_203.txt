ENTRY:
Force exerted on a charged particle by electric and magnetic fields. The electric component acts along the electric field, accelerating positive charges in its direction and negative charges opposite to it. The magnetic component is perpendicular to both the particle's velocity and the magnetic field, so it curves the particle's path, often into circles or helices. Together with [[Maxwell's equations]], the force law forms the foundation of classical [[Electromagnetism|electrodynamics]]. It underlies the operation of [[Electric motor|electric motors]], [[Particle accelerator|particle accelerators]] and the behavior of [[Plasma (physics)|plasmas]]. The law remains valid in [[Special relativity|special relativity]] but breaks down at small scales, where the intrinsic spin of particles gives rise to interactions it does not account for. [[Hendrik Lorentz]] completed the derivation in 1895, identifying the electric force a few years after Oliver Heaviside identified the magnetic force. Historians suggest the law is implicit in a paper by [[James Clerk Maxwell|James Clerk Maxwell]] published in 1865.

ENTRY:
Linear transformation between the space and time coordinates of two inertial frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other. Named after the Dutch physicist [[Hendrik Lorentz]], the transformations use the Lorentz factor γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²), where v is relative velocity and c is the [[Speed of light|speed of light]]. For motion along the x-axis, t′ = γ(t − vx/c²) and x′ = γ(x − vt), while y and z are unchanged. They supersede the Galilean transformation of Newtonian physics, which is a good approximation only at speeds much less than the speed of light. The transformations predict effects such as [[Time dilation|time dilation]] and length contraction; observers in different frames may measure different distances and elapsed times, yet the speed of light is the same in all inertial frames. [[Albert Einstein]] made them a cornerstone of [[Special relativity|special relativity]].

Pseudohistorical myth of the [[American Civil War|American Civil War]] holding that the Confederate cause was morally just and not centered on slavery. Demographic cohort born from 1883 to 1900, who came of age around [[World War I]]. Gertrude Stein is credited with coining it, and Hemingway popularized it as the epigraph of *The Sun Also Rises* (1926). Form of [[Gambling|gambling]] based on random drawing of numbers for prizes. Some governments outlaw lotteries, while others endorse them and run national or state lotteries. The most common regulations ban sales to minors and license ticket vendors. Keno slips from China's [[Han dynasty]], dated between 205 and 187 BC, are the first recorded signs of a lottery, and such lotteries may have helped finance the [[Great Wall of China]]. Electroacoustic transducer converting an electrical audio signal into sound. A loudspeaker system combines one or more drivers, an enclosure, and electrical connections. Current from the signal passes through a voice coil in a magnetic field, and the resulting movement, due to [[Faraday's law of induction]], drives a diaphragm that moves air. The dynamic speaker was invented in 1925 by Edward W. Kellogg and Chester W. Rice. Most high-fidelity systems use separate drivers: tweeters for high frequencies, mid-range drivers for middle frequencies, and woofers for low frequencies, with a crossover network directing each part of the signal. Smaller speakers appear in radios, televisions, [[Personal computer|personal computers]], and headphones, while larger systems serve home stereos, electronic musical instruments, and public address systems.

His reputation remains divided between a dangerous religious fanatic and a charismatic leader defending the Métis from federal encroachment.
