ENTRY:
Religion of [[Japan]] centered on the veneration of kami, supernatural entities believed to inhabit all things.

Shinto is often regarded by its practitioners as Japan's indigenous religion and as a nature religion. It has no central authority, single creator, or specific doctrine. The kami are worshipped at household shrines (kamidana) and public shrines (jinja). Priests called kannushi staff the public shrines and oversee offerings to the kami, intended to cultivate harmony and solicit blessing. Other rituals include kagura dances, rites of passage, and kami festivals; shrines also offer [[Divination|divination]]. Purity, pursued through ritual washing, is a major concern. Little emphasis falls on moral codes or afterlife beliefs, although the dead can become kami.

Historians debate when Shinto became a distinct religion. Kami veneration has been traced to Japan's Yayoi period (300 BC–AD 250). [[Buddhism]] entered Japan at the end of the Kofun period and spread rapidly; the two became functionally inseparable in a process called shinbutsu-shūgō. Kami worship was first recorded in the 8th-century [[Kojiki]] and *Nihon Shoki*.During the Meiji era (1868–1912), nationalist leaders expelled Buddhist influence and formed State Shinto, which some historians regard as the origin of Shinto as a distinct religion; citizens were encouraged to worship the emperor as a kami, though the emperor's descent from [[Amaterasu]] was a longstanding tradition. Shinto spread to other parts of East Asia with the [[Empire of Japan]]. After Japan's defeat in [[World War II|World War II]], it was formally separated from the state.

Shinto is numerically Japan's largest religion, with Buddhism second. Most Japanese take part in both Shinto and Buddhist activities, and aspects of Shinto appear in various [[New religious movement|new religious movements]].

Large watercraft designed for travel across the surface of a body of water, carrying cargo or passengers, or supporting specialized tasks such as warfare, oceanography, and fishing.

Ships are generally distinguished from [[Boat|boats]] by size, shape, load capacity, and purpose, but there is no universally accepted distinction. A common notion holds that a ship can carry a boat but not the reverse. Some large vessels are traditionally called boats, notably [[Submarine|submarines]]. The word has meant either simply a large vessel or, from the mid-18th century, specifically a full-rigged ship with three or more masts, all square-rigged.

The earliest historical evidence of boats is found in Egypt in the 4th millennium BC. Model ships from [[Mesopotamia]] date to the same period. The [[Austronesian peoples]], who originated in what is now [[Taiwan]], used catamarans, outriggers, and crab claw sails to colonize Pacific islands and [[Madagascar]]. For most of history, transport by ship was cheaper, safer, and faster than overland travel, a principle changed only by railways in the mid-19th century and commercial aviation in the second half of the 20th. Rome consumed about 150,000 tons of Egyptian grain a year in the first three centuries AD, delivered by sailing and oared ships.

Ship transport carries the largest portion of world commerce. In 2024, ships had a global cargo capacity of 2.4 billion tons; the three largest classes were dry bulk carriers (43%), [[Tanker (ship)|oil tankers]] (28%), and container ships (14%).

Paradox and [[Thought experiment|thought experiment]] asking whether an object remains the same when all of its original parts are gradually replaced. The Greek biographer [[Plutarch]] described it in his *Life of Theseus*, about the ship on which Theseus had sailed after slaying the [[Minotaur]]. The Athenians preserved the ship by replacing rotted timbers, and philosophers debated whether it stayed the same ship. In *De Corpore* (1655), [[Thomas Hobbes]] asked which ship was the original if the discarded planks were used to build a second ship. The paradox informs the study of identity over time in [[Metaphysics|metaphysics]] and the [[Philosophy of mind|philosophy of mind]].

Construction of [[Ship|ships]] and other floating vessels, normally in a shipyard. Shipbuilders, or shipwrights, follow a trade with roots before recorded history. Shipbuilding drove several industrial advances, including timber-sawing by [[Windmill|windmill]]-powered saws in Dutch shipyards in the first half of the 17th century. Shipbuilders also used the [[Logarithm|logarithm]], invented in 1615, to generate hull curves. Ancient Egyptians joined hull planks edge to edge, a technique known by 3100 BC. Brief work of narrative fiction in [[Prose|prose]] that can typically be read in a single sitting.

A short story focuses on a self-contained incident or a series of linked incidents, intended to evoke a single effect or mood. It uses the techniques of the [[Novel|novel]], but typically to a lesser degree. Defining it is problematic. [[Edgar Allan Poe]]'s essay "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) made the classic point that it should be readable in one sitting, and H. G. Wells said it should take from fifteen to fifty minutes to read aloud. Somerset Maugham held that a story must have a plot, while [[Anton Chekhov]] thought it should be a suggestive "slice of life" with neither beginning nor end. [[William Faulkner]] called it character-driven. Typical length is 1,000 to 4,000 words, with some up to 15,000; under 1,000 is flash fiction.

The form descends from oral storytelling traditions that produced epics such as the *Ramayana*, the *Mahabharata*, and Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, and it existed as legends, myths, and fables in ancient communities. The anecdote, a brief realistic narrative embodying a point, was popular during the [[Roman Empire]]; many were collected in the 13th or 14th century as the *Gesta Romanorum*. In Europe, written form developed in the early 14th century, most notably with [[Giovanni Boccaccio]]'s *Decameron* and [[Geoffrey Chaucer]]'s *Canterbury Tales*.

The modern short story developed in the early 19th century, and the term acquired its modern meaning in the 1880s, having initially referred to children's tales. Washington Irving, Nathaniel Hawthorne, Nikolai Gogol, and Guy de Maupassant shaped it, and the 20th century brought expansive experimentation. Its form has been closely tied to the publishing industry.

[[Passerine|Passerine]] bird family of 34 species in four genera. The family name, and that of the genus *Lanius*, derive from the [[Latin|Latin]] word for butcher, and shrikes are also called butcherbirds because they impale prey on thorns and barbed wire. The English name comes from Old English *scrīc*, alluding to the shrike's shriek-like call. Most species live in open Old World habitats such as [[Savanna|savannas]] and [[Steppe|steppes]] in [[Africa|Africa]] and [[Eurasia|Eurasia]]; only two species are native to North America. Impaled prey serves as food stores and helps attract females. Shrikes hunt insects, lizards, rodents and small birds. Several species have declined, notably the loggerhead shrike in North America, owing to habitat loss and pesticides.
