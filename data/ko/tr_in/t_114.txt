ENTRY:
Initiation and propagation of cracks in a material due to repeated loading. Once a fatigue crack forms, it grows a small amount with each cycle, typically leaving striations on the fracture surface, until it reaches a critical size and the structure fractures rapidly. Fatigue damage occurs at stresses far below a material's yield strength, usually starting at [[Stress (mechanics)|stress]] concentrations such as holes, persistent slip bands or grain boundaries in metals. The term metal fatigue reflects its traditional association with metal parts, and a 19th-century belief that railway axles failed because the metal had crystallized has since been disproved. Most materials, including composites, [[Plastic|plastics]] and [[Ceramic|ceramics]], also fail through fatigue. To predict fatigue life, engineers test coupons under constant-amplitude cyclic loading and average the crack growth over thousands of cycles.

[[Carboxylic acid]] with an aliphatic chain, either saturated or unsaturated. Most naturally occurring fatty acids have an unbranched chain of an even number of carbon atoms, from 4 to 28. They make up up to 70% by weight of the [[Lipid|lipids]] in some microalgae, and in most organisms occur as esters in triglycerides, phospholipids and cholesteryl esters. Short-chain fatty acids have tails of five or fewer carbons, while very long-chain ones have 22 or more. ENTRY:
Planar fracture or discontinuity in a volume of rock across which significant displacement has occurred. Large faults in Earth's [[Crust (geology)|crust]] result from plate tectonic forces, with the largest forming the boundaries between [[Plate tectonics|plates]], such as the megathrust faults of subduction zones and transform faults. Rapid movement on active faults releases the energy that causes most [[Earthquake|earthquakes]], although some faults slip slowly by aseismic creep. Friction locks a fault until stress builds beyond its strength, and the fault then ruptures, releasing strain energy partly as seismic waves. On a non-vertical fault, the hanging wall lies above the fault plane and the footwall below; in reverse faults the hanging wall moves up, and in normal faults it moves down. A fault zone is a cluster of parallel faults or the zone of crushed rock along a single fault.

ENTRY:
[[Emotion|Emotional]] state aroused by threatening situations, usually regarded as negative and unpleasant. Fear can result directly from sensory stimulation, such as sounds resembling screaming, from anticipating that undesirable events may occur, or from imagining threats that may not be real.

In humans, fear is correlated with more rapid breathing and heart rate, dilation of the pupils, spikes in [[Cortisol|cortisol]] levels, and elevated electrodermal activity, changes that overlap considerably with other negative, arousing emotions. Behavioral reactions include the startle, fight-or-flight, and freeze responses. The amygdala serves as a central hub for threat detection, interfacing with the hypothalamus and [[Brainstem|brainstem]] to trigger release of [[Adrenaline|adrenaline]]. Fear is distinguished from [[Anxiety|anxiety]], a diffuse, future-oriented apprehension, by its focus on specific, proximate stimuli. Its subjective experience is shaped by individual differences, situational factors, and culture, and it may be pleasurable and deliberately sought in thrilling activities and horror films. Maladaptive levels are associated with specific [[Phobia|phobias]] and with depression, anxiety, and suicidality.

Although many fears are learned, the capacity to fear is part of human nature, and certain fears, such as of animals and heights, are far more common and easier to induce than others, such as of flowers or clouds. This phenomenon, called preparedness, is theorized to be a genetic effect of [[Natural selection|natural selection]]. From an [[Evolutionary psychology|evolutionary psychology]] perspective, fear of heights may be common to all mammals and fear of snakes to all simians.

Fear conditioning was first studied in John B. Watson's Little Albert experiment in 1920, in which an 11-month-old boy was conditioned to fear a white rat; the fear generalized to other white, furry objects, including a rabbit, a dog, and a Santa Claus mask.

Feminism holds that modern societies are patriarchal, prioritizing the male point of view, and that women are treated unjustly in them. Originating in late 18th-century Europe, feminist movements have campaigned for the right to vote, run for office, work, earn equal pay, own property, receive education, and enter into contracts, as well as for access to contraception and legal [[Abortion|abortion]] and protection from sexual assault, sexual harassment, and domestic violence.

[[Mary Wollstonecraft]] is seen by many as a founder of feminism because of her 1792 book [[A Vindication of the Rights of Woman|*A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*]], which argued that women as much as men needed equal rights. The word *féminisme* first appeared in France in 1871, and the *Oxford English Dictionary* dates the English use in this sense to 1895; early uses were negative.

Many scholars consider feminist campaigns a main force behind historical changes in women's rights, particularly in the West, where they are widely credited with achieving [[Women's suffrage|women's suffrage]], reproductive rights, and the right to own property. The movement is commonly described in "waves": the first focused on suffrage and property rights, the second on employment discrimination and reproductive autonomy from the mid-20th century, the third on identity and representation from the 1990s, and the fourth on digital activism since the 2010s. Liberal, socialist, and radical feminism are sometimes called the "Big Three" schools. Some newer forms have been criticized for reflecting only white, middle-class, or heterosexual perspectives, prompting black and intersectional feminism. Critics argue that feminism underappreciates biological sex differences, a claim supporters dispute.

ENTRY:
Freestanding structure that encloses an area, usually built from posts joined by boards, wire, rails or netting. A fence differs from a [[Wall|wall]] in not having a solid foundation along its whole length. A ditch, sometimes filled with water to form a [[Moat|moat]], is an alternative. Fences serve many purposes: agricultural fencing keeps [[Livestock|livestock]] in and predators out, sound barriers reduce noise pollution, and privacy fencing provides security. Common constructions include chain-link fencing, made of wires woven together; picket fences, generally waist-high and decorative; and split-rail fences. Fences are believed to date to about 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia, where they protected livestock.

Combat sport of sword fighting in three disciplines: foil, épée and sabre. Each discipline has its own blade and rules, and points are scored by making contact with the opponent with one's sword. Modern fencing developed from historical European swordsmanship; the Italian school altered classical fencing, and the French school later refined that system. Competitive fencing has been featured in every modern [[Olympic Games|Olympics]]. The sport is governed by the Fédération Internationale d'Escrime (FIE), headquartered in Lausanne, Switzerland, which has 155 national federations. In pool rounds, bouts are fenced to five hits or a single period of three minutes. Knockout bouts continue until one fencer scores 15 hits or the nine-minute fencing time expires.

Smallest fox species (*Vulpes zerda*), native to the [[Desert|deserts]] of North Africa. Its unusually large ears dissipate heat and help it listen for underground prey. The fennec mainly eats [[Insect|insects]], small mammals and birds. Families dig sand burrows as large as 120 sq m (1,300 sq ft). Population figures are not precisely known, but sightings suggest the species is not threatened with [[Extinction|extinction]]. It is commonly trapped for exhibition or sale in North Africa and is considered an exotic pet in some parts of the world.

Flowering perennial herb (*Foeniculum vulgare*) in the [[Apiaceae|carrot family]]. It is native to the shores of the Mediterranean and has become widely naturalized elsewhere, especially on dry soils near the sea coast and on riverbanks. The stem can grow to 2.1 meters (7 feet) tall and bears yellow flowers and feathery leaves. Its licorice-like flavor comes from anethole, also found in [[Anise|anise]], and fennel and anise are primary ingredients of [[Absinthe|absinthe]]. Fennel is widely cultivated for its edible leaves and fruits. Florence fennel, a [[Cultivar|cultivar]] with a swollen, bulb-like stem base, is eaten as a vegetable.
