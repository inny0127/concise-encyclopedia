Synthetic [[Chemical element|chemical element]] with symbol Mc and atomic number 115. It was first synthesized in 2003 by a joint team of Russian and American scientists at the Joint Institute for Nuclear Research in Dubna, Russia. It was officially named in 2016 after the Moscow Oblast, where the institute is situated. All known isotopes are extremely radioactive. Capital and most populous city of Russia, on the Moskva River in Central Russia.

The city has more than 13 million residents within its limits, more than 19.1 million in the urban area, and more than 21.5 million in the metropolitan area. It covers 2,511 sq km and is the most populous city entirely in Europe. It is the northernmost and coldest [[Megacity|megacity]] in the world and is governed as a federal city.

Moscow was first documented in 1147. As capital of the [[Grand Principality of Moscow]] it led the unification of Russian lands in the 15th century, and it remained a political and economic center after the Tsardom of Russia was proclaimed in 1547. [[Peter the Great]] moved the capital to the newly founded [[Saint Petersburg]] in 1712, and Moscow declined in importance until the capital returned in 1918. It then became the political center of the [[Soviet Union]] and grew greatly. After the Soviet dissolution it remained the capital of the Russian Federation.

Moscow is Russia's political, economic, cultural, and scientific center. Its urban economy is among the world's largest, and it has the second-highest number of billionaires of any city, tied with Hong Kong. The city hosted the 1980 Summer Olympics and co-hosted the 2018 FIFA World Cup.

Its architecture includes Red Square, Saint Basil's Cathedral, and the Moscow [[Kremlin]], seat of the Government of Russia; several sites are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. Transit includes four international airports, ten railway terminals, and the Moscow Metro, the busiest metro system in Europe and one of the largest [[Rapid transit|rapid transit]] systems in the world.

Hebrew prophet in the [[Abrahamic religions]] who led the [[Israelites]] out of slavery in the Exodus from Egypt.

According to the Book of Exodus, Moses was born while the enslaved Israelites were increasing in number. When Pharaoh ordered all newborn Hebrew boys killed, his mother, Jochebed, hid him in the bulrushes along the [[Nile]], where Pharaoh's daughter found and adopted him, so that he grew up in the Egyptian royal family. Family of small [[Fly|flies]], the Culicidae, consisting of about 3,600 species. Mosquitoes have one pair of wings and a long proboscis adapted for piercing and sucking. Small, typically non-vascular flowerless plant in the division Bryophyta. Mosses form dense green clumps or mats, often in damp or shady places, with leaves usually only one cell thick. They do not have seeds and reproduce with [[Spore|spores]] released from a [[Sporophyte]] capsule. Unlike vascular plants, the haploid [[Gametophyte]] generation is dominant in the life cycle. Mosses absorb water and nutrients mainly through their leaves. Most peat is made of mosses, mostly the genus [[Sphagnum]], and mosses are also used in gardens and the florist trade. They help stop [[Erosion|erosion]], improve microclimate and absorb carbon, with mosses estimated to absorb 6.4 billion tons of carbon annually. The tallest moss, *Dawsonia superba*, can grow to 60 centimeters.

Group of mostly nocturnal winged insects in the order [[Lepidoptera]]. Moths make up the vast majority of the order, with approximately 160,000 species, many not yet described. They are characterized by dark or dull patterned wings. They were once classified as suborder Heterocera, but that group is paraphyletic with respect to [[Butterfly|butterflies]], and neither subordinate taxon is used in modern classifications. Moths undergo complete [[Metamorphosis|metamorphosis]]. ENTRY:
Change in the position of an object or fluid with respect to a [[Frame of reference|reference frame]] over a given time.

Motion is described mathematically by vector quantities such as displacement, velocity, and acceleration. The branch of physics describing motion without reference to its cause is [[Kinematics|kinematics]]; the branch studying forces and their effect on motion is dynamics. An object not moving relative to a given frame is said to be at rest. Because modern physics holds that there is no absolute frame of reference, [[Isaac Newton]]'s concept of absolute motion cannot be determined, and everything in the universe can be considered to be in motion.

[[Classical mechanics]] describes macroscopic objects moving much slower than the speed of light, from projectiles and machinery to spacecraft, planets, and stars. It rests on [[Newton's laws of motion]], first compiled in his *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, published on July 5, 1687. The first law states that a body at rest stays at rest, and a body in motion stays in motion, unless acted on by an external force; the second relates force to the change in momentum, equal to mass times acceleration for constant mass; the third states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction. These laws unified the motion of celestial bodies with that of objects on Earth.

Modern kinematics, developed alongside the study of electromagnetism, refers velocities to the speed of light and transforms them by Lorentz transformations; this is [[Special relativity|special relativity]]. W. K. Clifford and Albert Einstein used differential geometry to incorporate gravity, producing [[General relativity|general relativity]]. At the atomic and subatomic level, [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]] applies. Because of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle, a particle's location and velocity cannot be determined simultaneously, and matter and radiation show wave–particle duality.

ENTRY:
Elevated portion of a planet's surface, generally with steep sides showing significant exposed [[Bedrock|bedrock]].

No universally accepted definition exists; elevation, volume, relief, steepness, spacing, and continuity have all been used as criteria. A mountain is usually considered higher than a hill and is often thought of as higher than 2,000 feet (610 m), the threshold the U.K. government uses for access purposes. By one UN Environment Programme classification, 24% of Earth's land mass is mountainous.

There are three main types of mountains: volcanic, fold, and block. All are formed through [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], when portions of the crust move, crumple, and dive. Volcanic mountains, such as [[Mount Fuji]] in Japan and [[Mount Pinatubo]] in the Philippines, are built where a plate is pushed below another or at a mid-ocean ridge or hotspot. Fold mountains arise when two plates collide, shortening the crust along thrust faults. Once building ceases, mountains are slowly leveled by weathering, mass wasting, and [[Erosion|erosion]] by rivers and glaciers.

High elevations produce colder climates than sea level at similar latitude, so different elevations support different plants and animals. Because of the less hospitable terrain and climate, mountains are used less for agriculture and more for resource extraction, such as mining and logging, and for recreation, such as climbing and skiing.

The highest mountain on Earth above sea level is [[Mount Everest]] in the Himalayas, whose summit is 8,850 m above mean sea level. The highest known mountain on any planet in the Solar System is [[Olympus Mons]] on Mars, at 21,171 m. Measured from base to peak, [[Mauna Kea]] in Hawaii rises 9,330 m from the ocean floor, and some scientists consider it the tallest on Earth.

Navigable route through a [[Mountain range|mountain range]] or over a ridge. Because ranges are formidable barriers to travel, passes have long played a key role in trade, war, and human and animal migration. Passes are typically formed between two volcanic peaks or created by erosion from water, ice or wind. The lowest point along a ridge, the saddle, often provides the route, and the top of a pass is frequently the only flat ground in the area. Roads and railways have long been built through passes, and some high ones have tunnels, such as the Eisenhower Tunnel beneath Loveland Pass in the Rockies. Where a national border follows a ridge, a pass is typically on the border; [[Argentina]] and [[Chile]] share a 5,300 kilometers (3,300 mi) border that includes 42 mountain passes. The Brenner Pass in the [[Alps]] is a typical historic trade route.
