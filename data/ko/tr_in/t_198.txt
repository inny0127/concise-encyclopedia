ENTRY:
Simple machine consisting of a beam or rigid rod pivoted at a fixed hinge, or fulcrum. It is one of the six [[Simple machine|simple machines]] identified by Renaissance scientists. A lever amplifies an input force to provide a greater output force, a gain called mechanical advantage, equal to the ratio of output force to input force.

For an ideal lever, with no friction in the hinge or bending in the beam, power into the lever equals power out. The ratio of output to input force then equals the ratio of the distances from the fulcrum to the points where the forces are applied, a relation known as the law of the lever.

Levers are classified by the relative positions of fulcrum, effort, and resistance (load). In a Class I lever the fulcrum lies between effort and resistance, as in a seesaw, crowbar, or claw hammer; its mechanical advantage may be greater than, less than, or equal to 1. In a Class II lever the resistance lies between effort and fulcrum, as in a wheelbarrow or nutcracker; its mechanical advantage is always greater than 1. In a Class III lever the effort lies between resistance and fulcrum, as in a hoe, tweezers, or a fishing rod; its mechanical advantage is always less than 1.

The earliest known evidence of a lever mechanism dates to [[Ancient Egypt]] c. 5000 BC, in a simple balance scale. In Mesopotamia c. 3000 BC the shadouf, a crane-like device, was invented, and Egyptian workmen used levers to move obelisks weighing more than 100 tons. The earliest surviving writings on levers date from the third century BC and are commonly attributed to [[Archimedes]], who is quoted as saying, "Give me a lever (long enough and a fulcrum on which to place it), and I shall move the world."

Jefferson sought a practical water route to the Pacific, a declaration of U.S. sovereignty over Native American lands, and an American presence before European powers could claim the territory. The expedition also documented the West's biodiversity, topography, and geography and aimed to build trade relations with Native American tribes.

State of being free within society from restrictions imposed by authority on one's way of life, behavior, or political views. In U.S. constitutional law, ordered liberty means a balanced society in which individuals can act without unnecessary interference (negative liberty) and have access to opportunities and resources to pursue their goals (positive liberty). Some writers distinguish liberty from freedom, using "freedom" for the ability to do as one wills and "liberty" for the absence of arbitrary restraints, subject to the rights of others. The word comes from [[Latin]] *liber*.

The concept has long occupied philosophers. [[Thomas Hobbes]] held that a free man is one not hindered from doing what he has the will to do. [[John Locke]] rejected that definition: in political society, liberty consists of being under no lawmaking power except that established by consent, and freedom is constrained by laws in both the state of nature and political society. [[John Stuart Mill]]'s *On Liberty* (1859) was the first to recognize the difference between liberty as freedom to act and liberty as absence of coercion. [[Isaiah Berlin]] framed this as the distinction between negative liberty, protection from tyranny and arbitrary authority, and positive liberty, self-mastery over inner compulsions such as weakness and fear. Critics argue that positive liberty can justify coercion in the name of collective goals.

The political concept originates in Greek ideas of freedom and slavery: to be free was not to have a master. [[Aristotle]] linked this to democracy, though it applied only to free men. Capacity in matter, formed of one or more units called cells, for processes such as cell signaling, homeostasis, metabolism, cell growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. All life eventually reaches a state of death. Life exists all over Earth in air, water, and soil, with many ecosystems forming the [[Biosphere|biosphere]]; some harsh environments are occupied only by [[Extremophile|extremophiles]]. The life of a particular ecosystem is called its biota. Life is known to exist only on Earth, though extraterrestrial life is thought to be probable.

Defining life has long challenged scientists and philosophers, partly because life is a process, not a substance. At least 123 definitions have been compiled. With no consensus, most definitions in biology are descriptive, listing traits: homeostasis, organization in one or more cells, metabolism, growth, adaptation, response to stimuli, and reproduction. From a physics perspective, an organism is a thermodynamic system with an organized molecular structure that can reproduce itself and evolve. A NASA committee adopted the definition "a self-sustained chemical system capable of undergoing Darwinian evolution," based on a suggestion by Carl Sagan; critics note that by it a single sexually reproducing individual is not alive. Systemic definitions instead describe living things as self-organizing and autopoietic (self-producing). Whether viruses are alive is controversial: they possess genes, evolve by natural selection, and replicate by self-assembly, but do not metabolize and require a host cell.

Living things are composed of biochemical molecules formed mainly from a few core chemical elements. All contain two types of macromolecules, proteins and nucleic acids (usually both DNA and RNA); the nucleic acids carry the information needed to make each type of protein, and the proteins carry out the chemical processes of life. The [[Cell (biology)|cell]] is the structural and functional unit of life. Prokaryotes (bacteria and archaea) consist of small single cells, while eukaryotes may be single-celled or multicellular. Life is organized hierarchically, from molecular machines to cells, organs, organisms, populations, ecosystems, and the whole biosphere.

Study of life dates to ancient times. Empedocles (430 BC) held that everything is made of four eternal elements, earth, water, air, and fire, while [[Aristotle]]'s hylomorphism held that living things have souls and embody both form and matter. Classification also began with Aristotle; modern classification began with [[Carl Linnaeus]]'s binomial nomenclature in the 1740s.

Life originated at least 3.5 billion years ago, producing a universal common ancestor from which all existing species evolved, along with many extinct species, some of which left fossils. Photosynthetic [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]] oxygenated the atmosphere around 2.4 billion years ago. An estimated 8.7 million eukaryotic species exist, of which more than 2.2 million have been formally described. Many religions hold faith in an afterlife, reincarnation of the soul, or resurrection of the body.
