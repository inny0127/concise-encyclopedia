Study of [[Family|families]], family history and the tracing of [[Lineage (anthropology)|lineages]]. Genealogists use oral interviews, historical records, genetic analysis and other sources to demonstrate [[Kinship|kinship]] and pedigrees, presenting results as charts or narratives. In the narrow sense, a genealogy or family tree traces the descendants of one person, while a family history traces that person's ancestors, although the terms are often used interchangeably. Family history is broader, adding biography, traditions and community history. Amateurs usually research their own ancestry, while professionals may research for others, publish methods books or produce databases. Motives include curiosity about heritage, especially where records were lost through [[Adoption|adoption]] or [[Divorce|divorce]], scholarly or forensic work, and tracing legal next of kin under intestacy laws. Autosomal [[DNA]] analysis now confirms documentary links and identifies unknown relatives through shared genomic segments.

Geometric theory of gravitation published by [[Albert Einstein]] in May 1916. Branch of mathematics, also called point-set topology, that deals with the basic set-theoretic definitions and constructions used in [[Topology|topology]]. Its fundamental concepts are continuity, compactness and connectedness, defined by means of [[Open set|open sets]]. A topology on a set X is a family of subsets containing the empty set and X, closed under arbitrary unions and finite intersections, and a set with a topology is a [[Topological space|topological space]]. Different choices of open sets yield different notions of continuity. [[Metric space|Metric spaces]], in which a distance is defined between points, form an important class of topological spaces. General topology emerged from the study of subsets of the real line, the manifold concept and normed spaces, and took its present form around 1940. It is the foundation for [[Algebraic topology|algebraic topology]], differential topology and geometric topology.

ENTRY:
All individuals born and living at about the same time, regarded collectively. In kinship, the term designates the parent–child relationship. In demographics and social science it is a synonym for birth cohort, people who experience the same significant events within a given period. The average familial generation is about 20–30 years. In developed nations it is in the high 20s, having risen since the late 18th century with [[Industrialisation|industrialization]], urbanization and later first pregnancies, while in less-developed nations it remains in the low 20s. Social generations became a concept in the 19th century, when modernization and youth-centered political rhetoric encouraged dividing society by age. Critics argue that generational differences often reflect age and period effects rather than enduring traits.

Intentional partial or total destruction of a human group. In scholarly and legal fields, genocide occurs when the group itself is targeted, not merely when many individuals are killed. Its acts include killing, preventing reproduction within the group, forcibly transferring children to another group, and cultural genocide.

The Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin coined the term between 1941 and 1943, combining the Greek *genos* ('race, people') with the Latin suffix *-caedo* ('act of killing'); his interest was first sparked by the [[Armenian genocide]]. He lobbied for genocide to be outlawed by treaty and brought his proposal to the newly established [[United Nations]] in 1946. Opposition was greater than he expected, as states feared that their own policies, including the treatment of indigenous peoples, colonialism, and racial segregation in the United States, might be labeled genocide. The Genocide Convention, adopted by the UN General Assembly on December 9, 1948, restricted his broad definition to five specific acts "committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial, or religious group, as such." Proving this specific intent is the strictest requirement in international criminal law and has led to few convictions, among them those for the 1994 [[Rwandan genocide]]. The definition remains contested across law, history, and sociology.

Genocide has occurred throughout history, most often in wartime and in situations of imperial expansion, and it is associated with colonialism and with both world wars. It is most often a state crime, and perpetrators typically believe themselves to be existentially threatened. The most common pattern is the killing of adult men together with sexual assault and enslavement of women and children; forced displacement and destruction of cultural heritage are also common. ENTRY:
Science of determining the age of rocks, fossils and sediments using signatures inherent in the rocks themselves. Absolute geochronology relies on radioactive isotopes, while relative geochronology uses tools such as paleomagnetism and stable isotope ratios. Combining several geochronological and biostratigraphic indicators improves the precision of an age. Biostratigraphy places rocks within an interval of time by their fossil assemblages but gives no direct absolute age. Radiometric methods measure the decay of a radioactive parent isotope with a known [[Half-life|half-life]] into its radiogenic product. Uranium–lead dating, often applied to the mineral [[Zircon]] in igneous rocks, is one of the two most commonly used methods for geologic dating, along with argon–argon dating. Geochronology is the prime tool of chronostratigraphy, which seeks absolute ages for fossil assemblages and the geologic history of Earth.

Science of measuring and representing the geometry, gravity and spatial orientation of Earth in time-varying three-dimensional space. ENTRY:
Reference frame for representing the position of locations on Earth. Datums are crucial to [[Geodesy|geodesy]], navigation, surveying, geographic information systems, remote sensing and cartography. A horizontal datum measures position across Earth's surface as latitude and longitude, and a vertical datum measures elevation or depth relative to a standard origin such as [[Sea level|mean sea level]]. A standard datum consists of a model of Earth's shape (a reference ellipsoid or [[Geoid|geoid]]), an origin tied to a monumented location, and control points measured from that origin. The relationship between coordinates in different datums is undefined and can only be approximated; the disparity between two datums can reach kilometers, a discrepancy called datum shift. Since the rise of the global positioning system, the WGS 84 datum has supplanted most others in many applications, although local datums such as OSGB36 approximate particular areas more closely.
