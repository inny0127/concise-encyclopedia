ENTRY:
Study of the theoretical and conceptual foundations of politics, also called political theory. It examines the nature, scope and legitimacy of institutions such as [[State (polity)|states]], and the values that guide political action, such as justice, equality and liberty. As a normative field, it focuses on desirable norms, in contrast to political science, which emphasizes empirical description. Major ideologies include [[Anarchism|anarchism]], which rejects the coercive power of centralized governments; [[Conservatism|conservatism]], which seeks to preserve traditional institutions; [[Liberalism|liberalism]], which advocates individual rights and private property; and [[Socialism|socialism]], which emphasizes collective ownership. The field has roots in ancient Greece, where [[Plato]] and [[Aristotle]] discussed justice and ideal states, and in ancient China, where Confucianism, Taoism and legalism emerged.

Academic discipline that studies politics, systems of governance and power, political behavior and thought, and associated constitutions and laws. Unlike [[Political philosophy|political philosophy]], which is primarily normative and judges politics by values, it emphasizes descriptive and explanatory accounts and favors empirical evidence over ethical judgments.

The field emerged as a distinct discipline in the late 19th century, influenced by [[Empiricism|empiricism]] and [[Positivism|positivism]], from history, philosophy, and law amid the professionalization of universities. *Political Science Quarterly* was established in 1886, and its first issue defined political science as "the science of the state." The American Political Science Association (APSA) was founded in 1903 and the *American Political Science Review* in 1906; APSA now serves more than 11,000 members in more than 100 countries. A UNESCO initiative led to the International Political Science Association in 1949, with national associations in France in 1949, Britain in 1950, and West Germany in 1951.

The behavioral revolution of the 1950s shifted emphasis to explaining human behavior from a neutral point of view using [[Quantitative research|quantitative methods]] such as surveys and empirical testing. In the late 1960s and early 1970s, [[Game theory|game-theoretic]] modeling borrowed from economics was applied to institutions such as the U.S. Congress and to voting, led by William H. Riker and colleagues at the University of Rochester. The Perestroika Movement of 2000 reacted against what its supporters called the "mathematicization" of the field and argued for a plurality of methodologies.

Its main subfields are political theory, comparative politics, [[International relations|international relations]], American politics, political methodology, and [[Public administration|public administration]]. Critics have raised concerns about the field's replicability and about the political leanings of its practitioners.

System for classifying political positions in relation to one another, usually placed along one or more geometric axes. The left–right dimension appears in most long-standing spectra. Its terms derive from seating arrangements in the [[France|French]] parliament after the Revolution (1789–1799), where radicals sat on the left and aristocrats on the right. [[Communism]] and [[Socialism|socialism]] are usually regarded internationally as left, while [[Conservatism|conservatism]] and reactionism are usually regarded as right. [[Liberalism]] can fall on either side. Several political scientists have noted that a single left–right axis is too simplistic, so they add other axes, often splitting economic issues from socio-cultural issues. In 1950 Leonard W. Ferguson factor-analyzed ten attitude scales and identified three factors, which he named religionism, humanitarianism and nationalism.

Activity of settling affairs in an organized society, usually by resolving issues through a [[Government|government]]. People engaged in it are politicians, and the branch of social science that studies politics and government is [[Political science|political science]]. Methods include promoting one's views among people, negotiating, making laws, and exercising internal and external force, including warfare against adversaries. Politics operates at levels from clans and tribes of traditional societies through local governments, companies, and institutions to sovereign states and the international level. In modern states people often form parties to represent their ideas, and an election is usually a competition between parties. A political system is a framework that defines acceptable political methods within a society. The word may be used positively, as in a compromising and non-violent "political solution," or descriptively, as "the art or science of government," but it often carries a negative connotation.

The English word derives from Greek *polis*, "city." Aristotle's *Politiká* ("affairs of the cities") was rendered in the mid-15th century as *Polettiques*, which became Politics; the singular *politic* is attested in English from 1430.

Writers differ on what counts as political. Adrian Leftwich distinguished an extensive view, which finds politics across all human social relations, from a limited view that restricts it to certain contexts such as governance. The feminist slogan "the personal is political" belongs to the extensive view. Robert A. Dahl argued that politics may be defined by the use of power. Political moralists link politics closely to ethics; [[Hannah Arendt]] wrote that for Aristotle, being political meant that everything was decided through words and persuasion rather than violence. Realists, a group that some scholars say includes [[Niccolò Machiavelli]], [[Thomas Hobbes]], and Harold Lasswell, see its true nature in the human desire for rule and self-interest; Lasswell defined it as determining "who gets what, when, and how." Elmer Schattschneider held that conflict lies at the root of all politics, and Carl Schmitt saw its essence in distinguishing friend from foe, while Aristotle and Bernard Crick took more cooperative views. Michael Laver described it as a blend of conflict and co-operation.

Early bands and tribes lacked centralized political structures. Theories of early state formation include voluntary theories, which stress shared rational interest and the pressures that followed the development of agriculture, including the hydraulic hypothesis tying the state to large-scale irrigation; and conflict theories, which stress dominance of one group over others and warfare. The first states were early dynastic [[Sumer]] and early dynastic Egypt, which arose around 3000 BC. Egyptians, Romans, and Greeks were the first known to formulate a political philosophy of the state and analyze political institutions rationally; before that, states were justified through religious myths. The history of political thought reaches back to early antiquity, with seminal works such as [[Plato]]'s *Republic*, Aristotle's *Politics*, Confucius's political manuscripts, and Chanakya's *Arthashastra*. Greek city-states and the Roman Republic produced key innovations, including direct democracy in Athens, where citizenship was combined with directly democratic government.

Powdery substance produced by most types of flowers of seed plants for sexual reproduction. Pollen consists of grains, highly reduced [[Gametophyte|gametophytes]] that produce [[Sperm|sperm]] cells. Each grain has a hard coat of sporopollenin that protects it while it travels from the stamens to the pistil of a flower, or from the male cone to the female cone of a [[Gymnosperm|gymnosperm]]. If pollen lands on a compatible pistil or cone, it germinates and grows a pollen tube that carries sperm to the ovule. The smallest grains, those of forget-me-not, are 2.5–5 μm across, while corn pollen is about 90–100 μm. The study of pollen, palynology, is useful in paleoecology, [[Paleontology|paleontology]], [[Archaeology|archaeology]] and forensics. Pollen is infrequently used as food and is often contaminated by agricultural pesticides.

Transfer of [[Pollen|pollen]] from the anther of a plant to the stigma of a plant, which enables [[Fertilisation|fertilization]] and seed production. Introduction of contaminants into the natural environment that cause harm. Pollution can take the form of any substance (solid, liquid, or gas) or energy such as radioactivity, heat, sound, or light. Although it can be caused by natural events, the word generally implies a human source, such as manufacturing, extractive industries, poor [[Waste management|waste management]], transportation, or agriculture. It is often classed as point source, coming from a concentrated site such as a [[Factory|factory]], mine, or construction site, or nonpoint source, coming from widespread sources such as microplastics or agricultural runoff.

Major forms include [[Air pollution|air pollution]], [[Water pollution|water pollution]], litter, noise pollution, plastic pollution, soil contamination, radioactive contamination, thermal pollution, light pollution, and visual pollution. Common gaseous air pollutants include [[Carbon monoxide|carbon monoxide]], [[Sulfur dioxide|sulfur dioxide]], chlorofluorocarbons, and nitrogen oxides produced by industry and motor vehicles. Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances ([[PFAS]]), informally called "forever chemicals," are a major chemical pollutant.

In 2019, pollution killed approximately nine million people worldwide (about one in six deaths that year); about three-quarters of these deaths were caused by air pollution. Children, the elderly, and marginalized communities bear outsized impacts, a core reason for the environmental justice movement. Many sources went unregulated during 19th- and 20th-century industrialization until environmental regulation emerged in the later half of the 20th century. International efforts are now coordinated by the [[United Nations|UN]] Environmental Program and other treaty bodies.

Commonly encountered lugworms, bloodworms and sandworms inhabit shallow coasts and are used as fishing bait. Giant tube worms are extremophiles that tolerate near-boiling water near [[Hydrothermal vent|hydrothermal vents]]. Body plans vary widely, reflecting burrowing, swimming and tube-dwelling lifestyles.

Plane figure made up of line segments connected to form a closed chain. The segments are its edges or sides, and the points where two edges meet are its vertices or corners. An n-gon has n sides; a [[Triangle|triangle]] is a 3-gon, and a [[Quadrilateral|quadrilateral]] a 4-gon.

A simple polygon does not intersect itself and forms the boundary of a region of the plane called a solid polygon. A chain that crosses itself produces star polygons and other self-intersecting polygons. Some sources also count closed chains in [[Euclidean space]] that do not lie in a single plane (skew polygons). A polygon is the 2-dimensional example of the more general polytope.

Polygons are classified by number of sides, convexity, and symmetry. In a convex polygon, any line through it that is not tangent to an edge or corner meets the boundary exactly twice, and all interior angles are less than 180°. A concave polygon is simple but has at least one interior angle greater than 180°. A regular polygon has equal angles and equal edges. A cyclic polygon has all corners on a single [[Circle|circle]], the circumcircle.

The interior angles of a simple n-gon sum to (n − 2) × 180°, because it can be divided into (n − 2) triangles. The exterior angles of a convex polygon sum to 360°; in general the sum can be any integer multiple of 360°, such as 720° for a [[Pentagram|pentagram]]. Polygons form the basis of polygonal meshes in [[Computer graphics|computer graphics]], of tiling in architecture, and of boundaries in geographic information systems.

The region is generally defined as the islands within the Polynesian Triangle, drawn by connecting Hawaii, [[New Zealand]], and [[Easter Island]]. The [[Hawaiian Islands]] lie at its northern vertex.
