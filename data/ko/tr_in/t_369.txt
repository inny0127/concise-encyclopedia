ENTRY:
Basic element of language that carries meaning, can be used on its own, and is uninterruptible. Speakers usually have an intuitive grasp of what a word is, but linguists have no consensus on a definition, and attempts to find specific criteria remain controversial. Some linguists propose avoiding the term in favor of better-defined ones such as morphemes.

A word is distinguished from a [[Morpheme|morpheme]], the smallest unit of language with meaning, even if it cannot stand alone. Words consist of at least one morpheme. In English they generally include at least one root (*rock*, *god*, *write*) and possibly affixes (*-s*, *un-*, *-ly*, *-ness*). Words with more than one root, such as *typewriter*, are compound words.

Definitions can be consistent at particular levels. Phonologically, a word is the smallest segment of sound that can be isolated by word accent and boundary markers. Orthographically, it is a segment set off by blank spaces. Morphologically, it is the basic element of grammatical paradigms such as inflection. Semantically, it is the smallest relatively independent carrier of meaning in a lexicon, and in [[Syntax|syntax]], the smallest permutable and substitutable unit of a sentence. In some languages these types coincide; in others they correspond to elements of different size.

Phonological words can differ from intuition. In Walmatjari, an Australian language, a phonological word must have at least two syllables. The Finnish compound *pääkaupunki* "capital" counts as two phonological words because it does not conform to Finnish vowel harmony within words.

Dictionaries list a language's lexicon as lemmas, indicating what its writers regard as words.

Device or computer program for the input, editing, formatting, and output of text. Early word processors were standalone machines, but most are now software running on [[Personal computer|personal computers]] and [[Smartphone|smartphones]]. Mechanical forerunners include a machine for transcribing letters patented in 1714 by Henry Mill, and the first recognizable typewriter, created by Christopher Latham Sholes in the late 19th century. ENTRY:
Intentional human activity undertaken to meet one's own needs and wants, or those of others or of organizations. In economics, work is human labor that, alongside other factors of production, contributes to the creation of [[Goods|goods]] and services. It exists in all human societies, paid or unpaid, from gathering resources by hand in [[Hunter-gatherer|hunter-gatherer]] groups to operating complex technologies in industrial and post-industrial societies. A person's regular role in work is an occupation, or job. Societies coordinate work through institutions such as government programs, nonprofit organizations, cooperatives, and corporations.

What counts as work depends on context. Sport is a job for a professional athlete but a [[Hobby|hobby]] for someone playing for fun, and a paramedic on duty is working while a bystander giving [[First aid|first aid]] is not. [[Mark Twain]]'s whitewashed-fence scene in *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer* expresses the view that the label is somewhat subjective.

Hunter-gatherers varied their effort with the seasons; agriculture brought more sustained effort, peaking at harvest. In the early modern era, Protestantism and proto-capitalism emphasized the moral advantages of hard work, and industrialization intensified demands on workers to keep pace with machines. Restrictions on working hours and the ages of workers followed. According to a 2024 study, most current U.S. employment is in occupations introduced since 1940. Economists commonly divide economies into primary (extraction), secondary (manufacturing), and tertiary (services) sectors.

Work has been tied to power, class, tradition, and rights, and dirty, dangerous, or demeaning occupations can threaten survival. Paul Lafargue, David Graeber, and Bob Black are among those who have urged reducing or abolishing it. Retirement and, more recently, experiments with universal basic income aim to end the economic necessity of lifelong work.

ENTRY:
Energy transferred to or from an object by a [[Force|force]] acting along a displacement. For a constant force aligned with the motion, work equals the force multiplied by the distance traveled; in general it depends on the [[Angle|angle]] between the two vectors. Work is positive when the force has a component in the direction of displacement and negative when it opposes the displacement. A dropped ball gains positive work from gravity, while a thrown ball has negative work. Work is a scalar measured in [[Joule|joules]] in the [[International System of Units]]. The term was introduced in the late 1820s by French scientists Gaspard-Gustave Coriolis and Jean-Victor Poncelet. Earlier, [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] had shown that [[Simple machine|simple machines]] transform energy rather than create it.

Social class of people who depend on wages or salaries for their livelihood. In the United States the term usually covers blue-collar and pink-collar workers, or those whose income falls below the middle class. Socialists define it more broadly, to include all who must sell their labor to live. [[Karl Marx]] called this group the proletariat and, with [[Friedrich Engels]], argued in *The Communist Manifesto* that the working class would displace capitalism. Debates continue over whether the class is shrinking through educational expansion and growth in service jobs.

Global conflict (September 1, 1939–September 2, 1945) between two coalitions, the Allies and the [[Axis powers]]. Nearly all the world's countries took part, many in total war on an unprecedented scale. It was the deadliest conflict in history; estimates of deaths range from 60 million to 85 million, a majority civilians.

Causes included unresolved tensions after [[World War I]], the rise of fascism in Europe, and Japanese militarism. Most historians date the war's start to September 1, 1939, when Nazi Germany under [[Adolf Hitler]] invaded Poland and the United Kingdom and France declared war. The Soviet Union invaded Poland in mid-September, and the two states partitioned it under the [[Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact]]. France fell in June 1940. Germany invaded the Soviet Union in June 1941, opening the Eastern Front. In December 1941 Japan attacked American and British territories, including Pearl Harbor, bringing in the United States.

Japan's advance was halted at Midway in June 1942, and Axis forces were defeated in North Africa and at Stalingrad in early 1943. Germany surrendered unconditionally on May 8, 1945. On August 6 and 9 the United States dropped atomic bombs on [[Hiroshima]] and [[Nagasaki]]; Japan announced its surrender on August 15 and signed on September 2.

The war reshaped the world. The [[United Nations]] was created. The Soviet Union and the United States emerged as rival superpowers, setting the stage for the [[Cold War]], and the decline of Europe's great powers triggered decolonization in Africa and Asia.

It saw new weaponry, including machine guns, chemical weapons, and tanks.

Causes included the rise of the German Empire, which disturbed the balance of power in Europe, and imperial rivalries reflected in new alliances and an arms race. On June 28, 1914, [[Gavrilo Princip]], a Bosnian Serb, assassinated Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. In [[Ancient Greece|ancient Greece]], philosophers including [[Plato]] and [[Socrates]] practiced wrestling, and many of Plato's dialogues are set in wrestling schools. The first organized national tournament in the United States was held in [[New York City]] in 1888.

Act of creating a persistent, usually visual representation of language on a surface. As a structured system of communication, it is also known as written language. A set of symbols, called a script, together with the rules by which it encodes a spoken language, is a [[Writing system|writing system]]. In rare cases writing is tactile rather than visual. Its physical output, whether inscribed, mechanically transferred, or digitally represented, is called a text; construing a text is [[Reading|reading]], and the ability to read and write is [[Literacy|literacy]].

Writing systems are not languages in themselves but durable means of representing language so that it can be understood later. Language is universal across human societies, but most spoken languages are not written. Writing extends speech across space, as in correspondence, and stores it for future reading, as in libraries. It also lets people externalize their thinking in forms that are easier to reflect on, elaborate, and revise.

Writing arose independently in the ancient Near East, Egypt, China, and Mesoamerica. The earliest attested systems appeared around 3200 BC in [[Mesopotamia]] with proto-[[Cuneiform|cuneiform]], first used for accounting on clay tokens and tablets, and nearly simultaneously in Egypt with hieroglyphs. Systems later diversified into logographic, syllabic, and alphabetic forms. Writing emerged to meet the needs of societies growing in economic and social complexity: tracking produce and wealth, recording history, and administering through legal codes, census records, contracts, deeds, taxation, and treaties.

Implements have included fingers, styluses, ink brushes, pencils, and pens; surfaces have included stone and clay tablets, bamboo slips, [[Papyrus|papyrus]], wax tablets, vellum, parchment, paper, copperplate, and slate. The [[Typewriter|typewriter]] and the digital word processor let writers produce visually consistent text mechanically. Natural language generation software first produced formulaic texts such as weather forecasts and sports summaries; generative pre-trained transformers can now produce longer texts from a prompt.

Individual motivations include going beyond the limits of memory (lists, recipes, maps), spreading ideas (essays, petitions, manifestos), storytelling, maintaining social networks, business correspondence, and diaries. Digital communication such as [[Email|email]] and social media has made writing an increasingly important part of daily life. In many occupations, including law, accounting, and software design, written documentation is both the main product and the mode of work. Commerce, banking, and government rest on written documents, from contracts and tax records to national constitutions.

Conventional system for representing a particular language using a set of symbols, called a script, and the rules those symbols encode. The earliest writing systems appeared in the late 4th millennium BC. Each independently invented system emerged from proto-writing, in which a few ideographs were used in a way that could not fully encode language.

Systems are classified by how their symbols, called graphemes, relate to units of language. Phonetic systems, which include alphabets and syllabaries, use graphemes that correspond to sounds. [[Alphabet|Alphabets]] use letters that generally correspond to spoken [[Phoneme|phonemes]]: pure alphabets represent consonants and vowels, abjads generally only consonants, and abugidas consonant–vowel pairs. Syllabaries use syllabograms representing whole syllables or moras. Logographic systems use graphemes that represent units of meaning such as words or [[Morpheme|morphemes]]. Alphabets typically use fewer than 100 symbols, syllabaries and logographies hundreds or thousands.

Under most contemporary definitions, writing is a visual and tactile notation whose symbols correspond systematically to functional units of a spoken or signed language, which excludes drawings and maps. Most spoken languages have not been written, and all written languages rest on a spoken one. One language, such as Hindustani, can be written in several systems, and one system can represent several languages. [[Chinese characters]] were used to write [[Vietnamese language|Vietnamese]] from at least the 13th century until the Latin-based Vietnamese alphabet replaced them in the 20th century.

The grapheme is the basic functional unit of a writing system, comparable to the phoneme in speech; visually distinct variants of one grapheme are called allographs. [[Orthography]] is the set of rules and conventions for writing shared by a community, including spelling. English orthography, for example, uses uppercase and lowercase forms of 26 letters of the [[Latin alphabet]].
