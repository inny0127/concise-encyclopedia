Ruler of the [[Khmer Empire]] (r. 1113–1150). He is best known as the builder of [[Angkor Wat]], the largest Hindu temple in the world, which he dedicated to Vishnu and [[Shiva]]. Heterogeneous [[Mixture|mixture]] of a fluid containing solid particles large enough to settle out over time. Particles usually must be larger than one micrometer, and they eventually settle; sand in water is a typical example. Its particles are visible under a [[Microscope|microscope]] and settle if left undisturbed. A suspension differs from a [[Colloid|colloid]], whose particles are smaller and do not settle, and from a [[Solution (chemistry)|solution]], in which the solute does not exist as a solid. A suspension of liquid droplets or fine solid particles in a gas is an [[Aerosol|aerosol]]. In the atmosphere, suspended particulates include fine dust, [[Soot|soot]], sea salt, biogenic and [[Volcano|volcano]] sulfates, [[Nitrate|nitrate]], and cloud droplets. Suspensions are thermodynamically unstable but can be kinetically stable for a long time, which can determine a suspension's shelf life. Multiple light scattering is the most widely used technique to monitor a product's dispersion state.

Ability to continue over a long period of time; in modern usage, a state in which the environment, [[Economy|economy]] and [[Society|society]] continue to exist. Many definitions emphasize the environmental dimension, including problems such as [[Climate change|climate change]] and biodiversity loss. Swifts are among the fastest birds in level flight. The white-throated needletail has been reported at up to 169 km/h (105 mph), and the common swift can cruise at 31 meters per second. Swifts spend nearly their whole lives in flight, catching [[Insect|insects]] on the wing, and their weak feet prevent effective ground movement. Swiftlets navigate dark caves by echolocation. Some taxonomies place swifts with [[Hummingbird|hummingbirds]], which share the ability to rotate their wings from the base. About 100 species are grouped into two subfamilies. The name derives from the Greek *ápous*, meaning "footless."

Self-propulsion of a person through water, usually for recreation, sport, exercise, or survival.

Swimmers move by coordinating limb and body movements to achieve [[Buoyancy|buoyancy]] and hydrodynamic thrust, combining forward thrusting actions of the head and torso with arm strokes and leg kicks. The human body has an average relative density of 0.98 compared with water, so it floats, though buoyancy varies with body composition, lung inflation, muscle and fat content, and the salinity of the water. The density and [[Viscosity|viscosity]] of water create resistance that strokes use for propulsion but that also produces drag, so faster swimmers reduce resistance by holding a horizontal position, rolling the body, and extending the arms.

Four main strokes are used in competition and recreation: the front crawl (freestyle), breaststroke, backstroke, and butterfly. The front crawl is widely regarded as the fastest.

Swimming is consistently among the top public recreational activities, and swimming lessons are compulsory in the educational curriculum in some countries. It offers health benefits, including cardiovascular health, muscle strength, and flexibility, and is low-impact because water supports the body's weight. Risks include panic and exhaustion, which can cause drowning, and exposure to [[Infection|infection]] or hostile aquatic fauna; most facilities employ lifeguards.

Cave paintings from approximately 7000 years ago depict swimming, and organized practice existed in ancient Egypt around 2500 BC. [[Competitive swimming]] gained prominence in 19th-century Europe and has featured in every modern Summer [[Olympic Games]], beginning in 1896. Races cover distances from 50 to 1500 m. American swimmer Michael Phelps holds the record for most Olympic medals, with 28, including 23 golds.
