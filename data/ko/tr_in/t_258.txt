Flowering plant whose seeds and pods are eaten as a vegetable, *Pisum sativum*. Native to the Mediterranean region and southwest Asia, the pea is an annual, cool-season crop now cultivated nearly worldwide. [[Carl Linnaeus]] gave it its scientific name in 1753, meaning cultivated pea; some sources now classify it as *Lathyrus oleraceus*, a change that is disputed. Botanically, pea pods are fruit. Immature peas are eaten fresh, frozen or canned, while field peas are grown to produce dried peas, the basis of pease porridge and pea soup, staples of medieval cuisine. Pea plants can self-pollinate. Its large genome made the pea difficult to sequence; a draft reference genome was announced in September 2019.

ENTRY:
State of harmony in the absence of hostility and violence. In a societal sense, the term commonly means a lack of conflict and freedom from fear of violence between individuals or groups. Promotion of peace is a core tenet of many philosophies, religions, and ideologies, including [[Buddhism]], [[Christianity]], Jainism, and [[Islam]]; of figures such as [[Mahatma Gandhi]] and [[Martin Luther King Jr.]]; of writings such as [[Immanuel Kant]]'s *Perpetual Peace: A Philosophical Sketch*; and of pacifism. No universal definition exists; the meaning of peace and its symbols varies with culture, context, and history.

The English word comes from Anglo-French *pes* and Old French *pais*, from Latin *pax*. From c. 1300 it was used in greetings as a translation of the Hebrew *shalom*.

Peace has been forged through diplomacy, as in royal marriages between Greek princesses and the kings of Phrygia and Lydia around 800 BC and 600 BC. It has also been enforced by victors through violence and coercion. In *Agricola*, [[Tacitus]] attributes to the Caledonian chieftain Calgacus the charge that the Romans, "where they make a desert, they call it peace." Societal peace can be understood as the simple absence of war, or as absence of war accompanied by justice, mutual respect, respect for law, and good will. Scholars likewise distinguish negative peace, the mere cessation of direct violence, from positive peace, which includes equitable institutions and social justice. Debate continues over whether pacifist ideals suffice or whether military readiness and balances of power better secure stability, and the democratic peace theory faces scrutiny over its causal claims. Since 1945 the [[United Nations]] and its Security Council have operated under the aim of resolving conflicts without war, though nations have entered numerous military conflicts since.

Legume cultivated as a grain and oil crop, *Arachis hypogaea*, also called the groundnut. Native to South America, where cultivated peanuts arose from a hybrid of two wild species, it fruits underground. Hard, shiny object produced within the mantle of a living shelled mollusk. Pearls are composed of [[Calcium carbonate|calcium carbonate]], mainly aragonite, deposited in concentric layers. Natural pearls form without human intervention and are very rare, so most pearls sold are cultured or farmed from [[Oyster|pearl oysters]] and freshwater mussels. Gem-quality pearls are nacreous and iridescent, and their luster depends on light reflected from thin, numerous layers. Pearls can be dyed, and the most valuable have a metallic, highly reflective luster. Because they are made largely of calcium carbonate, they dissolve in vinegar. Most shelled mollusks can produce some kind of pearl, but most have no value except as curiosities.

Accumulation of partially decayed vegetation or organic matter, formed in waterlogged wetlands. Peat forms when plant material does not fully decay in acidic, oxygen-poor conditions, and [[Sphagnum]] moss is among its most common components. It accumulates at about a millimeter a year, and its deposits preserve pollen and plant remains that record past vegetation and climate. Peatlands cover 3.7 million square kilometers and store up to 550 gigatonnes of carbon, about 42% of soil carbon, although they cover just 3% of the land's surface. Outermost layer of the Earth, made up of [[Soil|soil]]. The name comes from Ancient Greek words for "ground" and "sphere." Malaysian state on Penang Island and the Seberang Perai mainland (pop., 2020: 1.74 million). The state lies on the northwest coast of Peninsular Malaysia, and its capital, George Town, is on Penang Island. Francis Light, an agent of the British [[East India Company|East India Company]], obtained the island from the sultan of Kedah in 1786 in exchange for military aid and established a free port there. === KEY: Pencil_b1e217
HEADWORD: Pencil = 연필

ENTRY:
Writing and drawing implement with a solid pigment core in a protective casing. Pencils make marks by abrasion, leaving a trail of core material on paper. Most cores are graphite mixed with [[Clay|clay]], which makes erasable gray or black marks. [[Charcoal|Charcoal]] cores are used mainly for sketching, and grease pencils mark smooth surfaces such as glass. The most common casing is hexagonal wood bonded to the core, which must be peeled to expose the point. Mechanical pencils have separate cores that can be extended and reloaded. Graphite was found in large deposits in Borrowdale, England, in the 16th century and was first wrapped in string or sheepskin. In 1795 French army officer Nicolas-Jacques Conté developed a method of mixing powdered graphite with clay, forming rods and firing them, which enabled mass production. The core is called "lead," though it contains no lead.

ENTRY:
Weight suspended from a pivot so that it swings freely under [[Gravity|gravity]]. When displaced from its equilibrium position, a pendulum is pulled back toward it and oscillates. The period, the time for one complete cycle, depends on the pendulum's length and, slightly, on the amplitude of its swing, but not on the mass of the bob. For small swings the period is nearly constant, a property called isochronism that makes pendulums useful for timekeeping. [[Galileo Galilei|Galileo]] observed isochronism in 1602. [[Christiaan Huygens|Christiaan Huygens]] invented the pendulum clock in 1656, and it was the world's standard timekeeper for several centuries. [[Quartz|Quartz]] clocks superseded it as a time standard in the 1930s. Pendulums are also used in seismometers and accelerometers, and in the [[Foucault pendulum|Foucault pendulum]], which demonstrates Earth's rotation.

ENTRY:
Flightless, semi-aquatic sea bird found almost exclusively in the Southern Hemisphere. Penguins have countershaded dark and white plumage and flippers for swimming. They feed on [[Krill|krill]], fish and [[Squid|squid]], which they swallow whole. Only one species, the Galapagos penguin, lives at or slightly north of the equator. The largest living species is the [[Emperor penguin|emperor penguin]], about 1.1 m tall and 35 kg in weight. The smallest is the little blue penguin, which stands 30–33 cm tall. Some prehistoric species were as tall or heavy as an adult human. The word first appeared in English in the late 16th century as a synonym for the [[Great auk|great auk]], a Northern Hemisphere bird to which penguins are not closely related.
