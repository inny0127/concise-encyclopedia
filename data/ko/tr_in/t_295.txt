Biogeographical region of semi-arid savanna in Africa, forming a transition zone between the [[Sahara]] to the north and the more humid Sudanian savannas to the south. The name derives from Arabic sāḥil, meaning "coast, shore," a figurative reference to the southern edge of the Sahara. The region stretches about 5,900 km from the [[Atlantic Ocean]] to the [[Red Sea]] and covers 3,053,200 square kilometers. Although it lies in the tropics, its hot semi-arid climate is not tropical. Rainfall varies from about 100–200 mm in the north to 700–1,000 mm in the south. Vegetation is mainly grassland and savanna, with [[Acacia]] the dominant tree. The population was approximately 80 million in 2018, and since the 2010s coups, insurgencies and foreign interventions have led to the region being called the Coup Belt.

ENTRY:
Tensile structure of fabric or other membrane material that uses wind power to propel sailing craft. Sails are made from canvas or polyester cloth, laminated membranes or bonded filaments, usually in a three- or four-sided shape. They produce propulsive force through [[Lift (force)|lift]] and [[Drag (physics)|drag]], depending on their angle of attack to the apparent wind, and on points of sail close to the wind they act as an airfoil. Sails are attached to a mast, boom or other spar, raised by a halyard and angled to the wind by a sheet, and battens can extend the trailing edge. There are two main rig types: the square rig, with yards set perpendicular to the keel, and the fore-and-aft rig, with sails set along the keel. Modern sails often use high-performance materials such as [[Kevlar]] and Dyneema. The origin of the sail is unknown, and many do not consider sails to have been used before the 5th millennium BCE.

Propulsion of a vehicle by wind power. Sailing uses wind acting on [[Sail|sails]], wing sails or kites to propel a craft on water, on ice or on land over a chosen course. From prehistory until the second half of the 19th century, sailing craft were the main means of maritime trade and transport, and the gun-armed sailing warships of the Age of Sail dominated navies. Steam gradually replaced sail over the late 19th century, as steamships ran scheduled services at higher speeds and gained fuel economy. In the 21st century most sailing is recreation or [[Sailing (sport)|sport]], divided into racing and cruising. Sailing relies on the lift and drag generated by the sails, resisted by the hull, keel and rudder, which lets a boat sail both upwind and downwind but not too close into the wind. It was probably invented independently in Island Southeast Asia and the Mediterranean, possibly including the [[Nile]] and the Guayaquil River in Ecuador.

Sea-going vessel that uses sails mounted on masts to harness wind power. Sailing ships carried square-rigged or fore-and-aft sails, or a combination of both in barques, barquentines and brigantines. Early sailing ships were used on rivers and coasts in [[Ancient Egypt]] and the Mediterranean. Competitive sport in which sailing craft race in fleets, in pairs or in teams. Races are held on closed courses or point to point, in sheltered waters, along coasts or on the open ocean, and are sanctioned by federations and yacht clubs. A competition among multiple vessels is a regatta, made up of several races, and the crew that performs best over the series is the overall winner. In fleet racing a race can include anywhere from four boats to hundreds, and a regatta must comprise at least three races to count. Match racing pits two boats against each other, with the aim of finishing ahead of the opponent rather than sailing as fast as possible. Team racing usually involves two teams of three boats each, and the team that scores ten or fewer points wins. The Racing Rules of Sailing govern yacht racing, windsurfing, kitesurfing and other racing around a course. The Barcolana regatta in Italy holds the Guinness World Record for the largest sailing race, with 2,689 boats and over 16,000 sailors at the starting line.
