Mathematical constant that solves the equation *x*² = −1, usually denoted *i*. Its two square roots, *i* and −*i*, are additive inverses of each other. The powers of *i* cycle through 1, *i*, −1 and −*i*. Historically denoted by the square root of −1, the symbol is now rare. Because *i* denotes electric current, [[Electrical engineering|electrical engineering]] normally writes the unit as *j*. The name imaginary is generally credited to [[René Descartes]], and [[Isaac Newton]] used the term as early as 1670. The *i* notation was introduced by [[Leonhard Euler]]. Combined with real numbers, the unit forms the [[Complex number|complex numbers]] of the form *a* + *bi*.

ENTRY:
Capacity to represent sensations or physical objects in the mind without any immediate input of the senses.

It is often described as forming pictures in the mind and is commonly equated with mental imagery, though imaginary experiences can also include other sensory experiences, thoughts, and emotions. Imaginings may re-create past experiences, with or without changes, or be completely invented. Imagination helps apply knowledge to solve problems and is fundamental to integrating experience and to learning. Drawing on actual perceptions, it engages both semantic and episodic [[Memory|memory]] to generate new or refined ideas. Children exercise it through pretend play, and it can be trained through storytelling in forms such as [[Fairy tale|fairy tales]] and [[Science fiction|science fiction]].

The English word derives from the Latin *imaginatio*, the standard translation of the Greek *phantasia*. Ancient Greek philosophers conceived it as working with mental pictures. [[Aristotle]], in *De Anima*, identified it as a faculty that enables an image to occur within a person and distinguished it from perception and rational thinking. [[Plato]] compared imagination about the future to a painter within the soul, an illustrator rather than a creator. In the Middle Ages the concept covered religion, literature, art, and above all poetry, and Chaucer wrote of the "mind's eye" in *The Man of Law's Tale* (c. 1390). Medieval faculty psychology treated it as an internal sense, alongside memory and common sense, that receives images, organizes them, and passes them to the reasoning faculties.

Psychologists distinguish reproductive from constructive imagination. Constructive imagination divides into voluntary forms driven by the lateral prefrontal cortex, such as mental rotation, and involuntary forms such as REM sleep dreaming, daydreaming, hallucinations, and spontaneous insight. Clinicians use visual imagery in treating anxiety disorders, depression, schizophrenia, and Parkinson's disease.

German philosopher, a central thinker of the Enlightenment.

Born Emanuel Kant in [[Königsberg]] in the [[Kingdom of Prussia]], into a Lutheran family that stressed Pietist values, he changed the spelling of his name after learning Hebrew. He enrolled at the University of Königsberg at age 16 and studied under Martin Knutzen, who introduced him to the physics of [[Isaac Newton]]. After his father's death in 1746 he worked as a private tutor, publishing his first philosophical work in 1749. In 1754 he argued that the Moon's gravity would slow the Earth's spin and eventually produce [[Tidal locking|tidal locking]]; the next year he extended this reasoning to the formation of the [[Solar System]]. He later lived so orderly a life that neighbors were said to set their watches by his daily walks.

Kant's philosophy centers on the human subject and aims to secure the possibility of both knowledge and morality against skepticism and determinism. In the [[Critique of Pure Reason]] (1781/1787) he argued for transcendental idealism: space and time are "forms of intuition" that structure all experience, and knowledge extends only to appearances, not to things in themselves. He likened this reversal, in which objects conform to the mind's forms and categories, to the Copernican Revolution. In ethics, set out in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) and the *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788), he held that reason is the source of morality and that the categorical imperative binds all rational agents. The *Critique of Judgment* (1790) treated aesthetics as arising from a faculty of disinterested judgment. He hoped perpetual peace could be secured through an international federation of republican states, and held that true religion is grounded on morality, though the exact nature of his religious views is disputed.

ENTRY:
Network of biological systems that protects an organism from disease.

It detects and responds to a wide variety of pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, and parasites, as well as cancer cells and foreign objects, distinguishing them from the organism's own healthy tissue. All responses depend on telling self from non-self molecules; antigens are non-self substances that bind to specific immune receptors and elicit a response. Nearly all organisms have some form of immune system. Bacteria have a rudimentary one, in the form of enzymes that protect against viral infection. Ancient mechanisms including phagocytosis, antimicrobial peptides called defensins, RNA interference, and the complement system remain in the modern descendants of early plants and animals.

Defense is layered, with increasing specificity. Physical barriers such as skin, the insect exoskeleton, and the waxy leaf cuticle come first, supported by coughing, sneezing, tears, and mucus. Chemical barriers include antimicrobial peptides, enzymes such as lysozyme in saliva and tears, and gastric acid in the stomach. Commensal flora act as biological barriers by competing with pathogenic bacteria for food and space. A pathogen that gets past these meets the innate immune system, found in all animals, which gives an immediate but non-specific response and confers no long-lasting immunity. Vertebrates also have an adaptive immune system, activated by the innate response, which tailors its response to each stimulus and retains it as immunological memory, allowing faster and stronger attacks on repeat encounters. This mechanism is the basis of vaccination.

Dysfunction causes disease. Immunodeficiency, an underactive system, leads to recurring and life-threatening infections and can result from genetic disease, acquired conditions such as HIV/AIDS, or immunosuppressive medication. Autoimmunity, in which a hyperactive system attacks normal tissue, underlies rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, and systemic lupus erythematosus. [[Immunology]] is the study of the system.

Depression in the surface of a solid astronomical body, formed by the hypervelocity impact of a smaller object. Unlike volcanic craters, impact craters typically have raised rims and floors lower than the surrounding terrain. They range from microscopic pits on lunar rocks to vast, multi-ringed impact basins. Impact craters dominate the surfaces of the [[Moon]], [[Mercury (planet)|Mercury]] and most small moons and asteroids. The cratering records of old surfaces show an intense early bombardment about 3.9 billion years ago.

Critics argue that strict impartiality leaves no room for the special concern people show for family and friends, a disagreement known as the partialist–impartialist debate. Impartiality is also a legal standard for courts and public administration.

Ancient [[Mesopotamia|Mesopotamian]] goddess of war, love and sex, also associated with political power and divine law. Originally worshipped in [[Sumer]], she was known to the Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians as Ishtar. Her primary title was "Queen of Heaven," and she was patron goddess of the Eanna temple at [[Uruk]]. She was worshipped in three forms, two of which reflect the phases of the planet [[Venus]]. Her symbols include the lion and the eight-pointed star. In her most famous myth she descends to the underworld, ruled by her sister Ereshkigal, is struck dead there and is rescued after Enki sends two beings. Her husband Dumuzid is taken in her place, and the cycle of the seasons results from his and his sister's alternating time in the underworld.

ENTRY:
Production of offspring from closely related organisms. Inbreeding increases homozygosity, which can expose harmful recessive traits and cause inbreeding depression, a temporary decline in a population's fitness. In humans the term more often refers to disorders that may result from [[Incest|incest]] and [[Consanguinity|consanguinity]]. Breeders use inbreeding to establish new traits and to reveal harmful recessive alleles, which can then be removed through [[Selective breeding|selective breeding]]. In plant breeding, inbred lines are used to create [[Hybrid (biology)|hybrid]] lines that exploit heterosis. Avoiding inbreeding is the main selective reason for outcrossing.

ENTRY:
Largest empire of pre-Columbian America, centered on the Andes in western South America (1438–1533).

Officially the Realm of the Four Parts (Quechua: *Tawantinsuyu*), it had its administrative, political, and military center in [[Cusco]]. The Inca civilization rose from the Peruvian highlands in the early 13th century. From 1438 to 1533 the Incas incorporated a large part of western South America through conquest and peaceful assimilation. At its largest the empire joined modern [[Peru]] with western Ecuador, western and south-central Bolivia, northwest Argentina, the southwesternmost tip of Colombia, and much of Chile. Its official language was Quechua. Inca nobles, probably numbering only 15,000 to 40,000, ruled a population of around 10 million. The empire was preceded by the Tiwanaku, around [[Lake Titicaca]], and the Wari, and drew many of its characteristics from these earlier Andean cultures.

The Incas built "one of the greatest imperial states in human history," in the anthropologist Gordon McEwan's words, without the wheel, draft animals, iron or steel, or a system of writing. Notable features were monumental stonework, an extensive road network (Qhapaq Ñan), fine textiles, agricultural innovation in a difficult environment, and knotted strings (*quipu*) for record keeping. The empire functioned largely without money and markets: exchange rested on reciprocity, and "taxes" took the form of a labor obligation to the state, which in return granted access to land and goods and provided feasts. Scholars have debated how to characterize this economy. Local forms of worship persisted, but the leadership promoted the sun god Inti, and the ruler, the Sapa Inca, was regarded as the "son of the Sun."

The Portuguese explorer Aleixo Garcia was the first European to reach the empire, in 1524. The Spanish began its conquest in 1532, and by 1572 the last Inca state was fully conquered.

[[Electric light]] that produces illumination by heating a filament until it glows. The [[Tungsten|tungsten]] filament is enclosed in a glass bulb that is evacuated or filled with inert gas to prevent oxidation. Bulbs work on either [[Alternating current|alternating]] or [[Direct current|direct current]], which made them widely used in homes, car headlamps and flashlights. Government efficiency standards have accelerated the shift from incandescent bulbs to LEDs.

ENTRY:
Aromatic material that releases fragrant smoke when burnt, or the aroma itself. Incense is used for aesthetic and religious reasons, [[Meditation|meditation]], aromatherapy, and as a deodorant or insect repellent. Direct-burning incense is lit by a flame and smolders, usually as a paste around a [[Bamboo|bamboo]] stick or extruded into a cone; indirect-burning incense needs a separate heat source. The word derives from Latin *incendere*, "to burn." One of the earliest known incense burners, from the A-Group culture (c. 3300–3000 BC), was found at Qustul in [[Nubia]]. In [[Ancient Egypt]] incense masked odors, repelled spirits and honored the gods. By about 2000 BC incense was used in ceremonies in China. Korean Buddhist monks brought it to Japan in the 6th century, and by the [[Heian period]] fine incense appreciation had become part of court culture.

=== KEY: Incest_56e223
HEADWORD: Incest = 근친상간
LEVEL: 4

  Inbreeding = 근친 교배

ENTRY:
Sexual activity between close relatives, such as siblings or a parent and child. The incest taboo is among the most widespread of cultural taboos, and most modern societies have laws against it. A common justification is avoiding [[Inbreeding|inbreeding]], since children of closely related parents face greater risk of congenital disorders. Anthropologists note, however, that prohibitions vary widely between cultures and do not necessarily maximize the avoidance of inbreeding. In [[Ancient Egypt]], royal families married within the family, such as brother and sister, to perpetuate the royal lineage. In [[Samoa]], a man could marry an older sister but not a younger one. Children of incestuous relationships have been regarded as illegitimate in some societies.
