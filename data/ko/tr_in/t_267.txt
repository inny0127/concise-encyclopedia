[[Chemical element]] with atomic number 94 and symbol Pu, a radioactive [[Actinide|actinide]] metal. It is silvery-gray, tarnishes in air and forms a powder that can ignite spontaneously. Because it is radioactive and can accumulate in bone, it is dangerous to handle. It was first synthesized in late 1940 at the University of California, Berkeley, by bombarding uranium with deuterons, and it was named after [[Pluto]], since uranium and neptunium had been named after Uranus and Neptune. Plutonium-238 generates heat for radioisotope generators that power some spacecraft. Producing plutonium in quantity was a major part of the [[Manhattan Project]] during [[World War II]], and plutonium cores were used in the bombing of [[Nagasaki]].

Inflammatory condition of the [[Lung|lung]] primarily affecting the alveoli, the small air sacs. Symptoms typically include some combination of productive or dry cough, chest pain, fever, and difficulty breathing; in elderly people confusion may be the most prominent sign. Severity varies.

It is usually caused by infection with viruses or bacteria, and less commonly by fungi and parasites. Common causes include *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, [[Influenza|influenza]] viruses, and [[Respiratory syncytial virus]]; in about half of cases no causative agent is isolated. It is classified by where it was acquired: community-acquired, hospital-acquired, or healthcare-associated. Risk factors include smoking, immunodeficiency, alcoholism, [[Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease]], [[Sickle cell disease]], asthma, diabetes, and heart failure. In children, additional risks include not being breastfed, malnutrition, and poverty.

Diagnosis is often based on symptoms and physical examination; chest X-rays, blood tests, and sputum culture may help confirm it. Treatment depends on the cause. Pneumonia believed to be bacterial is treated with [[Antibiotic|antibiotics]], such as [[Amoxicillin|amoxicillin]]; oxygen therapy may be used if oxygen levels are low, and severe cases generally require hospitalization. Prevention includes [[Vaccine|vaccines]] against certain causes, hand washing, and not smoking.

Each year pneumonia affects about 450 million people globally (7% of the population) and causes about 4 million deaths. Survival has greatly improved since antibiotics and vaccines were introduced in the 20th century, but the disease remains a leading cause of death in developing countries and among the very old, the very young, and the chronically ill.

ENTRY:
Form of literary art that uses aesthetic and often rhythmic qualities of language to evoke meanings in addition to, or in place of, literal ones. The word comes from the Greek *poiesis*, "making." Poets use devices such as assonance, alliteration, onomatopoeia, rhythm (via meter), and rhyme schemes to produce musical or other artistic effects, and organize them into structures that may be strict or loose, conventional or invented. Most written poems are set in verse, a series of lines that follow the poetic structure.

Not all traditions depend on rhyme and regular meter: Biblical poetry and alliterative verse use other means, and Somali poetry relies on complex systems of alliteration and meter independent of writing. Much modern poetry forgoes rhyme or set rhythm. Ambiguity, symbolism, and figures of speech such as metaphor and metonymy often leave a poem open to multiple interpretations.

Some scholars believe poetry predates literacy and developed from folk epics and oral genres; others suggest it did not necessarily predate writing. The oldest surviving epic poem, the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]], dates from the 3rd millennium BC in [[Sumer]]. The Egyptian *Story of Sinuhe* dates to c. 1800 BC, and the Pyramid Texts of the 25th century BC are among the earliest written poetry in Africa. Other early poems include the Chinese *Shijing*, the Sanskrit *Rigveda*, the Hebrew Psalms, the [[Iliad]] and the [[Odyssey]], Virgil's [[Aeneid]], and the Indian [[Ramayana]] and [[Mahabharata]]. [[Aristotle]]'s *Poetics* analyzed poetry within the uses of speech in rhetoric, drama, song, and comedy.

Constituted body of people empowered by a state to enforce the law and protect public order and the public itself. Police powers encompass arrest and the use of force legitimized by the [[State (polity)|state's]] monopoly on violence. Forces are usually public-sector services funded through taxes and are often defined as separate from the [[Military|military]], although the [[Gendarmerie|gendarmerie]] are military units charged with civil policing. Core functions include preventing and detecting crime, maintaining order, investigating offenses, controlling traffic, and responding to emergencies.

The word entered English in the early 15th century from Middle French *police* ("public order, administration, government"), from Latin *politia* and Greek *politeia*. [[Ireland]] uses the terms Garda and Gardaí for its national force and its members.

According to [[Michel Foucault]], the modern concept of police as a paid functionary of the state was developed by German and French legal scholars in the 17th and early 18th centuries, notably in Nicolas Delamare's *Traité de la Police* (1705). Under the German *Polizeiwissenschaft*, police duties extended to public health, urban planning, and the surveillance of prices. Preventive policing gained influence in the late 18th century; [[Jeremy Bentham]] promoted the view of [[Cesare Beccaria]] that preventing crimes is better than punishing them. London's Metropolitan Police, established in 1829, became a model for modern organized policing.

In some societies in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, police developed to maintain the class system and protect private property. Their role remains controversial: they may be involved to varying degrees in corruption, brutality, and the enforcement of authoritarian rule, and debates over accountability, use of force, and the balance between security and civil liberties continue.

Infectious [[Virus|viral]] disease, also called poliomyelitis, caused by poliovirus and found naturally only in humans. Most infections cause no symptoms, and about 25 percent cause minor illness such as sore throat and low fever. In about 1 percent of infections, the virus enters the [[Central nervous system]], and roughly one to five in 1,000 cases progress to paralysis. Polio spreads mainly by the fecal–oral route, through contaminated food or water. There is no specific treatment, so prevention depends on vaccination. As of October 2023, only Pakistan and Afghanistan remained endemic for wild poliovirus.

Organization that coordinates candidates to compete in elections and participate in governance. Members commonly hold similar ideas about politics, and parties may promote specific ideological or policy goals. Parties are a major part of the politics of almost every country; most countries have several, while others have one. They matter in autocracies, which often have a single governing party, as well as in [[Democracy|democracies]], and some political scientists consider competition between parties essential to democracy.

A party usually includes a leader, executives who handle administration, and members who may volunteer, donate, and vote for its candidates. Parties may cooperate closely with affiliated organizations such as a [[Trade union|trade union]], a youth organization, or an armed wing. They differ from parliamentary groups, internal factions, and advocacy groups chiefly in focusing on electing candidates, and tend to have larger membership and a deeper connection to the [[Election|electorate]].

Many parties are driven by ideology: liberal, conservative, and socialist parties are common in democratic elections, as are [[Communism|communist]], [[Populism|populist]], nationalist, and Islamist ones. Others have no ideology and engage mainly in patronage or clientelism, or are "big tent" parties seeking voters with varied positions.

Factions are ancient; the late Roman Republic had the Populares and Optimates. Scholars differ on when modern parties emerged. Some point to 17th-century Britain after the [[Glorious Revolution]], when the Whigs backed Protestant constitutional monarchy against absolute rule; others place the rise of mass-based parties in the 1820s–1840s. The [[Conservative Party (UK)|Conservative Party]] of the United Kingdom and the [[Democratic Party (United States)|Democratic Party]] of the United States are both frequently called the world's oldest continuous party. Critics, including James Madison, have warned that factionalism exacerbates social divisions, while defenders credit parties with aggregating interests into coherent platforms.
