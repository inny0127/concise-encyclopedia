Scientific and engineering discipline that applies the principles of physics to design, create, and operate devices that manipulate electrons and other electrically charged particles. A subfield of physics and [[Electrical engineering|electrical engineering]], it uses active devices such as [[Transistor|transistors]], diodes, and [[Integrated circuit|integrated circuits]] to control and amplify electric current and to convert it from one form to another, such as alternating to direct current or analog to digital signals. The semiconductor industry is the main driving force behind its advance and had annual revenues exceeding $481 billion in 2018.

Karl Ferdinand Braun's crystal detector in 1874, the first semiconductor device, and the identification of the electron in 1897 by Joseph John Thomson, followed by the vacuum tube, inaugurated the field. Ambrose Fleming's diode and [[Lee de Forest]]'s triode of the early 1900s made small voltages, such as radio signals, detectable. Vacuum tubes, the first active components, enabled radio, television, radar, and long-distance telephony. [[John Bardeen]] and Walter Houser Brattain invented the first working point-contact transistor at Bell Labs in 1947. Vacuum tubes remained important in microwave and high-power transmission and television receivers until the middle of the 1980s.

In April 1955 the IBM 608, with more than 3,000 germanium transistors, became the first all-transistorized calculator made for the commercial market. The MOSFET, invented at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960, was the first truly compact transistor that could be miniaturized and mass-produced. The integrated circuit of [[Jack Kilby]] and Robert Noyce placed all components on one block of semiconductor material, leading to small-scale integration in the early 1960s. In 2008 billion-transistor processors became commercially available.

Components are active, such as transistors and thyristors, or passive: capacitors, inductors, and resistors. Circuits are analog, using a continuous range of voltage or current, or digital, using discrete levels.

Branch of physics that studies stationary [[Electric charge|electric charges]] and the electric fields they produce, in cases where quantum and magnetic effects can be neglected. Since [[Classical antiquity]] it has been known that rubbed [[Amber|amber]] attracts light particles, the observation behind the word electricity, from the Greek for amber. [[Coulomb's law]] gives the force between two point charges as proportional to the product of their charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Electric charge is quantized in units of the elementary charge, about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs, and the total charge of an isolated system is conserved. Gauss's law relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge, which simplifies calculations for symmetric charge distributions. Familiar effects include [[Static electricity|static electricity]] and the operation of [[Photocopier|photocopiers]].

ENTRY:
Closed plane curve around two focal points, with a constant sum of distances to them. It generalizes a [[Circle|circle]], which is the case in which the two foci coincide. An ellipse's elongation is measured by its eccentricity, which ranges from 0 for a circle to 1 for the limiting case of a [[Parabola|parabola]]. Its area has a simple algebraic formula, but its exact perimeter requires integration. Ellipses are the closed type of [[Conic section|conic section]] and are common in physics and astronomy: the orbit of each planet in the [[Solar System]] is approximately an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. The name, from a Greek word meaning "omission," was given by [[Apollonius of Perga]].

Most ellipticals are composed of older, low-mass [[Star|stars]], with a sparse [[Interstellar medium|interstellar medium]], so star formation is typically minimal.
