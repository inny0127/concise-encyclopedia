Tool with a hollow chamber that exposes materials to a controlled hot environment. Ovens have been used since antiquity and differ by purpose and by how they generate heat. Most often they are used for [[Cooking|cooking]], usually baking and sometimes broiling. Ovens for ceramics and pottery are sometimes called kilns, metallurgical furnaces make metals, and glass furnaces make glass.

Some ovens burn fuel such as wood, coal, or natural gas; many use electricity. Electric ovens and furnaces use resistive heating, while microwave ovens expose materials to microwave radiation, which excites water molecules in food. Some ovens use forced convection, the movement of gases inside the chamber, to speed heating.

The earliest known ovens, in Central Europe, date to 29,000 BC: roasting and boiling pits inside yurts used to cook mammoth. In Ukraine from 20,000 BC, people used pits of hot coals covered in ashes. By 3200 BC cultures of the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] used ovens to cook food and make bricks. The tandır, a cylindrical oven that bakes unleavened flatbread stuck to its walls, was common in Anatolia and remains central to rural food culture there.

In the Middle Ages, Europeans used fireplaces with large cauldrons. Cast-iron stoves came into use in the early 1700s, and the coal oven was developed in the early 19th century. James Sharp patented one of the first gas stoves in 1826, and Gustaf Dalén invented the AGA cooker in 1922. Percy Spencer discovered microwave cooking in 1946, leading to the microwave oven.

Chemical element, symbol O, atomic number 8, a nonmetal of the chalcogen group in the [[Periodic table|periodic table]]. It is highly reactive and a potent oxidizing agent, readily forming oxides with most elements. It is the most abundant element in [[Earth's crust]], making up almost half of it, chiefly as water, carbon dioxide, iron oxides, and silicates, and the third most abundant in the universe after hydrogen and helium.

At standard temperature and pressure, two atoms bind covalently to form dioxygen (O₂), a colorless, odorless gas that is currently about 20.95% of Earth's atmosphere by molar fraction. The rarer allotrope [[Ozone|ozone]] (O₃) absorbs UVB and UVC radiation, forming a protective layer in the lower stratosphere, but at the surface it is a corrosive air pollutant. Oxygen is too reactive to remain free in air without continuous replenishment by [[Photosynthesis|photosynthesis]] in cyanobacteria, algae, and land plants.

Aerobic [[Respiration (physiology)|respiration]] extracts energy by reacting oxygen with organic molecules from food, releasing carbon dioxide. Proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, and fats contain oxygen, as do shells, teeth, and bone, and most of the mass of living organisms is oxygen in water.

The modern concept developed over five centuries, and no one person discovered oxygen. Michael Sendivogius isolated the substance before 1604, calling it *cibus vitae* ("food of life"). John Mayow proposed in 1668 that part of the air is consumed in both respiration and [[Combustion|combustion]]. Carl Wilhelm Scheele in Uppsala (1773 or earlier) and [[Joseph Priestley]] in Wiltshire (1774) discovered it independently; Priestley called it "dephlogisticated air" and did not recognize it as an element. In 1777 [[Antoine Lavoisier]] first recognized it as a chemical element and correctly described its role in combustion.

[[Probability]], assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining test results at least as extreme as those observed. The null hypothesis usually states that a parameter, such as a correlation or a difference between means, is zero. A small p-value means such an extreme result would be unlikely under that hypothesis. A result is statistically significant if it allows rejection of the null hypothesis, and smaller p-values are taken as stronger evidence against it. Ronald A. ENTRY:
Unsolved problem in [[Theoretical computer science|theoretical computer science]]. It asks whether every decision problem whose positive answer can be verified quickly can also be solved quickly, where "quickly" means in polynomial time. The class of problems solvable in polynomial time is P, and the class whose answers can be verified in polynomial time is NP. Most researchers believe P ≠ NP, which would mean some problems are harder to solve than to verify. A proof either way would affect [[Cryptography|cryptography]], [[Artificial intelligence|artificial intelligence]], economics and many other fields. The problem was formally stated by Stephen Cook in 1971 and independently by Leonid Levin in 1973, after [[Kurt Gödel]] asked in a 1956 letter to [[John von Neumann]] whether theorem-proving could be done in quadratic or linear time. It is one of the seven [[Millennium Prize Problems]], each carrying a US$1,000,000 prize.

Spanish painter, sculptor, printmaker, and ceramicist who spent most of his adult life in France. He co-founded the [[Cubism|Cubist]] movement, invented constructed sculpture, co-invented [[Collage|collage]], and produced about 50,000 artworks in a career of more than 76 years. Among his best-known works are the proto-Cubist [[Les Demoiselles d'Avignon|*Les Demoiselles d'Avignon*]] (1907) and the anti-war painting [[Guernica (Picasso)|*Guernica*]] (1937), a portrayal of the bombing of Guernica during the [[Spanish Civil War]].

Born in [[Málaga]], the first child of the painter José Ruiz y Blasco, he began formal training under his father at age seven. The family moved to A Coruña in 1891. After his seven-year-old sister Conchita died of diphtheria in 1895, they moved to [[Barcelona]], where Picasso, at 13, passed the entrance exam for the advanced class at the School of Fine Arts in a week. He settled in Paris in 1904.

His style changed repeatedly in the first decade of the 20th century. After 1906, the Fauvist work of the older artist [[Henri Matisse]] pushed him toward more radical styles and began a rivalry in which critics often paired the two as leaders of modern art. The commonly accepted periods of his work are the Blue Period (1901–1904), the Rose Period (1904–1906), the African-influenced Period (1907–1909), Analytic Cubism (1909–1912), and Synthetic Cubism (1912–1919). His work of the late 1910s and early 1920s is often neoclassical, and that of the mid-1920s shows traits of Surrealism. Later work combines earlier styles. He achieved great renown and fortune. Accounts of his relationships with partners such as Fernande Olivier, Olga Khokhlova, Marie-Thérèse Walter, Dora Maar, and Françoise Gilot describe them as domineering and emotionally abusive, prompting posthumous criticism of misogyny.
