State in which an individual or community lacks the financial resources and essentials for a basic standard of living. Statisticians and economists use two main measures. Absolute poverty compares income against the amount needed to meet basic needs such as food, clothing, and shelter; relative poverty applies when a person cannot meet a minimum level of living standards compared with others in the same time and place, and its definition varies between societies. The "dollar a day" line, introduced in 1990, is set by [[Purchasing power parity]] rather than market exchange rates.

Definitions differ by institution. The [[United Nations|UN]] describes poverty as "a denial of choices and opportunities, a violation of human dignity." The [[European Union]] measures it relative to each member country's income distribution. As of 2019, 85% of people lived on less than $30 per day in purchasing power parity dollars, two-thirds on less than $10, and 10% on less than $1.90.

Gender, disability, race, and ethnicity can exacerbate poverty, with women, children, and minorities frequently bearing unequal burdens. Poor people are more vulnerable to climate change and natural disasters, and economic pressure on poor communities frequently contributes to [[Deforestation|deforestation]], biodiversity loss, and [[Ethnic conflict|ethnic conflict]]. Governments and [[Non-governmental organization|non-governmental organizations]] have tried policies such as rural electrification. The UN's 2015 framework for poverty alleviation is Sustainable Development Goal 1, "No Poverty."

Extreme poverty is estimated to have afflicted over 90% of humanity in 1820 and under 10% today, a decline some analysts credit to economic growth in regions such as East Asia. Analysts disagree on causes, and some argue that relative definitions tied to national medians conflate inequality with destitution.

Mechanical power is the product of force and [[Velocity|velocity]], or of [[Torque|torque]] and angular velocity for a rotating shaft, and electrical power is the product of current and [[Voltage|voltage]]. Ability to direct the actions and conduct of others, a central concept of political science.

Political power is usually exercised through [[Government|government]] offices with authority, over political societies often termed [[State (polity)|states]]. It does not refer only to the threat or use of force (coercion) by one actor against another but may also be exerted through diffuse means such as [[Institution|institutions]]. Authority is the term often used for power perceived as legitimate or socially approved by the social structure; political authority grants members of a government the right to rule over citizens; citizens have an obligation to obey.

Thinkers have defined power differently. Robert Dahl views it as a form of "relations among people" in which one party can command another. [[Hannah Arendt]] defined it as an instrument of political rule and dissociated it from the ability to use violence. [[Max Weber]] defined it as "any chance (regardless of the basis of this chance) to carry through one's own will", even against outright opposition.

Scholars distinguish soft power, the ability to influence or persuade by persuasive means, from hard power, the use of [[Military|military]] and economic means to influence the behavior or interests of other political bodies.

Approach/inhibition theory, developed by D. Keltner and colleagues, holds that power promotes approach tendencies such as action, self-promotion, and seeking rewards, while reduced power promotes inhibition, such as self-protection and avoiding threats. In 1959 the social psychologists John R. P. French and Bertram Raven proposed five bases of power.

ENTRY:
Period of human history between the first known use of stone tools by hominins and the beginning of recorded history with the invention of writing.

The first stone tools date to c. 3.3 million years ago; the earliest known writing systems appeared c. 5,200 years ago. Because writing was adopted gradually across the globe, prehistory ended at different times in different places, and the term is less often used for societies where it ended recently. The period when a culture is written about by others but has no writing of its own is often called its protohistory. The beginning varies regionally too: African prehistory begins c. 2.5 million years ago, Asian 1.8 million, and European c. 1.3 million. Prehistory is generally held to have ended around 3100 BC in Egypt, in the 1870s in New Guinea, and in 1788 in Australia.

By definition there are no written records, so prehistory is known only from material archaeological and anthropological evidence: remains and artifacts, and the human remains themselves. It deals with archaeological cultures rather than named nations or individuals, and labels such as "Neanderthal" or "Iron Age" are modern terms whose definitions are sometimes debated. The word first appeared in English in 1836, and although some historians have been uncomfortable with it, archaeologists regard it as a meaningful and necessary distinction.

The principal researchers are anthropologists, including [[Archaeology|archaeologists]] and physical anthropologists, who use excavation, geological and geographic surveys, and other scientific analysis. Population geneticists and historical linguists also contribute, as do biology, geology, paleontology, and many other fields. Dating is the key step; the most common technique is [[Radiocarbon dating|radiocarbon dating]], and newer methods include forensic chemical analysis and genetic analysis of bones.

For much of Eurasia and North Africa, prehistory is divided by the three-age system, named for predominant tool-making technologies: the [[Stone Age]], [[Bronze Age]], and [[Iron Age]], with a transitional [[Chalcolithic]], or Copper Age in some areas. The Stone Age lasted roughly 3.4 million years, ended between 4000 BC and 2000 BC with the advent of metalworking, and accounts for 99% of human history. The earliest civilizations to develop scripts and keep records, including Sumer, the [[Indus Valley Civilisation]], and Egypt, did so in the early Bronze Age; most other societies reached the end of prehistory in the Iron Age. The three-age system is not generally used where hard metals arrived abruptly through contact with Eurasian cultures, such as Oceania, Australasia, much of sub-Saharan Africa, and parts of the Americas. For the Americas, see [[Pre-Columbian era]].
