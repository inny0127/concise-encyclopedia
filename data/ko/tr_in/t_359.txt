Art forms perceived chiefly through sight, including [[Painting|painting]], drawing, printmaking, sculpture, ceramics, photography, video, filmmaking, design, crafts, and [[Architecture|architecture]]. The applied arts, such as industrial, graphic, fashion, and interior design and decorative art, are also included, and disciplines such as performing arts, conceptual art, and textile arts involve aspects of the visual arts.

Current usage covers fine art as well as applied arts and crafts, but this was not always so. Before the [[Arts and Crafts movement]] at the turn of the 20th century, the term "artist" had for centuries often been restricted to practitioners of the fine arts such as painting and sculpture, and art schools maintained that a craftsperson could not be considered an artist. Both Western and East Asian art have tended to privilege painting, and to a lesser degree sculpture, as the arts that rely most on imagination and are furthest removed from manual labor. In Chinese painting, the valued "scholar-painting" was in theory practiced by gentleman amateurs.

Training began as apprentice and workshop systems, typically lasting 2 to 6 years; in many European and North American nations, academies later grew into universities and institutes. Museums and galleries serve as informal education.

Drawing makes a two-dimensional image by marking a surface with tools such as graphite pencils, pen and ink, charcoal, pastels, and markers, or with digital tools. Upper Paleolithic art includes figurative art beginning at least 40,000 years ago, with cave sites at Lascaux, Altamira, Maros, and Gabarnmung. Ancient Egyptians drew in ink on potsherds and limestone ostraca, and Greek vase painting developed black-figure pottery in the 6th century BC. With paper brought by Islamic traders from China, drawing on paper increased in the Middle East and Europe in the 14th century, and artists such as Raphael, Michelangelo, and [[Leonardo da Vinci]] treated it as an art in its own right rather than a preparatory stage.

Painting applies pigments suspended in a medium with a binder to a surface such as walls, canvas, or paper. The earliest evidence is natural pigments of ochre, chalk, and charcoal applied to stone; tools for processing pigments for body painting date to about 200,000 years ago in Zambia, and cave paintings to at least 50,000 years ago. Later traditions developed fresco, tempera (egg yolk, animal glue, or milk), and encaustic (hot wax), while ink wash painting became a major East Asian tradition. Oil paint on wood panels, later on stretched canvas, permitted shading and glazing and the use of chiaroscuro. In the 19th century the box easel and collapsible paint tube allowed *plein air* painting outdoors.

Movements since the Renaissance include Baroque, Rococo, Neoclassicism, Romanticism, Impressionism, Cubism, Abstract Expressionism, and Pop Art. Contemporary practice often incorporates performance, installation, and new media.

Partial or total inability of [[Visual perception|visual perception]]. Without treatment such as corrective eyewear, it may cause difficulty with daily tasks, including reading and walking. The most common causes worldwide are uncorrected [[Refractive error|refractive errors]] (43%), cataracts (33%) and [[Glaucoma|glaucoma]] (2%). Cataracts are the most common cause of blindness. Visual impairment can also result from brain problems due to [[Stroke|stroke]], premature birth or trauma. The [[World Health Organization]] estimates that 80% of visual impairment is preventable or curable. As of 2015, 940 million people had some degree of vision loss, including 246 million with low vision and 39 million who were blind. Most people with poor vision live in the developing world and are over the age of 50.

ENTRY:
Ability to detect light and use it to form an image of the surrounding environment. Photodetection without image formation is classified as light sensing. Most vertebrates have photopic (daytime) vision, scotopic (night) vision, or both. Visual perception detects light in the [[Visible spectrum|visible spectrum]] reflected by objects or emitted by light sources. It is an active process in which the brain interprets and organizes sensory information, influenced by prior knowledge, expectations and context. Visual perception is also adaptive and shaped by lifelong experience and changing cognitive capacities. The physiological components involved are called the [[Visual system|visual system]], which is studied in [[Neuroscience|neuroscience]], psychology and molecular biology under the name vision science.

Physiological basis of [[Perception|visual perception]], associated with the [[Eye|eye]]. It detects and interprets light to construct an image and a mental model of the surrounding environment. ENTRY:
Organic molecules, or sets of closely related molecules called vitamers, essential in small quantities to an organism for proper [[Metabolism|metabolic]] function, such as [[Vitamin C]]. They cannot be synthesized in sufficient quantities for survival and must be obtained through consumption. Most vitamins are groups of vitamers; vitamin E has eight, four tocopherols and four tocotrienols.

Major health organizations list thirteen vitamins: the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E, and K, and the water-soluble vitamin C and eight B vitamins. Their functions are diverse. Vitamin A regulates cell and tissue growth. [[Vitamin D]] acts like a hormone, regulating mineral metabolism for bones. The B complex vitamins serve as [[Enzyme|enzyme]] cofactors or their precursors. Vitamins C and E are antioxidants, and vitamin K aids blood clotting. Both deficiency and excess can cause illness, and there is little evidence that supplements protect healthy people against heart disease or cancer.

All vitamins were discovered between the 1900s and 1930s, but the effects of deficiency were recognized earlier. Long ocean voyages made [[Scurvy|scurvy]] common among ships' crews. In 1747 the Scottish surgeon James Lind ran a controlled experiment on 12 sailors showing that citrus foods helped prevent it, and his *Treatise on the Scurvy* (1753) recommended lemons and limes, which the British [[Royal Navy]] adopted. In 1884 Takaki Kanehiro of the Imperial Japanese Navy observed that beriberi struck low-ranking crew who ate mostly rice, but not officers with a Western-style diet.

Casimir Funk and Max Nierenstein coined "vitamine" in 1912 from "vital amine"; Jack Cecil Drummond proposed dropping the final "e" in 1920. Commercial vitamin tablets appeared in 1935. Some governments mandate adding vitamins to staple foods such as flour or milk.

Roman architect and military engineer, author of the treatise *De architectura*. Its discussion of perfect proportion inspired Leonardo da Vinci's drawing of the [[Vitruvian Man]].

ENTRY:
Vent or fissure in the crust of a planetary-mass object through which hot lava, volcanic ash, and gases escape from a [[Magma chamber|magma chamber]] below. On Earth, volcanoes lie mostly where tectonic plates diverge or converge, and because most plate boundaries are underwater, most volcanoes are too. The word derives from Vulcano, an island of Italy, and ultimately from Vulcan, the Roman god of fire.

According to [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]], most volcanic activity takes place along plate boundaries. At divergent boundaries such as the [[Mid-Atlantic Ridge]], rising mantle rock partly melts and creates new oceanic crust; these volcanoes are usually nonexplosive. Where a ridge rises above sea level, islands such as [[Iceland]] form. At convergent boundaries, one plate subducts beneath another, and released water lowers the melting temperature of the overlying mantle, producing extremely viscous magma. These volcanoes cause violent eruptions and form volcanic arcs, as around the Pacific [[Ring of Fire]], which holds about 75% of the world's active volcanoes. Volcanoes also form above hotspots, thought to be fed by mantle plumes rising from 3,000 km deep, as in the Hawaiian island chain.

By frequency of eruption, volcanoes are called active, dormant, or extinct. Dormant volcanoes have not erupted since the start of the [[Holocene]], about 12,000 years ago, but may erupt again. About 1,350 potentially active volcanoes exist on land. Principal types include shield volcanoes, broad and gently sloping, such as [[Mauna Loa]]; stratovolcanoes, steep cones of alternating lava and pyroclastic layers, such as [[Mount Fuji]]; cinder cones; and lava domes.

Hazards include lava flows, pyroclastic flows, ash falls, and lahars, or volcanic mudflows. Large eruptions can cool the troposphere, and have been followed by volcanic winters causing catastrophic famines. Volcanic soils support dense populations, and volcanoes supply geothermal energy, pumice, and sulfur. Venus has very numerous volcanoes.
