Political history of mainland China under Communist Party rule since 1949. On October 1, 1949, [[Mao Zedong]] proclaimed the People's Republic from Tiananmen, after the Communist Party's victory in the [[Chinese Civil War]]. Land reform included state-tolerated executions of between 1 and 2 million landlords. The [[Great Leap Forward]] industrialization drive caused an estimated 15 to 55 million deaths between 1959 and 1961, mostly from starvation. The [[Cultural Revolution]], launched in 1966, brought a decade of political upheaval. After [[Deng Xiaoping]] took power in 1978, reform and opening up transformed the economy, and China's economy grew sevenfold under Jiang Zemin. [[Hong Kong]] and Macau returned in 1997 and 1999 as special administrative regions under the principle of one country, two systems. China joined the [[World Trade Organization]] in 2001. [[Xi Jinping]], paramount leader since 2012, has consolidated power through an anti-corruption campaign.

Political and cultural history of the Philippine archipelago from early settlement to independence in 1946. Human activity on the islands dates to at least 709,000 years ago. Seafaring Austronesians, who form most of the current population, migrated south from Taiwan by around 3000 BCE. From the 1st millennium, small maritime states traded with [[China]], India, [[Japan]], Thailand and Vietnam, and some were influenced by larger empires such as the [[Ming dynasty]], [[Majapahit]] and Brunei. The first recorded European visit was [[Ferdinand Magellan]]'s expedition, which landed in 1521; Magellan was killed in a battle against Lapulapu of Mactan. Miguel López de Legazpi arrived from [[Mexico]] in 1565 and founded the first permanent settlement at Cebu. Spanish rule introduced Christianity and a code of law. After Spain's defeat in the [[Spanish–American War]] in 1898, the United States took control and suppressed a revolution led by Emilio Aguinaldo. Japan occupied the islands in 1942, and the Treaty of Manila in 1946 established the independent Philippine Republic.

Development of theater as an art form over roughly 2,500 years. Theater developed into an autonomous activity distinct from ritual. Athenian tragedy, a type of dance-drama, emerged in the 6th century BC and was staged in competitions held in honor of [[Dionysus]]. Playwrights [[Aeschylus]], [[Sophocles]] and [[Euripides]] shaped the form, and complete texts by all three survive. Greek theater comprised tragedy, [[Comedy]] and the satyr play. Other traditions arose independently: Sanskrit drama was codified in the Natyasastra around 200 BCE–200 CE, and Japanese Noh theater originated in the 12th century. Medieval European theater revived through church liturgical drama by 975 CE, and the first permanent public theater in Elizabethan England opened in 1576.

Evolution of travel and goods movement, from trails and animal power to powered vehicles. Early humans created tracks by carrying goods, often following trails. Animal-drawn [[Wheel|wheeled]] vehicles were probably developed in the ancient Near East in the 4th or 5th millennium BC. The [[Roman Empire]] built roads to maintain its territory. Early [[Rail transport|rail]] systems used wooden rails to carry coal from mines to rivers, and [[Cast iron|cast iron]] plates were used from the 1760s before rolled [[Wrought iron|wrought iron]] rails made them obsolete. The first passenger-carrying public railway opened at Oystermouth in 1807, using horse-drawn carriages. In 1802 Richard Trevithick built the first [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]] to run on smooth rails. In 1903 the [[Wright brothers]] achieved powered flight, and commercial air services followed by the 1920s.

Development of writing systems and their effects on the societies that used them. Indo-European people of [[Anatolia]] who formed one of the first major civilizations of the [[Bronze Age]]. The Hittites arrived in central Anatolia before 2000 BC and formed polities including Kussara and Kanesh before establishing an empire with its capital at Hattusa. The Hittite Empire peaked in the mid-14th century BC, encompassing most of Anatolia and parts of northern Syria and Upper Mesopotamia. From the 15th to 13th centuries BC they contended with the [[New Kingdom of Egypt]], the Middle Assyrian Empire and the empire of Mitanni. The Hittite language, with the closely related Luwian, is among the oldest historically attested [[Indo-European languages]].

Decentralized polity of Central and Western Europe, headed by the Holy Roman Emperor (800/962–1806).

The empire developed in the Early Middle Ages, beginning in either 800 or 962, and lasted a millennium. Space used as a permanent or semi-permanent residence for one or more human occupants, and sometimes companion animals.

A home provides sheltered rooms for sleeping, preparing food, eating, and hygiene, and for work and leisure such as studying and play. Its form may be static, such as a [[House|house]] or apartment; mobile, such as a houseboat, trailer, or yurt; or digital, such as a virtual space. The concept can be considered at scales from the individual dwelling to a town, country, or planet, and has been theorized across disciplines, from psychology to gender and politics. It encompasses exile, yearning, belonging, homesickness, and homelessness.

The earliest human homes were probably natural features such as [[Cave|caves]]. Early human species inhabited caves from at least one million years ago, including *Homo erectus* at Zhoukoudian in China, *Homo heidelbergensis* and [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] at Atapuerca in Europe, and *Homo floresiensis* in Indonesia. Early modern humans in southern Africa regularly used sea caves from about 180,000 years ago, the oldest known site being PP13B at Pinnacle Point. Huts and longhouses have been used for living since the late Neolithic.

By the Bronze Age (c. 3500–1200 BC), communities in [[Mesopotamia]] built permanent mudbrick dwellings; excavations at Uruk and Ubaid reveal single-room and multi-room houses around small courtyards, clustered along straight streets and sharing wells and ovens. In [[Ancient Egypt]], from the Old Kingdom (c. 2686–2181 BC), towns such as Amarna and Deir el-Medina had flat-roofed mudbrick houses in dense rows off narrow lanes. The [[Indus Valley Civilisation]] (c. 2600–1900 BC) used standardized fired bricks, and two-story houses at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa had private wells and indoor bathrooms with drainage. The Minoan palace at [[Knossos]] had residential quarters with light wells and lustral basins. In [[Ancient Rome]] by the 1st century BC, the affluent lived in domus built around an atrium and peristyle garden, while most people lived in multistory apartment blocks called insulae, often cramped and prone to fire.

After the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century, European domestic architecture reverted to simple timber-framed or wattle-and-daub huts, while the elite kept stone manor houses with great halls. In the Islamic world from the 8th century the inward-facing courtyard house predominated, and the Chinese siheyuan compound, standardized during the Yuan and Ming dynasties, housed several generations around a courtyard. In Renaissance Florence the Palazzo Medici Riccardi (begun 1444) introduced rusticated façades and symmetrical plans, and masonry chimneys gradually replaced central hearths.

Kirsten Gram-Hanssen argues that the strong tie between home and the physical building is rooted in the 17th-century Enlightenment; before then, a home was more public than private. Edward Coke's declaration that a man's house is his castle and fortress popularized the notion of home as house. The separation of home and work took shape in the 20th century, with home as sanctuary. Stable housing is linked to better health and children's cognitive development, while inadequate housing correlates with higher risks of illness.
