ENTRY:
Solar calendar of 365 days, with a leap day every fourth year. It was proposed by [[Julius Caesar]] in 46 BC and took effect in 45 BC, replacing the earlier Roman calendar, a lunisolar system whose irregular intercalations had drifted out of alignment with the seasons. The Julian calendar became the predominant calendar in the [[Roman Empire]] and most of the Western world for more than 1,600 years. In 1582 Pope Gregory XIII promulgated the [[Gregorian calendar]], which eliminated occasional leap days and sharply reduced the drift. The Julian calendar's average year of 365.25 days is longer than the solar year, so it gains one day every 128 years. The [[Anno Domini]] system of numbering years was not devised until 525. Most Orthodox countries adopted the Gregorian calendar for civil use in the early 20th century but retain the Julian calendar for religious purposes.

Philosophy of [[Law|law]], also called legal theory or legal science. It applies philosophical methods to questions such as what the law is and how it ought to be. Thinkers including [[Confucius]] and [[Aristotle]] wrote on law, and contemporary academic jurisprudence traces its origin to the early 17th century and the [[Age of Enlightenment]]. Sworn body of people, called jurors, convened to hear evidence in a [[Trial|trial]] and render an impartial verdict. The jury system developed in England during the [[Middle Ages]] and is a hallmark of [[Common law|common law]] systems. Juries are commonly used in countries with legal systems derived from the British Empire, such as the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, Australia and Ireland. ENTRY:
Concept of treating individuals fairly, central to morality, law, and political philosophy. A frequently cited core definition comes from the Institutes of Justinian, a 6th-century codification of Roman law: "the constant and perpetual will to render to each his due."

Early Western theories were developed by Greek philosophers. For [[Plato]], in *The Republic*, justice is balance and harmony: everyone having and doing what they are responsible for or what belongs to them. He compared the three parts of a person, reason, spirit, and desire, with the three parts of a city. [[Aristotle]], in the [[Nicomachean Ethics]], treated justice both as nearly synonymous with virtue and as the particular virtue of giving people what they deserve; his particular justice divides into distributive justice, which allocates goods in proportion to merit, and corrective justice, which restores balance after exchanges or harms. The classical Confucian theory treated justice as a personal and public virtue that did not include freedom or equality and was not part of the law.

Modern Western notions have roots in Christian theology and divine command theory, under which God's commands determine morality and therefore justice. In the 17th century, [[John Locke]] held that justice derives from natural law, while [[Jean-Jacques Rousseau]] advocated social contract theory, in which justice arises from mutual agreement to be governed. Modern frameworks include distributive justice; [[Utilitarianism|utilitarian]] theories, which look to the future consequences of [[Punishment|punishment]]; retributive justice, which matches punishment to past wrongdoing; and restorative justice, which focuses on the needs of victims, offenders, and society. Procedural justice concerns the impartiality of the processes by which outcomes are reached.
