Property of certain materials with exactly zero electrical resistance and expelled magnetic fields. Superconductivity was discovered in solid [[Mercury (element)|mercury]] on April 8, 1911, by Dutch physicist Heike Kamerlingh Onnes. Unlike ordinary metals, superconductors drop abruptly to zero resistance below a critical temperature, and a current in a superconducting loop can persist indefinitely. In 1933, Meissner and Ochsenfeld showed that superconductors expel internal magnetic fields, a phenomenon known as the Meissner effect. A theory based on electron pairing was developed in 1957 by [[John Bardeen]], Leon Cooper and John Robert Schrieffer. In 1986, cuprate ceramics were found to superconduct above 35 K; replacing lanthanum with yttrium raised the critical temperature to 92 K, above the boiling point of [[Liquid nitrogen|liquid nitrogen]] (77 K, −196 °C), making liquid nitrogen usable as a refrigerant. Applications include superconducting magnets in [[Magnetic resonance imaging|MRI]] machines and the [[Large Hadron Collider]].

ENTRY:
Powerful and luminous explosion of a star, occurring in the last evolutionary stages of a massive star or when a [[White dwarf|white dwarf]] undergoes runaway nuclear fusion.

The original object, the progenitor, can collapse to form a [[Neutron star|neutron star]] or [[Black hole|black hole]], or disperse completely into a diffuse nebula with no stellar remnant. Peak optical luminosity can be comparable to that of an entire galaxy before fading over several weeks or months.

Most supernovae are triggered by one of two mechanisms. In the first, a white dwarf re-ignites runaway fusion, thought to follow accretion from a binary companion or a stellar merger. In the second, a massive star's core collapses once radiation pressure no longer balances gravity, classically when the star begins producing iron. Supernovae can expel several solar masses of material at speeds up to several percent of the speed of light, driving a shock wave into the interstellar medium and sweeping up a shell observed as a supernova remnant. They are a major source of heavier elements, from oxygen to rubidium, and of cosmic rays, and may produce gravitational waves.

Supernovae occur in the [[Milky Way]] approximately 1.6 to 4.6 times per century on average, and several thousand are typically seen in distant galaxies every year. The most recent in the Milky Way to be observed were Tycho's Supernova in 1572 and [[Kepler's Supernova]] in 1604, both visible to the naked eye; they were used to argue against the Aristotelian idea of an unchanging universe beyond the Moon and planets. SN 1987A, in the [[Large Magellanic Cloud]], provided the only measurements of astronomical neutrinos other than the Sun's.

The term was coined by Guillaume Apollinaire in 1917, but the movement was not officially established until after October 1924, when Breton's Surrealist Manifesto claimed the name. The most important center was [[Paris]], and from the 1920s the movement spread around the globe, producing painting, writing, photography, theater, film and music.

Field of applied [[Statistics|statistics]] that studies survey methods, concentrating on human-research surveys. It covers the [[Sampling (statistics)|sampling]] of individual units from a population and techniques of data collection, such as questionnaire construction and methods for improving the number and accuracy of responses. Survey methodologists must decide how to identify sample members, contact hard-to-reach respondents, test questions, choose the mode of data collection and adjust estimates for identified errors. ENTRY:
Technique, profession, art and science of determining the terrestrial positions of points from the [[Distance|distances]] and [[Angle|angles]] between them. Surveyors draw on geodesy, geometry, trigonometry, regression analysis and physics, using equipment such as total stations, [[Theodolite|theodolites]], GNSS receivers, drones and [[Lidar|lidar]] sensors. Surveying has been part of human development since recorded history began, and it is used in most forms of construction, transportation, mapping and the definition of legal land boundaries. In [[Ancient Egypt|ancient Egypt]], a rope stretcher re-established boundaries after the annual floods of the Nile. The almost perfect squareness of the [[Great Pyramid of Giza]], built c. 2700 BC, reflects Egyptian command of surveying. In 1086, [[William the Conqueror]] commissioned the [[Domesday Book]], which recorded landowners and land quality but did not include maps showing exact locations.
