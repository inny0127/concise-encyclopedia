ENTRY:
Law or formal written enactment of a [[Legislature|legislature]]. Statutes typically declare, command or prohibit something. They are distinguished from [[Common law|common law]], which is unwritten, and from secondary legislation, or regulations, issued by an executive body under authority granted by a statute. Depending on the legal system, a statute may be called an "act." The word appears in English as early as the 14th century, derived from Old French *statut*. Many countries organize statutes into topical codes, which are amended as new statutes add, amend or repeal sections. In many nations, statutory law is subordinate to [[Constitution|constitutional law]]. The term also covers treaties establishing institutions, such as the Statute of the [[International Court of Justice]]. In Spain, an autonomy statute resembles a constitution and has the rank of organic law.

ENTRY:
Process by which courts interpret and apply legislation. Some interpretation is often necessary, because a statute's ambiguous words must be resolved by the judge. Judges use traditional canons of construction, legislative history and purpose. In [[Common law|common law]] jurisdictions, the same rules may apply to delegated legislation such as administrative agency regulations. English courts developed three main rules, the mischief rule, the literal rule and the golden rule, all aimed at the "intention of Parliament." Courts also presume that statutes do not change the common law, remove individual liberty or property, or apply retrospectively. Textualism prioritizes the ordinary public meaning of words, while purposivism infers meaning from a statute's objectives. The two approaches remain contested, particularly over the use of legislative history.

ENTRY:
Heat engine that performs mechanical work using steam as its working fluid. Steam pressure pushes a piston back and forth inside a cylinder, and a connecting rod and crank convert this force into rotation. They are external combustion engines. The term normally means reciprocating engines, though some authorities also apply it to the [[Steam turbine|steam turbine]] and to devices such as the aeolipile of [[Hero of Alexandria]], known in the first century AD. It can also mean a whole plant, such as a [[Steam locomotive|steam locomotive]].

In 1606 Jerónimo de Ayanz y Beaumont patented the first steam-powered water pump for draining mines. Thomas Savery built the first commercially used steam device, a pump, in 1698. The first commercially successful engine able to transmit continuous power to a machine was Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine, developed around 1712. In 1764 [[James Watt]] greatly improved fuel efficiency by removing spent steam to a separate vessel for condensation.

By the 19th century stationary steam engines powered the factories of the [[Industrial Revolution]], paddle steamers replaced sailing ships, and steam locomotives ran on the railways. Reciprocating piston engines were the dominant source of power until the early 20th century. A Rankine cycle efficiency of 91% and a combined thermal efficiency of 31% were demonstrated and published in 1921 and 1928.

Advances in the [[Electric motor|electric motor]] and the [[Internal combustion engine|internal combustion engine]] gradually replaced steam engines in commercial use, and steam turbines supplanted them in power generation. Large reciprocating piston steam engines are still manufactured in Germany.

Steam-powered vessel, typically ocean-faring, propelled by steam engines that turn propellers or paddlewheels. Steamships were preceded by smaller steamboats; the first working steamboat and paddle steamer, the *Pyroscaphe*, dates from 1783. Paddlewheels were standard on early vessels, but their performance fell when added weight submerged them. The change to the screw propeller made ocean-going steamers viable. In 1839, *Archimedes* became the first screw propeller-driven steamship for open-water seagoing. Because steamships depended less on wind patterns, new trade routes opened, and the steamship has been described as a major driver of the first wave of [[Globalization|trade globalization]] (1870–1913).

Alloy of iron and carbon with improved mechanical properties compared with pure iron. Its high strength and low raw material cost make it one of the most commonly manufactured materials. It is used in construction, bridges, tools, ships, trains, cars, machines, appliances, and weapons.

Steel is an [[Alloy|alloy]] of [[Iron|iron]] and carbon, and often other elements, with a carbon content up to 2.14% by weight. Plain iron–carbon alloys above about 2.1% carbon are [[Cast iron|cast iron]], which is not malleable even when hot but casts well. [[Wrought iron]], now largely obsolete, contains little carbon but large amounts of slag. Alloy steels contain deliberately added elements such as manganese, nickel, molybdenum, vanadium, and tungsten. [[Stainless steel|Stainless steels]] typically contain 18% [[Chromium|chromium]] for corrosion resistance, and galvanized steel is coated in zinc.

When iron is smelted from ore with coke or charcoal, the result is [[Pig iron|pig iron]], rich in carbon. Refining injects oxygen into the molten iron to remove excess carbon. Bloomery furnaces produced steel for thousands of years, but large-scale industrial use began after the blast furnace and crucible steel were introduced in the 17th century. The Bessemer process in England in the mid-19th century began an era of mass-produced steel, followed by the open-hearth furnace and basic oxygen steelmaking. Mild steel replaced wrought iron. American production centered on Pittsburgh, Bethlehem, Pennsylvania, and Cleveland, Ohio until the late 20th century.

More than 1.6 billion tons are produced annually, and [[China]] produced 54% of the world's steel in 2023. The industry contributes 8% of global emissions, but steel is one of the most recycled materials, with a global recycling rate of over 60%.

Physical law relating the thermal radiation of a black body to its temperature. First king of Hungary (1000 or 1001–1038) and Catholic saint. Born Vajk, he succeeded his father as grand prince in 997 and defeated his relative Koppány, who had large numbers of pagan warriors behind him, with the help of foreign knights. He was crowned with a crown sent by Pope Sylvester II. In a series of wars he unified the Carpathian Basin, and he forced the invading troops of Conrad II, Holy Roman Emperor, to withdraw in 1030. He established at least one archbishopric and six bishoprics, and organized local government into counties run by royal officials. Hungary enjoyed lasting peace under his rule and became a preferred route for pilgrims and merchants between [[Western Europe]] and [[Constantinople]]. He was canonized in 1083, and his feast day, celebrated on 20 August, is a public holiday known as State Foundation Day.

ENTRY:
Generalized belief about a category of people, studied in [[Social psychology|social psychology]]. Stereotypes concern a group's personality, preferences, appearance or abilities, and they simplify information processing by letting people rely on stored knowledge rather than new input. They are often faulty and resistant to new information. Research on their accuracy has yielded mixed results: studies of national-origin and astrological-sign stereotypes found them inaccurate, while studies of gender stereotypes found them more likely to reflect reality. Explicit stereotypes are conscious beliefs that people knowingly use to judge others, whereas implicit stereotypes are automatic, involuntary associations between a social group and a domain or attribute. Stereotypes are regarded as the most cognitive component of intergroup attitudes, often occurring without conscious awareness; [[Prejudice|prejudice]] is the affective component, and discrimination the behavioral one. Stereotypes, prejudice, [[Racism|racism]], and discrimination are related but distinct concepts.
