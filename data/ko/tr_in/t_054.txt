ENTRY:
Concealment of animals or objects by coloration, pattern, materials or illumination, either by making them hard to see or by disguising them as something else. Most camouflage aims at crypsis, through background matching, disruptive coloration and countershading. A third approach, motion dazzle, uses a conspicuous pattern to make an object momentarily harder to locate. Some animals, such as [[Chameleon|chameleons]] and [[Octopus|octopuses]], can actively change their skin patterns and colors, while [[Cephalopod|cephalopods]] such as [[Squid|squid]] use light for counter-illumination. Military camouflage developed rapidly during [[World War I]], when merchant ships were painted in dazzle patterns to confuse enemy submarines about a target's speed and heading. Since the mid-20th century, [[Radar|radar]] has largely made camouflage obsolete for fixed-wing military aircraft. Camouflage patterns are also widely used in [[Fashion|fashion]] clothing.

Geologic shield of exposed Precambrian igneous and high-grade metamorphic rock, centered on [[Hudson Bay]] in eastern and central [[Canada]]. Artificial waterway built for navigation, irrigation, drainage, or water supply.

Canals originated in ancient Mesopotamia more than 6,000 years ago, chiefly for irrigation. Most navigation canals use a series of dams and [[Lock (water navigation)|locks]] that create slack-water levels. A navigation runs roughly parallel to the valley of an existing river and shares its drainage basin, whereas a true canal cuts across a drainage divide to connect two basins. This is harder to construct and needs an external water source, as in the [[Panama Canal]], the best-known example. Where the channel must change elevation, locks, lifts, or elevators raise and lower vessels.

Canals fall into two broad types: waterways carrying vessels, either linking lakes, rivers, and seas or forming city networks such as those of Venice, Amsterdam, and Bangkok; and aqueducts, which convey water for drinking, hydropower, and irrigation. The [[Roman Empire]]'s aqueducts were water supply canals.

Canals once held immense importance for commerce. Bulk raw materials such as coal and ores were difficult and costly to move without water transport, and canals, including Britain's Bridgewater Canal, helped fuel the [[Industrial Revolution]]. Replacement began in the United Kingdom in the 1840s, when [[Rail transport|railways]] began to supersede canal shipping. By the early 1880s many canals could not compete and were abandoned, and after World War I motor trucks and expanding road networks further reduced canal freight. Today most ship canals serve bulk cargo and large vessels. The longest existing canal is the [[Grand Canal (China)|Grand Canal]] of northern China, stretching 1,794 km from Beijing to Hangzhou.

The term was once also applied to linear features seen on [[Mars]], which modern imaging showed to be a misnomer.

Durable plain-woven fabric used for [[Sail|sails]], [[Tent|tents]] and backpacks, and as a support for [[Oil painting|oil painting]]. Historically made from [[Hemp]], modern canvas is usually made of [[Cotton]], [[Linen]] or polyvinyl chloride (PVC). Unlike twill-woven denim, it is plain woven. Duck canvas has more tightly woven threads, and the term duck comes from the Dutch word for cloth, doek. The word derives from Anglo-French canevaz, traced to Vulgar Latin *cannapaceus*, "made of hemp". Canvas is usually stretched over a wooden frame called a stretcher and coated with gesso. Panel painting remained more common than canvas until the 16th century in Italy and the 17th century in Northern Europe.

ENTRY:
Deep cleft between escarpments or cliffs, also called a gorge or chasm, usually carved by a river over geologic time. Rivers cut downward through rock layers, and canyons form where softer strata erode while harder layers resist [[Weathering|weathering]]. Most canyons were formed by long erosion from a [[Plateau]] or table-land level. Canyons are much more common in arid areas, where physical weathering has a more localized effect. Canyon walls are often formed of resistant [[Sandstone]] or [[Granite]]. A box canyon has steep walls on three sides and was used as a corral in the western United States. Submarine canyons on continental slopes are formed by turbidity currents and landslides. The word derives from Spanish cañón and is generally used in North America, while gorge and ravine are used in Europe and Oceania.

ENTRY:
Chinese warlord, statesman and poet who rose to power at the end of the [[Han dynasty]]. In the 190s he recruited his own followers and formed his own army. In AD 196 he received Emperor Xian, who had been held by rival warlords, and as Imperial Chancellor from AD 208 he controlled the central government. He defeated Yuan Shao at the Battle of Guandu in AD 200 and went on to unify much of northern China. His defeat at the Battle of Red Cliffs in AD 208 halted his attempts to gain a foothold in southern China. His son Cao Pi founded the state of Cao Wei in AD 220, inaugurating the [[Three Kingdoms]] period. Opinions of Cao Cao have remained divided since the Jin dynasty, with some praising his achievements and poetry and others condemning his cruelty and cunning.

ENTRY:
Ability of an object to store electric charge, expressed as the ratio of the change in charge to the change in electric potential. Two closely related notions are commonly recognized: self-capacitance, measured between an object and ground, and mutual capacitance, measured between two components, as in a [[Capacitor]]. The capacitance between two conductors depends on their opposing surface area, the distance between them and the permittivity of any [[Dielectric]] between them. The SI unit is the [[Farad]], named after the English physicist [[Michael Faraday]]. A 1 farad capacitor charged with 1 [[Coulomb|coulomb]] has a potential difference of 1 [[Volt|volt]] between its plates. The self-capacitance of the planet Earth is about 710 μF.

ENTRY:
Passive electronic component with two terminals that stores electrical energy by accumulating charge on two closely spaced, insulated surfaces. Most capacitors have two conductors, often metal plates or foils, separated by a [[Dielectric|dielectric]] such as glass, ceramic, plastic film, paper, mica or air. The component was originally called a condenser, a term still found in names such as condenser microphone. Capacitors block direct current while passing alternating current, smooth power-supply output and tune radios to particular frequencies. Unlike a [[Resistor]], an ideal capacitor does not dissipate energy. In October 1745 Ewald Georg von Kleist found that charge could be stored in a water-filled glass jar, and the following year Pieter van Musschenbroek invented a similar device, the Leyden jar.
