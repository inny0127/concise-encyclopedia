ENTRY:
Seasonal state of minimal activity and reduced metabolism, entered by some animals to pass through the [[Winter|winter]] months. Hibernation is a form of heterothermy in which body temperature, breathing and heart rate are lowered, so that the animal conserves energy when sufficient food is unavailable. Before entering dormancy, animals must store enough energy to last the entire period: larger species become hyperphagic and build fat deposits, while many small species cache food instead. Some [[Mammal|mammals]] hibernate while pregnant; female [[Bear|bears]] give birth during or shortly after the dormancy. Although the term was traditionally reserved for deep hibernators such as [[Rodent|rodents]], it is now applied according to metabolic suppression rather than an absolute drop in body temperature. The summer equivalent is aestivation.

Atmospheric region where air pressure is higher than in surrounding areas, also called an anticyclone. Highs are middle-scale features that result from interplay between larger patterns of atmospheric circulation. The strongest form when cold air from polar regions spreads into cooler neighboring regions, while more frequent weaker highs result from subsidence, in which cool, drier air descends. [[Wind|Winds]] flow outward from the center and are curved by the [[Coriolis force]], rotating clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and counterclockwise in the Southern. High pressure typically brings clear skies, and in summer and winter it brings warm and cold weather respectively in temperate latitudes. On English-language [[Weather|weather]] maps, high-pressure centers are marked H. The term anticyclone was coined in 1877 by [[Francis Galton]].

ENTRY:
Mountain range in Central and South Asia, west of the Himalayas. It runs about 800 km (500 mi) from central and eastern [[Afghanistan]] into northwestern [[Pakistan]] and far southeastern [[Tajikistan]], dividing the valley of the [[Amu Darya]] to the north from the [[Indus River]] valley to the south. Its highest peak, Tirich Mir, rises to 7,708 m (25,289 ft) in Chitral District of [[Khyber Pakhtunkhwa]], Pakistan. The range was a historically significant center of [[Buddhism]], with sites such as the Bamiyan Buddhas, and it has long been a passageway for invasions of the Indian subcontinent. Kafiristan, within the range, kept polytheistic beliefs until the 19th century, when the Emirate of Afghanistan converted it to Islam and renamed it Nuristan. The name first appears in the 14th-century writings of [[Ibn Battuta]], and it is popularly translated as "Killer of Hindus," although its etymology is uncertain.

Lunisolar calendar used in the [[Indian subcontinent]] and [[Southeast Asia]], also called *Panchanga*. The Shalivahana Shaka of the Deccan region of southern India and the Vikram Samvat of [[Nepal]] and northern and central India both emphasize the lunar cycle and begin the new year in spring. In [[Tamil Nadu]] and [[Kerala]], the solar cycle is emphasized in the Tamil and Malayalam calendars. The lunar month is kept intact, and an extra month is inserted every 32–33 months so that festivals and crop rituals fall in the proper season. The calendar sets the dates of Hindu festivals and is used in Hindu astrology.

ENTRY:
Philosophical systems that developed alongside the first Hindu religious traditions in ancient and medieval India. The six orthodox (*āstika*) schools accept the [[Vedas]] as authoritative. Samkhya is the earliest school of dualism, and [[Yoga]] combines its metaphysics with meditation and breath techniques. Nyaya emphasizes logic and direct realism, and its offshoot Vaisheshika is concerned with atomism and naturalism. Mimamsa justifies ritual, faith and religious obligation, while [[Vedanta]] contains traditions that mostly embrace nondualism. Heterodox (*nāstika*) systems, including [[Buddhism]], [[Jainism]], Charvaka and Ajivika, reject or reinterpret central Vedic concepts such as *ātman*. They are regarded as Indian but almost never as Hindu. Theistic sub-schools, such as Pashupata Shaiva and Vaishnava, blend ideas from several orthodox systems, and each school has an epistemological literature called *pramana*.

Umbrella term for a range of Indian religious and spiritual traditions unified by adherence to [[Dharma]], a cosmic order maintained through rituals and righteous living, as expounded in the [[Vedas]]. With approximately 1.17 billion followers, around 15% of the global population, it is the world's third-largest religion and the largest ethnic religion. Hindus live largely in [[India]], [[Nepal]], Mauritius, and Bali, Indonesia.

Hinduism has no single founder and no central doctrinal authority, and many Hindus do not claim to belong to any denomination. Scholars recognize four major denominations: Vaishnavism, Shaivism, Shaktism, and Smartism. Six Āstika schools of philosophy accept the authority of the Vedas, among them Vedanta. Texts are classified as Śruti ("heard") and Smṛti ("remembered"); the major scriptures are the Vedas, the Upanishads, the Puranas, the [[Ramayana]], the [[Mahabharata]] (including the Bhagavad Gita), and the Agamas.

Prominent themes include [[Karma|karma]] (action, intent, and consequences), saṃsāra (the cycle of death and rebirth), and four Puruṣārthas, the proper aims of human life: dharma (ethics and duties), artha (prosperity and work), kama (desires), and moksha (liberation). Practices include devotion (bhakti), worship (puja), sacrificial rites (yajna), meditation, and [[Yoga]].

The word *Hindu* is an exonym derived from the Sanskrit *Sindhu*, the name of the Indus River, and was originally a geographical term. The traditional chronology presents Hinduism as existing for thousands of years, whereas scholars regard it as a synthesis of Brahmanical orthopraxy with various Indian cultures that emerged after the Vedic period, between c. 500–200 BC and c. AD 300. It flourished in the medieval period as Buddhism declined in India. Since the 19th century, modern Hinduism has acquired wide appeal in the West, reflected in the popularization of yoga and movements such as ISKCON.

Classical music of the northern regions of the [[Indian subcontinent]], also called *Shastriya Sangeet*. It diverged in the 12th century from [[Carnatic music]], the classical tradition of southern India. Carnatic compositions are largely in Sanskrit, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam, while Hindustani compositions use Sanskrit, Hindustani (Hindi-Urdu), Braj, Awadhi, Bhojpuri, Bengali, Rajasthani, Punjabi and Persian. Musicians learn through a network of schools called *gharana*. Performances are built on the *raga*, a melodic mode, and the *tala*, a rhythmic pattern, and instruments include the veena, [[Sitar|sitar]] and sarod. Ustad Bismillah Khan, Pandit Bhimsen Joshi and [[Ravi Shankar]] were awarded the Bharat Ratna, India's highest civilian award, for their contributions to the arts.
