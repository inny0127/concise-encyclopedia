Landlocked country in [[Central Europe|Central Europe]], also known as Czechia and historically as Bohemia. Its capital and largest city is [[Prague]]. [[Slavic languages|West Slavic]] language of the Czech–Slovak group, written in the [[Latin alphabet|Latin script]]. It is the official language of the [[Czech Republic|Czech Republic]] and is spoken by over 12 million people, including second-language speakers. Czech is closely related to Slovak and, to a lesser degree, to Polish. It is a fusional language with a rich system of [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphology]] and relatively flexible word order, and its vocabulary has been extensively influenced by Latin and German. The modern written standard was codified during the Czech National Revival in the late 18th to mid-19th century. The most widely spoken non-standard variety, Common Czech, is based on the vernacular of [[Prague|Prague]]. Czech has ten monophthongs, three diphthongs and 25 consonants, and a raised alveolar trill, written ř, that occurs as a phoneme in few other languages. [[Jan Hus|Jan Hus]] contributed significantly to the standardization of Czech orthography.

ENTRY:
Deoxyribonucleic acid, a [[Polymer|polymer]] of two polynucleotide chains coiled in a double helix, which carries the genetic instructions for the development, functioning, growth, and reproduction of all known organisms and many viruses. DNA and [[RNA|ribonucleic acid]] (RNA) are the two nucleic acids found in all life.

Each strand is a series of nucleotides, each made of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group, and one of four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), or thymine (T). The strands are antiparallel and held together by hydrogen bonds, which pair A with T and C with G. The double helix has a pitch of 34 ångströms (3.4 nm). The sequence of bases encodes genetic information; because both strands carry it, it can be replicated when they separate. Under the genetic code, RNA copies of DNA (transcription) specify the sequence of amino acids in [[Protein|proteins]] (translation).

In eukaryotes, DNA is organized into [[Chromosome|chromosomes]], stored mostly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Prokaryotes store DNA in the cytoplasm in circular chromosomes. The human genome comprises about 3 billion base pairs on 23 pairs of chromosomes; only about 1–2% consists of protein-coding genes, approximately 20,000 in total.

Friedrich Miescher first isolated DNA in 1869. In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod, and Maclyn McCarty showed that it is responsible for heredity in bacteria. In 1953 [[James Watson]] and [[Francis Crick]] proposed the double-helix model, building on X-ray data from [[Rosalind Franklin]] and Maurice Wilkins; it was published in *Nature*.

ENTRY:
Technique for determining an individual's [[DNA]] characteristics, also called DNA fingerprinting. In forensic investigations it compares suspects' profiles with DNA evidence. Modern techniques are highly reliable, but they give only a fallible probabilistic estimate of a match. British geneticist Sir Alec Jeffreys discovered the technique in 1984 at the University of Leicester, after realizing that some regions of DNA contain highly variable repetitive sequences unique to each individual. Although 99.9% of human DNA sequences are the same in every person, the variable regions allow individuals to be distinguished unless they are [[Twin|identical twins]]. DNA profiling is also used in paternity testing, to establish immigration eligibility, and in genealogical and medical research. Its first forensic use, in the murders of two teenagers in Narborough, Leicestershire, led to the conviction of Colin Pitchfork in January 1988.

ENTRY:
Process by which a cell makes exact copies of its [[DNA]]. It occurs in all organisms and is essential to inheritance, cell division and the repair of damaged tissues. Because each original strand serves as a template, every replicated molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand. Replication usually begins at origins of replication, where [[Enzyme|enzymes]] called helicases unwind the double helix and replication forks grow in both directions. DNA polymerase adds nucleotides that complement the template strand. Proofreading and error-checking mechanisms ensure near-perfect fidelity, with an error rate as low as one in 10⁹ nucleotides. Replication occurs during the S (synthesis) stage of interphase. It can also be performed *in vitro*, as in the [[Polymerase chain reaction|polymerase chain reaction]] (PCR).

Dahlias were known only to the Aztecs and other peoples of southern North America until the Spanish conquest, after which the plants were brought to Europe.
