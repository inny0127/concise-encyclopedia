Archipelagic country in Southeast Asia, in the western Pacific Ocean (pop. over 114 million).

It is the world's thirteenth-most-populous country. [[Manila]] is the capital and Quezon City the most populous city, both within Metro Manila.

Negritos, the archipelago's earliest known modern inhabitants, were followed by Austronesian peoples who arrived from Taiwan around 2200 BC. A 2016 international arbitral ruling rejected China's "nine-dash line" claims in the South China Sea and affirmed Philippine rights within its exclusive economic zone, though enforcement remains contested.

Systematic study of general and fundamental questions concerning existence, knowledge, mind, reason, language, and value.

The word comes from the [[Ancient Greek]] *philosophía*, "love of wisdom," from *phílos* ("loving, friend of") and *sophía* ("wisdom"). Some sources say the term was coined by the pre-Socratic philosopher [[Pythagoras]], but this is not certain. It entered English from Old French and Anglo-Norman around 1175 CE. Philosophy is a rational and critical inquiry that reflects on its own methods and assumptions. Socrates stated that "the unexamined life is not worth living" to highlight its role in understanding one's own existence.

Before the modern age the term was used in a wide sense that included most forms of rational inquiry, with [[Natural philosophy]] encompassing physics, chemistry, and biology. Isaac Newton's 1687 *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica* referred to natural philosophy in its title but is today considered a book of physics. Many individual sciences, such as physics and psychology, once formed part of philosophy but are now separate academic disciplines. Toward the end of the modern period the term acquired its narrower meaning, associated mainly with metaphysics, epistemology, and ethics, and distinguished from the empirical sciences and mathematics.

Influential traditions include Western, Arabic–Persian, Indian, and Chinese philosophy. Western philosophy originated in ancient Greece. A central topic in Arabic–Persian philosophy is the relation between reason and revelation. Indian philosophy combines the spiritual problem of how to reach enlightenment with the exploration of the nature of reality and the ways of arriving at knowledge. Chinese philosophy focuses principally on practical issues about right social conduct, government, and self-cultivation.

The major branches are [[Epistemology]], which studies what knowledge is and how to acquire it; [[Ethics]], which investigates moral principles and right conduct; [[Logic]], the study of correct reasoning; and [[Metaphysics]], which examines the most general features of reality, existence, objects, and properties. Other subfields include aesthetics, philosophy of language, mind, mathematics, science, religion, and history, and political philosophy. Within each branch, competing schools promote different principles, theories, or methods. Philosophers use conceptual analysis, reliance on common sense and intuitions, thought experiments, analysis of ordinary language, description of experience, and critical questioning. Philosophy also informs fields such as law, business, and journalism by addressing their ethical questions.

Attempts to define philosophy precisely are controversial and are studied in metaphilosophy. Some definitions focus on method, such as pure reasoning, and others on topic. [[Immanuel Kant]] held that its task is united by four questions: "What can I know?", "What should I do?", "What may I hope?", and "What is the human being?" Naturalistic philosophers such as W. V. O. Quine see it as an empirical yet abstract science, and it has been described as "the midwife of the sciences." Others regard it as conceptual analysis, finding the necessary and sufficient conditions for applying concepts, or as thinking about thinking.

Ancient Semitic-speaking maritime civilization of city-states along the Levantine coast of the eastern Mediterranean, primarily in present-day Lebanon.

The Phoenicians emerged directly from the Bronze Age [[Canaan|Canaanites]], and their cultural traditions survived the [[Late Bronze Age collapse]]. They called themselves Canaanites and their land Canaan. *Phoenicia* is a Greek exonym, from *phoînix*, which meant variously "Phoenician person," "Tyrian purple, crimson," or "date palm"; it did not correspond to a unified native identity. Modern scholarship generally views the distinction after c. 1200 BC as artificial.

Phoenician society was organized into independent city-states, notably Byblos, Sidon, and Tyre, each politically autonomous, with no evidence of a shared national identity. The Phoenicians established one of antiquity's most extensive maritime networks, active for over a millennium and linking Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Greece. They founded colonies and trading posts from Cyprus to the Maghreb and the Iberian Peninsula; [[Carthage]] became a major power by the 7th century BC. Because little native literature survives, the Phoenicians were long regarded as a "lost" civilization and became better understood only after the discovery of inscriptions in the 17th and 18th centuries. Their most enduring legacy is the earliest verified alphabet, a 22-consonant script derived from the Proto-Sinaitic script. It spread across the Mediterranean and gave rise to the [[Greek alphabet]], ancestor of the Latin and Cyrillic scripts.

Its 22 consonant letters left vowels implicit, though late varieties sometimes used matres lectionis to mark some vowels. Phoenician merchants spread it across the Mediterranean, and its descendants include the Aramaic and Samaritan alphabets and the [[Greek alphabet|Archaic Greek alphabets]]. Because each sound had its own symbol, it needed only a few dozen signs, far fewer than [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] or hieroglyphic writing, which required long training. The alphabet proper was used in [[Carthage]] until the 2nd century BC.

Basic unit of [[Phonology|phonology]], a set of speech sounds that speakers regard as a single sound distinguishing one word from another. All languages contain phonemes, and all spoken languages include both [[Consonant|consonant]] and [[Vowel|vowel]] phonemes. Written between slashes, as in /p/ and /b/, phonemes separate English words such as *pat* and *bat*. Phonemes are abstract, and the varying pronunciations of one phoneme are called allophones: the English /p/ is aspirated in *pit* but not in *spit*. Phonemes differ across languages, so [n] and [ŋ] are distinct in English, distinguishing *sin* and *sing*, but form one phoneme in Spanish. Linguists transcribe phonemes between slashes using the [[International Phonetic Alphabet]], and write precise pronunciations in square brackets, a distinction known as the difference between [[Phonetics|phonetic]] and phonemic transcription.
