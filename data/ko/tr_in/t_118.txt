ENTRY:
Wading bird of the family Phoenicopteridae, the only living family in the order Phoenicopteriformes. Six living species occur in tropical and subtropical regions: four in the Americas, including the Caribbean, and two in Afro-Eurasia. Their pink plumage results from [[Carotenoid|carotenoids]] in their diet. Flamingos have long legs, sinuous necks and downward-curving bills, and they feed by sweeping their heads through water and filtering algae, crustaceans and small invertebrates with lamellae in their bills. The lesser flamingo is the smallest species and often forms flocks of up to a million birds.

ENTRY:
Overflow of water (rarely other fluids) that submerges land that is usually dry. Floods are of significant concern in agriculture, civil engineering, and public health, and are studied in [[Hydrology|hydrology]] and hydraulic engineering. Natural types include river flooding, groundwater flooding, coastal flooding, and urban flooding, sometimes known as [[Flash flood|flash flooding]]. Land may also be flooded intentionally for agricultural, military, or river-management purposes, as when paddy fields are prepared for rice.

Flooding may occur when a river, lake, sea, or ocean overtops or breaks [[Levee|levees]], or when rainwater accumulates on saturated ground, called an areal flood. Rivers flood when the flow rate exceeds the capacity of the channel, particularly at bends or meanders. Slow-rising floods most commonly occur in large rivers with large catchment areas and may result from sustained rainfall, rapid snowmelt, monsoons, or tropical cyclones. In the extremely flat Red River Valley of Minnesota, North Dakota, and Manitoba, where the river drops only 72 m over 890 km, a hybrid of river and areal flooding known locally as "overland flooding" can occur.

Human changes to the environment, such as [[Deforestation|deforestation]], removal of wetlands, changes in waterway course, and flood controls such as levees, often increase the intensity and frequency of flooding. [[Climate change]], through an intensified water cycle and sea level rise, is also cited as increasing flood risk, while natural variability in precipitation remains a major driver. Many people live and work near rivers because the land is flat and fertile and rivers provide travel and commerce, so floods often damage buildings on natural [[Floodplain|floodplains]].

Secondary impacts include a short-term increase in waterborne and vector-borne diseases and long-term displacement of residents. From 1990 to 2022, floods affected more than 3.2 billion people, caused 218,353 deaths, and caused more than 1.3 trillion USD in economic losses. Notable events include the [[1931 China floods]] and [[Hurricane Katrina]].

ENTRY:
One of two kinds of photoluminescence, in which a substance emits light after absorbing [[Electromagnetic radiation|electromagnetic radiation]]. Ultraviolet exposure makes many substances glow with [[Color|colored]] visible light, the color depending on their chemical composition. Fluorescent materials generally stop glowing almost immediately when the radiation stops, which distinguishes them from phosphorescence. The emitted light may have a longer [[Wavelength|wavelength]] than the absorbed radiation, so invisible [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light can produce a visible glow. Fluorescence has many practical applications, including [[Mineralogy|mineralogy]], [[Medicine|medicine]], chemical sensors, fluorescent labeling and cosmic-ray detection. Its most common everyday use is in fluorescent lamps and LED lamps, where coatings convert ultraviolet or blue light into white light.

ENTRY:
[[Chemical element]] (symbol F; atomic number 9), the lightest [[Halogen|halogen]]. At standard conditions it is a pale yellow diatomic gas, extremely reactive with all elements except the light noble gases, and highly toxic in elemental form. Elemental fluorine was first isolated in 1886 by French chemist Henri Moissan using low-temperature [[Electrolysis|electrolysis]], a process still used in production. Industrial production for uranium enrichment began during the [[Manhattan Project]] in World War II. Because pure fluorine is costly to refine, most commercial uses involve compounds; about half of mined [[Fluorite|fluorite]] is used in steelmaking. Organofluorine compounds serve as refrigerants, electrical insulation and [[Polytetrafluoroethylene|PTFE]] (Teflon). Fluoride ions inhibit [[Tooth decay|dental cavities]] and are used in toothpaste and water fluoridation.

ENTRY:
Mineral form of [[Calcium|calcium]] fluoride, CaF₂, also called fluorspar. It typically forms cubic crystals and has a [[Mohs scale|Mohs hardness]] of 4. Pure fluorite is colorless and transparent, but impurities usually make it colorful. It is used as a flux in [[Smelting|smelting]] and in making certain [[Glass|glasses]] and enamels. Its purest grades supply fluoride for [[Hydrofluoric acid|hydrofluoric acid]]. Optically clear fluorite is used in apochromatic lenses. The name derives from the Latin *fluere*, meaning to flow.

ENTRY:
Mixture of [[Hydrogen fluoride|hydrogen fluoride]] and antimony pentafluoride. It is the strongest known superacid, stronger than pure [[Sulfuric acid|sulfuric acid]] by many orders of magnitude on the Hammett acidity function. Its lowest attained value of that function (H₀) is about −28, although some sources report values below −31. Its extreme acidity comes from the fluoronium ion, H₂F⁺, which forms when hydrogen fluoride accepts a proton. It protonates some [[Hydrocarbon|hydrocarbons]] to form carbocations and attacks glass, so it is stored in containers lined with PTFE (Teflon).
