Development of human efforts to understand the universe beyond Earth's atmosphere, from prehistoric observation to modern cosmology. Astronomy is one of the oldest natural sciences and grew from calendrical and religious practices. The Warren Field monument in Scotland, a lunar calendar created around 8,000 BC, is considered the oldest known [[Calendar|calendar]]. [[Babylonia|Babylonian]] astronomers recorded planetary positions and eclipses on clay tablets. [[Hipparchus]] compiled a star catalog around 150 BC, and [[Ptolemy]] formalized a geocentric model in the *Almagest* about 140 AD. In 1543, [[Nicolaus Copernicus]] proposed a heliocentric model, starting the Copernican Revolution. [[Johannes Kepler]] described elliptical planetary orbits, and [[Isaac Newton]] unified gravity as the force behind celestial and terrestrial motion. Later advances included the telescope, spectroscopy, and the [[Cosmic microwave background|cosmic microwave background]], which affirms Big Bang cosmology.

Development of the idea that [[Matter|matter]] is composed of indivisible particles, from ancient philosophy to modern physics. The word atom comes from the Greek atomos, meaning indivisible, but ancient atomism rested on philosophical reasoning rather than experiment. Development of the study and transformation of matter, from prehistoric crafts to modern science. By 1000 BC civilizations used technologies that formed the basis of later branches of chemistry, including the discovery of fire, the extraction of metals from ores, making pottery and glazes, fermenting beer and wine, and making glass and [[Alloy|alloys]] such as bronze. Possible "remains of biotic life" were reported from 4.1 billion-year-old rocks in Western Australia in 2015, and microfossils from the Nuvvuagittuq Belt may be as old as 4.28 billion years, though such fossils may have non-biological origins. Study of the origins of mathematical discoveries, methods, and notation.

Before the modern age, written examples of new mathematical developments have come to light in only a few locales. From 3000 BC the Mesopotamian states of Sumer, Akkad, and Assyria, followed closely by [[Ancient Egypt]] and the Levantine state of Ebla, used arithmetic, algebra, and geometry for taxation, commerce, and trade, and in astronomy to record time and formulate calendars. The earliest mathematical texts are Plimpton 322 (Babylonian, c. 2000–1900 BC), the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (Egyptian, c. 1800 BC), and the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (Egyptian, c. 1890 BC). All mention Pythagorean triples, so the [[Pythagorean theorem]] appears to be the most ancient and widespread mathematical development after basic arithmetic and geometry.

Mathematics as a "demonstrative discipline" began in the 6th century BC with the Pythagoreans, who coined the term from the Greek *mathema*, "subject of instruction." Greek mathematics introduced deductive reasoning and rigor in proofs. Chinese mathematics made early contributions, including a place value system and the first use of negative numbers. The [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]] evolved over the first millennium AD in India and reached the Western world through Islamic mathematics and the work of Khwārizmī. The [[Maya civilization]] of Mexico and Central America independently gave zero a standard symbol.

Greek and Arabic texts were translated into Latin from the 12th century. From the 15th century, beginning in Renaissance Italy, new developments came at an increasing pace. [[Isaac Newton]] and [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] developed infinitesimal calculus in the 17th century, and German mathematicians such as [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]] and [[David Hilbert]] made later discoveries.

Study of the development of medical treatments, practices, and knowledge from prehistory to the present.

The field is multidisciplinary, drawing on economics, health sciences, sociology, and politics, and on archaeology for periods without written sources. Evidence of healing practices exists for [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] and other early humans. Finds include psychoactive psilocybin mushrooms in the Sahara c. 6000 BC and primitive dental care at Riparo Fredian, in present-day Italy (c. 11,900 BC), and at Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan (c. 7000 BC). Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3230 BC) carried what archaeologist Patrick Hunt calls a prehistoric medical kit, including birch polypore fungus, poppy seeds, sloe berries, and sphagnum moss.

Early medical traditions arose in Babylon, Egypt, China, and India. Cuneiform tablets from [[Mesopotamia]] dating to the 18th century BC record recipes against poisoning, and Egyptian papyri such as the Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus describe wound management. [[Hippocrates]] is called the father of medicine for establishing clinical observation, prognosis, and natural explanations of disease. [[Galen]]'s animal dissections informed ideas of human physiology for centuries. Before the 19th century, [[Humorism|humorism]] was thought to explain disease; it was gradually replaced by the germ theory, associated with Pasteur and Koch. The invention of the [[Microscope|microscope]] followed improved understanding during the Renaissance.

[[Public health]] measures developed especially in the 19th century as growing cities required sanitation. Research centers, often connected with major hospitals, opened in the early 20th century, and the mid-20th century brought new biological treatments such as [[Antibiotic|antibiotics]]. Medicine was heavily professionalized in the 20th century; nursing opened to women from the 1870s and medicine especially after 1970.

Development of musical expression and practice from prehistory to the present.

Music is a cultural universal, though definitions of it vary. Its origins remain highly contentious and are often tied to the origin of language. Scholars divide among three views: that music began as a proto-language that led to language, that it is a by-product of language, or that both derive from a common antecedent. [[Charles Darwin]] proposed in *The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex* (1871) that music arose through sexual selection, a view criticized for lack of evidence of sexual dimorphism in musicality. Herbert Spencer and the composer [[Richard Wagner]] held instead that music and language share a precursor. No theory has achieved broad approval.

Prehistoric music is first firmly dated to c. 38,000 BC by Upper Paleolithic bone flutes. Ancient music in Chinese, Egyptian, Greek, Indian, Persian, Mesopotamian, and Middle Eastern societies was often characterized by monophony and improvisation, and the oldest surviving musical notation dates from this period. The [[Silk Road]] spread musical ideas and instruments; Tang dynasty music was heavily influenced by Central Asian traditions. Religion often drove musical development: the Vedas shaped [[Indian classical music]], the Five Classics of Confucianism laid the basis for Chinese music, and after the 7th century Islamic music dominated Persia and the Arab world. Music of the early Christian Church inaugurated the Western classical tradition, in which [[Medieval music|medieval music]] developed polyphony and staff notation.

Western music history is conventionally divided into the Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), the [[Baroque]] (c. 1600–1750), represented by [[Johann Sebastian Bach]], the Classical period (c. 1750–1820), represented by Haydn, Mozart, and [[Ludwig van Beethoven]], and the Romantic era (c. 1820–1900).

Systematic study of the historical development of philosophical thought.

The field has a historical component, which asks which philosophers held which views and how their contexts shaped them, and a philosophical component, which evaluates the theories for truth and validity. It is controversial how far the field is distinct from philosophy itself; Neo-Kantians such as Wilhelm Windelband argued that philosophy is essentially historical. Mystical and religious traditions are often excluded when their claims do not rest on rational argumentation, though some theorists include myth and proverbial lore.

Western philosophy originated with inquiry into the nature of the cosmos in [[Ancient Greece]], from the Pre-Socratics through Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, whose codification of deductive logic proved foundational. Written record of human religious thought and practice, beginning with the invention of writing about 5,200 years ago (3200 BC).

Development of science from ancient times to the present, covering the natural, social, and formal sciences.

Protoscience and natural philosophies such as alchemy and astrology, which existed from the Bronze Age through the Middle Ages, declined after modern sciences emerged in the Scientific Revolution. The earliest roots of scientific practice lie in Ancient Egypt and [[Mesopotamia]] in the 3rd and 2nd millennia BC. After the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Greek learning deteriorated in Latin-speaking Western Europe but thrived in the Byzantine Empire and was absorbed into the Arabic-speaking Muslim world during the [[Islamic Golden Age]]. The Soviet Union launched [[Sputnik 1]] in 1957 and later sent [[Yuri Gagarin]], the first human in space, into orbit in 1961. The United States landed the first men on the Moon in 1969 with [[Apollo 11]]. Long-duration habitation followed with space stations such as Mir and the [[International Space Station]], which has been continuously occupied since 2000. In 2020 [[SpaceX]] became the first commercial operator to launch a crewed mission to the ISS.

Development of organized physical competition from prehistoric activity to modern professional sport. Sport had early links with warfare and entertainment, and competition was used to determine whether individuals were fit for military service. Cave paintings in France appear to depict sprinting in the Upper Paleolithic, and rock art in Egypt shows swimming and archery. Human invention of tools and techniques, ranging from simple stone tools to genetic engineering and information technology.

The earliest direct evidence of tool use, found in the Great Rift Valley of Ethiopia, dates to 2.5 million years ago, and the Oldowan stone-tool industry dates to at least 2.3 million years ago. Mastery of fire came approximately 1.5 million years ago, and the transition to agriculture in the [[Neolithic Revolution]] around 10,000 BC enabled settled communities. Sumerians developed [[Cuneiform|cuneiform]] writing and a base-60 numerical system by 3300 BC. The Bronze Age (c. 3000–1200 BC) introduced metalworking, which the [[Iron Age]] (from c. 1200 BC) extended. China developed gunpowder, the compass, and movable type printing by the 11th century. The [[Industrial Revolution]] began in Britain around 1760, with James Watt's steam engine of 1776. The telegraph (1837), telephone (1876), and internal combustion engine (1885) followed, then the ENIAC electronic computer (1946), transistors (1947), and integrated circuits (1958), which fueled the [[Information Age]].

Technology is closely tied to the history of science and to economic history, and technological change affects, and is affected by, a society's cultural traditions. Sociologists and anthropologists such as Lewis H. Morgan, Leslie White, and Gerhard Lenski have declared technological progress the primary factor driving civilization. White measured cultural evolution by energy, using the formula P = E/T, and Nikolai Kardashev extended his theory in the Kardashev scale. Economists use productivity as a measure of progress; in developed countries productivity growth has slowed since the late 1970s, and U.S. manufacturing employment fell from over 30% in the 1940s to just over 10% 70 years later. Post-industrial theorists argued that services and information were displacing industry and goods.

Historical account of the [[Peloponnesian War]] (431–404 BC) by [[Thucydides]], an Athenian historian who also served as a general in the war. The work, apparently unfinished, ends mid-sentence in 411 BC and was divided into eight books by later scholars. It is regarded as one of the earliest scholarly works of history. Thucydides recorded events year by year, each year divided into a summer campaign season and a quieter winter, a method that contrasts with [[Herodotus]]. He used extensive speeches, including the funeral oration of [[Pericles]] in Book 2, which classicists doubt reproduce the words actually spoken. Scholars disagree on bias. J. B. Bury saw an objective, scientific work, while Ernst Badian argued for a strong pro-Athenian bias. Others read the work as literature, a view advanced by W. R. Connor.
