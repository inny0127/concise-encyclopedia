ENTRY:
In geometry, an infinitely long straight object with no width, depth, or curvature, and a special case of a [[Curve|curve]]. It idealizes physical objects such as a straightedge, a taut string, or a ray of light. Lines are spaces of dimension one, which may be embedded in spaces of dimension two, three, or higher.

[[Euclid's Elements]] defines a straight line as a "breadthless length" that "lies evenly with respect to the points on itself" and introduces postulates as unprovable properties on which the rest of geometry rests. In modern geometry a line is usually either a primitive notion with properties given by [[Axiom|axioms]] or a set of points obeying a linear relationship. In an axiomatic formulation such as Hilbert's, any two distinct points lie on a unique line, and two distinct lines intersect at most at one point. In the [[Euclidean plane]], lines that do not intersect are parallel; in higher dimensions they are parallel if contained in a plane, and skew if not. In the plane a line may be written y = mx + b, where m is the slope and b the y-intercept.

Three or more points are collinear if they lie on the same line. Three points in a plane are collinear if and only if the [[Determinant|determinant]] of the matrix of their coordinates is zero, or equivalently if the slope between one pair equals the slope between another.

With respect to a conic (circle, ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola), lines may be tangent, touching it at a single point; secant, crossing it at two points; or exterior, not meeting it. A transversal intersects two other lines.

Unilineal descent group tracing its ancestry to a demonstrably shared ancestor, known as the apical ancestor. The word derives from Middle English *linage*, from Latin *linea* ("line"). Lineages differ from [[Clan|clans]], which may lack a documented apical ancestor. Three forms are recognized: matrilineal, in which descent runs through the mother, as among the Minangkabau of [[Indonesia]] and the Khasi of [[India]]; patrilineal, which is more common globally and is found across much of [[Southeast Asia]]; and ambilineal, in which individuals choose their mother's or father's group, common in modern societies such as the [[United States]] and [[Western Europe]]. Branch of mathematics concerning linear equations such as a₁x₁ + ⋯ + aₙxₙ = b, linear maps, and their representations in [[Vector space|vector spaces]] and through [[Matrix (mathematics)|matrices]]. It is central to almost all areas of mathematics and is fundamental in modern presentations of geometry, including defining lines, planes, and rotations. [[Functional analysis]] may be viewed as the application of linear algebra to function spaces. It is also used in most sciences and fields of engineering, because linear structures are natural models for many physical phenomena, and in computing, including computer graphics and machine learning.

The procedure now called [[Gaussian elimination]] appears in the ancient Chinese text *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*. Systems of linear equations arose in Europe with the introduction of coordinates in geometry by [[René Descartes]] in 1637. The first systematic methods used determinants, first considered by Leibniz in 1693; in 1750 Gabriel Cramer used them to give explicit solutions, now called Cramer's rule. W. R. Hamilton discovered the [[Quaternion|quaternions]] in 1843, and Hermann Grassmann published his "Theory of Extension" in 1844. In 1848 James Joseph Sylvester introduced the term matrix. [[Arthur Cayley]] introduced matrix multiplication and the inverse matrix in 1856. Peano gave the first modern definition of a vector space in 1888. Linear algebra took its modern form in the first half of the 20th century, as earlier ideas were generalized into abstract algebra, and computers spurred research into efficient algorithms for elimination and matrix decompositions.

A vector space over a field F, often the real or complex numbers, is a set V with two operations: vector addition and scalar multiplication by elements of F. Under addition, V is an abelian group.

Branch of [[Anthropology|anthropology]] concerned with how language influences social life. Modern typology emerged in the 1960s: at a 1961 conference on language universals at Dobbs Ferry, Joseph Greenberg proposed 45 linguistic universals based on data from 30 languages.

Scientific study of [[Language|language]] and its structure, meaning, and use. Its core areas are [[Syntax|syntax]] (rules governing the structure of sentences), semantics (meaning), morphology (structure of words), [[Phonetics|phonetics]] (speech sounds and equivalent manual gestures in sign languages), [[Phonology|phonology]] (the abstract sound system of a particular language), and pragmatics (how the context of use contributes to meaning). Subdisciplines such as biolinguistics and psycholinguistics bridge many of these divisions.

Theoretical linguistics seeks to explain the universal nature of language, while applied linguistics uses findings about language for practical purposes, such as improving language education and literacy. Mathematical and computational linguistics apply mathematics and computational modeling to language.

[[Historical linguistics]] studies how languages change. Western work in the field dates to roughly the late 18th century, when it grew out of philology, and it was most widely practiced in the late 19th century. It was long the cornerstone of [[Comparative linguistics|comparative linguistics]], which sorts languages into families and reconstructs prehistoric proto-languages, beginning with the [[Indo-European languages]]. Attempts to link families into super-families such as a hypothetical Nostratic group are still not widely accepted.

Modern linguistics is associated with [[Ferdinand de Saussure]]'s structuralist view of language as a system of signs and with Noam Chomsky's generative grammar, which posits innate linguistic capacities underlying universal grammar. A continuing debate divides generative approaches, which stress formal rules and biological innateness, from functionalist and usage-based approaches, which stress language use, social context, and learning.

A liquid consists of atoms or molecules held together by [[Intermolecular force|intermolecular]] bonds of intermediate strength, which let particles move around one another while remaining closely packed. As temperature rises, molecules vibrate more intensely and move farther apart; at the [[Boiling point|boiling point]], cohesive forces no longer hold them together and the liquid becomes a gas. Liquids generally expand when heated; water between 0 °C and 4 °C is a notable exception. Water compresses by only 46.4 parts per million per bar. This incompressibility makes liquids suitable for transmitting hydraulic power but also causes water hammer and cavitation. Pressure in a liquid at rest increases with depth.

Body of written work, and more narrowly writing regarded as an art form, especially novels, plays, and poems. The term covers both print and digital writing, and in recent centuries it has expanded to include oral literature, much of which has been transcribed. Literature records, preserves, and transmits knowledge and entertainment, and it can also have a social, psychological, spiritual, or political role.

Definitions have varied over time. In Western Europe before the 18th century, literature denoted all books and writing. The word derives from Latin *literatura* or *litteratura*, "learning, writing, grammar," from *littera*, "letter." A value-judgment definition limits literature to high-quality writing in the *belles-lettres* ("fine writing") tradition; the 1910–1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* called it "the best expression of the best thought reduced to writing." "Literature" is sometimes used synonymously with literary fiction, written with the goal of artistic merit, but it can also include non-fiction genres such as biography, diaries, memoirs, letters, and essays. Literary criticism, one of the oldest academic disciplines, concerns the literary merit or intellectual significance of specific texts, while textual criticism and the history of the book treat texts as artifacts or traditions.

Oral literature is an ancient tradition found in all parts of the world. The earliest poetry is believed to have been recited or sung to remember history, genealogy, and law. Scholars describe the Vedic texts and early Buddhist texts as transmitted orally, though Goody argued that the vast and consistent Vedic literature likely involved both written and oral traditions. All ancient Greek literature was to some degree oral, and the earliest was entirely so; according to Michael Gagarin, [[Homer]]'s epic poetry was largely composed, performed, and transmitted orally. Native North American peoples north of Mesoamerica had no known writing systems before contact with Europeans, and their storytelling traditions served largely as practical moral and social lessons. Gunditjmara oral histories in south-western Victoria, Australia, tell of volcanic eruptions that a 2020 study dated to between 34,000 and 40,000 years ago.

Oratory was long considered a literary art; from ancient Greece to the late 19th century, [[Rhetoric|rhetoric]] was central to Western education. Around the 4th millennium BC the complexity of trade and administration in [[Mesopotamia]] outgrew human memory, and [[Writing|writing]] became a more dependable way to record transactions. In ancient Egypt and Mesoamerica, writing may instead have arisen from the need to record historical and environmental events. Print technology has since allowed ever wider distribution of written works, and the digital era has blurred the lines between electronic literature and other modern media.
