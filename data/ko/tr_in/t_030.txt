Siamese kingdom in [[Southeast Asia]] (1351–1767), centered on the city of Ayutthaya. Considered the precursor of modern [[Thailand]], it emerged from a group of maritime city-states in the lower Chao Phraya valley. Its name derives from the [[Sanskrit]] Ayodhya. From 1569 to 1584 it was a vassal of Toungoo Burma under [[Bayinnaung]], then regained independence. In the 17th and 18th centuries it became a center of international trade. In 1767, after a 14-month siege, Burmese forces destroyed the city, ending the 417-year-old kingdom. Within fifteen years the seat of authority had moved to Thonburi and [[Bangkok]].

Flowering shrub in the genus [[Rhododendron]] and family [[Ericaceae]]. Azaleas bloom in spring, often for several weeks, and tolerate shade. They do best in well-drained acidic soil at 4.5–6.0 pH and need little fertilizer. Breeders have produced thousands of cultivars, which are propagated by cuttings. *Azalea indica* was first introduced to the outdoor landscape in the United States in the 1830s, at a rice plantation near [[Charleston, South Carolina]]. Alliance of three Nahua city-states in central Mexico (1428–1521). Also called the Triple Alliance, it was formed from the victorious factions of a civil war against Azcapotzalco, and [[Tenochtitlan]] became militarily dominant. Polytheistic and monistic pantheism of the [[Aztec Empire]]. Nahua metaphysics centered on teotl, a sacred power construed as the supreme god Ometeotl. Priests and the upper classes held monistic views, while popular religion emphasized the mythological and polytheistic aspects of a pantheon of lesser gods and natural forces. The most important deities, including Huitzilopochtli, were worshiped at the Templo Mayor in [[Tenochtitlan]], and conquered peoples could add the imperial god to their own pantheons. Nahuatl-speaking peoples and Mesoamerican civilization of central Mexico, c. 1300–1521. The term is often restricted to the Mexica of Tenochtitlan but is also used for the Nahua peoples of central Mexico in general. It was not an endonym; some scholars have criticized it and proposed alternatives such as Culhua-Mexica.

Aztec society was organized into city-states (altepetl), divided between nobility (pipiltin) and commoners (macehualtin), and shared Mesoamerican traits including [[Maize]] cultivation, a pantheon, and a calendric system. The Mexica were late arrivals to the Valley of Mexico and founded [[Tenochtitlan]] on islets in Lake Texcoco, around 1325 by one account. In 1427 Tenochtitlan, Texcoco, and Tlacopan formed the Triple Alliance, or [[Aztec Empire]], to defeat the Tepanec state of Azcapotzalco. Texcoco and Tlacopan soon became junior partners. The empire was never a territorial state held by garrisons: it dominated client city-states by installing friendly rulers, arranging marriage alliances, and extracting taxes, and it extended its reach through trade and conquest. Religion included human [[Sacrifice]]; excavations at the Templo Mayor have uncovered skull racks.

The empire reached its greatest extent in 1519, when Spanish forces led by [[Hernán Cortés]] arrived. Cortés allied with city-states hostile to the Mexica, and epidemics including [[Smallpox]] struck the population. Tenochtitlan fell on 13 August 1521 with the capture of the emperor Cuauhtémoc, and the Spanish founded [[Mexico City]] on its ruins, ruling through local nobles. Aztec history is known from archaeology, Indigenous writings, Spanish eyewitness accounts, and works such as the bilingual *Florentine Codex*, compiled by the friar Bernardino de Sahagún with Aztec informants.

Undergraduate degree awarded by higher education institutions after a course of study lasting three to six years. The two most common are the Bachelor of Arts (BA) and the Bachelor of Science (BS or BSc). The term derives from Medieval Latin *baccalaureus*; in the 12th century *bachelor* referred to a young or poor knight. Two-player [[Board game|board game]] played with counters and dice. It is the most widespread Western member of the tables family, whose ancestors date back at least 1,600 years. Each player has 15 pieces, traditionally called men, which move along 24 points according to the roll of two dice. The objective is to move all of one's pieces around the board and bear them off first. Removing all pieces while the opponent is still far behind earns a triple win, known as a backgammon. The game combines strategy with luck, and a doubling cube lets players raise the stakes during play. The earliest record of backgammon itself dates to 17th-century England, where it descended from the 16th-century game of Irish.
