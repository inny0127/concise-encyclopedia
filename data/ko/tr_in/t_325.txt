Means by which a person dies or attempts to die by suicide. Worldwide, [[Hanging|hanging]], [[Pesticide|pesticide]] poisoning, and [[Firearm|firearms]] predominate, though the pattern varies by country. Ottoman sultan (r. 1520–66), the longest-reigning of the dynasty. His rule marked a peak in the [[Ottoman Empire]]'s economic, military, and political power and raised its subjects to at least 25 million. He succeeded his father [[Selim I]] on September 30, 1520.

Suleiman opened his reign with campaigns against the Christian powers of Central and Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean. [[Belgrade]] fell in 1521 after he encircled it with 250,000 soldiers and over 100 ships, and Rhodes, base of the [[Knights Hospitaller]], fell in 1522 after a five-month siege. His great-grandfather [[Mehmed II]] had failed to take Belgrade. At Mohács in 1526 he defeated Louis II of Hungary and broke the strength of the [[Kingdom of Hungary]], much of which was later incorporated into the empire. His defeat at the siege of Vienna in 1529 halted further advance into Europe. He fought for years against the Shia Safavid Empire of Iran and annexed Iraq. In North Africa, Ottoman Tripolitania was established, and the fleet dominated the seas from the Mediterranean to the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf.

He personally instituted major judicial changes concerning society, education, taxation, and criminal law. With the chief judicial official Ebussuud Efendi he brought together the two forms of Ottoman law: sultanic (Kanun) and Islamic ([[Sharia]]).

Suleiman was a distinguished poet and goldsmith and a patron of the arts, overseeing what is called the Ottoman "Golden Age" of art, literature, and architecture. In 1534 he broke with tradition by marrying Hürrem, a woman of his harem. After his death in 1566 he was succeeded by his son Selim II. Scholars typically regard the following period as one of crisis and adaptation rather than simple decline.

Polyatomic anion, SO₄²⁻, found in salts of [[Sulfuric acid]]. Its sulfur atom sits at the center of four equivalent oxygen atoms in a [[Tetrahedral molecular geometry|tetrahedral]] arrangement, as predicted by VSEPR theory. Sulfate is the conjugate base of hydrogensulfate (HSO₄⁻). Organic sulfate esters, such as dimethyl sulfate, are covalent compounds. Sulfate salts, acid derivatives and peroxides are widely used in industry, and [[Gypsum|gypsum]] (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is a common sulfate mineral. Bonding has been debated: [[Linus Pauling]] proposed double bonds using d orbitals in [[Valence bond theory]], but later work found d-orbital involvement less significant than he believed.

Sumerian farmers along the Tigris and Euphrates grew abundant grain and other crops, and the surplus enabled urban settlements. The world's earliest known texts come from the Sumerian cities of [[Uruk]] and Jemdet Nasr and date between c. 3350 and c. 2500 BC, following proto-writing from c. 4000 to c. 2500 BC.

The name Sumer comes from the Akkadian term for the non-Semitic-speaking inhabitants. The Sumerians called their land Kengir, "Country of the noble lords," and their language Emegir. The [[Sumerian language]] is a language isolate.

Their origins are disputed. Most historians suggest permanent settlement between c. 5500 and c. 3300 BC by a West Asian people. Other proposals include North African, Caucasian, and Dilmun (Bahrain) origins, or a homeland on the coast of Eastern Arabia before it flooded at the end of the Ice Age; none is established. A prehistoric people termed Ubaidians, never mentioned by the Sumerians, are assumed to have been the first civilizing force, draining marshes for agriculture and developing trade, weaving, leatherwork, metalwork, masonry, and pottery.

Sumerian civilization took form in the Uruk period (4th millennium BC), continuing into the Jemdet Nasr and Early Dynastic periods. The Sumerians progressively lost control to Semitic states from the northwest, and Sumer was conquered by the [[Akkadian Empire]] around 2270 BC.

One later account lists city-states such as Uruk, Ur, Eridu, Lagash, and Nippur, governed by priest-kings or assemblies, and gods such as Enki and Inanna. It credits the Sumerians with cuneiform writing, the wheel, and the [[Epic of Gilgamesh]].

Star at the center of the [[Solar System]], a G-type main-sequence star (G2V) and the main source of energy for life on Earth.

The Sun is a massive sphere of hot plasma, heated to incandescence by [[Nuclear fusion|nuclear fusion]] reactions in its core, radiating energy from its surface mainly as visible light and infrared radiation, with 10% at ultraviolet energies. It has been an object of veneration in many cultures and a central subject of astronomical research since antiquity. It orbits the [[Galactic Center]] at a distance of 24,000 to 28,000 light-years. Its mean distance from Earth is about 1.496×10⁸ km, or about 8 light-minutes; this distance was used to define the [[Astronomical unit|astronomical unit]] (au), now defined as 149.5978707×10⁶ km.

The Sun is the largest and most massive object in the Solar System. Its diameter is about 1,391,400 km (864,600 mi), around 109 times that of Earth, and its mass is around 330,000 times Earth's, about 99.86% of the system's total. It has no definite boundary; its radius is measured to the edge of the photosphere, the apparent visible surface. The Sun formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of matter within a large [[Molecular cloud|molecular cloud]]; most of the matter gathered in the center, and the rest flattened into a disk that became the Solar System. ENTRY:
Device that tells the time of day from the position of the Sun's shadow on a marked dial. In its narrowest sense it consists of a flat dial plate and a gnomon, whose shadow aligns with hour lines as the Sun moves across the sky. The style, the time-telling edge of the gnomon, must be parallel to Earth's axis for accuracy throughout the year, and its angle from horizontal equals the sundial's [[Latitude|latitude]]. Sundials show local solar time, so clock time requires corrections for the equation of time, which can be as great as 16 minutes, 33 seconds, and for the difference between the local [[Longitude|longitude]] and that of the official [[Time zone|time zone]]. Forms include horizontal, vertical, equatorial, polar and analemmatic dials. The earliest known examples date to ancient Egypt around 1500 BCE.

ENTRY:
Portion of the electromagnetic radiation emitted by the Sun and received by Earth, including visible, [[Infrared|infrared]] and [[Ultraviolet|ultraviolet]] light. It takes about 8.3 minutes to reach Earth. Scattered and filtered by the atmosphere, it arrives as daylight, and as sunshine when not blocked by clouds. Sources estimate a global average of between 164 watts and 340 watts per square meter over a 24-hour day. ENTRY:
Largest branch of Islam and the largest religious denomination in the world.

Sunnis hold that [[Muhammad]] appointed no successor and that his closest companion [[Abu Bakr]] (r. 632–634) rightfully succeeded him as caliph of the Muslim community, chosen at the meeting of Saqifa. This contrasts with the view of [[Shia Islam]], which holds that Muhammad appointed [[Ali|Ali ibn Abi Talib]] (r. 656–661) as his successor. Sunnis nonetheless revere Ali, together with Abu Bakr, Umar (r. 634–644), and Uthman (r. 644–656), as the four *rashidun* ("rightly guided" caliphs), and honor the *sahaba*, *tabi'in*, and *tabi al-tabi'in* as the *salaf* (predecessors).

The name derives from *sunnah*, the practices of Muhammad. The Arabic term is pre-Islamic, originally meaning "the right path that has always been followed," and gained political significance after the murder of Uthman. During the second Civil War (680–92) it acquired connotations critical of Shia doctrines. Its use as a short group name is relatively recent; Ibn Taymiyyah probably used it first, and scholars such as Muhammad Rashid Rida later popularized it.

The [[Quran]], together with [[Hadith|hadith]] (especially the Six Books) and *ijma* (scholarly consensus), forms the basis of all traditional Sunni jurisprudence. [[Sharia]] rulings are derived from these sources together with public welfare and juristic discretion, using principles developed by four legal schools: Hanafi, Hanbali, Maliki, and Shafi'i. In creed, the tradition upholds the six pillars of *iman* (faith) and comprises the Ash'ari and Maturidi schools of *kalam* (theology) and the textualist Athari school. Sunni Islam is sometimes called "Orthodox Islam," though some scholars, such as John Burton, argue that there is no such thing as "Orthodox Islam."

Daily disappearance of the Sun below the horizon as Earth rotates. It is defined as the moment the upper limb of the Sun disappears below the horizon, and atmospheric refraction means the solar disk is then about one diameter below it.
