Physical quantity, represented by a scalar, vector, spinor or tensor, that has a value at each point in space and time.

A weather map of surface temperatures is a scalar field, and a map of wind speed and direction is a vector field. Field theories describe how field values change in space and time and are central to physics; electrodynamics can be formulated with two interacting vector fields or as a single rank-2 tensor field. Such machines underlie regular expressions and model everyday devices such as vending machines, elevators and traffic lights.

ENTRY:
Rapid oxidation of a fuel in the exothermic chemical process of [[Combustion|combustion]], releasing heat, light, and various reaction products. Flames, the most visible portion of a fire, are produced when the fuel reaches its ignition point temperature. Flames from hydrocarbon fuels consist primarily of carbon dioxide, water vapor, oxygen, and nitrogen; if hot enough, the gases may become ionized to produce [[Plasma (physics)|plasma]]. The color and intensity of a flame depend on the fuel and the surrounding gases. Fire is sustained by four interdependent components: fuel, heat, oxygen, and a self-perpetuating chemical chain reaction, modeled as the fire tetrahedron; removing any one extinguishes it.

Fire directly affects land-based ecological systems worldwide. Its benefits include stimulating plant growth and maintaining ecological balance; its harms include hazards to life and property, atmospheric pollution, and water contamination. Burning vegetation releases nitrogen into the atmosphere, whereas potassium and phosphorus remain in the ash and are quickly recycled into the soil; the resulting loss of nitrogen produces a long-term reduction in soil fertility, which nitrogen-fixing plants such as clover, peas, and beans can help recover.

The fossil record of fire first appears with land-based flora in the Middle Ordovician, 470 million years ago. [[Wildfire]] became possible once atmospheric oxygen rose above 13%, and is first recorded in the Late Silurian, 420 million years ago, by fossils of charred plants. Fire became more abundant when grasses became dominant in many ecosystems, around 6 to 7 million years ago.

Control of fire was a dramatic change in the habits of early humans. It allowed cooking, which increased the variety and availability of nutrients and reduced disease by killing pathogens; provided warmth, allowing people to live in cooler climates; and kept nocturnal predators at bay. Evidence of occasional cooked food dates from 1 million years ago, and the oldest clear evidence of deliberate firemaking dates to around 400,000 years ago at Barnfield, England, where pyrite appears to have been struck against flint. Evidence becomes widespread around 50 to 100 thousand years ago. By the [[Neolithic Revolution]], people worldwide used fire in landscape management, including controlled burns and slash-and-burn agriculture, which remains common across much of tropical Africa, Asia, and South America. Globally, as much as 5 million sq km burns in a given year.

Fire has been used for cooking, heating, lighting, clearing land, signaling, propulsion, smelting, forging, waste incineration, cremation, rituals, and warfare, and was one of the four classical elements. In 1667 Johann Joachim Becher, and more formally in 1697 Georg Ernst Stahl, incorporated van Helmont's finding that burning charcoal released a gas into phlogiston theory. [[Antoine Lavoisier]] demonstrated that combustion did not involve the release of a substance but rather that something was being taken up. Most home fires start from unattended cooking, although cigarettes are another major cause; smoke detectors and sprinkler systems greatly reduce harm.
