ENTRY:
Agricultural practice of applying controlled amounts of water to land to grow [[Crop|crops]], landscape plants, and lawns. It has been a key aspect of agriculture for over 5,000 years. It sustains crops in dry areas and during below-average rainfall, and is also used to protect plants from frost, suppress weeds, and cool livestock. [[Drainage]] is often studied alongside it, and both may be needed to sustain high production.

Methods differ in how water reaches plants. Surface (gravity) irrigation is the oldest. Sprinkler irrigation distributes piped water through overhead high-pressure devices. Micro-irrigation delivers water at low pressure through a piped network, and drip irrigation delivers it directly to the root zone. Subirrigation raises the water table to moisten soil below the roots. Water comes from [[Groundwater|groundwater]], from rivers, lakes, or reservoirs, or from sources such as treated wastewater, desalinated water, and fog collection.

Early systems emerged in [[Mesopotamia]], Egypt, the Indus Valley, and China. Irrigated area worldwide grew from 8 million ha in 1800 to 94 million in 1950 and 235 million in 1990. The land equipped for irrigation reached 355 million ha in 2023, up 23% from 289 million ha in 2000, and made up 23% of all croplands. Asia holds 71% of the total. [[India]] (76 million ha) and China (75 million ha) have the largest equipped areas, far ahead of the United States (25 million ha). Irrigated croplands produce 48% of crops by value, and their yield averages 76% higher than that of rainfed land.

Irrigation has environmental costs. Overdrafting depletes underground [[Aquifer|aquifers]], and over-irrigation wastes water and chemicals, may cause water pollution, and can raise water tables, leading to salinity problems that require subsurface drainage.

Device used to irrigate agricultural crops, lawns, golf courses, and other areas. Sprinkler [[Irrigation|irrigation]] applies water in a controlled manner that mimics rainfall, through a network of pumps, valves, pipes, and sprinklers. Industrial rainguns operate at very high pressures and are also used for dust suppression and logging. Many industrial sprinklers are buried with their supporting [[Plumbing|plumbing]] and grouped into zones that a solenoid valve turns on and off. Home systems include impact, oscillating, and drip sprinklers, often on timers. Center-pivot irrigation was invented in 1940 by the farmer Frank Zybach of Strasburg, Colorado, and it transformed large-scale farming. Modern residential systems may use smart controllers and rain sensors to adjust watering to weather and soil moisture.

ENTRY:
Philosophical problem articulated by [[David Hume]] concerning whether [[Morality|moral]] statements about what ought to be can be inferred from objective statements about what is. Hume distinguished descriptive from prescriptive statements and argued that the transition between them is not obvious. In *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), he insisted the step needs explanation. Hume's law, or Hume's guillotine, is the thesis that an ethical conclusion cannot be inferred from purely descriptive factual premises. G. E. Moore's open-question argument reaches a similar view and was aimed at ethical naturalists, who identify moral properties with natural ones. The problem is closely related to the fact–value distinction in [[Epistemology]], although that discussion may also encompass [[Aesthetics|aesthetics]]. Responses include non-cognitivist views such as emotivism, which treat "ought" statements as expressions of attitude, and John Searle's 1964 argument that institutional facts such as promises carry normative force. Consensus on a resolution remains elusive.

English polymath: mathematician, physicist, astronomer, alchemist, theologian, historian, and inventor, a key figure in the Scientific Revolution.

His book [[Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica|*Principia*]] (1687) formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation and established [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]], the dominant scientific viewpoint for centuries until it was superseded by the theory of relativity. With it he derived Kepler's laws of planetary motion and accounted for tides, the trajectories of comets, and the precession of the equinoxes. His work on [[Optics|optics]], collected in *Opticks* (1704), rested on the observation that a prism separates white light into the colors of the visible spectrum; he also built the first reflecting telescope. He shares credit with the German mathematician [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]] for formulating infinitesimal [[Calculus|calculus]], though he developed it years earlier. His priority disputes, notably with Robert Hooke over gravitation and with Leibniz over calculus, were bitter.

Newton was a fellow of Trinity College and the second Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at the [[University of Cambridge]], appointed at the age of 26. A devout but heterodox Christian, he privately rejected the doctrine of the Trinity and refused to take holy orders in the Church of England. He devoted much time to [[Alchemy|alchemy]] and biblical chronology, but most of that work remained unpublished until long after his death.

He served as Member of Parliament for the University of Cambridge in 1689–1690 and 1701–1702 and was knighted by Queen Anne in 1705. He was Warden (1696–1699) and Master (1699–1727) of the Royal Mint, where he increased the accuracy and security of British coinage, and president of the Royal Society (1703–27). He was the first scientist buried in [[Westminster Abbey]].

Period of cultural and scientific flourishing in the history of Islam, dated from the 8th century to the 14th century. The institution attracted scholars from across the Muslim world to translate the classical knowledge of the known world into Arabic.

Activity flourished in other centers too, including Cairo and Umayyad Córdoba, capital of [[Al-Andalus]]. Works were translated from Greek, Syriac, Middle Persian, and Sanskrit, with Christians contributing as translators and physicians. The golden-age metaphor was applied in 19th-century writing on Islamic history and became frequent only in the second half of the 20th century. A dominant approach among scholars, the "decline theory," treats the period after the 11th century as an age of decline, a framing that has been challenged. Explanations for the end range from external shocks such as the Mongol sack to internal shifts toward theological orthodoxy, political fragmentation, and changing trade routes.

Piece of land, distinct from a [[Continent|continent]], completely surrounded by water. Islands occur in seas, lakes, and rivers, and no standard of size separates them from continents, which are defined geologically as the largest landmass of a tectonic plate.

Continental islands were split from a continent by [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]; oceanic islands were never part of one. Oceanic islands form through volcanic activity, grow into atolls from coral reefs, or build up from shoreline sediment as barrier islands. Many islands occur in [[Archipelago|archipelagos]]. Chains such as the [[Hawaiian Islands]] are thought to form as plates move over stationary volcanic hotspots, so that islands farther from the hotspot are older and more eroded; the oldest Hawaiian island is 25 million years old, and the youngest, Hawaii, is still active. New Zealand is what remains of a continent that shrank and sank; [[Zealandia]] has had 93% of its original surface area submerged.

Isolation shapes island life. Species reaching oceanic islands evolve in isolation, while continental islands share life with their parent continent but may diverge through natural selection. Islands cover about 6.7% of Earth's land surface yet support roughly 20% of terrestrial biodiversity and around 50% of threatened species. Habitat alteration and invasive species have caused many extinctions, such as the dodo's.

Up to 10% of the world's population lives on islands. European colonization put the majority of Pacific islands under European control, and decolonization left lasting effects related to industrialization, invasive species, nuclear weapons testing, and tourism. [[Climate change]] threatens islands: sea level rise could submerge the Maldives, the Marshall Islands, and Tuvalu completely, and more intense tropical cyclones damage infrastructure and habitats.

In 1508 he destroyed the tombs of the Abbasid caliphs, Abu Hanifa and Abdul Qadir Gilani, causing sectarian tensions in the [[Middle East]]. He was also a prolific poet, writing in a southern Turkic dialect under the pen name Khaṭāʾī.

ENTRY:
Albanian novelist, poet, essayist and playwright. Living under strict censorship in Albania, he used parable, myth, [[Fable|fable]], folk-tale and allegory to outwit Communist censors, who had banned three of his books. His first novel, *The General of the Dead Army*, made him famous internationally. In 1990 he defected to Paris to escape the Communist regime and its Sigurimi secret police. A Neustadt Prize juror called him the successor of [[Franz Kafka]], and some regard him as a universal voice against [[Totalitarianism|totalitarianism]]. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize in Literature 15 times and won the 2005 inaugural Man Booker International Prize. In 2023 he was granted citizenship of [[Kosovo]].
