Numeral system using ten as its base (radix). Its most common form is the [[Hindu–Arabic numeral system]], a positional system with ten digits, 0 to 9, which made very large numbers far easier to write and calculate with and was later extended to represent decimal fractions. A decimal separator, usually "." in English-speaking countries and "," in many others, marks the fractional part, as in 25.9703 or 3.1415. An infinite decimal represents a [[Rational number|rational number]] if and only if it repeats or has finitely many nonzero digits.

Process by which colonized territories gain independence or self-determination from imperial powers. Its meanings and applications are disputed; some scholars focus on independence movements and the collapse of global colonial empires.

Decolonization began in 1775 with the [[American Revolution]] against the [[British Empire]]. During the Napoleonic Wars the French colonial empire, the Spanish Empire, and Portugal faced the [[Haitian Revolution]], the [[Spanish American wars of independence]], and the independence of Brazil. A further wave followed World War I, another followed World War II, and a last came after the [[Cold War]] with the collapse of the Soviet Union, the independence of Palau, and the handovers of Hong Kong and Macau. Over 80 new states emerged after World War II through negotiated handovers, armed struggles such as the [[Algerian War]], and swift independences such as that of 17 African nations in 1960. Seventeen territories remain on the [[United Nations]] list of non-self-governing territories.

The UN holds that the right to self-determination is the core requirement and can be exercised with or without political independence; a 1960 General Assembly resolution characterized colonial foreign rule as a violation of human rights. Many scholars trace the ideological roots of independence movements to the Age of Enlightenment, though decolonial scholars have criticized its failure to extend liberty and dignity to Indigenous and subaltern peoples.

Early studies appeared in the 1960s and 1970s, notably *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) by [[Frantz Fanon]]. Decolonization often left arbitrary borders, resource-reliant economies, and weak institutions, which contributed to conflicts and uneven development.

ENTRY:
Process by which dead organic matter is broken down into simpler organic or inorganic substances, such as [[Carbon dioxide|carbon dioxide]], water, simple sugars and mineral salts. It is part of the nutrient cycle and recycles finite matter within the [[Biosphere]]. Abiotic decomposition involves chemical or physical processes such as hydrolysis, while biotic decomposition, or biodegradation, is the metabolic breakdown of materials by living organisms, typically [[Microorganism|microorganisms]]. Decomposers, or detritivores, include bacteria, fungi and animals such as [[Earthworm|earthworms]]. In animals, decay begins at death through autolysis, the breakdown of tissues by the body's own enzymes, and putrefaction by bacteria, which releases cadaverine and putrescine, the chief source of the [[Odor|odor]] of decaying tissue. Rates depend on temperature, moisture, oxygen and soil pH.

ENTRY:
Form of reasoning in which a conclusion follows logically from its premises. An inference is valid if the premises cannot be true while the conclusion is false, and an argument is sound if it is valid and all its premises are true. A classic example infers "Socrates is mortal" from the premises "all men are mortal" and "Socrates is a man." Deductive logic studies when an argument is valid, using rules of inference such as modus ponens and modus tollens; invalid deductive arguments are called formal fallacies. Unlike [[Inductive reasoning|inductive]] reasoning, deduction gives certainty within its premises but adds no information beyond them. Research in the [[Psychology|psychology]] of reasoning finds that people draw valid inferences more successfully from modus ponens than from modus tollens, and that concrete cases are easier than abstract ones. Natural deduction is a proof system built on self-evident rules of inference.

Supernatural being considered to have authority over some aspect of the universe or life, and often regarded as sacred and worthy of worship. English-speakers call a male deity a god and a female deity a goddess. There is no universally accepted consensus on what a deity is, and concepts vary considerably across cultures, from an infinite transcendent creator (God) to a finite entity or experience of special significance (god).

The English word derives from Old French *deité* and Latin *deitatem*, "divine nature," a form coined by [[Augustine of Hippo]] from *deus* ("god"). *Deus* shares a Proto-Indo-European root with Sanskrit *deva*, "shining one," Greek *dios*, "divine," and [[Zeus]]. The word "god" comes from Proto-Germanic *guthan*; one proposed origin is a root meaning "that which is invoked," another a root meaning "poured," referring to libations. Germanic cognates were originally neuter and became generally masculine after Christianization, whereas all ancient Indo-European cultures recognized both masculine and feminine deities.

Belief in at least one deity who may interact with the world is called [[Theism|theism]]. [[Monotheism]] accepts only one deity. [[Polytheism]] accepts many, usually assembled into a pantheon with accompanying rituals; the gods often represent forces of nature or ancestral principles. Monolatry accepts that many deities exist but holds that only one may validly be worshipped, and henotheism accepts several while regarding them as aspects of the same highest divine principle. Nontheistic religions deny any supreme eternal creator but may accept deities that live, die, and are reborn like other beings. Most monotheistic religions traditionally envision their god as omnipotent, omnipresent, omniscient, omnibenevolent, and eternal, but none of these qualities is essential to the definition of a deity. Monotheistic religions typically refer to their god in masculine terms, while other religions describe deities as male, female, hermaphroditic, or genderless.

[[Deism]] holds that a single deity created the universe but does not usually intervene in it, and was particularly popular among western intellectuals in the 18th and 19th centuries. [[Pantheism]] holds that the universe itself is God. Panentheism holds that divinity pervades the universe and also transcends it. Agnosticism holds that it is impossible to know whether any deity exists, and [[Atheism|atheism]] is the non-belief in any deity.

Many cultures, including the ancient Mesopotamians, Egyptians, Greeks, Romans, and Germanic peoples, personified natural phenomena. Some Avestan and Vedic deities were viewed as ethical concepts. The Upanishads of Hinduism characterize Atman (soul, self) as *deva*, asserting that the supreme principle (Brahman) is part of every living creature. Archaeologists once commonly interpreted prehistoric female figurines as a single primordial goddess, ancestor of goddesses such as Inanna and Aphrodite; this approach has been generally discredited.

ENTRY:
Confederacy of Greek city-states under [[Athens|Athenian]] leadership, founded in 478 BC to continue fighting the Persian Empire after the Greek victory at the Battle of Plataea. Its membership numbered between 150 and 330 states. The league functioned as a dual offensive and defensive alliance of autonomous states, similar to the rival Peloponnesian League led by [[Sparta]]. Its name derives from its meeting place on the island of Delos, where congresses were held in the sanctuary of the Temple of [[Apollo]]. By the mid-fifth century BC, Athens had established dominion over the league, which became known as the Athenian Empire, and its allies had lost much of their autonomy. In 454 BC, [[Pericles]] moved the league's treasury to Athens. The threat the league posed to Spartan hegemony helped prompt the [[Peloponnesian War]] in 431 BC. The league was dissolved at the war's end in 404 BC under Lysander, and Athens partly revived it as the Second Athenian League.

ENTRY:
Rapid onset of confusion caused by withdrawal from alcohol. The name is literally "trembling delirium." It typically begins 48–72 hours after the last use of alcohol and lasts 1–8 days. Symptoms include nightmares, confusion, disorientation, heavy sweating, elevated heart rate and blood pressure, and [[Hallucination|hallucinations]]. It can be fatal, especially without treatment, from very high body temperature, seizures, [[Respiratory failure|respiratory failure]], or [[Arrhythmia|cardiac arrhythmias]]. Aggressive treatment with [[Benzodiazepine|benzodiazepines]] improves outcomes.

Fixed belief resistant to conflicting evidence. Unlike beliefs based on false or incomplete information, [[Hallucination|hallucinations]], or other misleading perceptions, delusions are not revised when evidence contradicts them. Delusions occur in many physical and mental disorders and are of particular diagnostic importance in psychotic disorders, including [[Schizophrenia|schizophrenia]], paraphrenia, manic episodes of [[Bipolar disorder|bipolar disorder]], and psychotic depression. They are commonly divided into bizarre delusions, which are clearly implausible, and non-bizarre delusions, which are false but technically possible. ENTRY:
Syndrome of general decline in cognitive processes that impairs the ability to perform everyday activities, often associated with neurodegenerative diseases. It typically involves problems with memory, thinking, behavior, and motor control. Common symptoms also include emotional problems, difficulties with language, and decreased motivation. Dementia is incurable and progressive, ranging from mild to major severity, and is distinct from age-related decline in cognition and memory.

[[Alzheimer's disease]] is the most common cause, contributing to an estimated 60–70% of cases, followed by vascular dementia from impaired blood flow to the brain. Dementia can also be caused by brain injuries and [[Stroke|stroke]]. Subtypes are named for the underlying disorder, including [[Parkinson's disease]] dementia, [[Huntington's disease]] dementia, HIV dementia, frontotemporal dementia, dementia with Lewy bodies, and [[Prion|prion]] diseases.

Symptoms fall into cognitive, neuropsychiatric, and motor groups. Cognitive symptoms typically involve memory plus another region such as language, attention, problem solving, or perception. Neuropsychiatric symptoms affect more than 90% of cases at different stages and include agitation, disinhibition, depression, [[Hallucination|hallucinations]], delusions, apathy, and personality change.

Diagnosis is usually based on the history of the illness and cognitive testing with imaging. [[Blood test|Blood tests]] rule out reversible causes such as [[Hypothyroidism|hypothyroidism]], and fluid biomarkers and PET scans can identify Alzheimer's disease. Aging is the greatest risk factor, but dementia is not a normal part of aging.obesity]], hypertension, and diabetes.|obesity]], hypertension, and diabetes.

In 2021 approximately 57 million people worldwide were affected, and 10 million new cases are reported every year as of 2025. There is no known cure. Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil are used in some subtypes and may help in mild to moderate stages, though the overall benefit may be minor.

[[Twelve Olympians|Olympian]] goddess of the [[Harvest|harvest]] and [[Agriculture|agriculture]] in ancient Greek religion. She presided over [[Crop|crops]], grain, food, and the fertility of the earth, and she was also linked with health, birth, marriage, and the underworld. Her daughter Persephone was abducted by [[Hades]], and Demeter's grief brought famine until [[Zeus]] ordered Hades to return her. Because Persephone had eaten food from the underworld, she divided each year between her mother and Hades, which explains the seasons. Demeter and Persephone were central to the Eleusinian Mysteries, and Romans identified her with Ceres.
