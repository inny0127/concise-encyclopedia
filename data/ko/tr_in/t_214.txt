Island and overseas region of [[France]] in the [[Lesser Antilles]], eastern Caribbean Sea (pop., 2024: 349,925). It covers 1,128 sq km (436 sq mi). Martinique is an outermost region of the European Union but is not part of the [[Schengen Area]] or the EU customs union, and it uses the [[Euro|euro]]. French is the sole official language. Spain ceded the island to France in 1635, after 133 years of Spanish rule; French settlers under Pierre Belain d'Esnambuc landed that year and claimed it for [[Louis XIII]]. Plantation agriculture relied on enslaved Africans until emancipation in 1848. The 1902 eruption of [[Mount Pelée]] destroyed Saint-Pierre and killed approximately 30,000 people. Bananas, rum and tourism are major economic activities.

Political philosophy and method of socioeconomic analysis based on historical materialism, developed by [[Karl Marx]] and [[Friedrich Engels]] in the 19th century. Marxism treats class struggle as the central driving force of historical change and holds that a society's economic mode of production shapes its social, political and intellectual life. It argues that the [[Bourgeoisie|bourgeoisie]], who own the means of production, exploit the [[Working class|working class]], and that this leads to alienation, periodic economic crises and class conflict. Marx theorized that workers' revolutions would overthrow [[Capitalism|capitalism]] and establish [[Socialism|socialism]], a transition toward a classless and stateless society. The most prominent 20th-century form was [[Marxism–Leninism]], the official ideology of the Soviet Union. Other traditions, including Western Marxism and Marxist feminism, have often been critical of state socialism. Marxist concepts such as alienation and class struggle have become integral to the social sciences and humanities.

A person who builds masonry is called a mason or bricklayer, both classified as [[Construction|construction]] trades.

Masonry is one of the oldest building crafts, predating written history; Egyptian pyramids, Roman aqueducts, and medieval cathedrals are examples. Early structures relied on the weight of the masonry itself for stability against lateral movement. Since the mid-20th century, masonry has often included [[Steel|steel]]-reinforced elements to carry the tension in modern thin, light, tall building systems. Masonry is durable, adds thermal mass, is non-combustible, and resists debris from hurricanes and tornadoes. Mass can also be understood as a measure of the energy content of a body (E = mc²), and experimentally as a measure of [[Inertia|inertia]], the resistance to acceleration when a net force is applied.

Three phenomena can be used to measure mass: inertial mass (resistance to acceleration, F = ma), active gravitational mass (the strength of the gravitational field an object generates), and passive gravitational mass (the force exerted on an object in a known field). Experiments since the 17th century have shown inertial and gravitational mass to be identical, and since 1915 this has been incorporated in the [[Equivalence principle|equivalence principle]] of [[General relativity|general relativity]]. Mass differs from weight, which is a force: an object on the [[Moon|Moon]] weighs less than on Earth but has the same mass. In the [[Standard Model]], the mass of elementary particles is believed to result from their coupling with the [[Higgs boson]], in the Brout–Englert–Higgs mechanism.

The kilogram was first defined in 1795 as the mass of one cubic decimetre of water at the melting point of ice. In 1889 it was redefined as the platinum–iridium International Prototype of the Kilogram, whose mass and that of its national copies were found to drift over time. The redefinition that took effect on May 20, 2019, uses only the speed of light, the caesium hyperfine frequency, the Planck constant, and the elementary charge. Non-SI units still widely used include the tonne (1000 kg) and the dalton, approximately 1.66×10⁻²⁷ kg, convenient for atoms and molecules. Astronomers compare large masses with the [[Solar mass|solar mass]], approximately 1.99×10³⁰ kg.

Forms of media that reach large audiences through mass communication, including broadcast, digital, print, social, and streaming media, [[Advertising|advertising]], and events. Mass media encompasses news, advocacy, entertainment, and public service announcements, and intersects with the study of marketing, [[Propaganda|propaganda]], public relations, political communication, [[Journalism|journalism]], and technology. Its influence has also been analyzed from the standpoint of anthropology, economics, history, law, philosophy, psychology, and sociology. It is often controlled by media conglomerates and may be susceptible to media capture.

In the late 20th century mass media was classified into eight industries: books, the Internet, magazines, movies, newspapers, radio, recordings, and television. By the early 2000s a classification of "seven mass media" came into use: print (late 15th century), recordings (late 19th century), cinema (c. 1900), radio (c. 1910), television (c. 1950), the Internet (c. 1990), and mobile phones (c. 2000). The Internet and mobile phones are often called digital media, and radio and television broadcast media. The sociologist John Thompson identified mass communication as a "one-to-many" form in which products are mass-produced and disseminated to large audiences. The term is sometimes used as a synonym for mainstream media, though mass media may include alternative outlets that use mass communication technology.

The first dated printed book, the [[Diamond Sutra]], was printed in China in 868 AD, and movable type made of clay was invented there in 1041. [[Johannes Gutenberg|Gutenberg]] printed the Latin Bible with movable type in 1453, enabling books and newspapers on a far larger scale, though books stayed too expensive to be a mass medium for at least a century. Newspapers developed from c. 1612, with the first English example in 1620, and reached a mass audience in the 19th century; the first high-circulation papers, such as [[The Times]], arose in London in the early 1800s, made possible by the high-speed rotary steam printing press and railroads. Until after [[World War II]] the notion of mass media was generally restricted to print; radio, television, and video then followed, and television became widely used in the U.S. and England in the late 1940s.

Media consolidation has been extensive. Between 1985 and 2018 about 76,720 deals were announced in the media industry, with an overall value of around US$5,634 billion, in three major waves (2000, 2007, and 2015). The most active year by number was 2007, with around 3,808 deals. The largest deal in history was the acquisition of Time Warner by [[AOL]] Inc. for US$164,746.86 million. In democratic societies, media can inform the electorate about government and corporate entities, while some consider the concentration of media ownership a threat to democracy. In 1997 J. R. Finnegan Jr. and K. Viswanath identified theories of media effects, including the knowledge gap and agenda setting.
