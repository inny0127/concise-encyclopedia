Thermal electromagnetic radiation emitted by an idealized black body, an object that absorbs all radiation falling on it. A black body's emission is a continuous spectrum that depends only on its temperature, described by [[Planck's law]]. The term *black body* was introduced by [[Gustav Kirchhoff]] in 1860. At room temperature a black body radiates mostly in the [[Infrared|infrared]]; at about 500 °C it begins to glow dull red, and at higher temperatures it emits more orange, yellow, green and blue light. ENTRY:
Astronomical body so compact that its gravity prevents anything, including light, from escaping.

[[Albert Einstein]]'s theory of [[General relativity|general relativity]], which describes gravitation as the curvature of [[Spacetime|spacetime]], predicts that any sufficiently compact mass will form a black hole. The boundary of no escape is the [[Event horizon|event horizon]]; crossing it traps an object but produces no locally detectable change. General relativity also predicts a central singularity, where the curvature of spacetime is infinite.

The idea of a body from which light could not escape was proposed in the late 18th century by John Michell, whose letter of 1784 considered a star of the same density but 500 times the radius of the Sun, and independently by Pierre-Simon Laplace in 1796. The first widely accepted black hole was [[Cygnus X-1]], proposed through studies between 1971 and 1974.

Black holes typically form when massive stars collapse at the end of their life cycle, often in a supernova, and can grow by absorbing surrounding mass. Supermassive black holes of millions to billions of solar masses exist at the centers of most galaxies; the radio source [[Sagittarius A*]] in the Milky Way contains one of about 4.3 million solar masses.

Black holes are detected through their effects on nearby matter and light. Infalling plasma forms a glowing accretion disk, which in extreme cases powers a quasar. Merging black holes emit gravitational waves, and the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadows of M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022. Quantum field theory in curved spacetime predicts that event horizons emit [[Hawking radiation]], causing very slow mass loss.

ENTRY:
Specialized connective tissue and body fluid of the circulatory system in humans and other vertebrates, delivering nutrients and oxygen to cells and carrying metabolic waste away from them.

Blood is composed of blood cells suspended in plasma. Plasma makes up about 55% of blood, is straw-yellow, and is 92% water by volume, with proteins, glucose, mineral ions, and hormones making up the rest. By volume, red blood cells account for about 45% of whole blood and white cells about 0.7%. In humans, blood accounts for about 7% of body weight, with an average adult volume of roughly 5 liters (11 US pt).

The most abundant cells are red blood cells (erythrocytes), numbering 4.7 to 6.1 million per microliter in men and 4.2 to 5.4 million in women. They contain [[Hemoglobin|hemoglobin]], which binds oxygen reversibly and transports it. White blood cells (leukocytes), numbering 4,000–11,000 per microliter, are part of the [[Immune system|immune system]], destroying aberrant cells and attacking pathogens. Platelets (thrombocytes), numbering 200,000–500,000 per microliter, take part in clotting.

Blood is pumped through the vessels by the heart. Arterial blood carries oxygen from inhaled air to the tissues, and venous blood carries carbon dioxide to the [[Lung|lungs]] to be exhaled. Blood is bright red when its hemoglobin is oxygenated and dark red when deoxygenated. Its other functions include removing waste such as urea and lactic acid, detecting foreign material through antibodies, clotting after vessel injury, transporting hormones, and regulating core body temperature.

Human blood is classified into the ABO groups (A, B, AB, O) and Rh factor (positive or negative), which matter for safe transfusions.
