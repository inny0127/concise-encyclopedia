Scottish Franciscan friar, philosopher and theologian of the late Middle Ages. He was given the scholastic title *Doctor Subtilis* ("the subtle Doctor"). His best-known doctrines include the "univocity of being," the view that existence is the most abstract concept, applicable to everything that exists; the formal distinction; and haecceity, the property that makes each thing an individual. Scotus is traditionally said to have studied at [[Oxford]], and he lectured at Paris until he was expelled for siding with [[Pope Boniface VIII]] against [[Philip IV of France|Philip IV]] over the taxation of church property. He was beatified by [[Pope John Paul II]] in 1993.

Fine particles of solid matter, suspended in the air or settled on surfaces. On Earth, atmospheric dust comes mainly from soil lifted by wind, from volcanic eruptions and from pollution. When fine particles remain suspended for an extended time, dust is considered an [[Aerosol]]. Dust in homes and offices is composed of about 20–50% dead skin [[Cell (biology)|cells]], along with pollen, human hair, animal fur, textile and paper fibers, and minerals from outdoor soil. Total suspended dust concentrations range from 0.01 to 10,000 mg/m³ in outdoor settings such as residential, industrial and commercial areas, highways and quarries.

ENTRY:
Meteorological phenomenon in which strong wind lifts loose sand and dirt from dry surfaces, common in arid and semi-arid regions. Also called a sandstorm, a dust storm can reduce visibility, disrupt transportation and pose serious health risks. Gust fronts from thunderstorms can trigger storms, as can dry cold fronts, which were common during the [[Dust Bowl]] years. Drought, poor farming and grazing practices, and wildfires also contribute. Fine particles travel by saltation and suspension, and repeated storms can reduce agricultural productivity and contribute to desertification. The arid regions of [[North Africa]], the [[Middle East]], [[Central Asia]] and [[China]] are the main terrestrial sources of airborne dust, and it has been argued that poor management of drylands, such as neglecting the fallow system, is increasing the size and frequency of storms.

Chartered Dutch trading company, among the first joint-stock companies in the world. Established on 20 March 1602 by the States General of the Netherlands, it held a 21-year monopoly on trade in Asia and had quasi-governmental powers, including the ability to wage war and negotiate treaties. Shares could be bought and sold in open-air secondary markets, one of which became the [[Amsterdam]] Stock Exchange. Mathematical model of the time dependence of a point in an ambient space, such as in a parametric curve. Examples include the swinging of a clock [[Pendulum|pendulum]], the flow of water in a pipe and the number of fish in a lake each spring. A dynamical system has a state, often given by a tuple of [[Real number|real numbers]] or by a vector in a manifold, and an evolution rule that describes which future states follow from the current state. The rule is often deterministic, though some systems are stochastic. The most general definition unifies concepts such as [[Ordinary differential equation|ordinary differential equations]] and ergodic theory. The study of dynamical systems is the focus of [[Dynamical systems theory]], and dynamical systems are a fundamental part of [[Chaos theory]].

ENTRY:
Area of mathematics describing the long-term qualitative behavior of complex [[Dynamical system|dynamical systems]], usually by employing differential equations or difference equations. When differential equations are used, the theory is called continuous dynamical systems; when difference equations are used, it is called discrete. From a physical point of view, continuous dynamical systems generalize [[Classical mechanics]], in which the equations of motion are postulated directly and need not derive from a least action principle. The field studies systems such as planetary orbits, electronic circuits, and systems in biology and economics. Its central goals include finding the fixed points, or steady states, of a system and determining whether long-term behavior depends on initial conditions. Much modern research focuses on chaotic systems, and the branch that investigates chaos is called [[Chaos theory]].

Explosive made of [[Nitroglycerin|nitroglycerin]], sorbents such as powdered shells or clay, and stabilizers. It was the first safely manageable explosive stronger than traditional black powder-based explosives, and it was widely adopted as a safer and more robust alternative. Today it remains in use for controlled blasting in mining and demolition, though it has largely been supplanted by safer alternatives such as [[Ammonium nitrate|ammonium nitrate-based explosives]].

It used 17,468 [[Vacuum tube|vacuum tubes]], 7,200 crystal diodes, 1,500 relays, 70,000 resistors, and 10,000 capacitors, and it could perform up to 5,000 additions or subtractions per second. It was programmed with plugboards, switches, and cables rather than stored programs. Its speed was on the order of one thousand times that of electro-mechanical machines. ENIAC was dedicated in February 1946, accepted by the U.S. Army Ordnance Corps in July 1946, and operated at Aberdeen Proving Ground in [[Maryland]] until 1955.

ENTRY:
Mathematical constant that is the base of the natural logarithm and the exponential function, approximately 2.718281828459045. It plays important and recurring roles across mathematics, comparable in significance to π or the imaginary unit i. Like π, it is irrational (not a ratio of integers) and transcendental (not a root of any non-zero polynomial with rational coefficients); [[Leonhard Euler]] is credited with proving irrationality in 1737 and Charles Hermite transcendence in 1873. It is sometimes called Euler's number.

The constant is the limit of (1 + 1/n)ⁿ as n approaches infinity, the sum of the infinite series 1/0! + 1/1! + 1/2! + 1/3! + …, and the unique positive number a such that the graph of y = aˣ has a slope of 1 at x = 0. It is the base of the [[Natural logarithm|natural logarithm]], and the function eˣ equals its own derivative.

The first calculations involving it appeared in 1618 in a table in an appendix to a work on logarithms by John Napier; the table listed natural logarithms without recognizing the base. In 1661 [[Christiaan Huygens]] calculated a quantity that is, in retrospect, the base-10 logarithm of e. [[Jacob Bernoulli]] introduced the constant itself in 1683 while studying continuous compounding of interest: $1.00 at 100% annual interest becomes $2.00 if credited once a year, $2.25 twice, and $2.718281828… with continuous compounding. Euler began using e in 1727 or 1728, and it first appeared in print in his *Mechanica* (1736); why he chose the letter is unknown.

The constant also arises in probability: if a gambler plays n times a slot machine that pays out with probability one in n, the probability of losing every bet approaches 1/e, about 36.79%, as n grows.
