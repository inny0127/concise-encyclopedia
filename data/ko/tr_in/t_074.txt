Branch of [[Historical linguistics|historical linguistics]] concerned with comparing languages to establish their historical relatedness. Genetic relatedness implies a common origin, or proto-language. Comparative linguists construct language families and reconstruct proto-languages, prefixing an asterisk to any form not found in surviving texts. The fundamental technique compares phonological and [[Morphology (linguistics)|morphological]] systems, [[Syntax|syntax]] and the lexicon, using the comparative method, which developed through the 19th century. The method is less effective for distant relationships. Statistical approaches such as lexicostatistics compare basic vocabulary to determine relatedness but cannot reconstruct a proto-language.

ENTRY:
Instrument showing the [[Cardinal direction|cardinal directions]], used for [[Navigation|navigation]] and geographic orientation. It typically consists of a magnetized needle, or another element such as a compass card, that pivots to align itself with magnetic north. Compasses often show angles in degrees: north is 0°, east 90°, south 180°, and west 270°.

The magnetic compass works because the needle aligns itself with the horizontal component of [[Earth's magnetic field]], which exerts a torque on it, pulling its north end approximately toward Earth's North magnetic pole. The needle turns on a low-friction pivot, in better compasses a jewel bearing, and settles after a few seconds. Magnetic north differs from true north by an angle called magnetic declination, which varies with location and changes slowly over time, so maps usually give the local value. Modern compasses commonly seal the needle in a liquid-filled capsule, which damps oscillation.

One of the earliest known references to the magnetic properties of lodestone was made by the 6th-century BC Greek philosopher [[Thales of Miletus]]. The magnetic compass was first invented in China as a device for divination, as early as the [[Han dynasty]] (since c. 206 BC). The first compasses used for navigation appeared in China by 1088 during the [[Song dynasty]], as described by [[Shen Kuo]]; they used iron needles magnetized by striking them with a lodestone. The first recorded use in [[Western Europe]] occurred around 1190 and in the Islamic world in the 13th century. Dry compasses appeared around 1300, and the liquid-filled compass supplanted them in the early 20th century.

Interaction between organisms or species that share a limited resource, such as food, water or territory. Competition lowers the fitness of both participants, because each one's use of the resource reduces the amount left for the other. It affects [[Ecosystem|community]] structure, species diversity and [[Population (biology)|population]] dynamics. There are three mechanisms: interference, exploitation and apparent competition, the last involving a shared predator rather than a shared resource. Under the competitive exclusion principle, less suited species must adapt or die out, although exclusion is rarely found in natural ecosystems. ENTRY:
Number of the form a + bi, where a and b are [[Real number|real numbers]] and i is the [[Imaginary unit|imaginary unit]], satisfying i² = −1. The number a is the real part and b the imaginary part. The set of complex numbers is denoted ℂ.

Complex numbers extend the real numbers and support addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division under the same associative, commutative, and distributive laws, which makes them a [[Field (mathematics)|field]]. They are added by adding real and imaginary parts separately, and multiplied using the distributive law and i² = −1: (a + bi)(c + di) = (ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i. In electromagnetism and electrical engineering, j is often written instead of i, because i commonly denotes electric current.

Each complex number corresponds to a point (a, b) in the complex plane, or Argand diagram, with the real part on the horizontal axis. Addition then corresponds to translation of the plane, and multiplication to scaling and rotation. Complex numbers give solutions to all non-constant polynomial equations, a result known as the [[Fundamental theorem of algebra]].

Because the square of any nonzero real number is positive, square roots of negative numbers were historically considered impossible. In the 16th century such roots proved helpful in solving [[Cubic equation|cubic equations]]; [[Gerolamo Cardano]] encountered them while deriving formulas for these equations, though he regarded them with suspicion. They were named imaginary numbers in the 17th century. Acceptance grew through the work of [[Leonhard Euler]] and later [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]]. Complex numbers model waves and quantum states in physics and are used in analyzing alternating-current circuits, signal processing, and fluid dynamics.

Long-bodied decapods such as [[Shrimp]], prawns, [[Crayfish]] and [[Lobster]] alone have reflecting superposition eyes, which use corner mirrors instead of lenses.

Study of computation, information, and automation and their associated phenomena. It spans theoretical disciplines, such as algorithms, the theory of computation, and information theory, and applied disciplines, including the design and implementation of hardware and software. Its fundamental concern is determining what can and cannot be automated, and the Turing Award is generally recognized as its highest distinction.

Algorithms and data structures are central to the field. The theory of computation concerns abstract models of computation and the classes of problems they can solve. [[Cryptography]] and computer security study secure communication and the prevention of vulnerabilities. Human–computer interaction examines the interfaces between people and machines, and software engineering the principles of developing software. [[Artificial intelligence]] and machine learning aim to synthesize processes such as problem-solving, decision-making, and learning found in humans and animals.

The foundations predate the digital computer. Wilhelm Schickard built the first working mechanical calculator in 1623, and in 1673 Gottfried Leibniz demonstrated the Stepped Reckoner. [[Charles Babbage]] began designing the Difference Engine in 1822 and the programmable Analytical Engine in 1834, adopting punched cards derived from the Jacquard loom. In 1843 [[Ada Lovelace]] wrote, in notes to a translated article on the Analytical Engine, an algorithm to compute the Bernoulli numbers, considered the first published algorithm tailored for a computer. In 1936 [[Alan Turing]] introduced the universal Turing machine, formalizing the principles of computation. During the 1940s machines such as the Atanasoff–Berry computer and ENIAC led the word computer to refer to machines rather than people, and the field broadened to study computation in general.

Fundamental unit of cognition that classifies entities and encodes shared features. Concepts make it possible to form and combine ideas, draw [[Inference|inferences]] and refer to external objects. They act as the meanings of words and play a central role in cognitive processes including [[Perception|perception]], [[Memory|memory]] and reasoning.
