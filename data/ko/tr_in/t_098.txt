[[Vertebrate]] organ of [[Hearing|hearing]] and, in mammals, of balance through the vestibular system. In humans it has three parts. The outer ear consists of the auricle, the visible outer part, and the ear canal, which stretches for about 2.5 cm and ends at the eardrum; skin in the canal produces earwax, which migrates outward and so keeps the canal clean. The middle ear is an air-filled tympanic cavity containing the three ossicles, the malleus (hammer), incus (anvil), and stapes (stirrup), which transmit vibrations from the eardrum to the inner ear and amplify sound by nearly 15–20 times. The stapes is the smallest named bone in the body. The middle ear connects to the throat at the nasopharynx through the Eustachian tube.

The inner ear sits in the bony labyrinth. The semicircular canals enable balance and eye tracking when moving, the utricle and saccule enable balance when stationary, and the cochlea enables hearing. Its sensitivity spans about 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz in young adults. Because the two ears are arranged symmetrically, the brain can localize [[Sound|sound]] by comparing arrival times and intensities from each ear.

The ear develops in the early [[Embryo|embryo]] from the first pharyngeal pouch and six small swellings called otic placodes, derived from the ectoderm. Human ears have been adorned with earrings and other jewelry in numerous cultures for thousands of years and have been subjected to surgical and cosmetic alterations.

Historical period, c. 1500–c. 1800, with divisions based primarily on the history of Europe and the broader concept of modernity. No exact date marks its beginning or end, and its extent varies with the area of history studied. It is generally considered to have started at the beginning of the 16th or 17th century and to have ended in the 18th or 19th. In a European context it follows the [[Middle Ages]] and precedes the advent of modernity, though there is no universal agreement on these boundaries. Proposed starting points include the start of the [[Renaissance]], the printing revolution of the 1440s, the Fall of Constantinople in 1453, the [[Age of Discovery]], and the start of the [[Reformation]]. The transition into the [[Late modern period]] in the late 18th century was marked by the Industrial Revolution, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution, along with Napoleon's rise to power.

The concept grew from the traditional European division into ancient, medieval, and modern periods, made since the time of Petrarch. The term was first proposed to denote a distinct period by the medieval historian Lynn Thorndike in his 1926 work *A Short History of Civilization*; it spread through economic history in the 1940s and 1950s and became widely known among scholars during the 1990s. Attempts to apply it beyond Europe began by the 1980s, with scholars using it for societies such as Tokugawa Japan. Historian Jerry H. Bentley defined the early modern world as the era of about 1500 to 1800 in which cross-cultural interactions increasingly linked peoples throughout the world. He identified the connection of the world by sea, global biological exchanges, and the creation of an "early capitalist global economy" as its three main processes. Some historians have criticized the periodization as Eurocentric and as underemphasizing contemporaneous developments in non-Western regions, such as the Mughal and Ottoman empires.

Feudalism declined in Europe, and religious unity under the Roman Catholic Church ended. The Reformation provoked a backlash that expanded the Inquisition and led to the European wars of religion, including the Thirty Years' War (1618–48), which ended with the Peace of Westphalia. European colonization of the Americas began, and with it the [[Atlantic slave trade]]. The period also saw the Commercial Revolution, the [[Scientific Revolution]], the Golden Age of Piracy, and the rise of mercantilism. Third planet from the [[Sun]] and the only astronomical object known to harbor life, at a mean distance of 149.6 million km. Earth is rounded into an ellipsoid with a circumference of about 40,000 km and an equatorial diameter of 12,756 km. Collective term for the fields of natural science related to planet Earth, also called geoscience. It deals with the physical, chemical, and biological constitution of, and linkages among, Earth's four spheres: the biosphere, hydrosphere/cryosphere, atmosphere, and geosphere (lithosphere). It can be generalized into planetary science.

[[Geology]] is broadly the study of Earth's structure, substance, and processes, largely of the lithosphere. Its subfields include geochemistry, geophysics, paleontology, geomorphology, structural geology, mineralogy, and petrology. The mantle beneath the crust is heated by the radioactive decay of heavy elements, and its convection moves the lithospheric plates, a process known as [[Plate tectonics|plate tectonics]]. Plate boundaries are divergent, convergent, or transform. [[Earthquake|Earthquakes]] result from plate movement, often near convergent boundaries, and [[Volcano|volcanoes]] result primarily from the melting of subducted crust. In 1906, seismic wave analysis revealed Earth's molten core.

Atmospheric science developed in the late 19th century as a means to forecast weather through [[Meteorology|meteorology]]; atmospheric chemistry developed in the 20th century to measure air pollution and expanded in the 1970s in response to acid rain. The atmosphere has five layers, with 75% of its mass in the lowest, the troposphere, and consists of about 78.0% nitrogen, 20.9% oxygen, and 0.92% argon. Water vapor and CO₂ hold the Sun's energy through the greenhouse effect, making the surface warm enough for liquid water and life. The magnetic field, created by internal motions of the core, produces the magnetosphere, which protects the atmosphere from the solar wind.

[[Hydrology]] studies the hydrosphere and the movement of water, with subdisciplines including [[Oceanography|oceanography]], hydrogeology, ecohydrology, and glaciology. The earliest exploitation of groundwater dates back to 3000 BC, and hydrogeology as a science was developed beginning in the 17th century.

Shaking of the Earth's surface resulting from a sudden release of energy in the [[Lithosphere|lithosphere]] that creates seismic waves. Also called a quake, tremor, or temblor, an earthquake ranges from events too weak to be felt to those violent enough to destroy entire cities. The initial point of rupture is the hypocenter, or focus; the point at ground level directly above it is the epicenter.

Most earthquakes are caused by the rupture of geological [[Fault (geology)|faults]]; volcanic activity, landslides, mine blasts, and nuclear tests can also produce them, and human activities such as mining and fluid injection can induce smaller ones. Under the elastic-rebound theory, the sides of a fault lock at irregularities, strain builds as the plates keep moving, and the stored energy is released suddenly when the stress breaks through. Over 90% of earthquakes occur in the circum-Pacific seismic belt or along other convergent and transform margins.

Size is measured on the moment magnitude scale, which supersedes the [[Richter scale]] for larger events; shaking intensity is assessed with scales such as the [[Modified Mercalli intensity scale]]. Effects include ground shaking, soil liquefaction, and landslides, and an offshore event may displace the seabed enough to cause a [[Tsunami|tsunami]].

The [[1960 Valdivia earthquake|1960 earthquake]] in Chile, at 9.5 magnitude, is the largest measured on a seismograph. The [[1976 Tangshan earthquake]] in China, which killed between 240,000 and 655,000 people, was the deadliest of the 20th century. The [[1556 Shaanxi earthquake]] killed more than 100,000 people, many in collapsing hillside dwellings. Precise prediction remains elusive; risk reduction relies on preparedness, seismic retrofitting, and earthquake engineering.
