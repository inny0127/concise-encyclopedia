ENTRY:
Heavily armed, highly organized force primarily intended for warfare, also called the armed forces. A military is typically authorized and maintained by a [[Sovereign state]], and its members are identifiable by a distinct uniform. It may comprise one or more branches, such as an [[Army]], [[Navy]], [[Air force]], space force, marines, or coast guard. Its main task is usually defined as defending the state and its interests against external armed threats. In technical usage, a distinction is sometimes made in which a country's armed forces may also include paramilitary forces such as armed police.

Beyond warfare, militaries may be employed in other sanctioned and non-sanctioned functions within the state, including internal security, crowd control, emergency services, reconstruction, and ceremonies. A nation's military may function as a discrete social [[Subculture]], with dedicated infrastructure such as housing, schools, utilities, logistics, hospitals, legal services, food production, finance, and banking.

The profession of soldiering is older than recorded history. Images from [[Classical antiquity]] portray the feats of military leaders, and the Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC features in bas-relief monuments from the reign of Ramses II. The first emperor of a unified China, [[Qin Shi Huang]], created the [[Terracotta Army]] to represent his military might.

Militaries evolved from ad hoc tribal groups into structured organizations by antiquity, and professional standing armies emerged prominently in Europe from the 16th to 18th centuries. Core functions today include deterring potential aggressors, projecting power, and achieving superiority in battle. One analysis of engagements from 1600 to 1973 found that numerical advantage, leadership quality, and unit cohesion were decisive, more so than technology alone. Recurring controversies include coups in unstable states and debates over procurement inefficiencies.

ENTRY:
Highly varied group of small-seeded grasses grown as a staple [[Cereal|cereal]] crop. Most millets belong to the tribe Paniceae of the [[Poaceae|grass family]], and they are used both as food for humans and as fodder for [[Livestock|livestock]]. Millets are highly tolerant of drought and have a short growing season under hot, dry conditions, which suits the semi-arid tropics of Asia and Africa, especially India, Mali, Nigeria and Niger. They account for 97% of agricultural output in developing countries. Pearl millet is the most commonly cultivated millet, although the term sometimes includes the widely cultivated [[Sorghum|sorghum]]. Humans may have consumed millets for about 7,000 years, and they may have had "a pivotal role in the rise of multi-crop agriculture and settled farming societies."

ENTRY:
Arthropod of the class Diplopoda, with two pairs of jointed legs on most body segments. The name comes from [[Latin]] *mille*, meaning thousand, and *pes*, meaning foot, but no species was known to have 1,000 or more legs until the 2020 discovery of *Eumillipes persephone*, which can have over 1,300. Each double-legged segment results from two fused segments. There are about 12,000 named species in 16 orders, making millipedes the largest class of [[Myriapoda]]. Most are slow-moving detritivores that feed on decaying leaves and other dead plant matter. Most defend themselves with chemicals secreted from body pores, and many curl into a tight coil. Reproduction in most species is carried out by modified male legs called gonopods. First appearing in the [[Silurian]] period, millipedes are among the oldest known land animals. Unlike the faster, venomous and carnivorous [[Centipede|centipedes]], they are generally harmless to humans.

American economist and statistician. Among the intellectual leaders of the Chicago school of economics, he challenged Keynesian theory and promoted monetarism, arguing that a steady, small expansion of the money supply was preferable to rapid and unexpected changes.
