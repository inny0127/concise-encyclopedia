ENTRY:
Communication by representatives of states, intergovernmental institutions, or non-governmental institutions intended to influence events in the international system. It is the main instrument of foreign policy, and international treaties, agreements, and alliances are usually the result of diplomatic negotiations.

The term derives from the 18th-century French *diplomate*, based on the ancient Greek *diplōma*, roughly "an object folded in two," a folded document conferring an official privilege. Some scholars have criticized this account, pointing to the term's origin in the political context of the [[French Revolution]].

Some of the earliest known diplomatic records are the Amarna letters, exchanged in the 14th century BC between the pharaohs of Egypt and rulers of [[Canaan]]. After the Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC, the pharaoh and the ruler of the Hittite Empire concluded one of the first known international peace treaties. Ancient Greek city-states sent envoys for specific issues but kept no regular resident representatives. The Hellenistic states that succeeded [[Alexander the Great]], such as the [[Ptolemaic Kingdom]] and the [[Seleucid Empire]], often negotiated peace treaties through marriage alliances. In East Asia, rival states of the era of the strategist [[Sun Tzu]] relied on diplomacy to establish allies, barter land, and sign peace treaties.

Relations with the [[Ottoman Empire]] were particularly important to Italian states such as Genoa and Venice.

Modern diplomatic practice originated largely from 17th-century European customs. Beginning in the early 20th century, diplomacy became professionalized. The 1961 Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, ratified by most sovereign states, provides a framework for diplomatic procedures and conduct. Most diplomacy is now conducted by accredited officials, such as envoys and ambassadors, through [[Diplomatic mission|diplomatic missions]], most commonly consulates and embassies.

ENTRY:
[[Electromagnetism|Electromagnetic]] phenomenon in which equal and opposite charges or magnetic poles are separated by a small distance. An electric dipole, typically in atoms and molecules, is modeled as two equal and opposite [[Electric charge|charges]] separated by distance *d*, with dipole moment *p* = *qd* pointing from the negative to the positive charge. A magnetic dipole, such as an atom or electron, is modeled as a small current loop, with a moment equal to the current times the loop's area. Dipoles experience torques in external [[Electric field|electric]] and [[Magnetic field|magnetic]] fields that tend to align them with the field. In polar molecules such as water, the molecular dipole moment is measured in debyes (1 D ≈ 3.336 × 10⁻³⁰ C·m) and influences boiling points and solubility.

ENTRY:
Experience of any condition that makes it more difficult for a person to do certain activities or have equitable access within a society. Disabilities may be cognitive, developmental, intellectual, mental, physical, sensory, or a combination of these. They can be present from birth or acquired, and may be visible or invisible.

The [[United Nations]] Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities describes disability as including long-term physical, mental, intellectual, or sensory impairments which, in interaction with various barriers, may hinder full and effective participation in society on an equal basis with others. One estimate puts the number experiencing significant disability at about 1.3 billion people, or 16% of the global population.

Two main models seek to explain disability. The medical model treats disability as an undesirable medical condition requiring specialized treatment, and focuses on causes and cures, including assistive technology. The social model locates the limitation in society and focuses on accessibility and social and cultural attitudes. Critics of the social model argue that it underemphasizes biological constraints; its supporters emphasize the role of societal barriers.

The concept is debated among academics, the medical and legal worlds, and the disability community; one point of contention is whether to use person-first language (a person who is disabled) or identity-first language (a disabled person). Discrimination against disabled people is called [[Ableism|ableism]].

Historians dispute how disabled people were treated in antiquity. Some argue that the ancient Greeks practiced infanticide, citing the writings of [[Plutarch]], [[Plato]], and [[Aristotle]]. Others cite Hippocratic accounts and archaeological evidence of efforts to cure and assist disabled people, and argue that those writings reflect philosophical ideals rather than practice. Disability [[Activism|activism]] has pursued equal rights under the law and fostered a disability [[Culture|culture]].

Objects studied include integers, graphs, and statements in logic. The field excludes topics of continuous mathematics such as real numbers, calculus, and [[Euclidean geometry]]. It has been characterized as the branch of mathematics dealing with countable sets, whether finite or infinite, although there is no exact definition of the term.

Research increased in the latter half of the 20th century partly because of digital computers, which operate in discrete steps and store data in discrete bits. In university curricula, the subject appeared in the 1980s, initially as a computer science support course, and has since become a first-year course intended to develop mathematical maturity. The Fulkerson Prize is awarded for outstanding papers in the field.

Particular abnormal condition that adversely affects the structure or function of all or part of an organism and is not immediately due to external injury. Diseases are usually associated with specific signs and symptoms. They may be caused by external factors such as [[Pathogen|pathogens]] or by internal dysfunctions; for example, internal dysfunctions of the [[Immune system|immune system]] can produce immunodeficiency, hypersensitivity, allergies, and autoimmune disorders.

In humans, the term is often used more broadly for any condition that causes or can cause pain, dysfunction, distress, social problems, or death to the person affected or to those in contact with them. In this sense it sometimes includes injuries, disabilities, disorders, syndromes, infections, and isolated symptoms, though in other contexts these are treated as separate categories.

In developed countries, neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression and anxiety cause the most sickness overall. Pathology is the study of disease, including its causes.
