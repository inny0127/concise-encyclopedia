Indian religion and philosophy based on teachings attributed to [[The Buddha|the Buddha]], a wandering ascetic and religious teacher of the 6th or 5th century BC. The world's fourth-largest religion, it has an estimated 320 million to 540 million followers, about 4.1%–6.7% of the world population. It originated in the eastern Gangetic plain as a śramaṇa movement, spread through much of Asia via the [[Silk Road]], and reached the West in the 19th century.

According to tradition, the Buddha taught a path leading to awakening and liberation from dukkha (suffering, or unsatisfactoriness) by attaining [[Nirvana|nirvana]], the extinguishing of the passions. He described this path as a Middle Way between extreme asceticism and sensory indulgence. Widely observed teachings include the [[Four Noble Truths]], the Noble Eightfold Path, dependent origination, [[Karma|karma]], and the three marks of existence. Accounts of his life are inconsistent; tradition holds that he was born in Lumbini, achieved enlightenment at Bodh Gaya at age 35, taught for 45 years, and died about age 80 at Kushinagar.

The canon is vast, with textual collections in Sanskrit, Pali, Tibetan, and Chinese. Scholars generally recognize two major extant branches. [[Theravada]] (School of the Elders) emphasizes attaining nirvana to end the cycle of death and rebirth and is followed mainly in Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and Sri Lanka. [[Mahayana]] (Great Vehicle) emphasizes the bodhisattva ideal of working for the liberation of all sentient beings; it is the largest branch, predominant in East Asia, and includes Chan, Zen, Pure Land, and Nichiren. [[Vajrayana]], which incorporates esoteric tantric techniques, is practiced in Tibet and Mongolia and may be viewed as a separate branch or as part of Mahayana.

ENTRY:
Upward force exerted by a fluid, opposing the weight of a partly or fully immersed object. [[Pressure]] in a fluid increases with depth, so the pressure at the bottom of a submerged object exceeds that at its top, producing a net upward force. [[Archimedes' principle]] states that the force equals the weight of the fluid displaced by the object. An object with a [[Density|density]] greater than the surrounding fluid sinks, while a less dense object floats. Buoyancy is the most common driving force of [[Convection|convection]] currents, such as the spontaneous separation of air and water or of oil and water. The center of buoyancy is the center of gravity of the displaced fluid.

Method of final disposition in which a dead body is placed in the ground or in a structure such as a mausoleum; also called interment or inhumation. Some archaic and [[Early modern human|early modern humans]] buried their dead, and intentional burial with grave goods may be among the earliest detectable forms of religious practice. [[Neanderthal|Neanderthals]] used shallow graves furnished with stone tools and animal bones, at sites including Shanidar in Iraq, Kebara Cave in Israel and Krapina in Croatia, although some scholars argue that such burials may have been for secular reasons. Burial methods include natural or green burial, embalming or mummification, and containers such as shrouds, coffins and burial vaults. Alternatives include [[Cremation|cremation]], burial at sea and cryopreservation.
