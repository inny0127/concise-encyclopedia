ENTRY:
Indian religion, also called Jain Dharma, teaching a path to spiritual purity and liberation through disciplined nonviolence (*ahimsa*) toward all living creatures. It is guided by 24 *tirthankaras* ("ford-makers"), supreme teachers who have conquered the cycle of rebirth and attained omniscience (*kevala jnana*). Its goal is *moksha*, liberation from [[Karma|karma]].

Three principles form the core of Jain philosophy: *ahimsa*, *anekantavada* (non-absolutism, the view that reality has many aspects and no single viewpoint is complete), and *aparigraha* (non-possession). Purification of the soul is held to be achievable through the three jewels: right vision, right knowledge, and right conduct. Jainism teaches five vows: nonviolence, truth, not stealing, celibacy, and non-possession. They are called *mahavratas* (great vows) for monks and nuns, and *anuvratas* (small vows) for laypersons.

In tradition, the first *tirthankara* of the current cycle was Rishabhanatha. Historians date the 23rd, Parshvanatha, to the 8th or 7th century BCE. The 24th and last, [[Mahavira]], lived in the 6th or 5th century BCE, was a contemporary of [[The Buddha|the Buddha]], and was a central figure in the Śramaṇa movement of Greater [[Magadha]], which rejected the authority of the [[Vedas]].

Applying nonviolence, the community avoided agriculture and warfare, and many Jains became merchants and bankers in ancient and medieval [[India]]. It supported networks of temples, libraries, and charitable institutions. Jainism has between four and five million followers, mostly in India, with communities in North America, Europe, and East Asia. Two sub-traditions, the Digambaras ("sky-clad") and Śvētāmbaras ("white-clad"), differ on ascetic practices, gender, and canonical texts. Major festivals include Paryushana and [[Diwali]].

The city grew rapidly after independence, and migration from across Indonesia has shaped its population and culture. It has no ethnic majority; the largest groups include Javanese, Betawi, Sundanese, and Chinese Indonesians. Jakarta is Indonesia's financial and trade center and the center of national administration, and it hosts many corporate headquarters, media organizations, and universities, as well as the secretariat of [[ASEAN]].

Persistent problems include traffic congestion, flooding, land subsidence, air pollution, and pressure on water and waste services. In 2022 the Indonesian government began the legal process of moving the capital to Nusantara in East Kalimantan. Jakarta remains the capital until the transfer is formalized by a presidential decree.

Scottish physicist and mathematician who formulated the classical theory of electromagnetic radiation, the first theory to describe electricity, magnetism, and light as different manifestations of the same phenomenon. Born in Edinburgh, he graduated from Trinity College, Cambridge, in 1854, earning distinction in mathematics and the Smith's Prize. He later held the chair of natural philosophy at Marischal College in Aberdeen, where he proposed that the rings of Saturn were composed of numerous small particles, work that earned him the Adams Prize. From 1860 to 1865 he was professor of natural philosophy at King's College London. His 1865 paper "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field" showed that electric and magnetic fields travel through space as waves at the speed of light, and led to his prediction of the existence of [[Radio wave|radio waves]]. The four equations now known as [[Maxwell's equations]] appear in his 1873 *A Treatise on Electricity and Magnetism* and are regarded as the second great unification in physics, after that achieved by [[Isaac Newton]].

Maxwell was the first to derive the [[Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution]], describing the speeds of gas molecules, and was key in the creation of [[Statistical mechanics|statistical mechanics]]. In 1861 he presented the first durable color photograph, and showed that any color can be produced by mixing three primary colors: red, green, and blue. His 1863 paper "On Governors" is a foundation of [[Control theory|control theory]] and cybernetics. In 1867 he proposed the thought experiment known as [[Maxwell's demon]].

In 1871 he returned to Cambridge as the first Cavendish Professor of Physics and oversaw construction of the Cavendish Laboratory. He is regarded as a founder of modern electrical engineering, and his work laid foundations for relativity and [[Quantum mechanics|quantum mechanics]].
