ENTRY:
Post-harvest storage building for grain or seeds, usually raised above the ground. Granaries protect stored grain from [[Rodent|rodents]], pests, [[Flood|floods]] and bad weather, and they help dry grain to prevent mold. The oldest known granaries date to 9500 BC in the Jordan Valley. In [[Ancient Egypt]], grain was stored against years of scarcity. In the South Hams of southwest [[Great Britain]], small granaries were built on mushroom-shaped stumps called staddle stones. Historically a [[Silo|silo]] was a pit for grain, distinct from the above-ground granary. Modern granaries may use ventilation and temperature control to preserve grain quality, and modern grain farms often use steel granaries.

System of interconnected canals linking major rivers and lakes in North and East China. It was an important transport route between north and south in medieval and premodern China and is the longest artificial waterway in the world. The oldest sections were completed in the early 5th century BC during the [[Spring and Autumn period]] to supply the states of Wu and Yue. Emperor Yang of [[Sui dynasty|Sui]] completed the network in 609, but forced labor for its construction contributed to rebellions that hastened the fall of the Sui. The canal proved beneficial under the Tang dynasty, and each later dynasty treated its restoration as a duty. The capital moved to Khanbaliq (now Beijing) under [[Kublai Khan]]. The main stem links Beijing with [[Hangzhou]]. The section south of the [[Yellow River]] remains in heavy use by barges carrying bulk cargo. The canal is a [[World Heritage Site]].

Coarse-grained intrusive [[Igneous rock|igneous rock]] composed mostly of quartz, [[Feldspar|alkali feldspar]], mica and plagioclase. It forms when silica-rich [[Magma|magma]] with a high content of alkali metal oxides cools slowly and solidifies underground. Granite is common in the continental crust, where it occurs in intrusions ranging from dikes only a few centimeters across to batholiths exposed over hundreds of square kilometers. Granitic rocks are classified by the relative percentages of quartz, alkali feldspar and plagioclase, and true granite is rich in quartz and alkali feldspar. Most also contain [[Mica|mica]] or amphibole, although leucogranites contain almost no dark minerals. Granite is nearly always massive and tough, properties that have made it a widespread construction stone throughout human history.

ENTRY:
Fundamental interaction by which objects with [[Mass|mass]] or energy attract one another. The word comes from Latin *gravitas*, "weight." Gravity is the weakest of the four fundamental forces but has infinite range, and it is the primary driver of large-scale structure in the universe: attraction between primordial hydrogen and dark matter caused gas to coalesce into stars, galaxies, and clusters. The ratio of the gravitational attraction of two electrons to their electrical repulsion is 1 to 4.17×10⁴², so gravity can generally be neglected at subatomic scales. It determines the motion of satellites, planets, stars, galaxies, and even light.

[[Newton's law of universal gravitation|Newton's law]], formulated in 1687, describes gravity as an attractive force between any two bodies, proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. It remains a sufficient approximation for most applications. [[Albert Einstein]] proposed [[General relativity|general relativity]] in 1915, which describes gravity as the curvature of [[Spacetime|spacetime]] caused by the uneven distribution of mass. The most extreme example is a [[Black hole|black hole]], from which nothing, not even light, can escape. Gravitational waves, predicted by the theory, were directly detected in 2015. Scientists seek a theory of quantum gravity that would unify gravity with the other fundamental interactions.

On Earth, gravity gives weight to objects and contributes to lunar tides and weather patterns. In ancient Greece, [[Aristotle]] held that each classical element had a natural place in the universe and that a falling object's speed increases with its weight, a conclusion later shown to be false. In 628 CE the Indian astronomer Brahmagupta proposed that gravity is an attractive force that draws objects to the Earth.

ENTRY:
Spaceflight flyby that uses the gravity of a planet or other [[Astronomical object|astronomical object]] to alter a spacecraft's path and speed. Typically used to save propellant and reduce expense, the maneuver can accelerate or decelerate a spacecraft or redirect it. The first use came in 1959, when the Soviet probe Luna 3 photographed the far side of Earth's Moon. Interplanetary probes have used it from Mariner 10 onward, and the two Voyager probes made notable flybys of [[Jupiter]] and [[Saturn]]. [[Momentum]] gained by the spacecraft is matched by a loss to the body, in line with Newton's Third Law, but for a planet the change in speed is negligibly small. By minimizing reliance on chemical rockets, gravity assists have made ambitious deep-space missions feasible.

ENTRY:
Net acceleration imparted to objects by the combined effect of gravitation and the centrifugal force from [[Earth's rotation]]. Near the surface it is about 9.8 m/s², so a freely falling object gains about 9.8 meters per second in speed every second, ignoring air resistance. Its strength varies by around 0.7% across Earth's surface, from 9.7639 m/s² on the Nevado Huascarán mountain in Peru to 9.8337 m/s² at the [[Arctic Ocean]]. The standard value of 9.80665 m/s² was defined in 1901 by the third General Conference on Weights and Measures.

SI unit (symbol Gy) of [[Ionizing radiation|ionizing radiation]] dose, equal to the absorption of one [[Joule|joule]] of radiation energy per [[Kilogram|kilogram]] of matter. It measures the energy deposited in a unit mass of absorbing material and is used to measure delivered doses in [[Radiation therapy|radiotherapy]], food irradiation and radiation sterilization. The gray is important in predicting likely acute health effects, such as [[Acute radiation syndrome]], and is used with the [[Sievert|sievert]] to calculate equivalent dose. Adopted into the [[International System of Units]] in 1975, it is named after British physicist Louis Harold Gray, a pioneer in measuring X-ray and radium radiation. The older cgs unit, the rad, equals 0.01 Gy and remains common largely in the United States.

ENTRY:
Method of [[Animal husbandry|animal husbandry]] in which domestic [[Livestock|livestock]] range outdoors and consume wild vegetation. Grazing converts the cellulose in grass and other forage into meat, milk, wool and other animal products, often on land unsuitable for arable farming. Grazing livestock has been practiced since the beginning of agriculture, when [[Sheep]] and goats were domesticated by [[Nomad|nomads]] before permanent settlements around 7000 BC. In the [[Eurasian Steppe]], herds of sheep and horses were the primary means of food production for horseback nomads of Inner Asia. Ranching is the more common method today, sometimes supplemented with [[Hay|hay]] and fodder. Livestock grazing contributes to deforestation, extinction of native wildlife, soil degradation and desertification. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, grazing systems cover about 60% of the world's grassland.
