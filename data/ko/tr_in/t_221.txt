English chemist and physicist who contributed vastly to electrochemistry and [[Electromagnetism|electromagnetism]]. With little formal education, he became as an autodidact one of the most influential scientists in history. Born in Newington Butts, [[Surrey]], to a poor family, he apprenticed at 14 to a bookbinder and bookseller. In 1813 [[Humphry Davy]] appointed him Chemical Assistant at the Royal Institution, where he became its first Fullerian Professor of Chemistry, a lifetime position.

His research on the magnetic field around a conductor carrying a direct current established the concept of the [[Electromagnetic field|electromagnetic field]] in physics. He discovered the principles of [[Electromagnetic induction|electromagnetic induction]], diamagnetism, and the laws of electrolysis, and established that magnetism could affect rays of light. His inventions of electromagnetic rotary devices formed the foundation of electric motor technology. As a chemist he discovered [[Benzene|benzene]] and tetrachloroethylene, invented an early form of the Bunsen burner and the system of oxidation numbers, and popularized terms such as "anode," "cathode," "electrode," and "ion."

He conveyed his ideas in clear, simple language and thought in a visual manner; his mathematics did not extend beyond the simplest algebra. [[James Clerk Maxwell]] summarized his work and that of others in equations accepted as the basis of all modern theories of electromagnetic phenomena. He worked on lighthouses and protecting ships from corrosion, and with [[Charles Lyell]] investigated a colliery explosion at Haswell, showing for the first time that coal dust contributed to its severity. He refused to work on chemical weapons for the Crimean War, citing ethical reservations. He founded the Royal Institution's Friday Evening Discourses and popularized its Christmas Lecture Series. The SI unit of capacitance, the farad, is named after him.

Branch of economics concerned with how individuals and firms make decisions about allocating scarce resources, and with the interactions among them. It focuses on individual markets, sectors, or industries, as opposed to the economy as a whole, which is studied in [[Macroeconomics]]. Macroeconomics addresses growth, [[Inflation]], and [[Unemployment]] and national policies related to them, whereas microeconomics also examines the effects of policies such as changes in [[Tax]] levels on individual behavior.

One goal of the field is to analyze the market mechanisms that establish relative prices among [[Goods]] and services and allocate limited resources among alternative uses. Historically it has been pursued through general equilibrium theory, developed by Léon Walras in *Elements of Pure Economics* (1874), and partial equilibrium theory, introduced by Alfred Marshall in *Principles of Economics* (1890). Its formal name as a distinct field emerged in the 1930s.

Theory typically begins with a single rational, utility-maximizing individual, whose rationality means stable preferences that are both complete and transitive. The utility maximization problem, a constrained optimization in which the individual maximizes utility subject to a budget constraint, is the heart of consumer theory; its solution is called a Walrasian demand function. An alternative approach, revealed preference theory, takes consumer choice as primitive.

The theory of [[Supply and demand]] usually assumes perfectly competitive markets, with many buyers and sellers, none able to influence prices significantly. Where the assumption fails, as under monopoly, more sophisticated analysis is needed. Microeconomics also analyzes [[Market failure]], in which markets produce inefficient outcomes, as with public goods. Critics question assumptions of perfect information and rationality, citing behavioral findings such as loss aversion. Much modern macroeconomic theory is built on microfoundations, especially after the Lucas critique.

Laboratory instrument used to examine objects too small to be seen by the naked eye. The science of investigating small structures with one is called microscopy. Microscopes may be grouped by how they interact with a sample: by sending light or electrons through or onto it, by detecting photon emissions from it, or by scanning its surface with a probe. The most common, and first invented, is the [[Optical microscope]], which uses [[Lens|lenses]] to refract visible [[Light|light]] to form an observable image. Other major types are the fluorescence, [[Electron microscope|electron]], and scanning probe microscopes.

Simple microscopes date to the widespread use of lenses in eyeglasses in the 13th century. Compound microscopes, which combine an objective lens near the specimen with an eyepiece, appeared in Europe around 1620. The inventor is unknown, though Dutch spectacle makers and Cornelis Drebbel have been credited. [[Galileo Galilei]] built his own improved version after seeing Drebbel's instrument in Rome in 1624, and the name *microscopio* was coined by Giovanni Faber in 1625.

Microscopes were largely a novelty until the 1660s and 1670s, when naturalists began using them to study biology. [[Robert Hooke]]'s *Micrographia* (1665) had a huge impact. [[Antonie van Leeuwenhoek]] reached up to 300 times magnification with a single ball lens, rediscovered [[Red blood cell|red blood cells]], and reported micro-organisms on October 9, 1676. In 1893 August Köhler developed Köhler illumination, central to reaching the resolution limits of the light microscope. Frits Zernike's phase contrast (1953) and Georges Nomarski's differential interference contrast (1955) allowed imaging of unstained, transparent samples.

Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll built the first prototype electron microscope, a transmission type, in 1931. It uses electrons instead of light and electromagnets in place of glass lenses, allowing much higher resolution.
