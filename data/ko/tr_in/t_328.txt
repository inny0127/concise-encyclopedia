Capital of the Australian state of [[New South Wales]] and the country's largest city (pop., 2025: 5,638,830). The city grew from a British penal colony founded in 1788, when the First Fleet, led by Arthur Phillip, settled at Sydney Cove. The region has been home to [[Aboriginal Australians]] for at least 30,000 years, and its traditional custodians include the Darug, Dharawal and Eora clans. Landmarks include the [[Sydney Harbour Bridge]] and the World Heritage-listed [[Sydney Opera House]]. The city hosted the 2000 Summer Olympics.

Basic unit of organization within a sequence of speech sounds, such as a word. Syllables are often considered the building blocks of [[Word|words]]. A syllable usually consists of a nucleus, most often a [[Vowel|vowel]], which may be preceded by an onset and followed by a coda, usually a [[Consonant|consonant]]. The word derives from [[Ancient Greek|Greek]] *syllabē*, meaning "taken together."

Evolutionary theory, also called the endosymbiotic theory, on the origin of [[Eukaryote|eukaryotes]] from [[Prokaryote|prokaryotic]] ancestors. It holds that [[Mitochondria|mitochondria]] and [[Chloroplast|chloroplasts]] are endosymbionts descended from free-living prokaryotes that were engulfed by another prokaryote. Mitochondria appear to derive from Alphaproteobacteria, and chloroplasts from [[Cyanobacteria|cyanobacteria]]. The Russian botanist Konstantin Mereschkowski articulated the theory in 1905 and 1910, and Lynn Margulis substantiated it with microbiological evidence in 1967.

Close and long-term biological interaction between two organisms of different species. Harmonious balance of proportion or, in mathematics, invariance of an object under transformations such as translation, reflection, rotation or scaling. An object has reflectional symmetry if a line or plane divides it into mirror images, and rotational symmetry if it can be turned about a fixed point without changing shape. Symmetries that preserve a given property of an object form a [[Group (mathematics)|group]]. In calculus, an even function is symmetric about the y-axis, and in [[Linear algebra|linear algebra]] a symmetric matrix equals its transpose. In physics, symmetry principles underlie conservation laws, as stated in [[Noether's theorem]]. In nature, bilateral symmetry predominates in animals, while [[Starfish|starfish]] show radial symmetry. In art and architecture, symmetry creates visual harmony, as in classical Greek temples and [[Islam|Islamic]] geometric designs.

Invariance of a mathematical object under a set of operations or transformations, found in many branches of mathematics. For a structured object, a symmetry is a mapping of the object onto itself that preserves its structure. For a set with no additional structure, symmetries are bijections, which give rise to [[Permutation|permutation]] groups. For points in a plane with a metric, symmetries are [[Distance|distance]]-preserving bijections called isometries. In calculus, an even function satisfies f(x) = f(−x), and an odd function has rotational symmetry about the origin. The integral of an odd function from −A to A is zero, provided it is integrable. In [[Linear algebra|linear algebra]], a symmetric matrix equals its transpose, so only square matrices can be symmetric. A real symmetric matrix represents a self-adjoint operator on a real [[Inner product space|inner product space]].

ENTRY:
Group of interacting or interrelated elements that act according to rules or constraints to form a unified whole. A system is described by its boundaries, structure and purpose, and is shaped by its environment. Most systems are open, exchanging matter and energy with their surroundings, like a car or the [[Earth|Earth]]. A closed system exchanges energy but not matter, and an isolated system exchanges neither; the [[Universe]] is a theoretical example of the last. [[Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot]] pioneered the concept of a system in natural science in the 19th century, studying the working substance of steam engines in [[Thermodynamics|thermodynamics]]. In the late 1940s and mid-1950s, Norbert Wiener and Ross Ashby developed [[Cybernetics]], the mathematical study of control and communication. The word derives from [[Latin]] systēma, itself from Greek.
