Form of government in which political power is vested in the people of a state. Under a narrow definition, rulers are elected through competitive elections; broader definitions also require guarantees of [[Civil liberties|civil liberties]] and [[Human rights|human rights]]. No consensus exists on a precise definition.

In a direct democracy, the people deliberate and decide legislation themselves. In a representative democracy, the dominant form today, they elect officials to govern on their behalf, as in parliamentary or presidential systems. In liberal democracy, majority power is exercised within a representative framework, but a [[Constitution|constitution]] and supreme court limit the majority and protect minorities through individual rights such as [[Freedom of speech]] and freedom of association.political freedom]], and the Rule of law.|political freedom]], and the Rule of law.

The term appeared in the 5th century BC in Greek city-states, notably [[Classical Athens]], to mean "rule of the people," in contrast to aristocracy, "rule of an elite." Citizenship in democracies was initially restricted to an elite class and later extended to all adult citizens, in most modern democracies through the [[Suffrage|suffrage]] movements of the 19th and 20th centuries. Democratic systems have become more prevalent since the 19th century, in waves of democratization.

Abraham Lincoln described democracy as "Government of the people, by the people, for the people." Supporters point to improved health, education, and economic outcomes, and public opinion across regions tends to favor democratic systems. Critics cite vulnerability to demagoguery, short-term decision-making, and policy gridlock. According to the V-Dem indices and *The Economist* Democracy Index, less than half the world's population lived in a democracy as of 2022, and dissatisfaction with democratic performance is common even in established democracies.

The [[Kingdom of Kongo]] ruled near the mouth of the Congo River from the 14th to the 19th century. [[Leopold II of Belgium]] acquired rights to the territory in 1885 and called it the Congo Free State. Mass per unit volume of a substance, usually symbolized ρ. It is calculated as ρ = m/V, where m is mass and V is volume. Density is an intensive property: adding more of a substance increases its mass but not its density. ENTRY:
Instantaneous rate of change of a [[Function (mathematics)|function]]'s output with respect to its input. For a function of one variable, the derivative at a point is the slope of the tangent line to the graph there, and the tangent is the best linear approximation of the function near that point. Derivatives are defined as a [[Limit (mathematics)|limit]], and the process of finding one is called [[Differential calculus|differentiation]]. In Leibniz notation, named after [[Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz]], a derivative is written as a ratio of differentials. In physics, the first derivative of position with respect to time is velocity, and the second is [[Acceleration|acceleration]]. For functions of several variables, the derivative generalizes to the Jacobian matrix, which reduces to the [[Gradient|gradient]] vector for real-valued functions.

ENTRY:
Type of financial [[Contract|contract]] between a buyer and a seller. Its value depends on an underlier, such as a commodity, stock, bond, currency, interest rate or price index. Each derivative specifies the underlier, a future act such as a sale or purchase, a price, and a future date. Derivatives are used to hedge against price movements, to speculate, or to gain access to assets that are hard to trade. Common types include forwards, futures, options and swaps. The oldest recorded example, attested by [[Aristotle]], is a contract for [[Olive|olives]] made by the Greek philosopher Thales, who made a profit from it. After the [[2008 financial crisis]], there was increased pressure to move derivatives to exchanges.
