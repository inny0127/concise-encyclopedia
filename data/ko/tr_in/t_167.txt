Line that marks the boundary between one calendar day and the next, extending between the North and South Poles. It roughly follows the 180° meridian through the [[Pacific Ocean|Pacific Ocean]], deviating to pass around some territories and island groups. Modern form of the metric system and the world's most widely used system of measurement, abbreviated SI (from French *Système international d'unités*). It is the only system with official status in nearly every country and is used in science, technology, industry, and commerce. The International Bureau of Weights and Measures (BIPM) coordinates it.

The SI comprises seven base units: the [[Second]] (s, time), [[Metre]] (m, length), [[Kilogram]] (kg, mass), [[Ampere]] (A, electric current), [[Kelvin]] (K, thermodynamic temperature), [[Mole (unit)|mole]] (mol, amount of substance), and [[Candela]] (cd, luminous intensity). Twenty-two coherent derived units, always expressible as products of powers of the base units, have special names and symbols, and 24 prefixes, such as kilo- for 10³ and nano- for 10⁻⁹, produce decimal multiples and sub-multiples.

The base units are defined through seven defining constants with exact values: the speed of light, the hyperfine transition frequency of caesium, the Planck constant, the elementary charge, the Boltzmann constant, the Avogadro constant, and the luminous efficacy Kcd. The kilogram, for example, rests on the Planck constant h being 6.62607015×10⁻³⁴ J⋅s. A revision adopted by the 26th General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM) in 2018, effective May 20, 2019, redefined the kilogram, ampere, kelvin, and mole this way, completing a move away from physical artifacts, which can be lost, damaged, or changed.

The system arose from the diversity of units in the centimeter–gram–second (CGS) systems and a lack of coordination among disciplines. The CGPM, established by the Metre Convention of 1875, set the definitions and rules. An initiative begun in 1948 led to publication of the system in 1960.

Set of rules, norms and standards governing relations among states and other international actors. Also called public international law or the law of nations, it operates largely through [[Consent|consent]], because no universally accepted authority enforces it against [[Sovereign state|sovereign states]]. Its sources are international custom (general state practice accepted as law), [[Treaty|treaties]], and general principles of law recognized by most national legal systems. Violations, particularly of peremptory norms, can draw disapproval or coercive responses, including [[Diplomacy|diplomacy]], economic sanctions and [[War|war]]. Lassa Oppenheim defined it as "a law between sovereign and equal states based on the common consent of these states." Treaties such as the [[Geneva Conventions]] require national law to conform to treaty provisions.

Global system of interconnected computer networks using the [[Internet protocol suite]] (TCP/IP) to communicate between networks and devices. A network of networks, it links private, public, academic, business, and government networks by electronic, wireless, and optical technologies.

The internet carries services such as the hypertext documents of the [[World Wide Web]], [[Email|electronic mail]], internet telephony, streaming media, and file sharing. It has transformed telephone, radio, television, paper mail, and newspapers, and has enabled instant messaging, social networking, and online shopping. The web is only one of many internet services. About 5.5 billion people, 68% of the world's population, used the internet by 2024, with use correlating strongly with economic development.

Its origins lie in time-sharing of computer resources, packet switching, and computer networks. Paul Baran at RAND in the early 1960s and, independently, Donald Davies at the United Kingdom's National Physical Laboratory in 1965 pioneered packet switching. The [[ARPANET]], proposed by the Advanced Research Projects Agency of the [[United States Department of Defense]], first linked two nodes, at the [[University of California, Los Angeles]] and the Stanford Research Institute, on October 29, 1969. By the end of 1971, 15 sites were connected. Protocols for internetworking came from research commissioned in the 1970s by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) with universities and researchers in the United States, the United Kingdom, and France. The NSFNET backbone in the 1980s and privatization in the 1990s turned a research network into commercial infrastructure.

The internet has no single centralized governance, and each constituent network sets its own policies. The Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN) directs the [[IP address]] space and the Domain Name System, while the non-profit Internet Engineering Task Force standardizes the core protocols.

Cultural item, such as an idea, behavior or style, that spreads across the internet, now mainly through [[Social media|social media]] platforms. The term "meme" was introduced by [[Richard Dawkins]] in his 1976 book *The Selfish Gene* for a conceptual unit of cultural transmission, analogous to a gene. Mike Godwin coined "internet meme" in 1993 for the way memes proliferated through early online communities, including message boards, [[Usenet]] groups and email. The Soviet [[Red Army]] invaded eastern Poland on September 17 under a secret protocol dividing the country. The [[United Kingdom]] and [[France]] had declared war on Germany on September 3, but their aid was limited. Germany and the Soviet Union gained full control by October 6, 1939, ending the Second Polish Republic, though Poland never formally surrendered.

ENTRY:
Function reversing the effect of another function. In [[Mathematics|mathematics]], the inverse of a function f, written f⁻¹, exists if and only if f is injective (one-to-one). For a function f: X→Y, its inverse sends each element y in Y to the unique element x in X such that f(x) = y. For example, f(x) = 5x − 7 multiplies its input by 5 and then subtracts 7, so its inverse adds 7 to the output and divides the result by 5. The notation f⁻¹ was introduced by John Frederick William Herschel in 1813. Using [[Function composition|composition of functions]], f⁻¹(f(x)) = x for every x in the domain. The function f(x) = x² on all real numbers is not injective and has no inverse, but restricted to nonnegative reals it is invertible. The exponential function and the [[Logarithm|natural logarithm]] are inverses of each other.

Physical law of intensity inversely proportional to the square of distance from a source. Medieval conflict between church and state over the appointment of bishops, abbots and [[Pope|popes]] (1076–1122). It began in 1076 as a power struggle between [[Pope Gregory VII]] and Henry IV, then king and later Holy Roman Emperor, and lasted nearly 50 years. Under the Concordat of Worms, agreed in 1122 by Pope Callixtus II and Emperor Henry V, bishops swore an oath of fealty to the secular monarch, who held authority "by the lance" but left selection to the church. 16th-century Chinese novel of the gods-and-demons (*shenmo*) genre. Consisting of 100 chapters, it was first published in book form between 1567 and 1619, during the [[Ming dynasty]]. It is traditionally attributed to Xu Zhonglin and blends history, folklore, mythology, legend and fantasy. It is set in the decline of the Shang dynasty (1600–1046 BC) and the rise of the Zhou dynasty (1046–256 BC), and it romanticizes the overthrow of Di Xin, the last Shang ruler, by Ji Fa, who founded the Zhou dynasty. The novel is generally regarded as one of the most important works of Chinese gods-and-demons fiction, comparable to [[Journey to the West]].
