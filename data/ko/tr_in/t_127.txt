Inflammation of the [[Gastrointestinal tract|gastrointestinal tract]], including the stomach and intestine, also known as infectious diarrhea. Symptoms include diarrhea, vomiting, and abdominal pain, and may include fever and dehydration. Signs usually begin 12–72 hours after infection and typically last less than two weeks. Although it is unrelated to [[Influenza|influenza]], it is often called "stomach flu" in Canada and the United States.

The disease is usually caused by viruses, though bacteria, parasites, and fungi can also be responsible. In children, [[Rotavirus|rotavirus]] is the most common cause of severe disease; in adults, [[Norovirus|norovirus]] and *Campylobacter* are common causes. Norovirus accounts for about 90% of viral gastroenteritis outbreaks among adults in America, which occur where groups of people spend time close together, as on a [[Cruise ship|cruise ship]]. The disease spreads through improperly prepared food, contaminated water, or close contact with an infected person.

Dehydration is a common complication. Reactive arthritis follows about 1% of *Campylobacter* infections and Guillain–Barré syndrome 0.1%. Repeated infections, typical where sanitation is poor and malnutrition common, can cause stunted growth and long-term cognitive delays.

Treatment centers on fluids: oral rehydration solution for mild or moderate cases, and intravenous fluids for severe ones. Continued [[Breastfeeding|breastfeeding]] is recommended for breastfed infants and [[Zinc|zinc]] supplementation for children. [[Antibiotic|Antibiotics]] are generally not needed, except for young children with fever and bloody diarrhea. Prevention includes hand washing with soap, clean water, and proper disposal of human waste, and the rotavirus vaccine is recommended for children.

In 2021 there were 4.67 billion cases and 1.17 million deaths worldwide. Children and people in the developing world are affected most; in 2011 there were about 1.7 billion cases, causing about 700,000 deaths of children under five. Adults are less affected, partly because of acquired immunity.

[[Algorithm]] for solving systems of linear equations by row operations, named after [[Carl Friedrich Gauss]]. The three elementary row operations are swapping two rows, multiplying a row by a nonzero number, and adding a multiple of one row to another. These operations do not change the solution set. Forward elimination reduces the matrix to row echelon form, which shows whether a system has no solution, a unique solution or infinitely many, and back substitution then finds the solution. Range of social, psychological, cultural, and behavioral aspects of being a man (or boy), woman (or girl), or portraying a third gender. Gender often corresponds to sex, though a [[Transgender|transgender]] person may identify with a gender other than the sex assigned at birth. Most cultures have a gender binary; those outside it may fall under the umbrella term [[Non-binary|non-binary]]. Some societies have third genders, such as the hijras of [[South Asia]] and two-spirit persons native to North America.

The English word comes from [[Middle English]] *gender*, from Anglo-Norman and Middle French *gendre*, from [[Latin]] *genus*, meaning "kind", "type", or "sort". Before the mid-20th century it was uncommon to use it for anything but grammatical categories. A distinction between biological sex and gender then developed in psychology, sociology, sexology, and [[Feminism|feminism]], and in the 1970s feminist scholars adopted it to separate "socially constructed" aspects of male–female differences from "biologically determined" ones. The subject is contested. Critics of the sex-gender distinction argue that it obscures the biological reality of sex, which they define by the two gamete types, and that policies prioritizing gender identity disadvantage women in athletics. Reported prevalence of gender dysphoria has risen among youth since the early 2010s; proposed explanations include greater social acceptance, peer and online influence, and changes in clinical practice, and their relative weight is disputed. The social sciences often treat gender as a social construct, while the natural sciences investigate how far biology shapes it. Estimates of how common [[Intersex|intersex]] conditions are range from about 0.018% of births to about 1.7%, depending on definition.

ENTRY:
Basic unit of heredity in biology, with two meanings. The Mendelian gene is the classical unit of heredity and refers to any heritable trait; in *The Selfish Gene*, [[Richard Dawkins]] argues that it is the unit of evolution. The molecular gene, the sense more common in biochemistry and molecular biology, is a sequence of nucleotides in [[DNA]] that is transcribed to produce [[RNA]]. There are two types: protein-coding genes and non-coding genes. During [[Gene expression|gene expression]], DNA is first copied into RNA, which can be directly functional or serve as the template for synthesis of a protein.

Genes and their regulatory sequences are mainly responsible for determining an organism's physical traits, or phenotype. A gene can acquire [[Mutation|mutations]], producing variants known as alleles that may be expressed as different traits. Populations evolve when allele frequencies shift, mostly through [[Natural selection|natural selection]] and [[Genetic drift|genetic drift]].

Early work in molecular genetics suggested that one gene makes one protein. Genes producing repressor RNAs were proposed in the 1950s, and by the 1960s textbooks included noncoding genes for functional RNAs such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. Most definitions treat a gene as a transcription unit and regard regulatory sequences as controlling expression without being part of the gene; Kostas Kampourakis emphasizes that a gene must encode a functional product, so transcribed pseudogenes and transcription noise do not qualify. Some publications still restrict the term to protein-coding genes.

The discovery of introns in the 1970s showed that many eukaryotic genes are much larger than their functional products imply. Typical mammalian protein-coding genes are about 62,000 base pairs long in the transcribed region, and since there are about 20,000 of them they occupy about 35–40% of the mammalian genome. In the [[Human genome|human genome]], the approximately 19,000 to 20,000 protein-coding genes make up about 1.5% of the total DNA sequence.
