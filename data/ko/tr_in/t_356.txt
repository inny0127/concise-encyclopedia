ENTRY:
Symbol, typically a letter, that refers to an unspecified mathematical object. The variable is said to denote the object, and any valid candidate for the object is the value of the variable. The values a variable can take are usually of the same kind, often numbers, and may form a set such as the set of [[Real number|real numbers]]. The term was originally used chiefly for the argument of a [[Function (mathematics)|function]], whose value could be thought of as varying within the function's domain. A variable that stays fixed during the solution of a problem is often called a parameter, and one to be determined is an unknown: in the [[Quadratic equation|quadratic equation]] ax² + bx + c = 0, a, b, and c are parameters and x is the unknown. In [[Mathematical logic|mathematical logic]], a variable is a symbol that either represents an unspecified constant of the theory or is quantified over.

The earliest uses of an unknown quantity appear in the Moscow Mathematical Papyrus (c. 1500 BC). In [[Euclid's Elements|*Euclid's Elements*]] (c. 300 BC), mathematics was described geometrically. Diophantus of Alexandria used a syncopated algebra in his *Arithmetica* (c. 200 AD), calling an unknown number ζ. At the end of the 16th century, François Viète introduced representing known and unknown numbers by letters, using consonants for known values and vowels for unknowns. In 1637, [[René Descartes]] introduced the convention of x, y, and z for unknowns and a, b, and c for knowns, which remains in common use.

Star whose brightness, as seen from [[Earth]], changes over time. The variation may be intrinsic, when the star's luminosity itself changes as it swells and shrinks, or extrinsic, when an orbiting companion eclipses it. Changes range from under an hour to multiple years. Most stars oscillate to some degree; the [[Sun|Sun]] varies by about 0.1% over its 11-year solar cycle, while a [[Supernova|supernova]] can briefly outshine an entire galaxy. Of the 58,200 variable stars cataloged as of 2023, the most common are pulsating variables.

ENTRY:
Statistical measure of how far a set of numbers spreads from its average. For a random variable X, variance is the [[Expected value|expected value]] of the squared deviation from the mean, and the [[Standard deviation|standard deviation]] is its square root. Variance is written as σ² and is non-negative, with zero meaning that all values are identical. Its units are the square of the data's units, which is why the standard deviation is commonly reported. Its advantage is that it is easier to manipulate algebraically; the variance of a sum of uncorrelated random variables equals the sum of their variances. Population variance uses all observations, while sample variance estimates it from a subset. Variance is central to [[Statistics|statistics]], including descriptive statistics and hypothesis testing.

Complex conducting tissue in vascular plants, consisting of xylem and phloem. These tissues transport water, minerals and nutrients throughout the plant, and their cells are long and slender, somewhat like pipes. Vascular tissue is arranged in strands called vascular bundles, which also contain supporting and protective cells. In stems and roots, xylem lies toward the interior and phloem toward the exterior. Between them is the [[Vascular cambium|vascular cambium]], a meristem that divides to form new xylem and phloem, increasing the girth of the plant. In [[Leaf|leaves]], xylem faces the upper surface and phloem the lower, which is why [[Aphid|aphids]] are typically found on the undersides of leaves.

Oldest scriptures of [[Hinduism]], a large body of religious texts originating in ancient India. Composed in Vedic [[Sanskrit]], they constitute the oldest layer of Sanskrit literature. There are four Vedas: the Rigveda, the Yajurveda, the Samaveda, and the Atharvaveda. Each has four subdivisions: the Samhitas (mantras and benedictions), the Brahmanas (commentaries on and explanations of rituals, ceremonies, and sacrifices), the Aranyakas (texts on rituals, ceremonies, sacrifices, and symbolic sacrifices), and the [[Upanishads]] (texts discussing meditation, philosophy, and spiritual knowledge). Some scholars add a fifth category, the Upāsanās (worship).

The word *veda* means "knowledge". The Vedas are *śruti* ("what is heard"), distinguishing them from other religious texts, called *smṛti* ("what is remembered"). Hindus consider them *apauruṣeya*, "not of a man, superhuman" or authorless: revelations of sacred sounds and texts heard by ancient sages after intense meditation. They have been transmitted orally since the 2nd millennium BC with the help of elaborate mnemonic techniques. The [[Mantra|mantras]], the oldest part, are recited today for their phonology rather than their semantics and are regarded as "primordial rhythms of creation".

The Samhitas were composed between c. 1500 and 1200 BC (Rigveda books 2–9) and between 1200 and 900 BC for the other three, and contain invocations to deities such as [[Indra]]. The Brahmanas date from about 900 BC to about 700 BC. The older principal Upanishads were composed between 800 BC and the end of the [[Vedic period]] and are the foundation of Hindu philosophical thought.

Indian philosophical schools differ in their positions. The six "orthodox" (*āstika*) schools of [[Hindu philosophy]] acknowledge the primal authority of the Vedas, whereas *śramaṇa* traditions such as Charvaka, Ajivika, Buddhism, and [[Jainism]], which did not, are called "heterodox" (*nāstika*).

Edible part of a plant consumed by humans or other animals as food. In its original and still common meaning, the term covers all edible plant matter, including flowers, fruits, stems, leaves, roots, and seeds. An alternative definition, applied somewhat arbitrarily and often by culinary and cultural tradition, may include savory [[Fruit|fruits]] such as [[Tomato|tomatoes]] and squash, flowers such as [[Broccoli|broccoli]], and seeds such as pulses, while excluding foods derived from plants that are fruits, flowers, nuts, and cereal grains. "Fruit" has a precise botanical meaning, a part developed from the ovary of a flowering plant, so eggplants, bell peppers, and tomatoes are botanically fruits. In *Nix v. Hedden* (1893), the U.S. Supreme Court ruled unanimously that a tomato is correctly identified, and taxed under the Tariff of 1883, as a vegetable, while acknowledging that botanically it is a fruit.

Vegetables can be eaten raw or cooked and are mostly low in fat and carbohydrates but high in [[Vitamin|vitamins]], minerals, and dietary fiber. Many nutritionists encourage eating plenty of fruit and vegetables, with five or more portions a day often recommended.

ENTRY:
Measurement of speed in a certain direction of motion. It is a fundamental concept in [[Kinematics|kinematics]], the branch of [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]] that describes the motion of physical objects. Velocity is a vector quantity: both magnitude and direction are needed to define it. Its scalar magnitude is called [[Speed|speed]] and is measured in meters per second (m/s) in the [[International System of Units|International System of Units]] (SI). For example, "5 meters per second" is a scalar, whereas "5 meters per second east" is a vector. If speed, direction, or both change, the object is undergoing an [[Acceleration|acceleration]]. Constant velocity means motion in a straight line at a constant speed; a car circling at a constant 20 kilometers per hour has constant speed but not constant velocity.

The average velocity of an object over a period of time is its change in position, Δs, divided by the duration, Δt. The instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval approaches zero, that is, the [[Derivative|derivative]] of position with respect to time: v = ds/dt. In one dimension, the area under a graph of velocity against time is the displacement, so the [[Integral|integral]] of the velocity function is the displacement function. Average velocity is always less than or equal to average speed.

Acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time (a = dv/dt), and velocity is the integral of acceleration. For constant acceleration, v = u + at, where u is the velocity at t = 0, and v² = u² + 2(a·x), known as the Torricelli equation. In [[Special relativity|special relativity]], velocity remains a vector but is constrained by the speed of light as the universal maximum.
