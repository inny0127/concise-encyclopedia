Music of a people or community, traditionally passed on orally, and the later popular genre derived from it. Folk music has been defined in several ways: as music transmitted by [[Oral tradition|oral tradition]], music with unknown composers, music on traditional instruments, music about cultural or national identity, or music performed by custom over a long period. It has been contrasted with commercial and classical styles. The term originated in the 19th century, as an extension of [[Folklore|folklore]], a word coined in 1846 by the English antiquarian William Thoms. Observers find a precise definition elusive, since folk music cannot be clearly differentiated in purely musical terms. One widely used definition is simply "Folk music is what the people sing."

Early scholars, including Cecil Sharp and [[Béla Bartók]], saw it as the music of the country as distinct from that of the town, uninfluenced by art music and by commercial and printed song. Lloyd instead stressed economic class, associating it with a lower class.

Traditional folk music was learned by memory by largely illiterate people. It is culturally particular, tied to a region or culture, and often serves social cohesion among immigrant groups. It commemorates historical and personal events, marks holidays such as Christmas, Easter, and May Day, and may accompany weddings and funerals. This second folk revival reached a zenith in the 1960s and includes fusion genres such as folk rock and folk metal. In U.S. English it shares its name, and often its performers and venues, with traditional folk music.

Expressive culture shared by a particular group of people, culture, or subculture. Folklore includes oral traditions such as tales, [[Legend|legends]], [[Proverb|proverbs]], poems, and jokes; material culture, such as traditional building styles; and customary lore, covering actions based on folk beliefs, including [[Folk religion]], and the forms and rituals of festivals, weddings, folk dances, and initiation rites. Each of these, singly or in combination, is a folklore artifact or traditional cultural expression, and equally essential is its transmission from one region to another or one generation to the next. Folklore is not typically gained from formal schooling but is passed along informally, through verbal instruction or demonstration. Its academic study is called folklore studies or folkloristics.

The word, a compound of *folk* and *lore*, was coined in 1846 by the Englishman William Thoms to replace "popular antiquities" or "popular literature." *Lore* comes from Old English *lār*, "instruction." Thoms applied *folk* only to rural, frequently poor, and illiterate peasants. A more modern definition is a social group of two or more people with common traits who express shared identity through distinctive traditions, whether a nation or a single family. Folklorists describe the content as things people make with words (verbal lore), with their hands (material lore), and with their actions (customary lore). Folklore is not limited to what is old: new groups, such as surfers, motorcyclists, and computer programmers, create their own. Its artifacts are passed along as a rule anonymously and always in multiple variants. The folk group is community-based, in contrast to high culture, where a named artist's work is protected by copyright law.

Folklorists seek the significance of beliefs, customs, and objects for the group, since they would not be passed along without continued relevance. Meaning can shift: the [[Halloween]] celebration of the 21st century differs from the All Hallows' Eve of the Middle Ages, and the cleansing rituals of Orthodox Judaism, originally good public health in a land with little water, now signify identification as an Orthodox Jew for some people.

Folklore began to distinguish itself as a discipline during romantic nationalism in Europe. Johann Gottfried von Herder, in his writings of the 1770s, presented oral traditions as organic processes grounded in the locale. After the German states were invaded by Napoleonic France, Germans systematized recorded folk traditions and used them in nation building, an approach embraced by Finland, Estonia, and Hungary as they sought independence. Nineteenth-century European scholars focused on the oral folklore of rural peasants, regarded as survivals of the past; the best-known collection is *Kinder- und Hausmärchen* of the [[Brothers Grimm]] (first published 1812). American folklorists, led by [[Franz Boas]] and Ruth Benedict, studied Native American cultures and treated the totality of their customs and beliefs as folklore, aligning American folkloristics with [[Cultural anthropology|cultural anthropology]] and ethnology, while European folkloristics stayed with the humanities. The term *folkloristics* became widely used in the 1950s, and the American Folklife Preservation Act (Public Law 94-201) was passed in January 1976.

Any substance consumed by an organism for nutritional support. Food is usually of plant, animal, or fungal origin and contains essential nutrients such as carbohydrates, fats, proteins, vitamins, or minerals, which the organism's cells assimilate to provide energy, maintain life, and support growth. It is mainly composed of water, lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates; fat is the most energy-dense component. Inadequate or imbalanced intake leads to malnutrition, including undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies, and overnutrition-related conditions such as obesity.

In an ecosystem, food forms a web of interlocking chains with primary producers (algae, plants, bacteria, and protists that acquire energy from sunlight) at the bottom and [[Apex predator|apex predators]] at the top. Primary consumers are herbivores, and secondary consumers are the carnivores that eat them. Humans are omnivores, finding sustenance in vegetables, fruits, cooked meat, milk, eggs, mushrooms, and seaweed. The three main nutrients plants absorb from soil are nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium.

Cereal grain is a staple food that provides more food energy worldwide than any other type of crop. Corn ([[Maize|maize]]), wheat, and rice account for 87% of all grain production worldwide. Fungi and bacteria are used to prepare fermented foods such as bread, wine, cheese, and yogurt.

Human food is classified by content or by processing. Most systems include four basic groups: vegetables and fruit, cereals and bread, dairy, and meat; the Food and Agriculture Organization and World Health Organization use a system of eighteen or nineteen classifications. Humans generally use [[Cooking|cooking]] to prepare food. The industrial [[Food industry|food industry]] supplies most food energy through intensive agriculture and complex processing and distribution systems. Conventional agriculture relies heavily on [[Fossil fuel|fossil fuels]], and food and agricultural systems account for as much as 37% of total greenhouse gas emissions. The food system also bears on sustainability, biological diversity, economics, population growth, water supply, and food security.

Force is central to [[Classical mechanics|classical mechanics]], where it underlies all three of Newton's laws of motion. The second law relates net force to mass and acceleration (F = ma). The rotational version of force is [[Torque|torque]].

ENTRY:
Ecosystem characterized by a dense community of trees. More than 800 definitions of forest are used around the world. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations defines it as land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and a canopy cover of more than 10 percent, excluding land predominantly under agricultural or urban use.

By that definition, forests covered 4.14 billion hectares, approximately 31 percent of the world's land area, in 2025. They are the largest terrestrial [[Ecosystem|ecosystems]] of Earth by area, and 45 percent of forest land is in the tropics. Forests account for 75% of the gross primary production of the biosphere and contain 80% of Earth's plant biomass. They form distinct [[Biome|biomes]]: boreal forests in subarctic climates, tropical moist and dry forests around the equator, and temperate forests at middle latitudes. In the [[Amazon rainforest]], trees release water from their leaves in anticipation of seasonal rains, so the wet season begins two to three months earlier than the climate would otherwise allow.

[[Deforestation]] occurs mostly in tropical forests, and the vast majority is driven by the production of four commodities: wood, beef, soy, and palm oil. Over the past 2,000 years, forest cover in Europe fell from 80% to 34%, and large areas have also been cleared in China and the eastern United States, where only 0.1% of land was left undisturbed. Almost half of Earth's forest area (49 percent) is relatively intact, while 9 percent lies in fragments with little or no connectivity.

Manufacturing process that shapes [[Metal|metal]] using localized compressive forces from a [[Hammer|hammer]] or die. Forging is classified by temperature as cold, warm or hot. Hot forging, above the metal's recrystallization temperature, can be done more quickly and precisely, while cold forging typically causes work hardening. Forged parts range in weight from less than a kilogram to hundreds of metric tons. Smiths have made kitchenware, hand tools, edged weapons, [[Cymbal|cymbals]] and [[Jewellery|jewelry]] for millennia. Since the [[Industrial Revolution]], forged components have been used wherever high strength is required, and forging is now a major worldwide industry.
